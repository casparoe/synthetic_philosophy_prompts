Two friends of mine — one an economist who works on famines, the other a historian of modern Britain — spent most of a four-hour drive arguing about whether famines are properly described as natural disasters or as political crimes. I was in the back seat, I have no stake in the outcome, and I could not decide who was making the better case; each of them seemed to be winning at different moments. They have asked me to referee, and what I want is a careful point-by-point judgment rather than a verdict from the gut. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) The economist: The natural event is never the whole story. A blight kills a crop; it does not decide who eats. People die when the food that exists fails to reach them, and that is a distributional outcome with human authors. Bengal in 1943 is the clean case. Whatever happened to the harvest, it was nothing like the collapse in food actually reaching the people who died, and the deaths were concentrated by occupation — sharecroppers, fisherfolk, boatmen, people whose capacity to command food through work, sale, or transfer came apart. And that came on top of deliberate decisions: boats and rice stocks pulled out of the coastal districts to deny them to an invasion that never arrived, priority in distribution for the war economy and the classes the government wanted to keep in place, and a provincial government that never formally declared a famine at all. Ireland has the same shape. The blight was real, but grain and livestock kept leaving the country throughout, and London refused to stop it. In the Great Leap Forward the weather was bad, but so were the procurement quotas, the continued exports, the inflated harvest reports, and the refusal to admit what was happening in the villages. Natural disaster names the trigger; political crime names the killing.

(2) The historian: I grant you most of the facts. I do not grant you the framing. First, trigger is doing quiet work. Had the blight not come, the famine would not have come; that is causation, and relabelling a cause a trigger does not make it less of one. Second, crime imports culpability, and culpability needs more than a counterfactual. Yes, the Bengal authorities could have diverted shipping from the war in Asia; yes, Beijing could have accepted a slower industrialization. But those options were available only in the thin sense of being physically possible. The question is whether anyone could reasonably have been expected to choose them, given what they were being asked to trade off. Third — and this is what bothers me most — most famine deaths are not starvation deaths. They are cholera, malaria, dysentery, tuberculosis, in a population that has been weakened and set in motion. In Bengal, more than half the famine deaths came in 1944, after the food crisis had eased, and they were caused by disease. The chain from a quota or an export licence to a corpse runs through epidemiology, and I do not see how you read a crime off the far end of it.

(3) The economist: Thin feasibility is the only kind there is, morally speaking. If you could have done otherwise and did not, the fact that doing otherwise was hard, or that it conflicted with something you valued more, is not an excuse — it is the content of the charge. And you have just handed me the alternatives: a different allocation of shipping, a slower industrialization. Those were not mysteries; they were live options set aside as too expensive, which is precisely what it means to have chosen the deaths. As for disease: the epidemics follow the hunger, the migration, the collapse of wages, and the breakdown of the local economy. You cannot quarantine the policy from the mortality by pointing at the pathogen. And nothing in the charge requires a villain. Negligence, deference, groupthink, a treasury that outranks a province — that is what a political crime usually looks like.

(4) The historian: Then the category has swallowed everything. If the test is that you could have done otherwise and knew or should have known, it covers the 1918 influenza response, cholera in Victorian cities, the early years of AIDS, and probably road deaths. A category that takes in everything explains nothing and indicts no one in particular. And your framework has a worse problem: Ireland. There was a free press, a sitting parliament with Irish members, and a relief apparatus that actually ran — public works, soup kitchens, an extension of the poor law — and a million people died anyway. If democracy and information are what prevent famine, Ireland is the case that should not have happened. So either your criterion is too loose, or the mechanism you keep appealing to is not the mechanism at work.

(5) The economist: Ireland is not the counterexample you think it is. The Irish had no meaningful political voice. Executive power sat with a lord lieutenant appointed from London; most of the Irish members of parliament were landlords or landlords' sons; the people dying were not the constituency of the people deciding. That is not a refutation of the democracy-and-famine thesis, it is a specification of it. What prevents famines is not the existence of a parliament but a political cost that lands on the people who hold power. And the category is too broad is not an argument until you can say where it stops. Show me the famine that was nobody's decision and I will happily call it a natural disaster.

(6) The historian: Fine. Then let me put a boundary on it. A famine is a political crime when the excess mortality flows from a decision that (i) was made by identifiable agents, (ii) was made with knowledge of the risk, and (iii) was reversed only when the interests of those agents were themselves threatened. On that test, the Great Leap Forward is a crime and then some; Bengal is a crime with a colonial accent; Ireland is something else — imperial neglect and a failure of obligation rather than a decision to let people die; and the 1918 influenza falls outside entirely. Your test cannot draw those lines. Mine can. If yours cannot separate Ireland from the Great Leap Forward, it is not a test, it is an attitude.

(7) The economist: Then your test lets every government off. Knowledge of the risk is precisely what officials deny; the cadres reported bumper harvests, the famine was never declared, the reports coming out of the villages were suppressed. A criterion that requires proving what was in a minister's head hands the advantage to whoever controls the records. And condition (iii) is worse: it says a government is culpable only if it moved once its own skin was at risk, which makes your criterion an account of when the killers happened to be frightened, not of what they did.

That is where the drive ended. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, in the manner of someone who wants to know where each argument actually fails rather than who to side with. For each of the seven moves, say what is right in it, what is overstated or wrong, and whether it succeeds against the move it was aimed at. Then settle the substantive questions underneath the quarrel: (a) is the natural trigger a cause or a mere condition, and does the answer change the moral verdict? (b) Which of their two criteria — the feasible-alternative test or the identifiable-decision-plus-knowledge-plus-self-interested-reversal test — is the better analysis of what makes a famine political, and does either survive the cases that were supposed to test it, namely Bengal, Ireland, the Great Leap Forward, and the 1918 influenza? (c) Is the democracy-and-information thesis an empirical generalization or something close to a definitional truth, and what does Ireland do to it? (d) Is political crime a category with a boundary, or, as the historian suspects, an attitude? Finally, say what both of them are missing that neither said, and if you must name the one who is closer to right overall, name him and say why — but let that verdict come after the point-by-point work, not instead of it.
