The claim I'm defending: expected utility maximization is the wrong frame, and the right rule is to maximize the *geometric* expectation of your final utility — the E[log X] thing. Not "log utility happens to be my utility function," something stronger: that the form of the rule is geometric, and that this is why a positive expected value bet can be one you should refuse.

Here's the example, and it's the thing that'll be on the slide. You're offered, repeatedly, a bet where you win 50% of your current bankroll or lose 40% of it, 50/50. Arithmetic EV is +5% of your bankroll per round. Bet the whole roll every time and your bankroll grinds down to nothing almost surely. Bet a quarter of your roll each round and you typically grow about 0.62% per round — even though the arithmetic expectation of *that* strategy is only +1.25% per round. And now the part I expect to get killed on: the one-shot version, win 50% or lose 40% of everything, once, no repeats, has positive EV, and I want to say it's a bad bet. I also want to apply this to non-monetary stuff — years of life, health, whether to take a risky career move — which means taking log of *utility*, and utility can be negative, and it's only defined up to adding a constant so shifting where the zero is changes what the log does. I have a hand-wave for that and I know it's not good. That's definately my weakest point.
