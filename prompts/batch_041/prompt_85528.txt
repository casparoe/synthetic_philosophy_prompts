I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the ethics of public memory, and I serve as a graduate research assistant to the Commission on Public Memory and Civic Reconciliation. The Commission is an advisory body that rules on proposals concerning memorials, archives, apologies, and forgetting. Its mandate is framed in terms of a duty to remember and a case for forgetting, but its rulings have not settled how those commitments trade off. I am preparing a memo on a new case, and I want to know what its track record commits it to. I am giving you the facts and outcomes of its earlier rulings, but not its written rationales.

The earlier rulings are as follows. (1) A city proposed a permanent memorial that would list the names of victims but not the names of perpetrators. The Commission approved it. (2) Descendants of a perpetrator petitioned to have his name removed from a public truth-commission archive. The Commission rejected the petition. (3) A survivors' association asked that its members' testimonies be sealed for fifty years. The Commission approved the request. (4) A town proposed to burn its archive of a violent past as a ritual of forgetting. The Commission rejected the proposal. (5) A university proposed to remove the name of a eugenicist from a building. The Commission approved the removal. (6) A museum proposed an exhibit pairing victims' testimonies with perpetrators' letters, with the victims' experiences foregrounded. The Commission approved the exhibit.

The new case is this. A city wants to build a living memorial to a historical massacre. The memorial would be a garden, intentionally designed to be dismantled after twenty-five years; the land would then revert to ordinary public use, and no permanent plaque, monument, or museum would remain. The city argues that the act of remembering should not harden into a permanent identity of victimhood, that the victims' descendants support the plan, and that the community wishes to move forward. The Commission must decide whether to approve the proposal, reject it, or approve it with conditions.

Please predict the Commission's verdict. In doing so, (a) identify the rule or rules its earlier rulings are jointly consistent with, considering at least a victim-centered duty to remember, a reconciliation-consequentialist approach, a historical-truth-preservation approach, and a right-to-forget or autonomy approach; (b) say what each candidate rule predicts about the living memorial; (c) name the earlier ruling that is the closest analog and explain why; (d) give your best guess at the verdict and the reasons for preferring it; (e) if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, state what principle the Commission would be adopting by approving rather than rejecting, or vice versa; and (f) name a further case that would best distinguish between the surviving rules.
