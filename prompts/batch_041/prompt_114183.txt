I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I am drafting a section of my dissertation prospectus on the evidential status of behavioral criteria for mentality, using Turing's 1950 paper in *Mind* as the historical anchor. What I keep tripping over is the relationship between two of the objections Turing himself raises and then dispatches in quick succession: the argument from consciousness, which says that no amount of conversational performance could establish that a machine is genuinely thinking rather than merely simulating thought, and the argument from extrasensory perception, which says that the imitation game's results could be contaminated by telepathic influence passing between interrogator and machine (or, symmetrically, that a machine might be unfairly handicapped by a human interrogator drawing on non-sensory information). These look like they belong to the same family, since both treat the game's behavioral evidence as potentially misleading, but they seem to pull in opposite directions: the first says the test could be passed without thought, the second says the test could be failed or passed for reasons having nothing to do with the machine's cognitive capacities at all.

What I would like from you is a careful comparison of how Turing handles each objection and of whether his handling is consistent. In particular, I want to know whether his response to the ESP objection — which I read as treating telepathy as a straightforwardly empirical hypothesis to be controlled for experimentally, by running the game under conditions that screen out any such leakage — presupposes a different conception of what the test is doing than his response to the consciousness objection does. Is the imitation game, on Turing's own account, an operational definition of thinking, an evidential proxy for an independently real property, or something in between, and does the ESP discussion quietly shift it from one of these to another? Consider also the counterfactual: if we granted for the sake of argument that telepathy were a real and reasonably reliable phenomenon, would the imitation game survive as a criterion of machine intelligence at all, or would it collapse into a test of whatever channel happens to be carrying the information? I would be grateful if you would resist rehearsing the standard secondary literature on the Turing test and instead work through the tension as it appears in the two arguments themselves, taking a clear position on which of the two concessions does more damage to the game and why.
