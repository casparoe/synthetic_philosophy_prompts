Ok, so I need a dialogue for the Indian epistemology chunk of my history of phil course, and I keep getting the same recycled textbook exchanges back, so lets do something fresh.

Two characters. The first is a Naiyayika — a Nyāya epistemologist. Nyāya says there are four pramāṇas, four basic means of knowledge: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogy (upamāna), and testimony or "word" (śabda). The second is a Buddhist logician in the lineage of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, who says there are only two, perception and inference, and that comparison and testimony aren't really seperate sources of knowledge at all — they're just perception and inference in disguise. Both of them are sharp. Please dont make the Buddhist a punching bag; let them land real hits and make the Naiyayika work for it.

Keep it concrete rather than a debate about counting. Two things I want them to actually fight over:

(a) Upamāna. Don't let them coast on the old cow-and-gavaya example that's in every intro. Have them invent a fresh case: someone has never run into a certain kind of thing, gets told that the word for it names something like a familiar thing they already know, and then later meets one and thinks "ah, so that's what that word was for." The Buddhist's line is that this is just perception plus an inference about what people mean by their words; the Naiyayika has to show upamāna delivers something neither of those could. If the Naiyayika genuinely can't, let that round go to the Buddhist.

(b) Śabda. Here's the case I want them to chew on: someone asks an AI assistant a factual question and just takes the answer as knowledge. Nyāya's story for testimony is that it works when the speaker is an āpta — a reliable person who actually knows the thing and intends to tell you the truth. A chatbot arguably has neither knowledge nor intentions. So does the AI case show that testimony really isn't a seperate pramāṇa after all (Buddhist wins), or can the Naiyayika rescue it? Don't let either of them dodge the case.

They should respond to each other's actual points, not talk past each other, and it's fine to end without a tidy winner if that's where it honestly lands.
