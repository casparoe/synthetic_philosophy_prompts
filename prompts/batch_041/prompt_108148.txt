As the outgoing president of our criminal defence bar association, I have to give the closing address at the annual dinner, and I want to take as my subject the case that both makes us proud and makes us uneasy: the defence of the Nuremberg defendants. What we inherit from it is the image of counsel standing beside men the whole world had already judged; but Nuremberg is also the standing example of victors' justice, and the defence there had a real complaint — the war-crimes counts were old law, but the charter that defined crimes against peace and crimes against humanity was drafted by the victorious powers and then applied by them. Give me six minutes that honour the work of representing people the world has convicted in advance, without pretending that the retroactivity objection was baseless and without letting that concession slide into the view that the whole thing was a show trial; the speech should say something real about what, if anything, could make such a tribunal legitimate given that objection. Bear in mind that the defence's two loudest complaints — that the law was new, and that the victors had done the same things — pull in opposite directions, and I would rather the speech be honest about that than fudge it. The room is half defence lawyers, who bristle at any suggestion that the judgments were legitimate, and half guests — families, a couple of prosecutors, one retired judge — who bristle at any suggestion that they were not; the organizers have asked for something celebratory, which I cannot quite give them. Give me one sentence about Tokyo, where the same law was imposed by a single occupying commander, that does not simply repeat the Nuremberg point. I would rather not quote anyone: I do not trust myself to get the words right, and this room would know. Write the speech.
