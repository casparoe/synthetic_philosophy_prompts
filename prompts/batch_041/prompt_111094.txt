I keep running into a line from Nietzsche that people around me repeat like a proverb: history has to serve life, not knowledge for its own sake. Most recently a friend used it to argue that I should stop reading about a grim stretch of my family's past — dwelling on it is unhistorical, he said, and you need a certain amount of forgetting just to be able to act at all. I honestly can't tell whether that's a deep point or just a bit of sophistry with a famous name attached. So: what are the different precise things that slogan could actually be claiming, from the near-truism that someone who remembered every single detail of every day couldn't function, up to something much stronger about how a whole culture should stand toward its past? I know Nietzsche sorts history into monumental, antiquarian and critical kinds, though I've never understood how that maps onto the slogan. Lay the readings out as a menu for me — trivial, substantive, plainly false — say which of the three kinds each reading belongs to or matches, and tell me which reading my friend was most likely invoking when he told me to stop reading, and whether that reading actually gets him his advice, because that's the part I care about.
