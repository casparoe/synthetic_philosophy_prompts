Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Credences have to satisfy the probability axioms, and what makes a non-probabilistic credence irrational is the Dutch book: if your numbers don't cohere, there's a package of bets, each of which you'd call fair, that loses you money for sure. As far as I'm concerned that's the fundamental justification for probabilism — I don't think there's some deeper, purely epistemic story about why those particular axioms are the right ones. (2) Same deal for updating: conditionalization is justified by the diachronic Dutch book, where someone who knows your habits exploits the gap between your future credences and your current conditional credences. (3) Credences are not betting dispositions. The odds at which I'd bet on my flight being fine are nowhere near my actual confidence that the flight is fine — I'm risk averse and the stakes move me around a lot. Betting odds are a useful model of credence, not what credence is. (4) Imprecise credences are permissible, at least under deep ambiguity; sometimes the honest state is a range and a single sharp number is false precision. (5) Permissivism — the same evidence can leave more than one credence rationally permissible, so two people staring at the same data can both be rational with different numbers. (6) I'm an evidentialist: what makes a credence rational is how well it's supported by the evidence, and the point of a credence is to be accurate, like a good estimate. Prudential facts about what a clever bookie could do to me don't feel like they could be the whole story about epistemic rationality.
