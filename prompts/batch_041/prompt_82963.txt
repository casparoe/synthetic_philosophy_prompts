Setting: 300-level philosophy of mind seminar, 14 students, 90 minutes, and I get one shot at making them feel what's actually at stake in Dennett's real patterns / three stances business — that a pattern being real has something to do with whether a description of it pays for itself, and that the design and intentional stances can be cheap, powerful and correct for reasons that have nothing to do with there being a mind in there. I dont want them to hear the words physical stance, design stance, intentional stance until the debrief.

Here's my sketch. Tell me if the mechanics are sound or if you'd rebuild them.

Three black boxes the room has to predict. Box A is a deterministic mechanism I run behind a screen off a hidden rule sheet — transparent if you inspect it, expensive to write down. (My placeholder: output is 1 or 0 depending on the parity of the number of syllables in the last word I said out loud. Anything that behaves that way works.) Box B is a regulator with a hidden setpoint; predicting it as if it's trying to hold something at a level nails it. Box C is a confederate — my TA, or a planted student — playing a simple repeated game against the room and adapting.

The scoring is the whole thing and I want it tight. Teams of 3-4. Each team starts with 20 coins. A prediction costs 1 coin. A team's theory of a box costs 1 coin per word and lasts them for the rest of that box, amortized. A correct prediction pays 3. So a one-word theory that's right half the time can beat a fifteen-word theory that's always right, or loose to it, depending on how long the box runs — I want them doing that arithmetic themselves and getting it wrong the first time. No fair listing the last ten outputs and calling it a theory.

Halfway through I change Box B's setpoint and quietly alter Box A's rule once. Then the last move: each team stakes everything left on one of three claims about Box C — it has a mind, it doesn't, or there's no fact of the matter — and only then do I show them what C actually is.

The debrief needs to map the moments onto the ideas. A is the physical stance, real but never free. B is the design stance, nested inside the physical, good only inside its operating conditions, and it's what breaks when I turn the knob. C is the intentional stance, cheapest and most robust, and it works on something with no inner life — which is exactly what they'll want to resist. The word count is the compression criterion, and the team that wins may be the one whose theory is both best and, in some sense, about nothing. I also want one more thing in there that I half-remember Dennett having a name for, something like a free-floating rationale: a pattern that's normative for a system — what the system is doing is for something — even though nothing inside the system represents it at all. If that fits, work it in.
