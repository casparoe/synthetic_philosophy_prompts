The question was worth 20 marks and read: "In Shubik's dollar auction, a $1 bill is auctioned and both the highest and the second-highest bidder pay what they bid, though only the highest bidder receives the bill. (a) Explain why bidding commonly escalates past $1. (b) Some popular accounts claim the game shows that people are irrational. Assess this claim using the distinction between the sunk-cost fallacy and escalation of commitment."

The student wrote:

The dollar auction was introduced by Martin Shubik in 1971. The rules are: a $1 bill is up for auction, bidding opens at 5 cents, and when the auction ends the highest bidder pays their bid and receives the dollar, while the second-highest bidder also pays their bid but receives nothing. The escalation comes out of what the second-highest bidder is facing. Say I have bid 95 cents and you have bid $1. If I stop, I lose 95 cents and you break even. If I bid $1.05 instead, I pay $1.05 but I get the dollar, so I only lose 5 cents. Losing 5 cents is better than losing 95 cents, so I raise. You then face the same arithmetic one step down, and you raise again. Each raise is the cheapest way of reducing a loss that has already been locked in, so bidding runs past the point where the prize is worth anything, and by then both bidders are losing money while the auctioneer is guaranteed a profit, since the two highest bids together always add up to more than $1.

This is normally given as an example of the sunk cost fallacy. The bidders carry on because they have already put money in and can't stand to see it go to waste — the same pattern you see with the Concorde, with firms throwing money at failing projects, and with governments in wars they can't win. Shubik's point, though, is that the trap doesn't require any irrationality to get started: bidding a nickel for a dollar is obviously sensible, and it only becomes a trap because the rules make the second-highest bidder liable for their bid. So the escalation is really an artefact of the payoff structure rather than of anyone's psychology, even though the psychology (loss aversion, not wanting to be the sucker, wanting to look consistent) clearly makes it worse in real cases. The organisational research on escalation of commitment, such as Arkes and Blumer's 1976 knee-deep in the big muddy studies, documents exactly this pattern in capital investment decisions.

What the game does not show is that every individual step is irrational. Each bid above the dollar is a rational attempt to lose less, and that is the actual paradox: rational steps, disastrous destination. The practical lesson is that you should never sit down at the table in the first place, because once you're in there's no way back.
