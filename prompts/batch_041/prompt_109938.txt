I'm trying to work out how much of the lecture to trust, because the professor's summary seems to pull in two directions at once and I can't tell whether that's Hume or the professor. Here's roughly what he said, and I checked my notes against a friend's recording so I'm fairly sure I've got his main claims down:

"Hume's essay is really just a long argument that beauty is in the eye of the beholder. Since beauty isn't a property of objects but a feeling in the viewer, there's no fact of the matter about whether one poem is better than another, and Hume admits this openly. What saves him from total relativism is a bit of a sleight of hand: he says that if you collect enough experienced critics and take their joint verdict, that verdict becomes the standard of taste. So his answer to 'is beauty objective?' is no, but he swaps objectivity for consensus, roughly the way a democracy swaps truth for the will of the majority. He also gives a checklist of five qualities the ideal critic has to have — delicacy, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense — and says that anyone who has all five will end up making the same judgments. The nice example is the two men who taste leather and iron in a barrel of wine and turn out to be right when a key with a leather strap is found at the bottom. So in the end he does think some works are genuinely better than others, which sits awkwardly with his opening claim that taste varies without limit. Most philosophers since have read the essay as an honourable failure: a subjectivist who wanted a standard and couldn't quite admit he couldn't have one."
