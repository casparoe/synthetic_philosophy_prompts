Next Thursday I'm giving a 25 minute talk at a small cross-lab workshop — it's our neuromorphic hardware group, the computational neuro lab that's all-in on Bayesian-brain / probabilistic-inference models of cortex, and one historian of cybernetics who's coming because a mutual friend told her I was going to say something about Von Neumann and she is apparently already annoyed about it. My talk defends what I've been calling the reliability-first reading of Von Neumann's late work on the nervous system (the unfinished Silliman lectures that got published after he died, plus the 1950s stuff on building dependable machines out of undependable parts). The standard line is that Von Neumann thought the brain is a computer and spent his last years trying to figure out which kind of computer. I want to argue that's backwards: the computer is the thing being explained, and the brain is the constraint on the explanation. His real question was what kind of computation is even possible on a substrate that's slow, low-precision, and doesn't cleanly separate memory from computation the way a stored-program machine does — and the only part of the analogy he was willing to keep was the reliability problem, i.e. how you get globally dependable behaviour out of parts that fail locally. My further claim, and this is the bit I'm actually nervous about, is that reading him this way generates a testable prediction: cortical errors should look like independent noise that cancels across trials rather than systematic bias, which is roughly what you see when the same stimulus is presented over and over. A guy in my lab said that last part might just be me restating what noise-correlation studies already assume, and I genuinely can't tell if thats a fair hit or not.
