Ok so I'm running a small reading group on decision theory and we're doing the Pasadena game next week, and I want to open with the spec rather than the puzzle. Rough setup, in case it's useful: fair coin flipped until it lands heads; if the first heads is on flip n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n when n is even. The expected value is the series of plus and minus 1/n terms, which doesn't settle anywhere — reorder it and you can get any real number, or ±infinity. (Summed in the natural order you get ln 2, which is itself part of the problem.) So the standard rule, pick the option with the highest expected value, just goes silent.

What I want from you is the desiderata list for a theory of rational choice under this kind of uncertainty: the requirements a theory has to meet to count as adequate here, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and your call on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. Don't feel bound by my list, these are just the cases I keep coming back to:

- Pasadena itself. Must the theory return a verdict, or is principled silence fine? And if it's silent, is playing permissible, impermissible, or neither? Silence that quietly becomes permissiveness seems like a bug.
- Reordering. The value depends on the order of summation, so any rule that picks an order is picking a convention. Is order-invariance non-negotiable?
- The same game with a dollar added to every payoff (some people call this the Altadena game, I think). Still no expectation, but it obviously beats Pasadena and beats a sure dollar. Any theory that says never play an undefined-expectation game refuses it, which looks like a reductio. Same trick with a million added everywhere.
- Pasadena mixed 50/50 with its mirror image, where every payoff has the sign flipped. Each component has no expectation; the mixture has a perfectly well-defined expectation of zero. Can a theory treat the parts as unrankable and the whole as worth zero? Is that coherent?
- St Petersburg as a contrast. Infinite expectation, so a different failure mode, and intution says play. Does a fix for Pasadena keep St Petersburg playable?
- Finite approximations. Every truncation of the game has a well-defined value, but which truncation you take changes what the values converge to. Must the theory say how the infinite case relates to its finite approximations, and can it do that without privileging a summation order?
- Forced choice. The usual dodge is that you can always refuse a bad bet. That doesn't work when you have to pick between two undefined options. Does the theory handle that, or does it only handle opt-in lotteries?
- Description-invariance. Same gamble, described as one draw from an infinite partition versus a sequence of flips resolved in a different order. Verdicts shouldn't turn on bookkeeping.
- Money versus utility. If the payoffs are already utilities and utility is bounded, the game is well-defined and boring. Is bounding utility a principled constraint or a fiat that dodges the case?
- Completeness versus restraint. Refuse to privilege any summation order and you probably get massive incomparability; a theory that declares almost everything incomparable stops guiding choice. Is completeness a requirement or a luxury?

Then flag any pair or trio you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect order-invariance plus always giving a verdict plus respecting dominance is one such set, but tell me if I'm wrong and why.
