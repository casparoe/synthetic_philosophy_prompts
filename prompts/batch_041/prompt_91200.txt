I'm in a cognitive science reading group and we keep circling back to Dennett's four grades of creature — the ones that are just hardwired, the ones that learn by reinforcement, the ones that can test a hypothesis before they act on it, and the ones that get to think with the discoveries of other minds (tools, words, other people's mistakes). Every time we try to sort an actual case, we end up shouting past each other. Is a honeybee Darwinian, or is the hive doing something Skinnerian? Is a crow that drops stones into a tube of water a Popperian, or just a very good Skinnerian? Is a chess engine Popperian because it searches, or Gregorian because its evaluation function is a crystallized human discovery? And is a large language model any of the four, or a fifth thing nobody named? My suspicion is that the four labels are hiding the real questions, and that I'd learn more from the questions.

So please build me an actual decision tree — a sorting instrument, not a summary of the labels — that takes a concrete description of a creature and lands it on one of the four, with every branch node being a question that genuinely separates the grades, phrased so I could answer it about a specific animal or machine without already knowing where it leads. Eight to ten branch questions, and whatever leaves fall out: the four canonical ones plus any cells the questions open up that the canonical four don't fill. The angle I care about is culpability. What I find interesting is that a Darwinian creature can't really be wrong — it just dies; a Skinnerian can be wrong and only finds out by paying for it; a Popperian can be wrong and know it in advance, letting the hypothesis take the hit instead of the body; and a Gregorian creature can be wrong on behalf of others, or let someone else's error stand in for its own. So wherever the tree forks, I want the fork to be about what it takes for the creature to be wrong and who foots the bill. If the four grades really do form a ladder — each presupposing the one below — say so and show me where the ladder is forced by the questions rather than just assumed; and if you find a cell that nothing occupies, don't quietly drop it, tell me whether it's empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's looked. Flag any two labels your tree can't seperate cleanly instead of fudging it. No reading list, just the instrument. And take a definite line on the hard cases — the bee, the crow, the chess engine, the LLM — rather than telling me it depends.
