I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on the philosophy of mathematics, and I have spent the past few weeks trying to turn a seminar-room intuition into a clean argument. The intuition is this. Consider Hilbert's hotel: a hotel with countably many rooms, every one of them occupied. A new guest arrives; the manager shifts the occupant of room 1 to room 2, the occupant of room 2 to room 3, and so on, and installs the newcomer in room 1, and everyone is housed. What strikes me as decisive is not the familiar counterintuitiveness but an outright inconsistency: every room was occupied, and yet a room is found for the new guest, so some room must be simultaneously occupied and available. If the reply is that no single room is both, since each guest simply moves on, then the manager's procedure is an infinite sequence of relocations with no final step, and the new guest is never actually put to bed; the hotel therefore either harbours a contradiction or never completes the task. I have been reading about supertasks — Thomson's lamp, switched on and off infinitely often in two minutes, with no determinate state at the end — and it seems to me that the hotel is a supertask of precisely this kind, and that the impossibility of a last step is exactly what the finitist has maintained all along. I notice the same machinery lying underneath the claim that there are as many perfect squares as natural numbers, which Galileo already treated as an apparent paradox, so I take the problem to be structural rather than a quirk of hotels.

My question is how to write this up as a serious piece of philosophy of mathematics. I want the argument to be more than a rhetorical flourish: I would like to state the reductio precisely, say exactly which premise the infinitist must give up, and explain why the supertask analogy carries the weight I think it does. I would also like to know how the argument bears on the choice between potential and actual infinity, and whether it really gives the finitist anything he did not already have. I am not looking for a reading list — I have the standard material — so much as for help getting the logic right and for a frank assessment of whether the argument, as I have stated it, is one I can actually defend.
