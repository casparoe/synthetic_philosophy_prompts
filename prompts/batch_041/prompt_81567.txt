I write about war and ethics for a general audience, and before I file a piece I want to get my own instincts on record. Here is what my gut says about who may be killed in war. A conscript on the unjust side of a war of aggression, who has been told his country is defending itself and has no way to learn otherwise, is not someone the just side may target once he is out of the fight, though of course they may shoot back while he is shooting; and I don't think he acts wrongly when he fires. A volunteer on that same side, who joined for the paycheck and knows perfectly well the war is a land grab, is liable in a way the conscript is not; killing him is no different from killing a gunman in a bank. A soldier on the unjust side who has thrown down his rifle and is walking home is not a target, even if he would re-enlist tomorrow. A soldier on the just side who shoots that man in the back has committed murder; a soldier on the just side who shoots a fleeing volunteer in the back has not. A conscript on the unjust side who is defending his own village against the just side's advance, an advance that will kill his family if it succeeds, seems to me to be doing something permissible, and I cannot say the just side may kill him as easily as it may kill the volunteer. And a fifteen-year-old pressed into service on the unjust side is not liable to be killed, though I am less sure he is blameless.

Is there a principle that gets all of this, or do I have to give something up? If there is one, state it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, say what it implies about a case I have not listed — a soldier on the just side who has concluded his own war is unjust and keeps fighting anyway — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot keep.
