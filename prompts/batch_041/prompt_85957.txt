I am preparing to teach the long discussion of wine in Books I and II of Plato's Laws, and I have reached the point where I can follow the individual moves but cannot see how the argument is meant to hang together. I will paraphrase the stretch I have in mind rather than quote it, since I am working from a translation and do not want to garble the wording.

The Athenian Stranger is talking with Kleinias the Cretan and Megillus the Spartan, both of whose cities prohibit or severely restrict drinking. Against them he argues that wine is not simply a danger to be forbidden. It is a drug that produces fearlessness and a kind of childishness of soul, and precisely for that reason it can serve the lawgiver as both a test and a training. Just as a man's courage is tested by fear, so his self-control is tested by pleasure; and since it would be ruinous to test citizens by exposing them to real dangers and real temptations, the lawgiver can use the drinking party as a controlled substitute. In a properly conducted symposium, with a sober and wise president in charge, the drinkers can be observed as their inhibitions fall away, and what is revealed is what was already there: wine does not make a man vicious, it removes the concealment behind which vice was hidden. If the drinker becomes worse, more cowardly, more shameless, more talkative, more disobedient, that too is diagnostic, and the lawgiver learns what sort of souls he has to educate.

Around this, the Athenian frames the human being as a puppet of the gods, pulled by several cords, some of them iron and some golden. The iron cords are the passions; the golden one is the calculation of what is good for the whole, which the lawgiver must persuade the citizen to follow. Wine, on this picture, loosens the soul and makes it more malleable, but malleable in either direction. The Athenian also insists that the lawgiver must legislate not only for external conduct but for the whole of virtue, and that the drinking party, properly regulated, is a kind of gymnasium of the soul. In Book II he goes further and sketches actual legislation: an age-graded set of rules for drinking, a sober president, songs and toasts of the right kind, and the claim that wine was given to humankind as a remedy for the harshness of old age, to soften the soul and restore a kind of youthfulness.

What I cannot get straight is the relation between the three things the passage seems to be doing at once. It is a piece of legislation, a theory of the soul, and a defence of a practice that the interlocutors' own lawgivers rejected. My difficulty is not that I think the Athenian is confused; it is that I cannot see how the test and the training are supposed to be the same operation. If wine simply reveals a character that is already fixed, then the symposium is a diagnostic, and the lawgiver learns who is who, but nothing has been trained. If wine is supposed to train self-control, then the drinker must be resisting the wine, and the model of revelation is wrong, or at least incomplete. The sober president makes the party safe, but he also seems to make it irrelevant to the drinkers' virtue: it is he, not they, who exercises self-control.

There is a second puzzle. The Athenian treats courage as the virtue tested by fear and self-control as the virtue tested by pleasure, but he also says that wine makes the drinker fearless. If courage is the right attitude towards fear, then a drug that removes fear does not produce courage; it produces something that merely resembles it. So the drinker who is brave when drunk may be showing us something about his nature, but he is not showing us his courage. Does the Athenian have a way of distinguishing the two, or is he trading on the ambiguity?

I would like a careful reading of the passage that does three things. First, reconstruct the argument's structure: what the Athenian is claiming, what he needs for the claims to connect, and where the load-bearing premises are. Second, address the test-and-training puzzle directly, and say whether the puppet image helps or hurts; in particular, say whether wine is supposed to loosen the iron cords, the golden cord, or both, and what follows for the educational proposal. Third, say what the passage implies about the relation between law and the soul, that is, whether the Athenian is arguing for a kind of legal paternalism about character, or whether the drinking-party discussion is better read as a methodological demonstration, a way of showing the interlocutors that their own prohibitionist lawgivers were legislating against the symptom rather than for the virtue. I am not looking for a summary of Plato's views on drink, and I do not need citations; I need the argument laid out and the tensions named.
