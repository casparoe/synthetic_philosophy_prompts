ok, I need help with something that's been slowly ruining my reading group. I'm a 4th-year PhD student in history (20th c. science and technology, mostly) and we've been argueing about hindsight for like a year now and I'm convinced both sides are talking past each other.

Two camps. Camp 1, the de-biasers: hindsight bias is a real, measurable distortion, and the narrative fallacy is the same error one level up. Their ideal is history written from the evidence the actors actually had. Any causal claim that only starts to feel compelling once you already know the ending gets flagged as contaminated until someone shows it survives without the ending. They think there's a fact of the matter about how likely things looked at the time, and a fact of the matter about which causal claims are outcome-invariant.

Camp 2, the retrospective crowd: all of that is a fantasy. Hindsight isn't a lens you can just take off. The past is only intelligible from the standpoint of someone who already knows how it came out, and "how it looked at the time" is itself something you build after the fact — you can't even decide what counted as evidence for what without leaning on the ending. On this view inevitability is a feature of explanation, not a bug, and knowing the outcome is precisely what lets you find the causes, so hindsight is a resource rather than a contaminant.

What I want is the document these two should of produced a year ago: the joint statement they could both actually sign. So:

- A numbered list of claims both sides accept — about hindsight, about narrative, about what historical explanation is even trying to do here. Real claims, not truce-y platitudes.
- A disagreements table, with the residual disagreements stated in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own position. Where a single word is doing two jobs (bias, inevitability, the actors' perspective), say so.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. My guesses are whether the actors' evidence set is well-defined independently of the outcome; whether narrative form itself does causal work or merely presents it; and whether the de-biasing ideal is self-undermining, since the historian trying to see it as they did still knows the ending. Add any I'm missing.
- For each crux, a test or case both sides would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. Two protocols my group cooked up that I'd like you to use if they fit:
  (1) the sealed-ending protocol — take the Challenger launch, and here are all the facts you need: the contractor's engineers recommended against launching because of the cold and the O-rings, NASA managers disregarded the warnings about low temperature, it went up on a freezing morning in January 1986 and broke apart about a minute in. Give two historians the record with everything after the decision cut off and see whether their reconstructions converge.
  (2) the twin-history protocol — write the episode twice, once under the actual outcome and once under a plausible alternative (a successful launch), keeping the pre-decision evidence identical, and see which causal claims come through unchanged.
- And tell me straight how much of this is a verbal dispute. My suspicion is that hindsight-as-bias and hindsight-as-resource might not even be about the same thing.
