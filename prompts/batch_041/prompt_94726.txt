I am leading next month's session of a fortnightly discussion group — about a dozen people, mostly working software engineers, a couple of economists, no formal training in philosophy or game theory beyond what popular writing supplies. The subject is the cluster of ideas that has come to be called Moloch: situations in which a collection of agents, none of whom wants the outcome, are driven to it anyway, and especially the variant that behaves like a treadmill, where each participant must keep running merely to hold position. They have all met the word, and most of them use it as a loose synonym for capitalism or for the thought that things are bad and nobody is to blame. I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard. What I want from you is not an explanation of the idea but a plan for the explanation.

Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and the reason for each ordering: which distinction has to be in place before the next one can be understood at all, and what breaks if I run them the other way round. They can all recite the prisoner's dilemma and will reach for it within a minute, so I want the plan to exploit that reflex and then make it insufficient, rather than opening by correcting it. Four things the plan has to handle, because they are the ones I keep fumbling. First, the difference between a trap that dissolves under a binding agreement among the parties and one that survives universal agreement and universal compliance — where nobody is cheating and the race continues anyway, so that the agreement buys nothing, including the limiting case in which no one is worse off in absolute terms but all the slack has been consumed. Second, the case of a single agent with no competitor that nonetheless exhibits the same structure; I need the audience to be able to say which feature of the concept is doing the work there, since if a lone agent can be trapped, multipolarity cannot be essential to it. Third, what would have to be added to any example to turn it into a story about a villain, and why that addition changes the concept rather than illustrating it. Fourth, how the audience should mark the boundary between these traps and ordinary collective-action problems with an easy and well-known fix — I would rather they under-apply the idea than over-apply it.

Then the one example that should carry the whole session, with an account of what it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack; if the obvious candidates, a fishery or an arms race, will not do the job, say so and say why. Then the misconceptions you expect them to arrive with and to form during the session, each paired with the point in the sequence where it should be headed off — including the reification problem, where the personification of the trap is doing real work and is also actively misleading them, and the plan should say where it helps and where it has to be withdrawn. Then one question I can put to the room at the end that would separate people who have got the concept from people who have merely acquired the vocabulary. Then what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, saying so out loud, so that the session does not collapse into the questions I am not equipped to answer in ninety minutes. Finally, tell me what kind of thing I am teaching them — a mechanism, a prediction, a moral diagnosis, a piece of rhetoric — because I would like them to leave knowing what they have learned. No sources or further reading; I want the plan itself.
