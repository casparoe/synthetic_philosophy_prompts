I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been sitting in on a friend's consciousness reading group, mostly because the arguments are more interesting than code review. Last week we worked through a case that has been bothering me. Two expert coffee tasters, whom the group called Chase and Sanborn, both drink the same cup. Both report that the coffee tastes exactly as it always has: same bitterness, same acidity, same fruit, same body. Their discriminative judgments are indistinguishable from their judgments on previous occasions. But Chase now says he loves the coffee and Sanborn now says he hates it. The group then asked us to compare this with a second scenario in which their preferences have changed but their taste experiences have not changed at all. In that second scenario, every physical fact about the coffee, every neural fact about the tasters, and every behavioral fact about what they say and do is stipulated to be identical to the first scenario. The friend who presented the case then offered the following argument.

(1) If two situations are identical in every physical, neural, and behavioral respect, then there is no fact of the matter about whether they differ in qualia. (2) The first coffee-taster scenario and the second are identical in every physical, neural, and behavioral respect. (3) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the tasters' qualia changed. (4) If there can be no fact of the matter about whether qualia changed in a case designed specifically to isolate qualia from preferences and reports, then qualia are not genuine properties of experience. (5) Therefore qualia are not genuine properties of experience. (6) If qualia are not genuine properties of experience, then there is no fact of the matter about whether the coffee tastes bitter to me rather than sweet, or whether it tastes like anything at all. (7) That conclusion is absurd. There is plainly a fact of the matter about what my coffee tastes like. (8) Therefore at least one of (1), (2), or (4) is false.

I would like to keep (7) and, if possible, (1); the idea that two situations could differ in qualia while differing in no physical, neural, or behavioral respect seems to me to require a kind of magic. But I also cannot see how the argument gets from the tasters' case to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about the taste of my coffee. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that I must distinguish qualia from the felt character of experience, I would like to know exactly what that distinction buys me and what it leaves unexplained. If the answer is that (2) is not really identical in every relevant respect, I would like to know which respect is missing and whether that respect is one I can independently identify. And if the argument is valid but one of its premises is simply too expensive to keep, I want to know which premise is cheapest to give up and what the rest of my view of consciousness looks like after I give it up.
