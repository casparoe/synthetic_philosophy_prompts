I teach a 50-minute intro philosophy session to about 18 undergrads, no science background assumed, and I want them to actually feel the force of the "what is life?" problem rather than hear a lecture about it. Here's the mechanic I have in mind — build it out, and swap it for something better if you've got one.

Setup: a quarantine checkpoint. They're the sample review board for a Mars sample-return mission, working in groups of three. Each group gets a stack of specimen cards — a virus, a prion, a frozen mule embryo, a mitochondrion kept alive in culture, a self-replicating clay crystal, a lichen, a bacterial spore, a dormant tardigrade, a simulation of a metabolising cell, a synthetic protocell that reproduces but can't mutate — and I'll put all the relevant features on the cards so nobody has to look anything up. The board works from a pre-written charter with three bins, "living," "dormant," "inert," and a hard consequence attached: living gets preserved and protected, inert gets sterilised. I want the charter's criteria to be operational and jointly unsatisfiable in places — must maintain a proton gradient, must be capable of evolution by natural selection, must have a boundary membrane, must have a metabolism — so they have to apply them and walk straight into a case that meets two critera and violates the third, or whose bin changes if you freeze it.

Then the ambush: about two-thirds of the way through I hand each group one extra card that their own written justification sorts somewhere they find absurd, and they have to either live with the verdict or rewrite the charter — and write down what they gave up to do it.

I want from you: the full card list with features, including the deliberately contradictory cases; the charter text; the ambush card(s); a minute-by-minute plan for the 50; a debrief that maps the moments where they got stuck onto the real positions (necessary-and-sufficient definitions, cluster or family-resemblance accounts, disjunctive "metabolism or evolution" accounts, and the deflationary "life is whatever the definition is for" line) — but don't let the names surface until the debrief; the things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix them on the fly; and one honest paragraph on what this activity can't reproduce. My guess is it can stage the pull toward a sharp line and the cost of drawing it, but not the question of whether there's a fact of the matter about viruses.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
