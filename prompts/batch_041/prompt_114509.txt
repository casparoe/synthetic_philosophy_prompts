I'm a novelist and I've been rereading Jekyll and Hyde for a book I'm writing about a man who blames his own brain chemistry for the things he does, and I keep thinking the novella is a better document about the causal exclusion argument than anyone gives it credit for. If every act of Hyde's already has a sufficient physical cause — the draught, the brain, the body — then what work is Jekyll's will actually doing? And yet the book can't give the will up: the last chapter is a confession in which a man claims authorship of the murders, and the law hangs Hyde for them rather than the chemistry. So read it for me through the exclusion argument and tell me what the book is really committed to. Does the potion sit alongside the will as a second sufficient cause — overdetermination, in which case nothing gets excluded and the whole problem dissolves — or is it the only cause, and the confession is self-flattery? Which horn of the dilemma is Stevenson actually stuck on, and does the book know its stuck on one of them? Point me at the scenes that decide it (the Carew murder, the change on the bench in the park, Lanyon's narrative, the full statement), and be honest about where the novella resists the reading — where the two natures just don't line up with two causes and the critic has to say so. Then finish with what the fiction sees that the argument doesn't: why we keep assigning the deed to the person rather than to the chemistry, even when the chemistry was enough.
