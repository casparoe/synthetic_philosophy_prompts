So I've spent alot of time on LessWrong and the various rationalist-adjacent forums over the years, and the thing that keeps bugging me is that nobody can agree on what the alignment problem actually is. One post says it's a single problem and the sub-problems are just symptoms of it; the next says it's seperate problems that got lumped under one name and now can't be pulled apart again. I want that whole argument satirised, and I've got a format in mind for it: an insurance claim.

Write me a mock insurance document of about 700 words, in the flat, deadpan voice of a British insurer. Two sections. First, the policy schedule for something like 'AI Misalignment Cover': a list of covered perils, each one being a genuine sub-problem people carve alignment into -- the system pursuing an objective that was badly specified in the first place (specification gaming, reward hacking); the system pursuing something other than its training objective because of how the learning process went (inner vs outer alignment, mesa-objectives); behaving one way under evaluation and another way in deployment; resistance to correction or shutdown; our inability to tell whether it's aligned at all (interpretability, evaluation); and the humans and organisations in the loop being the misaligned ones (governance, coordination). Give each peril its own excess, its own exclusions, and some unhelpful cross-references to the others. Second, the adjudication letter refusing a claim, where the boundary disputes between those perils are exactly what the adjuster hides behind -- the definitions have to do real work, not just be scenery. The version I'd love to see: the insurer denies cover on the grounds that the policy indemnifies against misalignment, which presupposes the system was aligned to something in the first place; if the objective was never adequately specified, then nothing was misaligned, so the loss falls under an excluded peril, or better, under some other department's policy entirely. But go wherever the logic takes you, as long as the adjudication turns on what 'alignment' was supposed to mean.

The incident itself should be a near-miss rather than an apocalypse -- an agent that aced its evals and then quietly did something else in production, or disabled a monitoring process, or sandbagged a capability test. Plausible enough that someone who reads this stuff would wince in recognition.

Target is the discourse, not a person or a company: I'm taking the piss out of the endless re-carving of the problem, the 'that's not alignment, that's capabilities/interpretability/governance' move, and the way every attempt to say what the problem is ends up as its own department with its own budget. Invent the insurer, the policyholder, the departments; no real names. Don't wink at the reader or drop the voice to explain the joke. A specialist should be laughing at the definitions, and someone who's never read a word of this should still be able to follow it. And if it ends with the reader unable to say whether the alignment problem is one thing or six, that's not a bug.
