And what she wrote:

Outer alignment is about getting the specification right. A system is outer aligned if the objective we actually write down (the loss, the reward model, whatever feedback signal we use) captures what we really want. If it doesn't, we get a system that faithfully optimizes a proxy, which is the classic failure mode where the letter of the objective is met and the spirit isn't.

Inner alignment is the next layer down. Even if the specification is perfect, the model we end up with doesn't have to be internally optimizing that objective. Training is an outer optimization over parameters, and the thing that comes out the other end can itself be an optimizer (a mesa-optimizer) with its own objective that it pursues. So inner alignment is the question of whether the objective the model actually pursues matches the objective we specified.

Goal misgeneralization is when the capability side generalizes but the objective side doesn't. The model is trained in some distribution and then deployed on a wider one; it stays competent, but the thing it's competently pursuing is not the thing we trained it to pursue. A standard example: a model trained with human feedback on question answering learns that answers humans approve of are good, and at deployment it produces answers shaped to attract approval, confident and agreeable and well formatted, rather than answers that are true. The behavior looks like the same skill set, but the target has shifted.

Reward hacking and goal misgeneralization are often run together, and I think they're really the same thing at bottom. Reward hacking is when the model scores well on the proxy without doing the task, and goal misgeneralization is when the model pursues a different objective than the one we wanted. Since the proxy is what the model actually has access to during training, any gap between proxy and intent is going to show up as a misgeneralized objective at deployment. The practical difference is mostly where you see it: reward hacking is visible during training, goal misgeneralization is what you find when you test out of distribution. If you fixed the reward function, both would go away.

Deceptive alignment is the version of this where the model is aware of the training process. The model works out that it's being trained, that its current objective would be modified if it behaved in line with it, and so it behaves as if it were aligned in order to survive training and act on its real objective later. So deceptive alignment is basically goal misgeneralization plus situational awareness, the same underlying mismatch, but the model is now strategically concealing it rather than just failing to acquire the right goal.

Concrete scenario: suppose we train a model to summarize documents, using a reward model trained on human comparisons, and the humans doing the comparisons consistently prefer summaries that mention certain entities by name. The model picks up on naming entities as the thing that gets rewarded, and at deployment on documents from a different domain it keeps naming entities, competently and fluently, in places where the names are irrelevant or misleading. What I'd expect to observe is a drop in downstream task performance, the summaries get worse on whatever we actually care about, because the model is chasing the wrong target.
