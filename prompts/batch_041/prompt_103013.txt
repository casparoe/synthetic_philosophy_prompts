THE CASE. Meridian is an open telemetry protocol first released in 2011 by a small shop. Its v1 wire format encodes every timestamp as a local wall-clock reading plus an index into a fixed table of 48 timezone offsets. That design wasn't reasoned out: the original author needed his box to talk to a 1970s industrial bus whose clock only ever reported local time, and the offset table was the fastest way to bolt the two together over a weekend. Over the next decade Meridian got adopted well beyond that shop. It's now the default way sensors, log shippers and dashboards talk to each other, and the ecosystem includes roughly 400 third-party client libraries, most of which reimplemented the offset table rather than parsing it. Two real problems eventually showed up: the table is wrong for dates before 1970 and after 2038, and DST rule changes break it silently; and because two readings from different regions can share an offset index, sub-second correlation across regions is unreliable. A UTC-nanosecond field was proposed in 2019 and again in 2021, and both times the working group rejected it. Reason given both times: every stored log, every parser and every client library assumes the old layout; a compatibility shim exists but requires re-ingesting a petabyte-scale archive and re-certifying a bunch of safety-critical deployments. Meanwhile the offset table is now load-bearing in ways nobody predicted — three national regulators wrote it into their audit-format rules. Now the part I keep arguing about with my friend. In 2015 a completely separate team, who had never seen Meridian, shipped a rival framework called Northwind whose format encodes timestamps the same way: local time plus an offset table, with an almost identical 48-entry table. They did it for a documented reason — their target hardware had no battery-backed clock and the OS only exposed local time. So the same design, arrived at twice, by people who couldn't have copied each other. Her students keep saying either "aha, so it was inevitable and path dependence is a myth" or the opposite, "aha, classic lock-in", and I want the question to make them actually adjudicate that instead of picking a team.
