For a long-form essay I'm writing for the ideas section of the magazine, I want to read John Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance through the two competing pragmatist accounts of truth — Peirce's and James's — and I'd like a reading robust enough to survive the scrutiny of readers who know both the film and the philosophy. My interest is not in the well-worn essay on myth and the closing of the frontier; it is in the film as a test case for pragmatism, and I want the case argued rather than gestured at.

The film's central turn is that the deed for which Ransom Stoddard is celebrated, the killing of Liberty Valance, was in fact done by Tom Doniphon, who fired from the shadows and let Stoddard take the credit. Stoddard's subsequent rise — his election, his marriage to Hallie, his career in the Senate — rests on an account of the past that is false, and the newspapermen who finally learn the truth decide, in the closing scene, to let the legend stand. The film therefore stages the pragmatist question in its hardest form: an account that is not true by correspondence nonetheless does enormous work, and the people who know better choose it anyway.

To keep us on the same footing, I'll name the two commitments I want the reading routed through, stated in the form I intend to use them. Peirce's account: truth is the opinion that inquiry is fated to reach at its ideal limit, held by a community rather than an individual; truth is not whatever settles belief, but whatever survives the self-correcting method of inquiry, and Peirce's catalogue of the ways belief gets fixed instead — by tenacity, by authority, by a priori taste — is precisely a catalogue of settlements that are not truth-directed. James's account: truth is what it is expedient for us to believe, verified by its consequences in experience and made rather than found, with the standing difficulty that the escape hatch of the long run and on the whole may be doing all the justificatory work.

I'd like the essay to do four things. First, diagnose the legend in Peirce's terms: the newspaper's decision, and the political exploitation of Stoddard's fame, look like fixation by authority, and I want to know whether Peirce's own objection to that method — that it cannot stop people from comparing notes and so undermines itself — is dramatized or refuted by the film, given that Stoddard himself is the one who compares notes. Second, run James's test honestly: does the legend have a cash value, and for whom — the town, Hallie, Stoddard, Doniphon? The film takes pains to show what the useful falsehood costs, and I want that cost entered on the ledger rather than waved away. Third, tell me where the film refuses both philosophers. My suspicion is that its real subject is neither the limit of inquiry nor the expediency of belief, but the fact that the people who pay for a useful falsehood are usually not the people who enjoy it — which would be an objection to James's accounting rather than to his criterion. If that suspicion is wrong, say so, and say why.

Please do not reduce the film to an illustration of either view, and do not tidy up either philosopher to make the fit. Where a scene cuts against the reading — Doniphon's end, and the final interview in the newspaper office — I want the resistance registered rather than smoothed over. Close by telling me what the film contributes to the pragmatist debate about truth that the arguments by themselves do not, since that is the part of the essay that has to earn its place. Assume a reader who knows the film and the philosophers but has not seen this comparison made. No citations or reading lists are needed, and please don't worry about reproducing dialogue; I would rather have the scenes described accurately than lines reconstructed from memory.
