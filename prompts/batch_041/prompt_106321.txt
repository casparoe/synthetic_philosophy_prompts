I have been trying to work out what I think about choosing when nothing favours either option, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. The cases are ordinary, but my reactions to them are not obviously consistent, and I would rather have the inconsistency exposed than papered over.

1. The old parable of the beast that starves between two identical bales, equidistant from it and equally nourishing. My verdict: the story is a reductio of a bad premise, not a demonstration of anything. If the beast starves, that is a failure of appetite or nerve, not of reason. A rational agent with no preference simply takes one.

2. Two dishes on a menu that I rate exactly alike. I pick one without ceremony. Nothing has gone wrong. Had the waiter brought the other I should have been equally content, and I owe no one an explanation.

3. Two job offers that I judge equal on every dimension I care about. I flip a coin. My verdict: rational, and not a concession to impatience. A friend who says I should have deliberated longer has misunderstood the situation; there is nothing left to deliberate about.

4. The same two offers, except that now I cannot rank them at all: one is nearer my family, the other is better work, and I do not believe there is any fact of the matter about which of these matters more to me. Here the coin feels wrong. I resist it, and it seems to me the right response is not to break the tie but to wait, or to become someone with a ranking. Yet I cannot say what is defective about the coin beyond my distaste for it.

5. Naming a child, where I have no preference between two names. The coin is fine. But I would object if someone said I was indifferent between them: if the registrar quietly wrote the other name, I would not be content, and I would feel something had been taken from me. So for me no preference and equal preference come apart, and the coin's legitimacy seems to attach to the first rather than the second.

6. A trustee dividing a fixed sum between two projects of equal merit. The coin is fine; no one has grounds for complaint; this strikes me as the paradigm case in which a coin is not merely permitted but nearly required, since any other method smuggles in a preference that does not exist.

7. Two candidates I judge exactly equal on merit. I must choose, and I think choosing by a tie-breaker I acknowledge as arbitrary wrongs no one. But if the tie-breaker tracks something that should not matter — an accent, or a private fondness I have concealed under the coin — my verdict changes, even though the distribution of outcomes is the same.

8. Whatever I choose in case 3, I later learn that the other offer would have suited me slightly better in a way I could not have foreseen. My verdict: no regret is owed, and no one should reproach me.

My tentative principle is this: a choice is rational just in case no alternative available to the agent is better by the agent's own lights; where two options are equally good, each is permissible; and a coin is nothing more than a device for settling which of several permissible options to take. This seems to cover cases 2, 3, 6 and 8. It says nothing useful about case 4, it seems to make case 5 incoherent (if the names are neither equally good nor unequal, what is the coin doing?), and it cannot explain why in case 7 the outcome should depend on which arbitrary procedure I use.

What I want is the smallest set of principles that accommodates the verdicts I am most attached to, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case; an honest statement of which of my verdicts has to be surrendered; and a statement of what the resulting account implies for a case I have not raised — a legislature that breaks a deadlock by lot, say, or someone who cannot decide whether to have a child and settles it with a coin. I am not asking for a survey of what others have written on the subject; I want the principle.
