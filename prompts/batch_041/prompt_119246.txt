I'm working through the self-consciousness chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit in an older English translation and keep getting stuck on how the opening claim about recognition is supposed to connect to the lordship and bondage outcome that follows. The passage I'm staring at runs: "Self-consciousness exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or 'recognized'." A few paragraphs later the same discussion concludes, "They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another." But the episode that follows produces an asymmetric relation in which the master is said to enjoy a recognition that is somehow hollow or one-sided while the bondsman ends up with something the master lacks.

Help me understand what work "recognition" is doing across these paragraphs. Specifically: what must recognition be for the mutual version to be the truth of the relation, and why does the master's one-sided recognition fail by that standard rather than simply counting as a different, weaker kind of recognition? I want the distinct senses in which the term is functioning here (acknowledgment by another, being confirmed as a subject, being acknowledged as an equal, being recognized as a master, self-recognition mediated through another) and an account of which sense makes the argument hang together. I'm also unsure whether the master's deficiency is meant as a contradiction internal to his position, such that the outcome cannot be a stable resting point, or as a further substantive claim about what recognition requires — and if the latter, whether the requirement of mutuality is derived from the concept of self-consciousness or imported from somewhere else. Lay out the readings rather than settling on one, and tell me which parts of the passage each reading can and cannot accommodate.
