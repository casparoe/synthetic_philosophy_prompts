I am a high school senior and the coordinator of my school's philosophy club. At the end of each term we run a mock examination for members who are preparing for university interviews, and I am responsible for the philosophy of mind paper. I would like you to write one essay question on the badness of pain, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

I was drawn to this topic after reading about patients who, following certain kinds of brain damage, report that they can still feel pain — they can locate it, and describe its intensity and character — but that it no longer distresses them, and who may even smile or reach toward the limb while it is being injured. I believe the clinical name is pain asymbolia, though I am not confident of the details, and I would rather the question not depend on the label. What troubles me is that the obvious answer to the question of why pain is bad is that it hurts, and these cases appear to drive a wedge between the hurting and the badness. I want a question that makes students confront that wedge instead of reciting that pain is intrinsically bad.

Please observe the following constraints:

- The question should be answerable in about forty-five minutes of writing, so roughly five hundred to seven hundred words.
- It should not be answerable by summarizing a familiar position. It should require students to take a stand on whether the badness of pain belongs to the sensation itself or can be peeled away from it, and to say what follows for the claim that pain is intrinsically bad.
- Do not build the question around names, dates, or citations. Students will have no library or internet access during the paper, and I do not want the marks to depend on recalling who said what.
- Invent one short case or thought experiment and include it in the question itself, so that no prior reading is required. The case should do real work — it should be the kind of thing a student has to reason about, not merely an illustration.
- The question should also press on at least one further issue that a strong student will have to address — for instance, whether what we call pain names the sensation or the aversion, or whether a creature that felt pain but never minded it should be thought of as suffering at all.

Then please give me three things: first, the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper; second, a model answer of about six hundred words; and third, a marking scheme out of twenty marks that lists the specific moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth, and, for each, a one-line note on the mistake that most often costs students those marks. The marking scheme should make plain what separates an A answer from a B answer.
