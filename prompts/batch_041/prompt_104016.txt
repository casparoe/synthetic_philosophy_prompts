For about a year I have been working through the literature on the naturalism–normativism dispute over the concept of disease, and I am struck by how little the exchange has moved despite the participants being careful and well informed. The naturalist line — paradigmatically the biostatistical approach, on which a disease is a statistically subnormal deviation from species-typical functional organization, with anything evaluative quarantined into a separate notion such as illness — and the normativist line, on which harm or disvalue is already internal to the concept of disease, keep trading the same paradigm cases. The hybrid harmful-dysfunction analysis, which adds a harm requirement to a dysfunction requirement, is supposed to split the difference, but it seems to me to inherit the disagreement rather than resolve it. What I want from you is not another attempt to decide who is right but a diagnosis of the impasse: what kind of disagreement this really is, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis.

My working hypothesis — please treat it as a hypothesis to test, not as a premise — is that the stall is partly an artifact of the participants running together several claims that all get called "normativism." At least three seem separable: (i) a semantic claim, that the meaning of the term "disease" contains an evaluative component; (ii) a classificatory or epistemic claim, that applying the term to particular cases inevitably requires a value judgment even if the concept itself is descriptive; and (iii) a constitutive or metaphysical claim, that the property of being a disease — or of being a dysfunction in the first place — is partly constituted by facts about value or about what matters to the organism. If the naturalist rejects (i) and (iii) while quietly relying on something like (ii), and the normativist argues for (i) using evidence that really only supports (ii), the two could be talking past each other in a way that superficially resembles a substantive clash.

So I would like you to consider which of the following best characterizes the dispute, giving reasons from what each side actually argues rather than from a survey of positions: a verbal dispute, in which the two sides attach one word to two different concepts and can both be right; a substantive dispute that has merely been relocated, for instance into the theory of biological function, so that the health/disease debate is downstream of whether function-talk can be naturalized; a case of two sides answering different questions under a single heading, one analyzing the concept as it functions in epidemiology and public-health classification and the other as it figures in first-person experience and clinical care; or a genuinely evaluative disagreement all the way down, bottoming out in starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. Please stress-test the hypothesis against two cases: a condition that is statistically typical in one population and atypical in another (so that species-typicality looks relative to a reference class that is itself chosen), and a dysfunction that causes no harm and is not disvalued by the person who has it, together with the reverse case of a harmful condition with no detectable functional dysfunction. Tell me what each case does to the naturalist and the normativist positions, and close with an honest assessment of what — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all — could actually move the debate.
