I'm putting together a handout for a grad reading group on collective action and individual obligation — the whole "my one vote, my one burger, my one flight doesn't make a difference, so why would it be wrong" mess. I want it to be a decision tree that starts from a concrete commitment and ends at a named position, so people find out where they actually land instead of picking the label they already like.

Take the role of someone who's spent years in the weeds on complicity and the inefficacy objection. Think carefully about the logical space before you write anything, and brainstorm first: list the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then arrange those into a tree, then hang the names on the leaves.

Rules for the tree:
- Every node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. No "do you accept the complicity account of accountability?"
- Branches should be labeled by the claim that splits them, not by author names.
- Build it from the space first: give me the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then tell me which combinations are actually occupied and which are empty, and for each empty one say in a sentence whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any two positions your tree can't really separate. Dont fudge it.

It has to include these leaves (add more if the space needs them): the flat inefficacy/no-obligation view; act-consequentialist expected-value marginalism; the threshold/collective-impact view where token acts count as part of a set that together does the damage; a complicity/participatory view; a Kantian universalizability or rule-consequentialist view; a virtue/expressive view where the act is wrong because of what it expresses rather than what it causes; and a structural view where the obligation flows from your position in a structure rather than from your causal footprint.

Two distinctions I care about, and I want the tree to force them:
- obligation vs blameworthiness as separate axes — some views say there's no obligation but you're still fair game for criticism, others say there is an obligation but no blame attaches. At least one branch has to pull these apart.
- token act vs act-type — whether "was this particular act wrong?" is even the right question, or whether the real unit is the pattern/practice.

Cases each branch has to be able to run on: a single vote in a safe seat; one burger; one transatlantic flight; a fundraiser that only hits its goal if enough people chip in (so your tenner is neither necessary nor sufficient); and the version where a thousand people each turn a dial a hair's breadth, so no single turn changes what the victim feels, but all together they cause agony.

Keep it to about 8 branch nodes and 7-8 leaves. Write it as a handout someone could actually use at the table, and end with the three emptiest or most awkward corners of the tree and one line on each. And dont worry about citing anyone — just the positions, no bibliography.
