I am a graduate student in moral philosophy preparing a seminar paper on moral revolutions—specifically, the puzzling fact that practices like chattel slavery and aristocratic dueling were once defended by serious people and then, within a couple of generations, became not merely illegal but unthinkable. I keep running into a standard three-way menu of explanations: argument (the moral case was made and eventually won), interest (the practice became too costly or inconvenient for powerful groups), and honor (the practice came to be seen as beneath a person of honor rather than as unjust). I want to add two more mechanisms to the menu and have you rank all five by explanatory power. The first addition I will call reputational precommitment: individuals publicly bind themselves to a norm not because they accept it but because the cost of being early to defect is high, and the norm collapses when a critical mass of high-status people make the same calculation. The second I will call moral lag: the practice ends first among elites who never relied on it for status, and only later spreads downward, so the apparent argument is really a post hoc rationalization of a change that had already happened in elite honor culture.

Please sort the five—argument, interest, honor, reputational precommitment, and moral lag—into tiers for each of the two cases, dueling and slavery, and for each case assign weights summing to 100% across the five. I care less about a literature review than about the conceptual anatomy: which mechanism is doing the justificatory work, which is doing the causal work, and which is merely a transmission belt. Give me a definite ranking and weights rather than a survey of possibilities, and tell me which single consideration would most change your ranking. I will add citations myself, so do not worry about those, and please do not hedge by saying that the answer depends entirely on the case; make the call and then explain what would have to be true for you to revise it.
