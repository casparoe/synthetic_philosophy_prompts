I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been helping prepare material for a small reading group that is working through the debate over middle knowledge — the claim, associated with the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, that God knows not only what is necessarily the case and what God will in fact decide, but also what any free creature would freely do in any circumstances it might be placed in, with that third kind of knowledge available to God prior to any decision of God's own will. I want to understand what that third category is actually buying, so I'd like you to construct a single thought experiment to specification that isolates the work it is supposed to do.

Specifications:

- One paragraph, realistic setting. Two or three ordinary people, a mundane decision — a job, a debt, whether to report something, whether to speak up. No supernatural machinery inside the scenario itself; the scenario is the human situation, and the theological claims are made about it from outside.
- The pivotal fact must be a counterfactual about a free choice in circumstances the person will in fact never occupy. Plain foreknowledge of what actually happens must not be sufficient to do the work.
- The person's character, upbringing, beliefs, and desires must not settle the counterfactual. The person is genuinely torn, and either answer must remain a live option for someone with exactly that psychology in exactly those circumstances. If the person's nature already fixes the answer, the case collapses into knowledge of what creatures of that kind could do.
- The choice must not be causally determined, and must not be produced or guaranteed by God. The person could do otherwise.
- The success of whatever plan the scenario turns on must depend on this specific counterfactual, not on a general truth about how people like this tend to behave.
- Nothing in the scenario may stipulate the answer. If the case only works because you have asserted the conclusion, it fails.
- Do not reuse the stock illustrations that circulate in this literature.

Then, after the scenario:

1. State the counterfactual of freedom precisely, and say at what point in the order of explanation it has to be true — before the person exists, before any divine decision, before the person's actual choices — and why the case requires it to be true there.
2. Give the verdict of the middle-knowledge position and explain which step of the scenario it relies on.
3. Give the verdict of a position that denies such counterfactuals have anything to make them true, or denies they could be known prior to God's own decision, and explain why it reaches a different verdict.
4. Give the verdict of a position on which God's decision fixes the counterfactual in the first place, and explain why that position classifies the case as ordinary foreknowledge rather than as anything new.
5. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the load-bearing work. Then run two variants: in one, ground the counterfactual in the person's nature; in the other, ground it in God's decision. Say what each variant does to the disagreement.
6. Say how a defender of whichever position comes out badly would attack the scenario — what they would deny, and whether denying it costs them anything elsewhere.
