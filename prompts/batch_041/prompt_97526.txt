I am trying to pin down the philosophical commitments built into the way frontier language models are aligned, and I would like a careful reconstruction rather than a survey of the literature.

The practice I have in mind is the now-standard pipeline: a lab recruits a pool of human annotators, shows them pairs of model responses to a prompt, records which response they prefer, fits a reward or preference model to those judgments, and then trains the deployed model to score highly under it; some labs add a short written set of principles and have annotators (or a model) apply those principles when ranking. The result is a single model, with a single behavioral profile, released to a worldwide and ideologically heterogeneous user base. Two features of this puzzle me. First, the lab speaks as though it is aligning the model to human values in general, yet the values that actually get baked in are those of a particular, chosen slice of humanity, filtered through the lab's own written principles. Second, the lab presents itself as neutral among the competing conceptions of the good held by its users, yet it is plainly selecting which conceptions get aggregated and which principles govern the aggregation.

What I want from you is an account of what this practice presupposes about value. In particular:

- What does it presuppose about the aggregation of values — that individual preferences can be summed or averaged into a single ordering, that they are interpersonally comparable, that the resulting ordering is a genuine representation of a collective will rather than an artifact of the rater pool and the aggregation rule?
- What does it presuppose about neutrality — is a lab that selects its raters and writes its principles ever in a position to be neutral, and if the practice nonetheless presupposes neutrality, in what sense?
- Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out: that there is no single fact of the matter to aggregate, that the authority to set a model's values belongs to a political process rather than a firm, that some values are not tradeable against others at any exchange rate, that different users should simply get different models?
- For each commitment, tell me whether the practice strictly requires it or merely fits it comfortably, and how one would tell the difference — what observation or counterexample would show the practice surviving without it.
- Finally, what would have to change in the pipeline if the presupposed view were replaced by a serious rival — say, by radical value pluralism, or by a refusal to compare preferences across persons?

Give me your analysis in prose, and then a structured summary. For the summary, output a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "practice_summary": a one- or two-sentence restatement of the practice analyzed.
- "presuppositions": an array, each element an object with "name" (short label), "presupposed_claim" (the proposition the practice commits to), "status" (one of "strictly required", "merely compatible", "unclear"), "rival_views_excluded" (array of strings), and "test" (how one could tell whether the practice really depends on it).
- "internal_tensions": an array of strings, each a place where the parts of the practice seem to presuppose inconsistent things.
- "underdetermined": an array of strings, each an aspect the practice leaves open that several incompatible theories could equally license.
- "counterfactual_changes": an array of strings, each a change the pipeline would need under one named rival view.
