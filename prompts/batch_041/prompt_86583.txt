I'm a features writer who covers science and ideas, and I'm about six months into a long piece on heritage — how towns and communities decide what counts as theirs, and what happens when someone turns up with the receipts showing that a practice everyone treats as ancient is really about seventy years old. I'd like you to answer as a cultural anthropologist slash historian of nationalism who's read widely in the invention-of-tradition literature, and before you settle on anything I'd like you to brainstorm the candidate concepts out loud, then say which one actually fits and where it breaks down. I'm not just after a label — a label I can get from a thesaurus. I need the distinction carved precisely enough that I can use it in a 6,000-word piece without overclaiming.

Here's the pattern I keep running into. Case one: a small coastal town near where I grew up has a summer festival with a procession, a costume, a specific song, a whole set of rules about who carries what. The local historical society has the minutes from 1954 showing it was dreamt up by the chamber of commerce to get people off the new highway and into the guesthouses. Everybody involved in the founding is dead now, and the festival is simply the towns own version of itself — people talk about their grandparents doing it, which is true, and about it being the old way here, which is not. Case two: a craft that had genuinely lapsed for two generations gets revived by enthusiasts in the 1970s, who reconstruct it from a couple of museum pieces and a bit of guesswork, codify it into a formal syllabus, and now teach it as the authentic thing — and the codified version is stricter and more uniform than the historical record suggests it ever was. What interests me is the shape of both: nobody is lying, there's no forgery and no cover-up, but the thing gets more rigid as it gets older rather than less, and the community that made it up becomes its fiercest defender, going after a neighboring village that does a slightly different version and calling that version fake.

I know the obvious answer is Hobsbawm and Ranger on invented traditions, and I know Anderson has the imagined-communities half of this. But as I remember it, Hobsbawm is mainly interested in traditions cooked up to legitimate something new — a state, a new institution, a class that's just come to power — and in his telling the invention stays a bit of a con aimed at the people from above, with the inventors knowing exactly what they're doing. My cases aren't that; the making-up is diffuse, it happens in the open, and it succeeds so completely that it stops being making-up. I've also got a half-memory of someone saying that customs are allowed to drift while traditions can't, but I may be inventing that myself. And a colleague told me tourism studies has a term for the thing where a performance put on for visitors slowly turns into the real thing, though I couldn't tell whether that's the same phenomenon or just adjacent. So: is there an established name for the specific slice I'm describing — an invented tradition that becomes genuinely old, or a living custom that gets frozen into an invariant one — and is there a body of work under that name? If there's no name, coin one, define it, and tell me what it excludes. And please be explicit about the boundaries, because that's the seperate question I actually care about: what distinguishes my cases from (a) invented tradition in Hobsbawm's sense, (b) a community that's simply mistaken about how old one of its practices is, and (c) the ordinary drift of any custom over time? At what point does an invented tradition stop being invented — is that even a coherent question, or am I chasing a line that won't hold?
