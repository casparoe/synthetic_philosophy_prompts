I'm a third-year PhD student in a science and technology policy program, and I've gotten stuck on something that started as a throwaway comment in a seminar and won't leave me alone. We were talking about what people call the unilateralist's curse — the rough idea being that when a bunch of people can each independently decide to do some potentially harmful thing, and it only takes one of them actually doing it for the harm to land, the group tends to end up doing the thing even when every single person involved is thoughtful and well-meaning and would rather the harm not happen. The mechanism is basically that from any one person's point of view the odds that their own action is the decisive one look tiny, so each of them talks themselves into going ahead, and the harms pile up across the population even though nobody wanted the pile.

The practice I keep chewing on is the norm of coordinated (or "responsible") disclosure in security research — the convention that if you find a serious vulnerability you quietly tell the vendor, give them some window, often around ninety days, and only publish after coordinating the timing with everyone else who knows. It's essentially an anti-curse device: it stops the disclosure decision from being one that each researcher makes on their own. What I want to know is what this practice philosophically presupposes. Does it need a particular view about when an individual's action counts as making a difference — i.e., does it have to treat the marginal-contribution argument ("my publishing won't move the expected harm, so it's fine") as actually invalid, or can it get by with a purely rule-consequentialist story? Does it presuppose that responsibility for the bad outcome is genuinely shared or collective rather than landing on whoever publishes first? And the bit that really nags me: the same research community that insists on this coordinated restraint also, in other contexts, treats open publication as close to sacred — the whole ethos being that information wants to be free and withholding it is a kind of paternalism. Do those two stances presuppose consistent commitments, or is the community quietly holding two incompatible pictures of what information and individual action are even for?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory, and be clear about what the practice strictly has to presuppose versus what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined, i.e. several different theories would license the same norm. And please keep separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested — if there's real disagreement about, say, whether the curse even picks out a distinct phenomenon or whether marginal reasoning is defensible after all, don't smooth it over by presenting one side as the consensus.
