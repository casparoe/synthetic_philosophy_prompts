Some context first: I spent thirty-odd years as a physicist — condensed matter, nothing glamorous — and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, trying to teach myself the subject. So be gentle with me.

Here's what's been nagging at me. Every philosophy book I open eventually gets around to the big question, why there is something rather than nothing, and treats it like the deepest problem there is. But I can't shake the feeling that we physicists settled this one a while back and the philosophers just haven't caught up.

My reasoning goes like this. Back in my old field we learned early on that empty space isn't nothing — it's a roiling sea of vacuum fluctuations, virtual pairs, zero-point energy, the whole zoo. And in cosmology there are perfectly respectable models where the universe nucleates out of a quantum nothing, or has no boundary at all, so that asking what came before it is like asking what's north of the North Pole. So we've got a mechanism that gets you a universe out of no universe, plus models where the question of an earlier state doesn't even arise. That looks like an answer to me: there's something rather than nothing because quantum mechanics won't let you have a stable nothing in the first place, and you don't need a First Cause or a necessary being to explain it.

One friend in my reading group mumbled something about the principal of sufficient reason, but as far as I can tell that just means everything has a cause, and my radioactive source decays when it decays for no reason at all, so that principle looks dead on arrival. Another friend just sighs and says I'm not even asking the right kind of question, which sounds to me like a dodge.

So please be blunt with me. Is my reasoning actually wrong somewhere, and if so, where exactly? Or is it my philosopher friends who are confused? I'd genuinely like to know whether I've been making a fool of myself every month at reading group.
