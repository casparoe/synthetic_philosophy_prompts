I cover the politics of morality legislation — bans, repeals, exemptions, the whole dreary calendar of it — and after a few years on the beat I keep running into an argumentative shape I have no settled name for. In my own notes I have been calling it a "victimless injury," which I am aware is a contradiction in terms, and that is more or less the problem. The shape is this. A prohibition is defended not by showing that the conduct hurts anyone, and not quite by pleading that it offends people either, though offense is usually what it looks like from a distance. The claim is that the norm itself is a shared possession, and that letting the conduct go unpunished — or letting the state stop endorsing the norm — damages the community in a way that cannot be located in any particular person's condition. When I press, the damage gets described as a change in what people may henceforth expect of one another: a legislator told me last month that the objection to repeal was not that anyone would suffer but that the country would no longer be able to tell its own children that the thing is wrong. Two features of the move interest me. First, the injury has no bearer — nobody is worse off in health, income, liberty, or, on the account offered, even in felt distress — and yet it is not treated as merely symbolic either; it is supposed to count in the ledger alongside harms that do have bearers. Second, the evidence offered for the injury is frequently the very erosion of support that the law is meant to prevent, so the argument predicts a change in attitudes and then cites the prediction as the reason to forbid the change.

I have watched the same shape travel outside the criminal law. In arguments over redefining a public institution, opponents often concede that no one's circumstances would worsen and insist that the institution itself would be lost to everyone, including people who never used it. In arguments over removing a public marker, the complaint is again that something held in common disappears, and again there is no one whose condition is worse, only a standard that no longer exists to be appealed to. What makes me doubt that I am just re-describing something familiar is that this seems different from the old British argument, the one between Hart and Devlin, about whether the criminal law may enforce a shared morality. Devlin's worry, as I understand it, was at bottom empirical — a common morality is what holds a society together, and a society that stops enforcing it comes apart — whereas the move I keep hearing makes no prediction of collapse and does not need one; the disappearance of the standard is supposed to be the damage itself, prior to any downstream consequence. It also seems different from ordinary offense, since the offended have a condition that can be described and weighed, and different from the familiar cases in which each individual act is harmless but the acts together are ruinous, since there the ruin has obvious victims.

So: is there an established name for this — a harm consisting in the disappearance of a shared standard rather than in anyone's condition getting worse — and if the name that exists belongs to something narrower or wider than what I have described, where exactly do the lines fall? Please do not simply hand me a label and a reading list; I can find the literature once I know what I am looking for. What I need is the carving. Is this a distinct species of harm, or legal moralism with a new coat of paint, or the ordinary harm principle stretched until it covers a kind of loss it was never meant to cover? Can it survive the obvious objection that a norm no one was obliged to follow has not really been taken from anyone? And where are its edges — does the shape change when the people who valued the norm are identifiable and report that they are distressed, so that bearers reappear after all, or when the predicted erosion is offered as a reason, so that it collapses into an ordinary consequence argument? If nothing established fits, then coin the term, define it, and show me the boundary cases on either side of it.
