I'm prepping a session for our reading group on supererogation -- acts that go beyond the call of duty -- and I want us to start from the spec rather than from any one author. So before anyone assigns a paper, give me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of supererogation actually has to deliver.

You're a philosopher who's spent a lot of time in normative ethics and on the deontic categories, so think this through carefully -- brainstorm first, then write the list. I don't want a literature summary or a tour of who said what; I want the requirements, each one tied to the case or consideration that motivates it.

Some of the pressure points I want covered, though don't feel boxed in by my list:

- The theory has to face the alleged paradox: if an act is morally better than its alternatives, it starts to look like the act you ought to do, which would make it required rather than optional -- and then it isn't supererogatory at all. Whatever the view is, it has to either dissolve this or explain why it's a pseudo-problem.
- It has to respect the difference between the supererogatory and the merely permissible, and also the difference between the supererogatory and the obligatory. You can't just collapse "beyond duty" into "allowed" or into "required."
- I keep getting stuck on cases where the sacrifice is basically free for the agent. A billionaire who gives away a tenth of their fortune does a lot of good but never feels it; someone who gives up a kidney, or a parent who wrecks their own health caring for a dying child, pays in a way that's hard to compare. The theory needs a principled line on whether cost to the agent belongs in the definition at all.
- Related: is there such a thing as supererogation toward oneself? Can you go beyond the call of duty to yourself, or does the "call of duty" only bind you in relation to others?
- And the treadmill worry: as an agent gets more virtuous, what used to be heroic becomes ordinary, and supererogation looks like it keeps evaporating. Does the theory survive that, or does it turn moral progress into a machine that deletes its own subject?
- Whether supererogatory acts can be omissions and not just actions -- can declining to do something be beyond the call?

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it. Then tell me which requirements you think are non-negociable and which are really just hang-ups of particular camps. The part I most want is this: flag any pair or set of requirements you suspect can't all be satisfied at once, because if that's right, then the list itself is the result.

Don't hedge into a survey -- take a definite position on the contested calls and give your reasons, even where you're not fully sure. I'd much rather argue with a committed list than a fence-sitting one. Finish with a line on which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular theories, and why.
