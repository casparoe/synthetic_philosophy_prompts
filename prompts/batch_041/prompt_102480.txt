So I'm a third-year PhD student, no dissertation chapter written yet, and I've got maybe a year and a half of real working time before my committee starts asking pointed questions. The thing I keep circling back to is the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. You know the shape: there seem to be reasons to have some attitude -- believe that p, admire someone's painting, intend to do something -- that don't bear at all on whether the attitude is fitting or correct, like a demon who'll torment you unless you believe something obviously false, or the fact that a colleague will be hurt if you don't admire her terrible painting. Everyone grants there's *some* line between the reasons that bear on the attitude's correctness and the ones that only bear on having it, but nobody agrees on what the line is, or whether it's one line at all (object-given vs. state-given, a fittingness-first story, some scope or constitutive restriction on reasons, etc.), and I have a sneaking suspicion the disagreement is doing more work in a bunch of neighbouring debates than people admit. A friend of mine keeps insisting the whole distinction just collapses if you go expressivist about normative talk, which I can't tell if that's deep or nonsense.

What I actually want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Given that year and a half -- and given that what I need out of it is one chapter plus hopefully a standalone paper, and that I'm decent at value theory and the metaphysics of normativity but pretty weak on epistemology and decision theory -- which of the open questions here is worth the time, and in what order? Here's the kind of stuff I've been mulling, so you can tell me what I'm missing or overrating: (1) whether the distinction is a distinction between *kinds of reasons* at all, or really between kinds of *favoring*, or maybe just the basing relation in disguise; (2) whether it generalizes cleanly from belief to conative attitudes like intention and desire, or whether belief is a special case that's been misleading everyone; (3) whether it actually threatens the buck-passing account of value or whether buck-passers have a cheap escape; (4) whether it's better seen as a fact about the *content* of reasons rather than their *source*, which I keep flip-flopping on; (5) whether reasons that are partly constitutive of a practice -- reasons to intend generated by a promise, or by the rules of a game -- are a separate species or just more of the same.

Please rank these (or replace them with better ones if I've got the landscape wrong) and defend the ranking, not just list it. I care about why one question is upstream of another -- like, if I settled whether the distinction is about content vs. source, how much of the rest would that actually decide? -- and about tractability for someone with my background, and about which ones are neglected versus which have fifty people already grinding on them. Tell me which two I should start with, what a good six-month unit of work on each would even look like, and what evidence or argument over the next year would move something up or down the list. If one of these is a trap that looks juicy but dead-ends, say so.
