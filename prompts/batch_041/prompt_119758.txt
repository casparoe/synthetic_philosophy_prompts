Our teacher said this is the Boltzmann brain problem and then ran out of class time, so me and two friends have been argueing about it at lunch for a week and we keep talking past each other, because one of them thinks it's a reason the physics must be wrong, another thinks it's a reason to be a total skeptic about everything, and I keep saying the whole thing is cheating with probabilities somehow. So I want to make a decision tree / flowchart I can bring to class that sorts a person into the position they actually hold, not the label they'd give if you just asked them "so, are you worried about Boltzmann brains?"

The fork I want the tree built around is this: I think the disagreement is really about whether the problem is (a) about the physics, (b) about the probability measure, i.e. how you're supposed to count observers in an endless universe, (c) about what even counts as an observer — does a one-moment brain with fake memories, no body, no world around it and no second thought actually count? — (d) about what evidence is, i.e. whether "I am this particular observer rather than that one" is the kind of fact that can make planet-born me more likely than brain-me, or (e) about whether the argument eats itself, since if I really were a brain, my reasoning that I'm probably a brain would just be a fluke, so can I trust it? Every branch node has to be a concrete yes/no question or a small case a classmate could answer without knowing where it leads — nothing like "do you accept the self-sampling assumption?", since that jargon is exactly what I'm trying to avoid. I need at least these leaves, each with a short name and one line on its most awkward commitment (or write "nobody defends this" if that's the honest answer): the view that the prediction is a reductio so the cosmology has to be wrong; the view that the fix is a better measure or cutoff that simply dosen't count the brains (like only counting observers that could have shared a causal history with each other); the view that brains don't count as observers so there's no group to be typical in; the view that we should just accept we're probably brains and be skeptics about our apparent world; the view that indexical facts aren't evidence, so the inference never even gets going; the view that the argument is self-undermining and should be discounted; and the view that the question has no well-defined answer because probabilities in an eternal universe were never defined in the first place.
