I'm doing an MA in mental health counseling and we just finished a unit on Ian Hacking -- looping effects, "making up people," that whole thing -- and I walked out thinking I got it, but now I'm not so sure, and I have a sinking feeling I've got something backwards. The way it was told to us: some diagnoses are what he'd call interactive kinds, so when the classification shifts, the people being classified shift too, because they can find out about the label and sort of take it up, or resist it. The examples were dissociative identity disorder going from almost nothing to an epidemic in the 80s and 90s and then shrinking again, anorexia, and those 1890s European "mad travellers" who walked for hundreds of miles in a trance -- transient mental illnesses that apparently need some kind of "niche" to flourish and then just wither away. Okay. But here's the thing I can't stop chewing on: if the condition changes shape whenever the label changes, isn't the label kind of doing the work? Like these aren't really diseases so much as scripts we hand out, and the patients are basically responding to the diagnosis the way you'd respond to a suggestion. A classmate who's read way more than me says obviously Hacking is a social constructionist who thinks mental illness is invented and that's the whole point of him being anti-medical-model.

So, assuming that's right: does the looping effect mean we should be pulling back on diagnosing, since every time we hand someone a label we're partly creating the thing we're supposedly treating? And can a category like this ever be a natural kind -- because if the same diagnosis in 1890 and today is picking out totally different symptom clusters, it can't really be one thing that's out there in the world, can it? I mostly want to not embarrass myself in seminar next week, so if I've got the whole picture tilted, tell me where.
