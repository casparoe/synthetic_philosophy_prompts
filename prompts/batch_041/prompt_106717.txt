I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophical problems in biology, and I need a second opinion on one exam answer before I return the papers. The question comes from the unit on the species problem; the framework it uses was developed in lecture and does not appear in any of the assigned readings, so I have reproduced the question in full. Please grade the student's answer that follows.

The exam question (20 points) read:

"Biologists disagree about which species concept to use. Interbreeding-based, ancestry-based, and ecology-based concepts each classify the living world differently. The lecture distinguished two readings of the resulting problem.

(E) The epistemic reading: the difficulty is that our concepts disagree, and no single concept does all the explanatory work we want. The problem is to choose a concept, or to justify using several.

(M) The metaphysical reading: the difficulty is whether the living world divides into discrete, objective, non-conventional groups at all — whether anything in nature answers to our word 'species,' independently of which concept we happen to favor.

Answer both parts.

1. Are (E) and (M) independent? Can either be settled without the other? Defend your answer.

2. Consider this argument:

P1. Species boundaries are vague: speciation is gradual, ring species exist, hybridization occurs, and the fossil record shows continuous change.
P2. If a boundary is vague, then it is not a boundary in nature but a decision we make.
C. Therefore species are conventional groupings — the boundary between them is like the boundary between red and orange.

Reconstruct the argument as charitably as you can, then evaluate it. If it fails, identify precisely which step fails, and why."

The student's answer:

"Part 1. The two readings are logically independent. (E) is a question about our classificatory practices: it asks which concept, or which set of concepts, we should use. (M) is a question about the world: it asks whether there is anything in nature for those concepts to get right. No amount of agreement about the first settles the second, and no discovery about the second tells us which concept to prefer in a given research context, so neither reduces to the other.

The epistemic problem is also the more tractable of the two. The biological species concept handles sexual organisms well, though it famously stumbles on ring species, where the relation 'can interbreed' fails to be transitive, so that adjacent populations come out conspecific while the two terminal populations do not. The usual fix is to use the biological species concept for sexual organisms and a phylogenetic or ecological concept for asexual ones, which is more or less what working biologists do.

Once (E) has been settled pragmatically in this way, however, (M) largely dissolves. Whether species are 'real' was always just the question whether our concepts succeed in grouping organisms usefully, and the concepts we have adopted do succeed, so there is no further fact left over for (M) to be about. Species pluralism supports this: if there is no single correct concept, then there is no single fact of the matter that a concept would have to capture, and pluralism is therefore best read as a form of antirealism about species. The thesis that species are individuals rather than kinds, associated with Ghiselin and Hull, makes the same point from the other direction: if a species is a particular rather than a class, there is no essence for it to have or to lack, so nothing is at stake in asking whether the group is 'real.'

Part 2. The argument is valid. P1 reports familiar facts: speciation is gradual, ring species and hybridization occur, and the fossil record shows continuous change. P2 says that a vague boundary is not a boundary in nature but a decision we make. From these it follows that species are conventional groupings, and the comparison with red and orange is apt.

The argument is not sound as it stands, because P1 is overstated. There are cases in which species boundaries are perfectly sharp: two species can occupy the same region, share a habitat, and never interbreed, and there the line between them is not vague at all. The argument should therefore be restated with a weaker first premise — species boundaries are vague in a great many cases. With that repair the inference goes through, since P2 is true (a boundary that admits of no sharp line is a boundary we have drawn rather than found), and vagueness of that sort is pervasive in the living world. So the conclusion stands: where one species stops and the next begins is not a fact of nature but a line drawn by us for our own convenience, just as there is no fact of the matter about where red stops and orange begins."
