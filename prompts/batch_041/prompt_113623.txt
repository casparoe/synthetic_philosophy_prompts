ok, wierd request. I'm a backend/infra engineer, zero philosophy training, and I've been falling down the AI safety rabbit hole on nights and weekends. Here's what keeps bugging me. In my day job, when we call a system "safe" or "correct," we mean something specific: it holds under the failure model we wrote down, and we can point at the assumption that breaks when it doesn't. Worst case, with the adversary named. Then I read alignment stuff and it's all "it works on the eval set," "average-case performance," and everyone nods. Fine — but nobody ever tells me what a *theory* of worst-case safety for these models would have to deliver before I'd actually ship with it.

So write me the spec. Not a survey, not "here are three schools of thought" — the requirements list. What does any theory of worst-case guarantees for an AI system have to get right for me to take it seriously? Give me the criteria, each with the case or consideration behind it: the failure modes it has to rule out, the distinctions it can't hand-wave (e.g. "guaranteed not to *try* to do X" vs. "guaranteed not to *manage* to do X" — are those even seperate requirements or am I just confused?), and whatever it has to stay consistent with.

Two things I want baked in as requirements, because I think they're where this gets hard and I never see them treated that way:

1. The system can read the guarantee. The moment you publish "this model is safe under conditions C," the model knows C, the next model trained on its outputs knows C, the guy deploying it knows C. So: the guarantee has to survive the thing it's guaranteeing knowing about itself. Is that even statable, or does it eat itself?

2. The adversary picks the distribution after seeing your defense. In security we assume the attacker knows the protocal. Most robustness stuff I read seems to assume a fixed distribution that doesn't move. Does the spec have to demand an adversary-adaptive guarantee, and if so is that achievable, or does it just collapse into "you can't beat an adversary who picks the world after you've picked your move"?

Then: which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste, and which single requirement does the most work at killing off the theories people actually propose. And flag any two requirements you think can't both hold at once — that's the part I actually want, the list becoming a result in itself.

Last thing, and I mean this: tell me where you're least sure. Which requirement are you most likely wrong to include, and what would you have to see to drop it? If someone showed you X, would the whole spec change? I'd rather have a spec with the weak point flagged than a confident one I can't audit.
