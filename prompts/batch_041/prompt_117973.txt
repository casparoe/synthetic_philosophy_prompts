I'm in a policy seminar on military manpower and armed neutrality — we've spent two weeks on the Swiss militia, Finnish conscription, and the Nordic arguments over whether to keep, restore, or abandon compulsory service — and one framing keeps coming back, from my professor and from most of the room. It is that conscription is simply a tax. The state may compel you to surrender money to pay for defense, so it may compel you to surrender labor for the same purpose; both are compulsory transfers of a resource you own toward a public good, and the only difference is the medium, hours instead of currency. The analogy is not decorative — it is made to carry three conclusions. First, that the draft is not slavery, since we do not regard the income tax as slavery and the two are alike in the relevant respect. Second, that a universal obligation is fairer than a market in soldiers, because everyone pays and no one buys an exemption. Third, that a volunteer force is really the same tax in regressive form, its risk offloaded onto those with the fewest alternatives. I would like an honest audit of how far the comparison holds, because I suspect it is bearing more weight than it can, but I don't want to be the person who simply asserts that bodies and bank accounts are different and stops there.

Give me the correspondence explicitly, as a table whose columns are: element of the tax analogy; what it maps onto in conscription; how tight the fit is (tight, loose, or fails); and what turns on that fit for one or more of the three conclusions. Work through the pairs the analogy supplies — the taxable base, the rate, the bracket structure, exemptions and deductions, the commutation fee or paid substitute, evasion and emigration, the collecting authority, the deadweight loss — and then the features it has no slot for: that a term of service is indivisible and cannot be paid in instalments, that the burden falls on a narrow age-and-ability cohort rather than on income, that its opportunity cost varies enormously across people who are equally liable, that it exposes the payer to death and to the obligation to kill, and that it is often defended as a formative civic experience rather than as a cost. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the three conclusions from those that are merely picturesque, and for each of the three say whether the analogy supports it, undercuts it, or is silent about it.

Close with a JSON object with exactly these fields: analogy (the comparison as stated, one sentence); mappings (array of objects with fields source, target, fit, consequence); load_bearing_disanalogies (array of strings, each naming a break and the conclusion it damages); incidental_disanalogies (array of strings); conclusions (array of objects with fields claim, verdict, reason, where verdict is one of supported, unsupported, or silent); and better_analogy (one or two sentences proposing a comparison that fits the intended work more closely, or an explicit statement that no single comparison will do).
