Ok so I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly Python and Go), no philosophy training at all, and I've been trying to write a short essay about truth for my blog. I'd like you to comment on it, and harshly. I don't need encouragement, I need to know where it's wrong and where it's boring.

Here's the gist of what I'm arguing.

I think "true" isn't a property that statements have, the way "red" is a property of a fire truck. It's more like a pronoun, or a compression trick. If I say "snow is white" and then I say "that's true," I haven't added a second fact to the world. I've just re-said the first thing in a shorter form. I half-remember reading somewhere that a philosopher made basically this point -- that "that's true" functions like "he" or "it," standing in for a sentence the way a pronoun stands in for a noun, instead of naming some property the sentence has. I might be butchering it, but that's the intuition I'm running with.

In code terms: I keep wanting to say truth is like a function that takes a sentence and hands back the same sentence, or like a variable you can assign a claim to and pass around. It's plumbing, not a property. What it buys you is that you can agree with someone without repeating their whole sentence, and you can say things about a pile of claims at once. "Everything the auditor said is true" -- try deleting "is true" and you can't say it anymore, you'd have to relist all the claims one by one. So the word is doing something. It's just not describing a feature of the world.

Now the parts I'm less sure about, and where I'd like your comments:

1. Explanations. People say things like "the theory works because its predictions are true," or "the bridge failed because the engineer's assumptions were false." That really sounds like truth and falsity are doing causal work, which definately can't be right if truth is just plumbing. Is my account dead here, or is there a way to hear those sentences that doesn't turn truth into a force?

2. Beliefs. My pronoun analogy works ok for sentences, because there's a sentence to point back to. But "her belief that the meeting is at 3 is true" -- the belief isn't a sentence. Does the analogy survive that, or do I need a different story?

3. The liar. "This sentence is not true." Under my story I want to say "true" here is still just echoing the sentence, but then the sentence is echoing itself and I get dizzy. I genuinely don't know if this is a problem for my view specifically or a problem for everybody. Please tell me which.

4. Whether any of this is actually a claim. A friend said my essay amounts to "truth is just a word we use," and that this is either trivial or secretly a big controversial thesis, and I should pick one. I couldn't tell him which. Can you?

What I want from you: (a) tell me if the core analogy is actually correct, or if I've smuggled in a real theory of truth while claiming not to have one; (b) give me the specific cases where the analogy breaks -- I'd rather have three sharp counterexamples than a general "it's more complicated than that"; (c) flag anything I've stated as fact that's just wrong (I'm worried I've garbled the pronoun thing); (d) tell me honestly whether this is an interesting angle or something everyone in the field settled decades ago and I'm about to look silly. Don't go easy on it.
