I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am marking a mock exam answer for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind. I would be grateful for your assessment of the answer below.

The question was:

Explain Dennett's distinction between the physical, design, and intentional stances. Then assess whether his doctrine of real patterns commits him to realism or instrumentalism about beliefs and desires. Illustrate your answer with the example of a glider in Conway's Game of Life and the example of a chess-playing computer.

The student's answer was:

Dennett distinguishes three stances that we can adopt when predicting and explaining the behaviour of a system. The physical stance predicts behaviour from the laws of physics and the system's physical constitution. The design stance predicts behaviour from the assumption that the system is designed to perform certain functions; it asks what the system is for. The intentional stance predicts behaviour by attributing beliefs and desires to the system, and then assuming that it will act rationally given those beliefs and desires. These stances are nested: the design stance presupposes the physical stance, and the intentional stance presupposes the design stance. But they are not merely different descriptions of the same physical facts. Each stance reveals a pattern that is real if it supports predictions and compresses information about the system's behaviour.

Dennett's example of a glider in Conway's Game of Life illustrates this. At the physical level, the glider is just a configuration of cells following the rules of the game. But the glider pattern is real because we can predict its movement and use it to explain other events in the game without simulating every cell. The pattern is not a fiction; it is a real pattern in the system. Similarly, a chess-playing computer can be described physically, in terms of transistors, or at the design level, in terms of its program and its goal of winning. But the most efficient way to predict its moves is often to adopt the intentional stance: to attribute to it the belief that its queen is threatened and the desire to protect it.

Does this make Dennett a realist or an instrumentalist about beliefs? Dennett is often read as an instrumentalist because he says that beliefs and desires are useful fictions. He denies that there are any inner representations that correspond to our belief attributions. But this is not quite right. Dennett's real patterns are objective in the sense that they are there to be discovered, even though they are interest-relative. A pattern is real if it is detectable from multiple descriptions and supports predictions. So Dennett is a mild realist about beliefs: they are not mere fictions, but they are not inner objects either. They are real patterns at the intentional level. The intentional stance is not just a useful way of speaking; it latches onto a pattern that is genuinely present in the system's behaviour.

However, Dennett also insists that the intentional stance is only appropriate for systems that are conscious or that have genuine mental states. A chess computer does not really have beliefs and desires; we only attribute them to it for predictive convenience. So the realism about patterns does not extend to the mental states of non-conscious systems. This is why Dennett's position is sometimes called interpretationism: mental states are real only relative to the interpretive stance we adopt.
