Two of my friends, Ines and Marcus, got into a thing at dinner last week about a contract our other friend Dana signed with her brother, and they're still going at it. I got asked to referee and I honestly could'nt, so I'm outsourcing it.

The setup, since it matters: Dana has stretches where she's up for days and does stuff with money she massively regrets afterwards. So during a stable stretch she wrote up an agreement with her brother — he holds a chunk of her savings, and he can refuse to release any of it if he thinks she's heading into one of those stretches. Three details seem to be doing all the work. (a) The criteria for him saying no are things like "unusually energetic," "sleeping way less than usual," "making big plans" — her own words, but obviously not a checklist. (b) Revocation isn't forbidden, but there's a ratchet: if she tries to cancel, the agreement automatically extends for another year. (c) The only way to change it is a vote of three people — her brother, her sister, and a friend she picked at the time — and she only gets one of those three votes herself.

Ines says the whole thing is indefensible. You can't consent on behalf of someone who doesn't exist yet; the Dana who signed and the Dana who gets her money frozen are, morally speaking, two different people, and the second one never agreed to anything. She says it's guardianship with a friendlier name, that the brother is the one who decides when "unusually energetic" kicks in, and that vagueness is exactly where discretion hides — if the criteria were mechanical enough to be fair they'd be gameable, and if they're vague enough to be useful they're just his judgment about her mind. The ratchet is the tell: a device that punishes you for wanting out isn't a promise, it's a trap.

Marcus says thats a load of it and proves way too much. Every promise binds a future self, and if future selves can't be bound then promises, marriages and student loans are all out the window. And if the two Danas really are different people, the calm one is the one who has to live with the wreckage the other one leaves, so she's entitled to defend herself. He says a commitment device you can undo at the exact moment you most want to undo it does nothing, that exit and appeal are precisely what make these things useless, and that we do this all the time at a bigger scale — constitutions bind future majorities, Odysseus had himself tied to the mast.

Ines came back with: the constitution thing cuts the other way, since any constitution that lasts has some way of being amended, and the ones that don't are just whichever side won the last fight nailing the board down. Also a constitution binds a state, and its authority comes from the ongoing consent of the governed, not from one person's judgment about another person's mood. Marcus said she'd specified the criteria herself, while calm, with people she chose, which is about as literal a case of self-government as you can get — and that Ines's alternative, where only the present self's voice counts, means whoever's at the wheel right now rules, which is worse, not freer. Ines said "you chose the panel" only helps if the panel is applying her criteria rather than its own, and from outside there's no way to tell the difference.

Can you go through this move by move and rule on each one? Not who wins overall so much as where each of them is right, where each is overreaching, and what both of them are missing. If you do want to say which of them is closer to right overall, fine, but the point-by-point is what I actually need. And please don't just hand me the names of positions on this — I want the arguments themselves assessed.
