I am a novelist, part-way through a book whose central character is a regulator — the sort of person who decides whether something is allowed to happen — and I have reached the scene in which she must give a name to a pattern she has spent her career noticing. I would rather she named the right thing than a plausible near-miss, so before I write the line I want the concept properly sorted out. Please act as a philosopher of decision-making with a working knowledge both of the cognitive-bias literature and of risk regulation. Think carefully and at length; begin by brainstorming the candidates before committing to any of them, and be explicit wherever you are unsure or wherever the field itself is divided.

Here is the pattern, in the plainest terms I can manage, drawn from the arguments I have been reading and staging.

- In a dispute about whether to do something — approve a drug, drain a marsh, release an organism, ban a pesticide, intervene in a system currently left alone — the harms of doing it must be proved, case by case, while the harms of leaving it alone are simply the way things are and are entered in no column at all.
- The asymmetry survives agreement about the numbers: even where everyone concedes the probabilities, the harm of acting is treated as real and the harm of not acting as speculative.
- When a safeguard is proposed that itself carries a cost — a delay that will cost lives, a prohibition that will push the practice out of sight — the cost of the safeguard is booked as a fresh harm, while the harm it was meant to avert is not booked at all, as though the good intention had cancelled it.
- The person who wants to act must show that nothing will go wrong; the person who wants to leave things be is asked to show nothing, because leaving things be does not count as a decision.

A friend in law tells me this is the precautionary principle, and another tells me it is simply omission bias, but neither answer satisfies me. The precautionary principle appears to be a rule about what to do when we do not know enough, whereas my pattern persists when we know perfectly well. Omission bias appears to concern how we judge a person who has done a harm against one who has merely let it happen, whereas my pattern concerns how the harms themselves are counted, by people who are not judging anyone in particular.

So: does this configuration, taken as a whole, have a name? If an established concept covers part of it, I want to know exactly which part, and what the remainder is. If nothing covers it, please coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary cases along with a case that resembles it but is not it. It would help me to see, side by side, each candidate concept with what it does and does not account for, and to be told which of them are descriptions and which are prescriptions. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I am asking for the carving. Finally, in a sentence or two, tell me whether the asymmetry is ever defensible — irreversibility, option value, some such consideration — because I need to know whether I am writing a villain.

Please answer in British English.
