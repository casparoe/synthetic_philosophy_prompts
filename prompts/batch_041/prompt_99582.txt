I run a seminar on logic and AI, and after last week's session two of my students, Ana and Ben, got into an argument in the hallway that I got pulled into and then couldn't referee. It was about logical omniscience and I lost the thread around the third round. I'd like you to go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins overall. Here's my best reconstruction.

Ana opened: she said the standard possible-worlds way of modeling knowledge is a dead end for AI, because it forces every agent to know every logical truth. If knowing P just means P is true in all the worlds you haven't ruled out, then since a tautology is true in all worlds, you know it — even if you've never heard of it. Worse, if you know P and you know P→Q, then in every accessible world Q holds too, so you know Q. She said no actual reasoner is like that, so the model doesn't describe anyone.

Ben said that's like complaining the ideal gas law doesn't include van der Waals forces. The model is an idealization, he said, and 'knows' in it means an idealized, omniscient knower; of course an omniscient knower knows all the consequences. The real question is which idealizations earn their keep, not whether they're literally true of us.

Ana said the gas analogy fails because knowledge is factive and it's supposed to be *the agent's* knowledge. An idealization that hands me a theorem I've never entertained isn't an idealized version of my knowledge, it's a different thing using the same word. And she said the real culprit is closure, not the worlds: you could keep the worlds and simply refuse to let knowledge close under implication.

Ben said dropping closure is much worse. If you know P and you know P implies Q and you don't know Q, what exactly are you doing when you reason? He said closure is just the shadow of the fact that accepting the premises and accepting the inference *is* accepting the conclusion. The fix isn't to drop closure, he said, but to admit that 'knows' in the model means something closer to 'is committed to' or 'would accept on reflection' than to 'has it occurrently in mind.' Nobody thinks all their knowledge is before their mind at every moment.

Ana said that relabeling just moves the bump under the rug: committed-to knowledge is still closed under all the consequences, so the agent is committed to infinitely many things, and if it's also committed to denying one of them somewhere down the chain it's inconsistent rather than merely ignorant. Then she gave an example. Imagine a machine that knows the axioms and the inference rules and can produce any proof up to a million steps, but there's a theorem whose shortest proof is a million and one steps. Ben says the machine knows it, because it follows. Ana says it plainly doesn't know it, and that this is the whole problem in miniature: the model can't tell 'knows' from 'could work it out with more time and paper.'

Ben said you can fix that either by indexing the accessibility relation to a resource bound or by adding an 'awareness' set, so that the agent only knows formulas it's aware of and the long theorem isn't known until it's entertained. He said that's not ad hoc, it matches ordinary talk: you can't know a proposition you haven't so much as considered.

Ana said the awareness patch has its own holes. If awareness is arbitrary, the agent still knows every consequence of the formulas it's aware of, so you get omniscience relative to the awareness set — the same disease with a smaller domain. And if awareness is not closed under consequence, then the agent can be aware of P, aware of P→Q, and aware of Q, and still not know Q, which just breaks closure again. She said she'd even tried a version where knowledge is indexed to a question the agent is actually trying to answer, so only consequences bearing on that question count, but that looked like awareness with extra steps and hit the same fork. She said the deeper thing the model is missing is that inference costs something and takes time; 'knows' is being treated as a static relation when it's really the output of a process. She added that any cutoff will be arbitrary, and Ben said exactly, arbitrary cutoffs are a reason for suspicion, and Ana said arbitrary cutoffs are fine — our theories of vision and memory have them — the problem is when the line is drawn by the modeler's convenience instead of the agent's actual architecture.

Ben said fine, but that's an argument for bolting a proof theory or a computational account onto the semantics, not for throwing the semantics out. He said you want one theory for what's true of an agent at a time and another for how the agent changes, and Ana keeps asking one model to do both jobs and then blaming it for not doing the second. Ana said then he should stop using the first model to make claims about what actual AI systems can know. Ben said fair, but then she owes him a positive account, and 'inference takes time' isn't one. She said it's a constraint on any account, which is already something. Thats where it ended, with both of them looking at me.

So: please adjudicate move by move. For each step, say whether the person making the move is right, wrong, or right but for a shakier reason than they think, and briefly why. Then tell me whether any part of this is just the two of them using 'knows' in different senses, and what each of them is missing that the other isn't. Theres alot to untangle here and I'd like to know which parts are genuinely open and which parts are one of them just not seeing the other's point.
