I teach a 90-minute session in a small upper-level seminar (14 students) on self-reference and strange loops, and this time I want to run Escher's Drawing Hands as an activity instead of a slide. You know the print — two hands on one sheet, each drawing the other, and the thing that actually stops the infinite regress is a hand that isn't in the picture at all, the level that isn't part of the tangle but that the tangle can't run without. In my own notes I just call that the inviolate level. I want the students to build a tangle of their own, play it for a while, and then walk into the wall where the only way to make the game make sense is to posit something outside it — and I don't want to say any of that out loud untill the debrief.

So design me the activity. Seed idea if it helps, throw it out if it's bad: put them in three teams arranged in a ring, where each team gets to rewrite the rules governing the next team around the circle, everyone plays a few scored rounds, then they try to patch the game by agreeing who's allowed to change what — and every patch either spawns a new level or forces them to freeze one rule they can't justify from inside the game. Movable chairs, index cards, a whiteboard, no laptops. The scoring should be real enough that they actually care who wins.

Give me the response as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no intro, no wrap-up. (1) setup and materials, (2) the rules and scoring in enough detail that I could run it cold, (3) the timeline minute by minute, (4) the debrief, mapping specific moments in the activity onto the tangled hierarchy and the outside level and telling me exactly where to keep my mouth shut, (5) what predictably goes sideways the first time I run it, plus the honest bit about what this activity can't reproduce so I know what I still have to say myself. Keep each item to a sentence or two.
