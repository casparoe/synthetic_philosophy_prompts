I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a draft, and I keep getting stuck on the same stupid problem. My protagonist runs a small hospice in a town where everyone knows everyone, and in scene after scene she has to decide whether some particular person counts as family — for visitation, for who gets told what, for who gets to be in the room at the end. I keep writing her as either a pushover who says yes to everybody or a rules-lawyer who says no to everybody, and neither one feels like a real person thinking.

I roughly know the Wittgenstein stuff about family resemblance and language games, but I can't get it off the page and into her hands. What I want is a procedure she can actually run in the moment, in the scene: a short ordered list of questions she asks herself, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the list bottoms out in a no, and where it stops and hands the thing to her as a choice rather than a fact. Keep it faithful to what the theory actually says, not a vibes version, but written so someone who's never read a word of philosophy could run it while a family is standing in the doorway. And since family resemblance famously won't give you a threshold for how much overlap is enough, tell me where the threshold has to come from instead, and be honest that it's a stipulation rather than something the theory hands her.

Then run it on three cases from the book. One: a paid caregiver of twelve years, no blood relation, whom the patient calls her daughter. Two: the patient's estranged biological son who shows up on the last day. Three — and this is the one I'm attached to and I suspect you're going to wreck — the patient's ex-husband, who was cruel to her for years, whom I have my protagonist admit because family is family. If the procedure says no to him, fine, say so and tell me why, and tell me what I'd have to change to earn the yes honestly.

Also: how does she tell the difference between a genuine borderline case, where the word is just doing its fuzzy thing, and someone using the word in a different game altogether — a different practice with different stakes — where it isn't really a disagreement at all? That distinction is the whole reason I'm asking.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words. Lead with the procedure itself, then the three worked cases, then a short bit on where it breaks down and where my editor is going to have to just make a call. Don't bury the procedure under throat-clearing — I need to be able to copy it onto an index card.
