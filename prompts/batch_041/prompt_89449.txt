I am currently in a graduate seminar that is part epistemology and part formal methods, and I have been trying to get a precise grip on the problem of logical omniscience. The textbook presentations tend to say that standard possible-worlds models of knowledge and belief validate closure principles—if an agent knows A and knows that A implies B, then she knows B; and if A is a logical truth, she knows A—and that this is implausible for finite, resource-bounded reasoners. I understand the complaint in a rough way, but I want a more conceptual explanation of what the problem really amounts to, why it is not simply the uncontroversial fact that people sometimes make deductive mistakes, and how it relates to neighboring puzzles such as the problem of old evidence, the difficulty of learning logical truths, and the difference between explicit and implicit belief. I would also like you to explain why the problem seems to reappear even in probabilistic frameworks: if an agent's credence function is defined over a logically closed algebra, logically equivalent propositions must receive the same credence, yet ordinary agents seem to have credences in sentences under particular syntactic guises and not in all their logical equivalents. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of citations; I want the conceptual map.

After a seminar, a friend proposed a distinction that I cannot decide whether it is deep or merely verbal. He distinguishes an agent's deductive reach—what follows from the representations she explicitly has, assuming unlimited time and perfect inference—from her doxastic grip—what she is actually disposed to affirm when asked, given her cognitive limitations. He claims that logical omniscience is only a problem if we identify knowledge or belief with deductive reach; if we model it as doxastic grip, then closure failures are not counterexamples to a theory but simply data about human psychology. I suspect this is too quick: it threatens to make logic irrelevant to what one ought to believe, and it leaves unanswered what the objects of belief are supposed to be if not propositions individuated by logical content. Please adjudicate between his distinction and my worry, explain which traditional response to logical omniscience it most resembles, if any, and indicate how one might state the closure principle in a way that is both non-trivial and psychologically realistic. Be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the point you are least sure about.
