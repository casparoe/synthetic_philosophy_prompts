You are a philosopher of decision theory with a strong background in analysis and probability. Your task is to give a careful, self-contained explanation of a conceptual problem in the foundations of expected utility theory. Think step by step, and before you begin writing the explanation proper, spend some time brainstorming the mathematical setup and the possible diagnoses so that your final answer is well organized.

Consider a game — call it the Pasadena game — played as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads for the first time. If heads first appears on toss n, the player receives a payoff of

(-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n

units of money (so the payoff is positive when n is odd and negative when n is even, and its magnitude grows with n). The probability that the game ends on toss n is 1/2^n.

Please explain the following, in an integrated way rather than as a list of unrelated remarks:

1. Why the expected monetary payoff of this game is naturally written as the alternating harmonic series, why that series converges, and what value it converges to. Be explicit about the difference between convergence and absolute convergence here.

2. Why the failure of absolute convergence creates a philosophical problem, not merely a technical inconvenience. In particular, explain why the value of the expectation appears to depend on the order in which the outcomes are summed, and why that is troubling given that the outcomes of a probability distribution do not seem to come with a privileged ordering. Is the natural ordering by number of coin tosses somehow special, or is appealing to it ad hoc?

3. What it means to say that the game has an undefined expected value, as opposed to an infinite one. Contrast this briefly with the St. Petersburg game, where the expectation diverges to infinity. Why is undefinedness arguably a deeper problem for expected utility theory than infinite value?

4. Whether it is rational to play the Pasadena game for a small fee, and how a decision theorist might try to answer that question without relying on a well-defined expected value. Discuss at least one strategy that does not simply stipulate a value for the game, and assess its strengths and weaknesses.

5. What the Pasadena case suggests about the ambition of expected utility theory to provide a complete theory of rational choice under risk. Does the existence of prospects with undefined expectation show that the theory is incomplete, that it is false, or merely that it needs supplementary principles? Defend your answer.

Aim for an explanation that a mathematically literate reader who has not encountered this puzzle before could follow, and make clear at each stage which claims are mathematical and which are interpretive or philosophical.
