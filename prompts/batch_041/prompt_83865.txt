I have taught in both a German and an American philosophy department, and I keep noticing a pattern in how people describe someone who follows a rule against the grain. The same conduct gets opposite descriptions depending on whether the observer approves of the rule. A pharmacist who refuses to dispense a drug he thinks does harm is 'principled' to one audience and 'a fanatic hiding behind a rule' to the other. A civil servant who carries out an order he finds repellent is 'conscientious' to one audience and 'a coward who has outsourced his conscience' to the other. The reverse case flips in the same way: the man who follows his own judgment against a rule is 'making an exception for himself' when the observer approves of the rule and 'a man of conscience' when the observer does not. So 'integrity' and 'conscience' seem to be awarded on the strength of our verdict on the content of the judgment rather than on anything about the agent's relation to his own judgment, which makes me doubt the words do any work of their own. I notice a second thing as well: each of the two traditions I move between — one that holds a rule binds because it is the rule, one that holds only a man's own judgment binds him — has a well-worn name for the other's characteristic failure (legalism, blind obedience; self-righteous individualism, conscience as a licence) and no name for its own, which it does not count as a failure at all.

Is there an established concept for either pattern? I have been told the first is just 'thick concepts', but that seems to concern the meaning of the words rather than the conditions under which we hand them out, and I doubt the second has a name at all. Don't simply supply a label: say what each phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — including what would count as integrity or conscience that survives a verdict on the content. Give me a definite verdict rather than a survey of options: which existing concept comes closest, whether it is close enough to do the work, and if not, what you would call the thing instead and why that carving is better.
