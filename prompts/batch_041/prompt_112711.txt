I keep returning to the Hart–Fuller debate and its long afterlife, and I have stopped believing that either side is ever going to move the other. Hart's line is that a sufficiently iniquitous rule can still be law, just bad law, and that calling it law is not to endorse it; his critics insist that a rule of that kind fails to be law at all. The same handful of moves get re-run every decade. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Is this a verbal dispute, the two sides attaching different criteria to the word law so that a sentence like "that is a law" comes out true on one usage and false on the other with nothing else at stake? Is it two different questions wearing one name — a descriptive-analytical question about what officials in fact treat as valid, and a normative question about what they owe fidelity to? Or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement, and if so, about what: whether legal validity is in part a moral matter, or about the methodology of the concept itself — whether we should analyse the concept of law we have or engineer a better one to replace it?

My working hypothesis is that the third reading is correct but that each side argues as though the first were, which is why the exchange stays live and fruitless. Tell me whether that holds up, and then say what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a changed question, a bit of conceptual engineering — or whether an impasse of this shape is simply what we should expect.
