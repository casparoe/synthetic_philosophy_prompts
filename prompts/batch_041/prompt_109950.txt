I have been defending a position in a long-running argument with friends, and what I want from you is a map of its exposure rather than encouragement. The position is that the rationality of cooperation has nothing essentially to do with causal influence. What matters is whether my choosing to cooperate is evidence about what the other party does — the Newcomb-style point, generalized. If the world is large enough (many civilizations, many agents who reason as I do, plausibly many instances of me), then the number of agents whose choices are evidentially correlated with mine may be vast, and the local cost of cooperating with them may be negligible beside it. So I claim that I ought to cooperate with strangers I can never affect and who can never affect me; that I ought to be willing to give up a great deal, including things my own community cares about, on behalf of those distant correlated agents; and that none of this waits on signaling, reputation, enforcement, or travel. The rival view — that cooperation is rational only where it can be detected, reciprocated, punished, or rewarded — seems to me a superstition about causation, a refusal to notice that the very feature of me that makes me defect is evidence about what my counterparts do.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this. I mean defeaters of both sorts: arguments and results that attack the conditional (if choices are evidentially correlated in the right way, cooperation is required), and evidence that attacks the premise (that the world is large in the relevant sense, and that the population of agents is correlated with me rather than merely similar in some incidental respect). Sort them by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a committed defender would themselves concede, as opposed to the ones critics rehearse and defenders shrug off. I am especially interested in whether the evidential relation survives what a causal decision theorist will say screens it off: if my counterpart's choice is already fixed by a common cause we share, does my choice still carry information about it, or has that information been absorbed by everything else I know? I also want to know what happens when the correlations run the wrong way — an agent whose choices are anti-correlated with mine, or a population in which cooperating and defecting are equally diagnostic of me, so that I have no way to select which counterparts I am correlated with. And I want to know whether the view needs a measure over agents, and what becomes of it if the population is infinite or its size unknowable — whether the arithmetic that gives the argument its force survives that. One further worry I cannot settle: whether the position collapses into a claim about what type of agent I am, in which case my deliberation is not the lever at all and the view describes character rather than choice.

Finally, tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all or has been quietly insulated by qualifications to the effect that it holds only when the correlation is of the right kind. If it has been insulated, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a defect in a view of this sort or simply the price of a view that is part conceptual and part empirical. I should admit what would not move me: I am unimpressed by the complaint that evidential cooperators do not end up richer, since by hypothesis the payoff is not local — tell me if that reply is a dodge. What I would find most persuasive is either a case in which the position, taken seriously, licenses something its own adherents could not accept on reflection (a betrayal of the near for the sake of the far), or an argument showing that the evidential and causal framings converge after all, so that the position is not a rival to the ordinary view but a restatement of it.
