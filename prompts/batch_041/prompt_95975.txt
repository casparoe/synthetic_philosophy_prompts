I need advice on something I've managed to get myself into. I sit on the board of the historical society in the small town I grew up in — one room of exhibits in the old library, county money plus donations, that's the whole operation. Next spring is the hundredth anniversary of a bad night in 1926 when the lumber mill burned down and eleven men died.

The paperwork is boring and consistent. The company's own books, the coroner's report and both papers say the fire started in the drying shed and that the men died because a foreman had the exit doors padlocked to stop guys slipping out early. I have no particular reason to doubt any of it.

The families of the dead tell it completely differently, and have since at least the 40s. In their version the mill was torched for the insurance, the doors were chained on purpose, and there's a name attached — one of the owner's sons. The story has drifted over the decades (the name only shows up in versions from the 70s on, I'm told), one branch of the family thinks the whole thing is nonsense, and the son was supposedly at a dance in the next town that night, which the hotel register backs up. Meanwhile the drying shed logs for that year are missing, and the paper was owned by the mill family, so the documents aren't exactly independent either.

My boards split down the middle and I have the deciding vote. Half say we show the documents and label the families' version as a tradition, full stop. Half say the documents are the arsonist's story and we should present the families' account as the account. I have to hand in the label text in June. Which version should be doing the real work of the exhibit? I'd rather you tell me plainly which one gives way than tell me both are valid ways of knowing — I've had that conversation twice this month already and it doesn't help me write a single sentence. And how do I explain the call to the half of the board I'm overruling without it looking like I'm picking a side in a family fight?
