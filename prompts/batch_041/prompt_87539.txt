I am a graduate student who has been asked to run the undergraduate tutorial on Berlin's two concepts of liberty next week, and I have spent the last few days trying to fit republican liberty onto the same map so that I can say something sensible about it. My difficulty is that I suspect I have drawn the map wrongly, and I would much rather be corrected now than in front of a room of students. The way I have it set out, negative liberty is freedom from interference, positive liberty is freedom to be one's own master, and these two exhaust the field; on that picture republican freedom as non-domination has to be a species of positive liberty, since its whole point appears to be that you are not subject to another person's will, that you stand to yourself as a master rather than as a servant. When I was told that republicans themselves insist their view is a form of negative liberty, I assumed this was a verbal manoeuvre, because non-domination is after all the absence of something rather than the presence of some activity, and one can always gerrymander the labels. A colleague also mentioned that the more revealing divide is between theories that treat liberty as an opportunity and theories that treat it as an exercise, and that republicans belong on the first side of that line, but I cannot see how that amounts to anything more than a restatement of the negative and positive split.

What I would like is an explanation of where the mistake lies, if there is one, and of the axis along which these views are actually best arranged. In particular I want to understand what the difference between interference and domination is supposed to be, and why the case of someone who holds the power to interfere but never exercises it — a slave whose master is kind and never present, a despot who has never once overruled his subjects — is supposed to count as unfreedom for the republican and as perfect freedom for the person who identifies liberty with non-interference. And if republicans are right that non-domination is not self-mastery in Berlin's sense, I do not see what prevents their view from collapsing back into the negative liberty it claims to improve upon: when a law forbids me to do something my choices have been interfered with, and whether that law is arbitrary seems to me beside the point, or at most a matter of how resentful I happen to feel about it.

Please write the answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a long email; I want to be able to follow an argument rather than read a list of positions, and I would much rather be told plainly where my framing goes wrong than have it politely accommodated.
