I keep a running list of cases where I'd say I know something versus cases where I'd only say I believe it, and I want to find the principle behind the list. For context: I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. A friend lent me a book on classical Indian epistemology, and the framework in it recognizes four means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and testimony from a trustworthy source. I'd like to test my intuitions against that framework, or against something simpler if my list won't fit it. Here is the list, with the verdict I actually have.

I see smoke rising over the ridge and conclude the cabin is on fire. I'd say I know the cabin is on fire.

I read 101.4 off a digital thermometer and conclude I have a fever. I'd say I know I have a fever, even though I have no idea whether the thermometer is calibrated or who made it.

My doctor examines me and tells me I have strep throat. I'd say I know it.

A stranger in line at the store, with no particular expertise, tells me I have strep throat. I'd say I don't know it, even if it turns out he's right.

A park ranger tells me that a gavaya looks just like a cow except for its horns. Later I see one in the forest and recognize it as a gavaya. I'd say I know what a gavaya is, and I'd say the ranger's description is what gave me that knowledge.

I remember locking the front door last night. I'd say I know I locked it — even though the book says memory doesn't get its own slot among the means of knowledge, because it only replays a cognition some earlier means already produced.

A friend tells me the movie starts at seven. I'd say I know it starts at seven.

Now the two that I can't get to sit together. First: I glance at the sundial in my yard and read the time. I'd say I know the time. Second: a neighbor glances at the same sundial and tells me the time. I'd also say I know the time. But a printed card taped to the theater door that reads "showtime 7:00" — I would not say I know the showtime from the card alone, because I don't know who printed it or whether it's current. All three are just marks on a surface that I'm taking as telling me something. I keep wanting to say the sundial is different because I'm reading it directly and the card is different because someone is speaking to me through it, but I can't state the difference in a way that survives contact with the next case.

Here's the tentative principle I came up with on my own, which I'd like you to test and repair rather than just accept: a belief counts as knowledge when it was produced by a process that reliably tracks the fact in question, and I have no specific reason to doubt it on this particular occasion. That principle seems to get the smoke, the doctor, and the sundial right, and to get the stranger and the printed card right. But I notice it treats my memory of the door and the stranger's claim about strep the same way — both are fallible processes that happened to be right this time — and yet I accept the first and reject the second. So either the principle is wrong, or one of those two verdicts has to go.

What I want from you:

First, say whether any single principle captures my whole list, or whether I need a small set of them. If it takes a set, state each principle precisely and tell me which case in my list motivates each one.

Second, if the four-means framework from the book is the best fit, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — and be honest about whether it actually explains my verdicts or merely relabels them. If a simpler principle fits better, tell me which of my verdicts it overturns and why I should accept the loss.

Third, apply whichever account you land on to two cases I haven't listed, and commit to a verdict for each. The first: I measure a distance on a topographic map with a piece of string and conclude the summit is three miles away. The second: I read a pressure value off a factory gauge, and it turns out the gauge is broken but happens to display the correct value at that moment. I want to know whether your account says I know in each case, and if the two come out differently, what exactly does the work.

Fourth, tell me where the account runs out — the kind of case it can't decide, or the intuition it can't honor no matter how it's patched.
