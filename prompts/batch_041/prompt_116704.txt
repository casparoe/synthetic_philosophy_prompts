I have no formal training in philosophy or linguistics, but I keep a notebook of arguments I have overheard and of the sentences people use to settle them, and I try to work out what those sentences are actually committed to. One sentence keeps turning up, and I can no longer tell what it is supposed to establish: "It's not what you said, it's what you meant."

I have heard it do at least two incompatible jobs. A friend made a remark about another friend's cooking that landed as an insult; when the other friend was hurt, he answered with that sentence, apparently meaning that because he had not intended to wound, he was not answerable for the wound. Weeks later, in a meeting, a manager heard his own words read back to him and replied that what he had said was not what he had meant, so the rest of us were simply misreading him; there the sentence seemed to enlarge his responsibility rather than shrink it. I have also heard it invoked in what sounded like the opposite spirit, when someone insisted that a book can mean something its author never intended.

I suspect the sentence cannot be doing all of these jobs at once, and that at least one use of it is confused. What I want is not a general essay on language but a disambiguation: the distinct precise claims this one sentence could be making, set out as a menu, with a verdict on each. Is it claiming only the obvious point that people often convey more than their words literally state? Is it claiming that what a speaker means is fixed entirely by the speaker's private intention, so that no listener may ever correct them? Is it claiming the reverse, that meaning is fixed by what a reasonable listener would infer, so that the listener's reading is authoritative? Is it claiming that words have no meaning of their own at all? Is it, as I sometimes suspect, merely a way of declining to be held to anything one has said?

For each reading I would like to know whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and which reading is really in play when the sentence is used to excuse a remark that landed badly, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. I have also come across the name Grice in connection with the idea that what a speaker means and what the sentence means can come apart, and I would like to know whether the slogan is his position or a distortion of it; if the strongest version of the slogan actually contradicts the theory it is usually credited to, I would rather be told so plainly than be flattered. Please write your answer in American English.
