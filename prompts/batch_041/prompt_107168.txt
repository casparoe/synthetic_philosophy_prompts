I teach a first-year seminar called Persons and Possibility, and next week I have to explain origin essentialism—the claim that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg—to students who have had exactly one prior session on possible worlds and the necessary/contingent distinction. They are bright and argumentative, but they have no background in metaphysics beyond that. I do not want you to explain the view to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it in a single seventy-five-minute class. What has to come first, second, and third for the argument to feel like a discovery rather than a stipulation? For instance, must the possible-worlds apparatus be fully on the table before the biological case is introduced, or does the biological case itself help fix the meaning of that modal phrase? What exactly does the notion of an essential property need to carry here, and where does it threaten to do too much work?

Then tell me which single example should carry the whole explanation, and why not the ones I am tempted by. I was thinking of using the case of a couple who create several embryos through IVF, implant one, and later tell the resulting child that it could have been one of the others. But I worry that this example smuggles in the assumption that the embryo is already the person, and so begs the question in favor of origin essentialism rather than illustrating it. Is there a better example—perhaps one involving identical twins, or a case where the same sperm and egg would have produced a different person if the timing of fertilization had differed? What does the carrying example need to have, and what must it conspicuously lack, so that the students see the modal claim rather than a biological claim? Then map the misconceptions they will predictably form: that origin essentialism is just genetic determinism; that it implies you could not have been adopted or raised differently; that it confuses the identity of the organism with the identity of the person; that it is a religious view about the soul; that it proves too much because it would also mean you could not have been born in a different city; and that it is obviously false because you are your memories or your character, not your DNA. Tell me at which point in the sequence I should head off each one, and give me one closing question that would show whether they have grasped the modal structure of the view rather than just memorized the sperm-and-egg formula. Finally, tell me what I should explicitly leave out at this level—what technical apparatus or controversial move I should say I am setting aside—so that the session stays honest about its limits.
