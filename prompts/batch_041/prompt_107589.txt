I'm trying to get clear on lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain for a seminar. The rough idea is that pain has lexical priority over pleasure up to some threshold — any amount of pain below the threshold outweighs any amount of pleasure, and above the threshold you can trade pain against pleasure at some rate. I've got a list of ways to fill in the details and I want you to rank them into tiers by how defensible they are as a moral view, not by how often they're held. Here's the list: (1) pure lexical negative utilitarianism: any pain, however mild, outweighs any pleasure, however great; (2) threshold at intense suffering: only pain above some severity counts lexically, mild pain is commensurable with pleasure; (3) threshold at a life: only pain that makes a life not worth living has lexical priority; (4) a two-parameter rule with a threshold and a post-threshold exchange rate, where the rate can be set to make pleasure arbitrarily cheap; (5) a grace-band view: pain has lexical priority only when it's undeserved or unchosen, otherwise it's commensurable; (6) a view where pains and pleasures are only ordinally comparable and lexical priority is just a tie-break rule; (7) an agent-relative view: my pain has lexical priority for me, but not for a utilitarian aggregator. Rank them into maybe three tiers, say what each gets right and wrong, and tell me which one you'd actually defend if forced. Also, if the whole lexical-threshold framing is confused, say which item is closest to the confusion.
