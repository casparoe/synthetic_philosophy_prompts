I am a graduate student in a seminar on computability and the philosophy of mind, and we have been spending several weeks on the Church-Turing thesis and its epistemic status. The professor handed out a passage from Turing's 1938 PhD thesis, Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals, and asked us to explain what Turing is doing with the distinction between "computable function" and "effectively calculable." The passage reads:

"It was stated ... that "a function is effectively calculable if its values can be found by some purely mechanical process". We may take this literally, understanding that by a purely mechanical process one which could be carried out by a machine. The development ... leads to ... an identification of computability† with effective calculability."

The footnote attached to "computability" reads:

"We shall use the expression "computable function" to mean a function calculable by a machine, and let "effectively calculable" refer to the intuitive idea without particular identification with any one of these definitions."

I am having trouble understanding the epistemic status of the thesis in light of this passage. Turing seems to be saying that we can take the phrase "purely mechanical process" literally, as meaning a process that could be carried out by a machine, and that this leads to an identification of computability with effective calculability. But is that identification supposed to be a definition, a stipulation, an empirical hypothesis, or a substantive philosophical claim? And why does the footnote say that "effectively calculable" refers to the intuitive idea "without particular identification with any one of these definitions"? Does that mean Turing thinks the thesis cannot be proved, and if so, what kind of justification does he think it has? I have read that in the 1936 paper his argument for the thesis is often described as an appeal to intuition, and that he himself regarded this as mathematically unsatisfactory. How does that fit with the 1938 passage, where he seems to be saying that we may simply take the mechanical process literally? Is he changing his mind, or are these two ways of putting the same point? I would like help unpacking the passage and the surrounding argument, especially the distinction between the formal definition and the intuitive idea, and what it means to "identify" them. I am not looking for a bibliography or citations; I just want a clear explanation of the philosophical moves here.
