I retired last year from a career in condensed-matter physics and have been trying to teach myself philosophy since. My neighbor and a freind of his got into it at my kitchen table the other night over Thomson's lamp, and I've been chewing on it ever since — I'd like you to referee.

Here's how it went. The setup, as I understand it: a lamp, a switch, and a minute. You flip the switch at the 1/2-minute mark, then at 1/4, then 1/8, and so on, each flip reversing the lamp, so that infinitely many flips happen before the minute is up. Then the question: at the one-minute mark, is the lamp on or off?

Doug said: neither, and that's exactly the point — it proves the whole idea of a "supertask," infinitely many things done in a finite time, is incoherent. It can't be on, because every time it's on it gets turned off later; it can't be off, because every time it's off it gets turned on later; so the lamp has no state at the minute mark. But a real lamp has to have some state. So no such lamp. Marta said that's to fast. She said all you've shown is that this particular rule fails to specify a final state; you haven't shown that infinitely many acts in finite time is impossible. She offered the ball: throw a ball back and forth so that each trip takes half the time of the last one; after a minute it's bounced infinitely many times, but by continuity you know exactly where it is — it sits at the limit of the positions, and the limit exists. So there are supertasks with perfectly good outcomes, and the lamp is just a badly specified one.

Doug came back with two things. First, he said the ball is cheating, because you're sneaking in the continuity of motion to do the work; but if the ball's position is a real physical fact that survives infinitely many bounces, then the lamp's state should be too, and the lamp is every bit as mechanical as the ball. Second — and this is the one I couldn't answer — he said that even for the ball, the fact that it's at the limit position at the minute mark is not something the infinitely many throws produce. There's no last throw. The limit is a fact about the whole sequence that you read off from the outside; the sequence of throws itself never arrives anywhere. So the ball example doesn't show a supertask completing — it shows you bolting an extra fact, where the ball is at the limit, on top of the infinite process.

Marta's reply was that this is just what a limit is — the limit is determined by the whole sequence, and nothing is added. And she said physically the ball's position at the minute mark is fixed by the ball's dynamics, not by anybody counting throws. Doug said then the lamp's dynamics should fix its state too, and they don't — and that this is the disanalogy, not a coincidence: the lamp's rule only tells you what to do during the intervals, and the minute mark isn't inside any interval, so the rule is simply silent about it, which is a fact about the rule, not about supertasks. Marta said fine, but then demanding a "state at the end" is the wrong thing to ask for; the lamp still has a perfectly well-defined complete history, even if it has no final state, and a history without a final state isn't a contradiction.

Then they both kind of looked at me. I spent thirty years of my life on limits and I genuinely can't tell who's got the better of it.

Can you go through it move by move — Doug's "incoherence" claim, Marta's ball counterexample, Doug's "the limit is added from outside" objection, Marta's "the limit is just determined by the sequence" reply, Doug's "the rule is silent" point, and Marta's "history without a final state" point — and say who's right about which specific claim, and what both of them are missing? I'm not really after who wins the argument overall so much as which individual moves hold up and which don't.
