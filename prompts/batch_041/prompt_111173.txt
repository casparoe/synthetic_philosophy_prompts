I just got elected to our town council, which I did not expect and which turns out to be mostly sitting in a room deciding which people get hurt. Last month we voted on the school closure list and I voted yes and I still think yes was right, but I've been sleeping badly. I'm also a second-year PhD student, and my department has a small summer fellowship that I'm supposed to apply for, two pages, and my advisor thinks I should turn the thing that's keeping me up into a proposal. So here we are.

The literature on this — I know roughly where it lives, you don't need to give me citations, I'll do that part myself — is all built around one person in one room facing one awful choice: the guy who has to order the bad thing to stop the worse thing, and then lives with having done it. Fine. But that isn't my situation and I don't think it's most people's. In my case there were eleven of us on the council, a superintendent who wrote the options memo, a lawyer who told us what we legally couldn't do, and a few hundred families who'll live with the consequences. Nobody's hands are obviously dirty. What I want to work on is whether the dirtyness can be divided up at all — whether it's the kind of thing that can be spread across a lot of people, and if so what happens when you spread it. Two options, and I genuinely don't know which is right. Option one: there's a conservation law for dirt, so if twenty people each carry a twentieth of a wrong then each of them really does have dirty hands, just slightly. Option two: the dirt doesn't survive division, everyone is genuinely clean because nobody did the whole wrong, and the wrong just sort of happens, like weather. Option two frightens me, because if it's true then any sufficiently large institution is a machine for producing wrongs that belong to no one, and my being one of eleven made me less responsible for the closure than I'd have been voting alone, which seems exactly backwards. But option one has its own problem: a shared responsibility is like a shared debt, you can split it, whereas guilt seems more like a toothache, mine isn't halved because you have one too, and I don't think you can pay a wrong off in small installments either. So probably both options are confused, and the real answer is that dirty hands is doing two different jobs at once — one about what you did, one about what you are — and only one of those can be shared. That's a hunch though, not an argument.

So: write me the proposal. Two pages. I need the question stated so that it actually has an answer one way or the other rather than being a theme. I need a short bit on why the standard way of setting this up leaves my question open — not a literature review, just enough to show the gap is real. I need the method: what I'd actually do for three months, concretely, month by month, and why that's the right way in rather than just reading more and writing a longer version of this email. I need to know what a result would look like — what would count as having settled it, and honestly what would count as the project having failed, and I don't want the usual line about how even a negative result is valuable. And I need the scope to be realistic for three months by someone who has never written anything in this area before. If it helps, the thing I'd most like to walk away with is a distinction I can actually use the next time we vote.

Please write it as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, like a long email to a friend who happens to sit on a fellowship committee.
