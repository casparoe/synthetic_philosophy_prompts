A colleague handed me the following argument at the end of a seminar on meaning and communication, and I cannot decide whether it is a real result or a sleight of hand. Every step looks reasonable to me and the conclusion looks absurd.

(1) To mean something by an utterance is to intend one's audience to recognize one's intention to convey it — and to recognize it, in part, by recognizing that very intention.

(2) Recognition is an achievement of the audience, and what an audience recognizes is fixed by the evidence available to it together with the background assumptions it brings; it is not fixed by what the speaker privately had in mind.

(3) The relevant background assumptions are the conversational maxims — roughly: be as informative as the exchange requires and no more; say what you believe and have evidence for; be relevant; be perspicuous — together with the standing assumption that the speaker is cooperating.

(4) Where the literal content of an utterance would flout one of these maxims, the audience infers that the speaker meant something other than the literal content; that non-literal content is what we call a conversational implicature.

(5) But the maxims belong to the audience's assumptions rather than to the words, and audiences differ. An audience that cares about punctuality hears 'the meeting is at nine' as conveying 'nine sharp'; an audience indifferent to punctuality hears the same words as conveying nothing of the kind. Which maxim an utterance flouts shifts with the audience's interests and expectations in just the same way.

(6) So whether an utterance carries a given implicature — and indeed what the utterance means — depends on the assumptions of the audience rather than on the intention of the speaker.

(7) But by (1) it was the speaker's intention that was supposed to fix what the utterance means.

(8) Hence either (1) is false, and meaning is not fixed by speaker intention, or (3)–(5) are false, and the maxims do no real work.

(9) Take the first horn and speakers no longer control what they mean; take the second and audiences no longer recover meaning by reasoning about what speakers intend.

(10) Neither horn is acceptable, so the framework cannot be right as stated.

I think the conclusion is wrong — speakers do mean things, and audiences do work out what they mean — and if I have to give something up I would rather keep (1) than lose it, though I cannot see which of the middle steps is doing the damage. Tell me which step or steps to reject, why they looked so plausible, and what rejecting each of the live options would cost. If I am to abandon (1), I want to know what I am buying in exchange. Finally, say what would change your verdict: which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would make you blame a different step, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
