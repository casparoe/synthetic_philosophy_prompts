I am a member of my school's philosophy club, and this term we have been working slowly through an introductory logic text that my older sister left behind when she went away to university. We have just reached the chapter on epistemic logic, the formal study of knowledge and belief, and I have not been able to stop thinking about an argument that one of the older members put together at our last meeting. The discussion had begun with a much simpler question, whether a machine could be said to know anything at all, and it ended with him claiming that the standard way of treating knowledge in logic has an absurd consequence built into it from the first line. I have written his argument out below as carefully as I can, because I want to be certain I am not misrepresenting him.

(1) If a person knows that p, and p entails q, then that person knows that q. He was firm that this closure principle is not optional: without it, knowing something would never entitle one to conclude anything from it, and knowledge would be inert. (2) Any proposition whatever entails every logical truth, since a logical truth holds in every case and therefore also in every case in which the proposition holds. (3) From (1) and (2) it follows that anyone who knows anything at all knows every logical truth. (4) The same pattern repeats one level up, by repeated application: the axioms of arithmetic entail each of its theorems, so anyone who knows the axioms knows every theorem, including the ones no one has yet proved and, if I have understood the incompleteness theorems correctly, the ones that cannot be proved from those axioms at all. (5) But this is plainly false. Mathematicians spend entire careers establishing results they did not previously know, and no one supposes that a first-year student who has memorised the axioms already knows everything that follows from them. (6) Therefore (1) is false, and knowledge is not closed under entailment in the way he stated it.

What I want to know is which step I ought to reject, and what rejecting it costs. I would rather keep (1) than (5), since I cannot make sense of a kind of knowing from which nothing whatever follows, but if (1) has to go I want to understand what goes with it. Our club president observed that the skeptic's argument depends on being able to pass knowledge along from a claim to whatever that claim entails, though I could not tell whether it needs (1) or only something weaker, and our faculty adviser said merely that formal models of knowledge describe an ideal agent rather than any of us, which struck me at the time as changing the subject rather than answering. So: if the fault lies in (1), why did it look so obviously right, and what would a repaired version have to say about the first-year student in (5)? If the fault lies in (2), or in the notion of entailment it relies on, can the argument be rebuilt in a weaker form that keeps the principle without the absurdity? And if the argument is sound as far as it goes, which of the premises is least costly to surrender, and where does the cost actually fall — on our ordinary practice of expecting people to know the consequences of what they know, or on the skeptical problem, or somewhere else entirely?
