Free-floating rationales — reasons that inhere in a system's design or history without being held by any reasoner — are usually introduced with the same handful of examples. I want to test the notion against a harder set. Rank the following seven cases from the one that most securely qualifies as a free-floating rationale to the one that does not qualify at all, give a one-sentence justification for each placement, and flag any adjacent pairs whose order is genuinely close, saying what tips them.

- A stick insect's resemblance to a twig, whose point is that it evades predators.
- A household thermostat's switching on and off at a set point.
- A chess engine's evaluation function, learned by self-play rather than written by a programmer.
- A market price, which behaves as if it registered the relative scarcity of a good.
- The meandering of a river, which can be redescribed as solving the problem of dissipating energy.
- The fact that human males have nipples.
- The reasoning embedded in a legal precedent that later courts follow but that no living judge authored or presently intends.

Then answer: which single criterion does the most work in separating the cases that qualify from those that do not, and does that criterion make the boundary sharp or vague? Finally, identify the case where your criterion yields a verdict you find counterintuitive, and say whether you would revise the criterion or keep the verdict.
