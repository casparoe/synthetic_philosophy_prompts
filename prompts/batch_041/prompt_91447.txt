I'm a novelist, and the climax of my current book turns on a regulatory decision: a character must decide, on incomplete evidence, whether to license a chemical whose harms would be serious if they materialised. I want his reasoning to be genuinely sound rather than hand-waving, and I keep running into a conceptual problem I cannot resolve on my own. Stated as a rule of decision, the precautionary principle seems to forbid any action whose consequences are uncertain — yet inaction is itself an action with uncertain consequences, so it either paralyses or quietly becomes ordinary risk assessment with the dials turned differently. Stated instead as a shift in the burden of proof, it seems to amount to nothing more than cost-benefit analysis with a lower evidential threshold. Please explain as precisely as you can what, if anything, distinguishes these two readings, and whether either survives the objection that it cannot be applied consistently — including to itself, since deciding how much precaution to take about precaution is the same kind of decision. Where the distinction depends on a stipulation I would have to make (how much more weight the unfamiliar hazard gets than the familiar one), tell me so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
