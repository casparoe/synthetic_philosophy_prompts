I retired from field geology about three years ago and, mostly to keep the gears turning, I've been sitting in on a reading group at the library that's slowly working through the Greeks — we did the Meno last month and I can't stop chewing on the last third of it. Socrates has already gotten Meno to admit that true belief gets you to Larissa just as well as knowledge does, and then he pulls out the bit about Daedalus's statues running away unless you tie them down, and the slave boy who gets the geometry right but can't say why, and asks what on earth knowledge adds over a true belief that happens to be true. My first reaction was that this is a fake problem — if the belief is true and you act on it, who cares how you got there. But the more I poke at it the more I think the difference is real and just badly located by everyone who discusses it. So I've written a short essay (four or five pages) and I'd like your comments on it.

The gist: I claim the advantage of knowledge isn't extra accuracy or some special ingredient in the belief itself, it's what I've been calling recoverability. A true belief is a fact you're holding; knowledge is a true belief you can *regenerate* — if it gets knocked loose, or someone challenges you, or the situation shifts and you need the neighboring facts, you can rebuild it from material you've still got, without having to go back and get lucky again. The value is then an insurance value, or an option value: you're not paying for the belief, you're paying to be able to find it again cheaply. My image for it is the difference between picking up a loose fossil that happens to be lying on the surface and having the stratigraphic section that tells you where to dig, why it's there, and where the next one is. And I tack on a second, more uncomfortable claim: that on this view knowledge is partly a *social* status rather than a purely psychological one — it's the true belief you can be held to, that you can be asked to stand behind, which is why we care about it in teaching and testimony and not just in private. The cases I use to test it: a field naturalist who just *sees* which rock is which and is right nearly every time but genuinely can't say how (my account seems to call that mere true belief, which bothers me); a man who read the trial data once, remembers the conclusion, and could reconstruct the reasoning from a notebook he can no longer read (knowledge?); and someone whose true belief would evaporate the instant anything pushed on it, but who is right today (not knowledge, on my view — but then is he any worse off than the man with the notebook, for any purpose Meno cares about?).

What I want from you: tell me whether the central distinction is actually doing work or whether it's a relabeled version of something I'd recognize if I'd read more — a friend of mine who teaches intro philosophy glanced at it and said it was just reliabilism with extra steps, which annoyed me partly because I suspect he's right. Second, I worry the account is circular: if the material you use to regenerate the belief has to be reliable or known, haven't I just moved the problem one step back? Third, does this answer Meno's question or quietly change the subject — does it explain why knowledge is *better* than true belief, or only why it's more *convenient*, and does the difference matter for the argument Socrates is actually running? Fourth, tell me which of my test cases the account gets wrong and why; I'd rather find out now than after I've shown it to the reading group. And fifth, the social-status half — I half think it's the real insight and half think it collapses into saying knowledge is just what we're willing to blame people for not having, which would be a reductio. Don't bother pointing me to papers or books to read, I'm not going to chase them down; I care about whether the thing holds together.
