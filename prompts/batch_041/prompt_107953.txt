In three weeks I am giving the closing talk at a small invitational workshop on animal welfare science, and I am defending a conclusion I expect most of the room to resist. The audience is about two dozen people: empirical welfare scientists who work on pain and affective state in farmed and wild animals; a few veterinary ethologists; and three or four philosophers of sentience, most of whom have spent years arguing that we should extend protective concern to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans on the strength of marker evidence — nociception, opioid modulation, trade-off behaviour, avoidance learning — rather than any settled theory of what makes a state count, though one of them dissented from that extension and regards it as sentimental overreach. They are empiricists by temperament, allergic to metaphysics, and their working instinct is that the moral circle should widen only as fast as the evidence does.

My thesis is that the marker-based precaution they already accept for invertebrates is substrate-blind, and that consistency therefore requires them to extend a presumption of moral status to artificial systems meeting a marker profile I set out in the talk. I call it a welfare-relevant architecture (WRA) and define it as an architecture that (i) integrates signals across modalities into states that globally modulate subsequent processing, (ii) attaches a valence-like sign to those states such that the system learns to seek or avoid them, and (iii) carries a self-model representing the system's own condition as its own. I argue that WRA is sufficient for a presumption of status but not necessary for it, and that their own invertebrate precedent commits them to it.

I can guess where the objections will come from, but I want them in this room's vocabulary rather than in mine, and I have a bad habit of conceding too much early and then sounding evasive. Write me a Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people — not the ones I would get from a machine-learning audience, and not the ones I would get from a theology faculty — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way one of them would actually put it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Say which objections I should concede outright rather than fight, and which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks. British English, no more than 1,000 words, in prose I can read from rather than bullet fragments.
