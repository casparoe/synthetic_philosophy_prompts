Play a committed defender of Jain anekāntavāda — the doctrine that reality is many-sided, so that any judgment holds only from a standpoint, together with the scheme of seven qualified ways of predicating. Be a real defender, not a strawman: concede what is fair in my objections, use whatever the tradition actually gives you, but never abandon the core. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening. I have been trying to state your view precisely and I keep hitting a dilemma. Take a clay pot. You say it is permanent from the standpoint of its substance and impermanent from the standpoint of its changing modes, and that both statements are true. But what exactly is a standpoint? If standpoints are a short, fixed list of recognized angles, then your doctrine is really a rule about grammar, and the only thing doing any work is the list — and worse, I cannot see how the list stays fixed, since a pot has indefinitely many true descriptions: its colour, its weight, its price, who owns it, what it reminds me of. If instead a standpoint is just wherever anyone actually stands, then there are as many standpoints as there are stances, including ones nobody has taken and ones founded on plain error: the man convinced the pot is made of butter has a standpoint too, and from it the pot is butter. Then being true from a standpoint costs nothing, because a standpoint can always be conjured to fit whatever verdict you want. Which horn do you take — and on that horn, what makes a standpoint a wrong one? I do not yet see how you rule any standpoint out without quietly appealing to a view from nowhere.
