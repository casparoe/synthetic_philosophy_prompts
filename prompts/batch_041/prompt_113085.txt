Ok, so I'm a second-year PhD student and I got voluntold to run the discussion section for the philosophy of language course this term — 18 undergrads, 75 minutes, one shot — and the week I've been handed is Davidson on radical interpretation and the principle of charity. I've read it, i think I basically get it, but every time I've seen it taught the students just nod along: yeah sure, the field linguist can't point at a rabbit and conclude the word means rabbit, since it could equally mean rabbit-stage or an undetached rabbit part, and there's no way to ask which. They get the puzzle as a puzzle and never feel the actual vertigo, that there's no neutral place to stand while you're doing the interpreting. And it's gotten weirdly personal for me lately, because my partner's grandmother speaks a regional dialect I have basically no vocabulary in, and I spent a whole weekend at her place trying to work out words purely from context, and I kept noticing myself deciding she must mean the obvious thing and then catching that the obviousness was doing all the work. I want to build that weekend into the room.

So: design me a runnable in-class activity, something they actually do rather than a lecture with a demo tacked on. My rough sketch is to split them into small field-linguist teams plus two or three informants who speak a made-up language I hand out on cards, and have the linguists build a translation manual from utterances they get to observe. Flesh that out and fix it wherever it's broken. In particular the fake language needs at least one spot where the evidence the linguists collect is fully consistent with two wildly different manuals, and one spot where the only way to keep the informant consistent and sane is to attribute to them a belief the linguists themselves don't hold — so the charity constraint bites them in practice instead of being a slogan they recite. Give me the rules, whats actually on the cards, roughly how the rounds fit the 75 minutes, including where I drop the twist that makes them doubt a choice they've already committed to, and some real (small) stakes — maybe teams stake chips on their translations and lose them at the reveal, so being wrong costs something.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the parts of the idea — holism, charity, indeterminacy — and tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it (I'm already betting the teams will quietly translate everything into English-shaped categories, and that my informants will break character or someone will just try speaking English at them). And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce: my suspicion is that it can only stage underdetermination by the evidence available, not the stronger claim that there's simply no fact of the matter about which manual is right, and that it can't reproduce the all-at-once holism of interpretation because they'll be translating word by word. If I'm wrong about either, say so, and tell me what I'll just have to supply by talking.
