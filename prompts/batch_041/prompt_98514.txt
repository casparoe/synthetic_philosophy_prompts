I keep a private notebook of the ways I come to know things, and for some time I have been circling a pattern that I cannot place. I would like to know whether it has an established name — but a name alone would not satisfy me. What I actually want is to know whether I am looking at one phenomenon or several, where the lines between them fall, and what each of the nearest well-known concepts leaves out.

Here is what I keep noticing. First: I open the refrigerator and know there is no jam. I did not see a thing called the absence of jam; the shelf is bare, and yet "there is no jam" is something I know, and know by looking. If pressed on what I perceived, I stumble — I perceived a shelf, and no jam, and somehow the second half of that counts as knowledge rather than mere ignorance. Second: my brother eats enormously at dinner every night and I have never once seen him eat lunch; I conclude that he must eat lunch somewhere I do not see. I did not perceive the lunch, and I cannot point to a general rule from which it follows; I posit it because without it what I do observe does not add up. Third: a colleague has not answered my message, and I know she is not coming to the meeting. Here the work is done by the absence of something I expected — a reply — and the reply, had it come, would have been someone telling me something.

Are these three one thing or three? Is there an established concept for any of them — I have read in passing that a certain old tradition keeps a short list of the basic sources of knowledge and quarrels over what belongs on that list, so I suspect something of the sort exists — and if so, exactly which of my cases it covers and which it leaves out? If nothing established fits, then coin a term, define it, and tell me what it excludes. I care less about the label than about the carving.

Please structure your answer under the following headings, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

1. "The pattern, restated" (~150 words): my three cases restated precisely enough that we agree on what is being asked.
2. "Names that already exist" (~250 words): any established terms for the phenomenon or its parts, and in broad strokes the tradition each comes from — no citations or reading lists, just what the terms mean and who owns them.
3. "The nearest relatives, and the gaps" (~350 words): for each nearby concept, what it shares with my cases and precisely where it parts company.
4. "One thing or several" (~300 words): your verdict on whether my three cases are one phenomenon or distinct ones, and if nothing established fits, your proposed name and definition.
5. "Edges and exclusions" (~200 words): boundary cases near the concept and cases it should clearly exclude, so I can see where its edges are.
6. "Where this is shaky" (~150 words): the parts of your answer you are least confident in, and what would settle them.
