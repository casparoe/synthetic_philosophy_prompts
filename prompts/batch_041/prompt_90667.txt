I'm a novelist at work on a book set in the generation after the Trojan War, and I keep meeting the same picture whenever the question of the war's historicity comes up: the legend is a game of Chinese whispers (telephone, as we say at home), in which some real event was whispered down the generations and arrived at the far end garbled beyond recovery. The skeptics I read lean on it to license the conclusion that no historical kernel can be recovered from the tradition at all; my editor leans on the same picture, read backwards, to license the thought that a kernel must be in there somewhere. Take the role of an expert in oral tradition, folklore, and the philosophy of history, and think this through carefully rather than reaching for a verdict. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you set out what the game actually involves and what an oral tradition actually involves, before you commit to anything. Then audit the analogy: give me a table of correspondences — what plays the role of the original message, the whisperers, the noise, the last teller — mark where the mapping breaks, and say which of those breaks matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do and which are idle. Tell me what the picture quietly imports, about what transmission is for, about who stands in the chain, and about whether there is a single chain at all, and whether either conclusion, the skeptic's or my editor's, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy suggests itself, offer it. Finally, say how the verdict lands differently across the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. Write your answer in British English.
