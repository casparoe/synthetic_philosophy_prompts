I am a graduate student in political philosophy, halfway through a seminar on Rawls, and I keep running into the same friction. The difference principle is stated as a constraint on how the least advantaged fare, but before it can say anything about a policy it needs a measure of advantage, and any index of primary goods — rights and liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, the social bases of self-respect, whatever else belongs on the list — needs weights, and the weights are where the argument always gets thin. In seminar we wave this off as a technicality about the index and move to the interesting part. I would like you to design a case that makes it concrete instead of gesturing at it.

What I want is a scenario engineered to order. One society, one live policy decision, ordinary realistic detail, no science fiction and no invented social science, and short — the scenario itself in a single paragraph. The requirement is that the difference principle return opposite verdicts under two indices of advantage, with everything else held fixed: the same two groups, the same policy, the same facts, only the measure varying, so that adopt-versus-reject flips when the index flips. Constraints on how you may do it. Both indices have to be ones a serious Rawlsian could actually use in applying the principle, not one of them a caricature built to lose. The disagreement must not be a measurement artifact: no differing local price levels, no unmeasured in-kind benefits, nothing a better statistician would wash out. The group that comes out worse on one index must not be worse off because it does not know what it is missing, because it has adapted its preferences downward, or because the other group has expensive tastes — those are the cheap ways to dissolve the puzzle and I want them closed off. And the two groups must genuinely trade places, rather than being ranked differently on dimensions that all point the same way.

Then do the demonstration. State the scenario, then the verdict the difference principle gives under each index, and say exactly which comparison is doing the work in each. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the indices diverge, and tell me what happens if I remove it — does the case collapse into an ordinary one where any reasonable index agrees, and if so, does that mean the flip was an artifact of your construction after all? Then address the thing I actually care about: whether the weights in the fuller index can be fixed without the parties behind the veil knowing their own conception of the good. If they can — if the idea that primary goods are all-purpose means, worth wanting whatever one's final ends happen to be, or something else I am not seeing, settles the weights — then say so and tell me what the case shows instead. Give me the strongest Rawlsian response to your own case, the reading on which the flip is a defect of my index rather than of the principle, and say whether it succeeds. Give me a verdict rather than a survey, and do not hedge: I would rather be told that my framing is confused than be handed a case that meets my specifications by fiat. If the constraints I have imposed make the task impossible, say that plainly, and tell me which one to drop and why.
