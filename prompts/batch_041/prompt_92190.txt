Okay, so I'm teaching the last session of my ancient phil seminar and I've decided to do something slightly unhinged: I want to read Oedipus Rex next to the Death in Damascus case from decision theory. What I keep noticing is that Oedipus reasons exactly like a causal deliberator. He treats the oracle's prediction as a fixed fact about the world — whether he kills his father and sleeps with his mother is already settled, nothing he does will move it — so he changes the one variable he thinks he still controls, which is where he lives, and flees Corinth. And that is precisely the move that hands him the prophecy. Death in Damascus has the same shape: if the appointment is fixed, flight is pointless, and yet the flight is what does it. What I want from you is a real reading of the play through this, not a plot summary and not "it's about hubris." Does Sophocles come down on the side of the old idle argument — if it's fated that you'll recover, the doctor is neither here nor there, and if it isn't fated, the doctor is neither here nor there — or does the play show that it's a particular style of deliberation, the kind that treats the future as a fact to be dodged rather than something you're in the middle of making, that destroys him? Jocasta's business about chance ruling our lives and prophecy being worthless is the obvious third position, and the play treats her about as badly as it can; I want to know what to do with that, because it's tempting to read the whole thing as saying the only thing worse than believing in fate is thinking you've escaped it, and I'm not sure that reading survives contact with the text. There's also a wrinkle I keep noticeing in the decision theory version: the causal reasoner's advice confirms itself once you actually take it, and the guy who reasons his way to staying put stays put and dies. I'd like to know if the play knows about that or just stumbles into it.

Second thing, and this is the part I actually care about: where does the analogy break? My suspicion is that the oracle isn't like Death at all — Death shows up in the market and you can at least read the appointment off his face, whereas the oracle speaks in riddles and the riddle only becomes legible backwards. But there's a sharper version of the disanalogy. In Death in Damascus, Death's location tracks your choice because Death knows your decision procedure; the oracle is reliable because it knows Oedipus's character, and his attempt to escape is the most character-revealing thing he ever does. If that's right, then "fatalism" in the play is really just accurate psychology, and the prophecy has no causal power whatsoever — it's a mirror, not a mechanism. Which I think is either the deepest thing about the play or a total cop-out, and I'd like you to tell me which, with reasons.
