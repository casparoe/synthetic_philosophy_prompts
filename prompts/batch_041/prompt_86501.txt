So I'm running a session in this odd little seminar we've got going — it's nominally called "Concepts, Categories, and Kinds," but honestly half the room are design students who drifted in because a friend recommended it and half are philosophy grad students. 16 of us, one 90-minute block, one room, no projector (the one in the room has been broken since before I got here and I've given up asking IT). What I want to do is something hands-on about a thing that's been nagging at me: every lowercase 'a' I've ever seen is different from every other one, and yet I've never once been genuinely unsure whether some squiggle is an 'a' — until, that is, I start pushing on the strange ones, and then I suddenly can't say where 'a' stops and 'not-a' begins. I vaguely remember a project by Hofstadter (the GEB guy) called Letter Spirit, where the aim was to get a computer to render all 26 lowercase letters in a whole series of invented but internally consistent styles, and the thing it kept leaning on was a distinction between the essence of a letter — whatever it is that makes every 'a' an 'a' — and its style, the particular way this one batch of letters is drawn. I want people to *feel* that distinction in their hands rather than hear me lecture it.

So: design me a runnable 90-minute activity, for 16 people, that does this. My rough seed, which you can keep, ditch, or rework: everyone gets coarse grid paper and the rule that a letter is built only from straight horizontal/vertical/diagonal strokes between grid points (so it's cheap and everyone's on the same footing — no drawing skill required). Round one, people each draw an 'a' on the grid and we compare: all recognizably 'a', all different. Round two, split into four groups of four, and I hand each group a few seed letters drawn by me in some made-up consistent style, and the group has to extrapolate the style to more letters and then to *their own* 'a' in that style — then pass their set to another group. Round three, the forgery round: each group takes another group's set and slips in one letter that isn't really an 'a' but looks like it could be, plus one letter that's a genuine 'a' but breaks the house style — and the others have to catch both and say which violation is which. Round four is the one I care most about: take a real 'a' and nudge it through a chain of tiny, single-step grid changes until it's clearly become something else (an 'o', a 'd', a blob) and have the group call out, at each single step, "still an 'a'" or "not an 'a' anymore." I expect them to fight about it and to discover they can't locate the line even while insisting there is one. Fill this out into something I can actually run: materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, what I say (and don't say) at each stage, roles, and how to keep a group from freezing when it can't agree.

Then give me the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the ideas — the essence/style split, why a checklist of features won't capture 'a'-ness, the difference between a style violation (still an 'a', just not ours) and an essence violation (no longer an 'a' at all), and the weird fact that the category feels perfectly real and yet its boundary can't be pinned down. I want them to leave having felt both the pull of "there must be a real 'a' in there somewhere" and the pull of "there's just a shifting family of drawings," without me having told them which side to take. Two more things I need: tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it (I especially worry about the forgery round collapsing into "well, technically anything is an 'a' if you squint," and about one loud person steamrolling the boundary calls in round four), and be frank about which feature of the whole idea this activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in words at the end — I suspect it's something about the essence being a matter of analogy and perception rather than a definition, but you tell me. Don't name any of the theory terms (prototype, family resemblance, Platonic form, whatever) until the debrief, and please don't ask me to source any readings or citations, I just want the activity.
