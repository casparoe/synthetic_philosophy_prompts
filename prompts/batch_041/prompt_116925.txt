I am a professor of political philosophy, and this year I am serving as the academic member of the standing body that screens, for our legislature, every proposal that moves a public task up or down a level of government — from the municipality to the region to the state to the supranational organisations we belong to. Our country is midway through a decentralisation settlement, so the volume is high: three analysts, all recent graduates with no training in political theory, see thirty to forty proposals a session and can give each about an hour. What they write goes to elected members who sign off, and it will be quoted in debate, so a procedure that merely gestures at subsidiarity is worse than useless. I need a decision procedure they can actually run.

Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with a finding that the proposal fails. Some steps will not be decidable by an analyst: mark clearly which ones must be handed to the elected members because they turn on values rather than facts, and say why that step is a value question rather than a failure of diligence. Build in the distinctions the analysts will otherwise collapse: between which level may act and which level must act; between the allocation of a task and the manner of its execution; between subsidiarity, which fixes the level, and proportionality, which judges the measure once the level is fixed; and between subsidiarity as a presumption against the higher level and subsidiarity as a duty on the higher level to assist where the lower one cannot manage alone. I also want the procedure to use a test of my own devising, which I call the reversibility test: where a decision can be cheaply undone later, the case for taking it at the lower level is strengthened, and where it locks in infrastructure, standards, or expectations for a generation, the case for the higher level is strengthened. Say how an analyst is to apply this without being able to forecast, and where it will mislead. The body I sit on is itself located at one level and its members have views about the settlement; the procedure should say how the analysts are to handle the fact that the assessor is also an interested party.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, a supranational minimum standard on the portability of patients' health records across member states — a case where the cross-border effects are real but the claim that nothing less will do is contested. Second, a national statute that fixes a curriculum requirement binding on regional school authorities and attaches conditions to the funding that pays for it, where the lower level objects that it is being conscripted into administering a national policy it rejects; I will tell you now that I favour this one, and I want the procedure followed wherever it leads. Third, a regional levy on a shared river basin, where the region has the legal power but not the administrative capacity, and the question is whether the higher level's silence is subsidiarity or abandonment.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Where will it misfire — where the necessity test cannot be checked before the fact, where the lower level is a legal fiction or is captured, where the relevant community is itself in dispute? End with the residual questions that no ordering of questions will settle, and which an elected member must simply decide.
