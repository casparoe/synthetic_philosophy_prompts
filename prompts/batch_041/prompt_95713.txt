I trained as a process-safety engineer and spent a decade doing hazard analysis in the chemical industry before moving into a policy shop, so my working vocabulary is the one you get from safety cases and risk registers: design margins, single points of failure, defence in depth, fail-safe versus fail-dangerous, ALARP reasoning, accepted versus carried residual risk, and the well-documented drift by which a deviation that would once have stopped work quietly becomes routine. I am fluent in that vocabulary and in very little else; my political theory is at the level of someone who reads the odd essay on the train. A colleague in the theory group keeps returning to the problem of dirty hands — the claim that a political leader can be genuinely required to do something wrong, not merely regrettable, in order to avert something worse, and that the wrongness does not evaporate when the consequences turn out well. Include both readings of the position if they come apart: the consequentialist reading, on which the wrong is a cost that a sufficiently large benefit can license, and the reading on which something remains after the arithmetic is done — a remainder that attaches to the person who acted rather than to the ledger.
