Alright, here's what I need, and I want you to build it, not find it.

Quick scaffolding so we're on the same page about why this is hard. There are basically two ways people ground the authority of an advance directive. One says the binding thing is the earlier choice's autonomy: it was made competently and with information, so it stands — full stop — and the fact that the later self has changed her mind is neither here nor there, because the whole point of the earlier self's authority is that it isn't hostage to what she'll want later. The other says the binding thing is that it's *her* — the past decision binds the present self the way a promise to yourself binds, which means the strength of the binding should track how much of her is actually still there: memory, character, commitments, the stuff that makes the later person the continuation of the earlier one. Those two come apart, and in my experience students can only see it when you make them.

So: construct me one case, realistic, hospital or care home, no brain transplants, no teletransporters, no sci-fi. In it, the later self is barely continuous with the earlier one — not a shift in taste, an actual rupture — and yet someone who holds the first view says the directive binds in full, and binds for reasons that have nothing whatsoever to do with how much continuity is left. Make the directive a refusal of some treatment, and make the later self's wish a positive request for that same treatment. She's competent. Don't lean on incapacity or on her being confused; that's cheating and it makes the case do no work.

Constraints: the case itself in one paragraph. The low-continuity fact has to be load-bearing, not decoration, so it has to be something a real person could undergo — religious conversion is worn out, find me something else, or if you insist on it then make it structurally interesting rather than just a change of values. And don't stipulate that she's "not the same person anymore." Show it, let me infer it.

Then the analysis. (a) What the autonomy story says and why, what the continuity story says and why. (b) Which single feature of the case is doing the work of pulling them apart, and what happens to the disagreement if I take that feature out. (c) The best move available to the continuity theorist to make the case not count — how would they redescribe it? (d) If the autonomy story wins here, what stops it from being a general licence to bind people to their past selves? If nothing does, say so plainly.

One more thing, briefly at the end: does anything change if the directive was written with a clause to the effect that if she ever asks to revoke it, the asking itself should be read as a symptom and set aside? Two or three sentences on whether that's a legitimate exercise of the earlier self's authority or just bootstrapping.
