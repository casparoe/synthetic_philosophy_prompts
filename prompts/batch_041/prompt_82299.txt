I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to get straight on a conceptual puzzle in the philosophy of physics: the argument, commonly credited to Hilary Putnam and independently to C. W. Rietdijk, that the special theory of relativity obliges us to treat the future as already as real as the past. Please explain the issue to me, and please make the explanation self-contained — I have no way to look anything up, so I would rather you argue the matter out than gesture at a literature I cannot consult.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, and I would like you to work from this reconstruction rather than from whatever canonical statement you happen to have seen. Special relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative. For an event e and any event f that is spacelike separated from it, there are inertial observers for whom e and f are simultaneous, others for whom f precedes e, and still others for whom f follows e. Now fix my present experience, the event e0 here and now, and choose any event f in my future light cone. There is an observer O1 for whom e0 and some intermediate event e1 are both present; there is an observer O2 for whom e1 and some e2 are both present; and by continuing this chain I can reach an observer for whom the last intermediate event and f are both present. Now suppose it is a fact about an event that it is real relative to an observer, in the sense of belonging to that observer's present rather than merely receiving a coordinate. Then reality appears to propagate along the chain: e1 is real because it is real relative to O1, and O1 is real relative to me; e2 is real because it is real relative to O2, and e2 is real relative to O1; and so on out to f. If the relation real-relative-to is transitive, then f is real relative to me now, even though f lies in my future. Since f was arbitrary, every event in spacetime is real now, and the division into past, present, and future is a fact about us rather than about the world.

What I want explained is where this goes wrong, if it goes wrong at all. I have been framing real-relative-to as a relation N on events, holding between x and y when some observer has both x and y in her present, and I notice that N is reflexive and symmetric but — precisely because simultaneity is frame-relative — not transitive, so that its transitive closure is far larger than N itself. My suspicion is that the argument silently substitutes the transitive closure of N for N, which would make it a fallacy rather than a substantive thesis about time. But I suspect I am conflating several distinct questions, and I would like them separated:

(i) Does the argument really need transitivity of real-relative-to, and if so, is that premise a substantive metaphysical claim or a trivial consequence of what we mean by real?

(ii) If there is a defect, does it lie in the transitivity premise or in the relata — that is, is an observer's present the right kind of thing to be transitive at all, given that it is a slice whose orientation depends on that observer's state of motion?

(iii) Can the argument be repaired, for instance by treating my own here-and-now as a distinguished event rather than as one perspective among many, and what would such a repair cost?

Finally, please tell me whether a structurally similar argument would go through in a classical, Newtonian setting where simultaneity is absolute. I suspect it would not, since there the chain of present-with relations would collapse into a single global present and could never reach the future, but I cannot tell whether that collapse makes the argument trivially true or instead removes the very premise it needs. If relativity is doing the essential work, I would like to understand exactly which feature of it is doing that work.
