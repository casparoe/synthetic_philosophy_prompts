Hey — I'm running a single 90-minute session of a second-year metaphysics seminar, 18 students, most of whom know Leibniz only as the punchline to Voltaire. I want them to feel the pull of the best-of-all-possible-worlds claim before they're allowed to laugh at it. Please act as an experienced philosophy teacher who designs classroom games, and think carefully about the mechanics before you answer: start by brainstorming two or three candidate activities, tell me which one you'd run and why, then give me the full design.

Some things to build into the game, since I dont want to assume they know them and I dont want the activity to depend on anyone remembering them:

- Leibniz's God doesn't pick arbitrarily. A perfectly wise and good will can't prefer the worse to the better, so if God creates at all, what gets created has to be the best world there is. That principle is what's doing the work — not any particular nice feature of our world.
- "Best" isn't "happiest." For Leibniz it's the most perfection, which he unpacks as the greatest variety you can have together with the greatest order: more kinds of things, more thoroughly connected, like the single curve that best fits a huge scatter of points.
- Compossibility: not every set of good features can be co-actualized. Some possible things rule each other out, so the choice is over the best *combination*, not the best items one at a time.
- Evil gets in as a condition or by-product of the best combination. The best world isn't the one with the least suffering; it's the one where the suffering there is buys the most perfection overall.
- The objection I want them to reach on their own: with infinitely many possible worlds there need not be a best one — "the best world" might be like "the greatest number." Leibniz's reply is roughly that possible essences have a kind of claim or striving toward existence, and that perfections limit one another, so the ascent bottoms out at a maximum instead of running forever.

So: design the activity. Rules, materials (I have a printer, a whiteboard, and index cards, nothing fancier), roles if you want them, and a timeline for the 90 minutes. Make them commit to something — build it, rank it, vote, seal a choice — rather than just discuss. Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the five points above without ever lecturing, and that lets them hit the no-best-world wall themselves and have to climb out of it. Also tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of Leibniz's actual position the activity can't reproduce and that I'll just have to state for them. If it's useful, the debrief can gesture at the later objection that a good God needn't create the best at all (the thought that any world with finite goods is a gift rather than an obligation), but keep that as a closing extension, not the point of the exercise.
