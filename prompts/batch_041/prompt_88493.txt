I voted for the first time this year, as a new citizen, and I spent the last month of the campaign canvassing in a district that ended up being decided by a margin of a few hundred votes. What I still cannot settle is the worth of a single vote, and everyone I know reaches for the same picture: a vote is a drop in the ocean. My brother-in-law uses it to conclude that one drop more or less cannot move the sea, so staying home costs nothing; my canvassing partner uses the identical picture to conclude that the ocean is nothing but drops, and that every one of them counts. I want the image audited, not a verdict on whether to vote.

Give me the correspondences first: what plays the drop, the ocean, the sea level, the tide, the other drops, the weather. Then show me where the mapping breaks, with attention to the features that matter here — that votes are not additive in the way water is, that an election resolves at a threshold rather than as a sum, that drops are interchangeable while voters are not, and that my abstention is not quietly offset by someone else's extra vote the way my missing drop is. Tell me which of the disanalogies bear on the two conclusions being drawn and which are merely picturesque, and whether either conclusion survives them. If the ocean is the wrong picture, say what the right one is, and be specific about what a better comparison — the lottery ticket, the last brick in an arch, the firing squad — would have to preserve.

One further matter: a friend who teaches political science tells me the standard repair is to abandon expected-utility maximization in favour of a minimax-regret rule. I would like to know whether that repairs the argument or only relocates it. I mean the paradox of not voting, not Condorcet cycling. And if the instrumental framing is itself the error, tell me what the reasons for voting become once causal efficacy is set aside, and whether they can be stated honestly to someone who has already read the turnout numbers.
