I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm reporting a feature on a dispute that keeps surfacing in different guises: when a person changes radically — through dementia, through religious conversion, through years of addiction and recovery — which self has authority over the life? At a workshop on dementia care, a philosopher summed up his position with a line I have since heard from several others: the self is just the story the brain tells about itself. On his account there is no deeper fact of the matter about which of a patient's selves is the real one, so the wishes she expresses now should weigh at least as heavily as the advance directive she signed back when, as he put it, she was a different person. The room seemed to treat the line as settling the question. I doubt that it does, but I cannot yet say precisely where the slide happens.

The line is a cousin of Daniel Dennett's image of the self as a centre of narrative gravity, and I have heard versions of it enlisted for at least four incompatible conclusions: defer to the present self; discount the present self because its narrative is thin; treat criminal responsibility as a fiction; and extend forgiveness to people who have changed beyond recognition.

What I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a defence. Set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the line could be making, from the near-trivial to the strongly metaphysical, and for each say whether it is true, false, or defensible but contentious, and what it would actually license. Separate the readings wherever they genuinely diverge: claims about what exists (no self at all; a self that is an abstract object rather than a thing; selves as social or linguistic constructions), about how selves are known (no non-narrative access to oneself), about persistence (identity just is narrative continuity, so a broken narrative is a broken person), about authorship (the brain, the culture, the person, no one), and about what follows normatively for blame, for deference to a patient's wishes, and for self-revision.

Two distinctions matter especially. First, the difference between a self that is an abstractum — real and indispensable, like a centre of gravity, yet not made of anything — and a self that is a mere fiction or façon de parler; say whether that difference bears on the normative conclusion, and if so how. Second, the difference between the line and the flat observation that people change; say what the line adds, if anything, and what would be lost by dropping it.

Then tell me which of the readings would actually support the philosopher's conclusion at the workshop, and which of them you would defend. Do not treat the line's familiarity as evidence for any reading; I want to know what would have to be true for it to do the work it was asked to do. I am writing for publication, so please use British English and avoid the throat-clearing that makes philosophy journalism unreadable. Aim for something I could put to the philosopher afterwards without his feeling that I have strawmanned him.
