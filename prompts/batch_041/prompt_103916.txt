On questions about restricting speech I keep running into a pattern I have no name for. An utterance is challenged, and its defender asks what harm it does; the honest answer is that no identifiable person is made worse off — no one is persuaded, defrauded, or incited — and yet the utterance, together with the many others like it, leaves the shared conversation measurably worse, because it consumes the attention and the credibility that everyone needs in order to find out what is true, and it makes replying costly for anyone who tries. The harm is real but unowned: it falls on a commons rather than on a victim, and its very diffusion is what makes it effective, since no single reply ever seems worth the cost. I have seen 'tragedy of the commons' and 'the chilling effect' used for neighboring ideas, but the first concerns the depletion of a resource and the second the silencing of a speaker, whereas what puzzles me is a harm that lands on no one in particular and so leaves no one with standing to invoke the harm principle. Does this shape of harm have an established name? If it does, give it and say precisely where my description departs from it; if it does not, coin a name and define it. I care more about the carving than the label: what the phenomenon is, how it differs from the nearest concepts — including the harm principle's demand for an identifiable victim, the chilling effect, and ordinary externalities — and what falls outside it. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
