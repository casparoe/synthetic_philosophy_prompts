I'm on the academic affairs committee at my university and we vote Thursday on whether to fund a new first-year "dialogue across difference" program the provost is pushing. The pitch is roughly: polarization is bad, so make every freshman sit through a workshop and follow a curated mix of opposing-view accounts for a semester. The provost's office keeps citing those studies where following the other side's accounts for a month lowers affective polarization, and a trustee is threatening to pull a donation unless we do "something about viewpoint diversity."

Two of my colleagues are dug in on opposite sides. One says this is just teaching students what to think, and that a university has no business running social engineering on 18-year-olds. The other says we're overthinking it — critical thinking is critical thinking, and exposure to the other side is obviosuly good.

Here's whats been nagging me. We had a consultant in last month and she kept hammering a distinction I think we're all glossing over. Some students are in what she called epistemic bubbles: they're simply not getting certain information, and for them more exposure basically works. But some are in what she called echo chambers, where the problem isn't missing information at all — it's that they've been taught to distrust everyone outside the group, so the group's shared distrust is doing the real work. And she claims the standard remedy (here, read the other side) can actually backfire for those students, because the pushback just gets read as more proof that outsiders are hostile. She also said the nasty part is that from the inside you usually can't tell which situation your in.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting. Can you draft it? I need a concrete recommendation — fund it, kill it, or fund it with conditions — with an actual rollout plan and timeline, and I need it to take both of my colleagues' worries seriously instead of just waving them off. If the bubble/echo distinction genuinely changes what we should do, say how; and if the consultant is overselling it, say that too.
