I retired last year after 35 years in sensory physiology — psychophysics, mostly, which means I spent a whole career getting people to report on what they saw and then trying to figure out how much of the report was the stimulus and how much was them. Now I'm reading philosophy of mind and I keep hitting the claim that introspection is unreliable, and I think the field is makeing a mess of it. I've written a short essay about it and I'd like comments. Draft below.

Everyone cites the confabulation results — people confidently give reasons for a choice that demonstrably had nothing to do with it, split-brain patients invent explanations for actions the other hemisphere started, and so on — and concludes that introspection is unreliable. But this trades on an ambiguity. Introspection is asked to do two different jobs: report occurrent states (I'm in pain, I'm seeing red, I want that) and explain why I'm in them, i.e. give causes. The confabulation results are all about the second job. Nobody in those studies fails to report that they chose; what they get wrong is the causal story. So the headline claim is an overgeneralization, and I think it's a category error — explaining yourself isn't introspecting, it's theorizing about yourself, and theories can be bad for ordinary reasons.

Second move: even for occurrent states, "unreliable" is doing no work as stated. In my old line we never called an instrument unreliable, full stop; we separated sensitivity from criterion. Signal detection theory asks how well you can discriminate signal from noise, and separately where you set your threshold for calling something a signal. Almost everything the unreliability crowd points to is criterion — bias, underreporting, expectation — not sensitivity. And nobody has a way to measure introspective sensitivity, because there's no independent standard to check the report against. That's the real problem, and it isn't skepticism about introspection, it's the absence of a calibration procedure.

Third move, and the part I'm least sure of: maybe there is a calibration standard, and it's other people's language. My old lab calibrated proprioception against vision — you can move where someone feels their hand is by messing with what they see. Maybe introspection gets calibrated the same way, against how the surrounding culture talks about mental states. That would explain why people are reliably wrong in culturally patterned ways, and would mean the right question isn't whether introspection is reliable but reliable relative to what.

What I want from you, in this order:

- Fact check (a paragraph): am I getting the confabulation literature and signal detection theory right, and is the sensitivity/criterion split genuinely applicable here or am I forcing an analogy from my old field?
- Does the second move work (a few paragraphs): is "no independent standard" really a problem for introspection specifically, or does it apply to any first-person report?
- The third move (a few paragraphs): is calibration-by-language a defensible idea or a metaphor dressed up?
- The objection I'm not seeing (one paragraph).
- Bottom line (two or three sentences): is there a real essay here, and what's the one-sentence thesis I should actually be defending?
