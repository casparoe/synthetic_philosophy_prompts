I am a software engineer by training with no formal background in philosophy, but I have spent the better part of a year reading on the side about the philosophy of mind, and I have arrived at a position I would like you to stress-test. Roughly, it is this: what makes something a mental state — a belief, a desire, a pang of fear — is not the material it is made of but the role it plays, its causal and computational relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and the other internal states around it. On this view the mind is closer to a program than to a kind of tissue, and so the same mental state can in principle be realized in a brain, in silicon, in some sufficiently elaborate arrangement of pipes and valves, or in the coordinated activity of a large enough group of people. Two consequences I care about follow from this: that psychology is to some degree autonomous from neuroscience, since its kinds are defined at a level of description that the physical details can vary beneath; and that nothing about being minded requires carbon. I find the view compelling precisely because it matches how I think about software — the same abstract computation can run on any number of machines — but I suspect I am holding it too cheaply, and I would like to know what would count against it.

What I want from you is a map of the position's exposure. Which arguments, intuitions, thought experiments, or empirical findings should actually move me, and which of them turn out to be sharper than they first appear? I have a vague sense that there are cases involving systems that seem to satisfy every functional requirement while evidently having no inner life, and mirror-image cases where two systems are functionally identical yet differ in experience, and I want to know whether these are genuine problems or whether the defender has a principled reply the rest of us keep missing. I am also bothered by a worry of my own that I cannot tell is deep or merely verbal: if a state is individuated by its total web of causal relations, then changing anything anywhere in the system may change every state's role, in which case the same functional state across two different systems becomes hard to specify at all, and multiple realizability might quietly collapse. There is a further puzzle I find genuinely unsettling, which is whether the view can say anything about what a system is not allowed to implement, since it seems as though on some natural formalizations almost any physical arrangement can be described as implementing almost any computation.

So please take the position as stated and tell me, in order of how much each should weigh with me, what would count against it — separating the considerations that would be genuinely decisive from those that are merely awkward, and saying for each whether a committed defender would concede it as a problem or would deflect it, and how. I would also like to know which components of the position are doing real work and which are doing none, and to end with an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all: whether it excludes possibilities or commits its holder to consequences that rivals do not, or whether it has been qualified into something that cannot be argued against. Assume I have no interest in a reading list — I want the reasoning itself, laid out.
