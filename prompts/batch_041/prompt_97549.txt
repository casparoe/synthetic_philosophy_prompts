My friends and I got into a long argument after our metaethics seminar and I'd like to see it written out as an actual dialogue, because I'm pretty sure we were definately talking past each other. Set it in a café near campus, two philosophy grad students, both sharp, neither one a strawman. Call them R and S. R defends the idea that value can be analyzed in terms of there being reasons to have a certain pro-attitude — the fitting-attitude thing — and she thinks the usual 'wrong kind of reasons' objection is defused once you distinguish reasons that bear on the object (the yacht is beautiful, so admire it) from reasons that bear on having the attitude (a billionare will pay you if you admire it). She says the analysis should just be restricted to object-given reasons and that's the end of it. S thinks that's a cheat: he'll say you can't spell out 'object-given' without already presupposing what's valuable, so the repair is circular, and that we should drop the whole reductive project and just take value as primitive. Make them actually engage — when R draws a distinction, S should attack that exact distinction, and R should answer the attack instead of gliding past it.

Also I want S to try out a case we came up with that R probably hasn't thought about: a collector who offers to pay you to find his terrible paintings beautiful, and where the very fact that he's trying to buy your admiration is itself a reason to think less of him, so the 'wrong kind' reason seems to convert into a right-kind one about the person. Let R either absorb this point or show why it doesn't generalize to other cases (threats, pills, etc.). Neither side should win outright; by the end they should have located exactly where they disagree — whether the trouble is a bug in the analysis or a bug in the very idea of a reason for an attitude. Keep it a real back-and-forth with short turns, no speeches, and please don't cite any literature or give me a reading list, I just want the argument itself.
