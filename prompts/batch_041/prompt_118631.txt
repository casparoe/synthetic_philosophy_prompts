I am the science curriculum lead at a national examinations board. Roughly every five years we revise the specifications for school physics and chemistry, which means that model by model I have to decide what stays in the syllabus, what stays but is relabeled, and what goes. The decisions are taken by a committee of subject specialists with no philosophical training, in a single half-day meeting, and each one has to be defended in writing to a board that will not accept a rationale amounting to tradition. The specifications reach tens of thousands of students and dictate what examiners may set.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run on a candidate model: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the tests that settle a case, the points at which the procedure stops with a verdict of keep or drop, and the points at which it must hand the matter to a person because what remains is a question of pedagogy or values rather than of truth. It has to be usable by a specialist who knows the physics and chemistry but has never read any philosophy of science, and it has to fit on a page. Do not give me a reading list; I cannot get at the literature and will not be citing it.

The reason I want a philosophical procedure rather than a survey of what teachers happen to like is that the question I am really asking is the question of verisimilitude: whether a theory we know to be false can nonetheless be closer to the truth than a rival, and how we could tell. Please build the procedure on the best surviving account of that notion, and take the following as given, since I have had it summarized for me and cannot check it myself:

Popper's original proposal was to measure a theory's closeness to the truth by setting its true consequences against its false ones; in 1974 Tichý, Harris and Miller showed independently that on that definition no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other, which makes it useless for precisely the cases I care about. One diagnosis of the failure is that consequence counting is too generous, since a false theory can be decorated with cheap true consequences such as disjunctions with a true disjunct, so any repair has either to restrict which consequences count or to abandon counting. The alternative family of accounts takes likeness literally: a theory is closer to the truth when the worlds in which it would be true are on the whole more like the actual world, so what matters is how far off the structure is rather than how many sentences come out true. A further strand keeps a truth factor and a content factor apart, so that a cautious theory which says little is not thereby close to the truth. I am told there is even an impossibility theorem for the whole project, showing that no single measure can satisfy every plausible requirement at once; if so, I want the procedure to say which requirement it is sacrificing.

The procedure should distinguish at least the cases that actually divide my committee: a theory wrong in its ontology or structure but quantitatively right within a restricted domain; a theory structurally right but quantitatively wrong because a term or parameter has been left out; and a theory that is superseded and survives only as history. It should also address the relation to the successor theory, in particular whether the old model is recovered from the new one as a limiting case, and whether that should count in its favor.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the verdicts. First, the Bohr model of the atom: I should say plainly that I want to keep it, since teachers lean on it and its hydrogen spectrum is right, so if the procedure says to keep it only as a labeled fiction or to remove it from the assessed syllabus, say so and defend it. Second, Newton's law of universal gravitation, which at school level we teach as exact. Third, the octet rule and Lewis structures, which are false for a great many molecules yet carry most of elementary organic chemistry. Finally, tell me how the procedure behaves on a case it ought to refuse to decide, such as whether phlogiston belongs in a chemistry textbook as history, and what it cannot do at all. Mark clearly the places where the procedure is a judgment call wearing the costume of a test.
