I am a second-year graduate student preparing to lead discussion in a seminar on the history of analytic philosophy, and I have drafted the opening section of a paper on Frege's diary; I would like a careful reader's comments before I go further. The seminar has had us reading Frege and Russell side by side, which has made me suspicious of the way my discipline handles the diary: the usual move is to treat it as embarrassing but philosophically inert, bad politics on one side and good logic on the other, and a friend in the seminar insists that the whole question is a distraction from real philosophical work. I half agree with him and half do not, and I want to know whether the half that does not agree has any substance. Here is the draft.

Frege kept a diary in March and April 1924, in the last year of his life; it was published in German in 1994 and in English translation in 1996. It is not a philosophical work. In it he opposes parliamentary government and universal suffrage, denounces socialists, liberals and democrats, is hostile toward Catholics and the French, and writes about Jews in terms that are plainly antisemitic, including the wish that they would leave Germany. Since its publication the standard move has been compartmentalisation: the politics are regrettable, the logic is untouched by them, and a philosopher's character is not evidence about his arguments.

I want to argue for a middle position. Suppose we distinguish three things that might be meant by saying that the diary bears on the philosophy. First, the character claim: the diary shows that Frege was a certain sort of man, which is of biographical interest and no philosophical interest. Second, the genealogical claim: his politics caused or shaped the content of his doctrines, so that, for example, his insistence that the laws of logic hold independently of how anyone happens to think is an authoritarian fantasy in technical dress. Third, the affinity claim: the diary and the logicism share a structure without either causing the other — in both, the deliverances of the intellect are commands that precede and outrank the opinions and practices of the many, and the proper relation of ordinary people to them is submission. My paper argues that the second claim is chronologically hopeless, since the doctrines were in place by 1903 while the diary belongs to the last year of Frege's life, and that its politics were unremarkable for a man of his class and generation; and that the third claim, though true, is too weak to do the work it is asked to do, because it cannot explain why Frege's texts say what they say rather than what Russell's or Hilbert's say. My conclusion is that the diary bears on the history of the discipline — on how a field selects and polishes its founding fathers — but only indirectly on the philosophy. The parallel I keep returning to is the argument about Kant's racial writings and the critical philosophy, though I cannot tell whether it supports me or cuts against me.

I would like two kinds of comment. First, on accuracy: is anything here wrong, and is any claim doing more work than it can bear — in particular, is my move about what was unremarkable for a man of his class and generation a legitimate point about evidence, or is it the excuse people use in order to stop thinking? Second, on the argument: have I mis-stated the strongest version of the second claim, and is there a version of the bearing thesis that survives my objection — one that concerns not the content of the doctrines but how we should read them, or whether the charity we extend to a text should depend on what we know about its author? Please say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of, with your reason.
