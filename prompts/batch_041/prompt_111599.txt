Working on a long piece about the politics of memory -- the whole fight over whether a community owes its past anything, Margalit arguing that remembering is a duty and Rieff arguing that forgetting is how societies actually get well -- and before I start calling historians and museum people I want the spec, not the vibes. What would a theory of our obligations to remember, and our license to forget, actually have to deliver before I'd call it adequate?

Give me the requirements list. For each item, the case or consideration that forces it, so I can tell the genuine constraints from the pet peeves of one side. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular answers.

I want it to handle at least these, and add whatever I'm missing:

- the gap between one person owing something to their own past and a collectivity owing something on behalf of the dead, who never agreed to be remembered and can't be compensated
- remembering that something happened versus remembering how to be a certain kind of people -- the propositional kind versus the practical, identity-carrying kind -- and whether the duty attaches to either, both, or only to the handoff between them (this is the bit I keep chewing on; I think alot of the literature slides between the two without noticing)
- the fact that a duty to remember is often really a duty not to be complicit in forgetting, which is a different animal
- cases where remembering is itself the harm: retraumatizing survivors, hardening an old grievance, freezing a group at the moment of its worst treatment
- the case where the memory is institutional rather than in anybody's head -- archives, monuments, street names, museum labels -- and the last living witnesses have died, so what's left is a building and a plaque
- the town near me is about to tear down a memorial to a massacre because nobody visits anymore and the land is worth a fortune; is that forgetting, or just letting go, and does the theory have to come down one way?

Also: are there results it can't contradict? I know from the psych people that memory is reconstructive, that every act of recall rewrites the thing recalled, so any theory demanding faithful transmission of a fixed past is fighting biology. Is that a real constraint on the ethics or just a fact the ethics has to wave away?

And flag any two requirements you suspect can't both be satisfied -- that's the part I'd actually put in the piece. My hunch is it's accuracy versus present-day cohesion, but tell me if I'm off. Skip the tour of who said what; I can do that myself.
