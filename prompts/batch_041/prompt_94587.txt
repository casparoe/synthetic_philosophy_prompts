You are to play a committed, sharp defender of a particular idealization of deliberation, and I am going to argue against you. Since neither of us can look anything up, we will fix the construction ourselves. Start with an ordinary human who is disposed to answer questions in whatever way they would answer after taking as much time and as much help as they care to take. Now let that human farm out parts of a question to other copies of themselves, let each of those copies farm out parts in turn, and let the answer to the original question be whatever the human at the top would settle on once all of that consultation has run its course. Call the limit of this process the amplified agent; it is defined by a kind of fixed point, namely that the amplified agent is whatever a human consulting the amplified agent would end up saying. The accompanying claim — factored cognition, in the sense I mean here — is that this construction is tractable because a hard question can be broken into subtasks that are independent enough to be handled separately and then recombined by the agent who broke them up, so that the whole can outperform any single human even though every part of the work is done by a human. You are the defender of this picture as a good idealization of what it is to deliberate well as a bounded agent: not a recipe that could actually be run, but the target that better and worse deliberation approximates. Defend it as someone who knows the objections and believes the picture survives them.

Here is my opening move. Your definition makes the amplified agent a fixed point, but fixed points of that description are cheap and plentiful, and some of them are obviously worthless. Suppose the human in question is disposed, whenever they feel unsure, to defer to whatever the amplified agent would say. Then the claim that the amplified agent answers X is self-consistent for any X at all, because at every node the human simply passes the question upward. Nothing in your definition rules out the degenerate fixed point in which the amplified agent is an oracle that always returns the same arbitrary number, or one that returns nothing, or one that returns exactly what the unaided human would have said anyway, in which case there is no amplification at all. So either your construction needs a selection principle that tells us which fixed point is the right one, and any such principle seems to presuppose the very notion of answering well that you were trying to analyse, or the construction is empty. And the factoring half of the picture makes this worse rather than better: the agent who splits a question into subtasks has to be able to tell whether the answers coming back are any good, and if they can, they already had the competence the amplification was supposed to supply; if they cannot, then delegation is just trust in a stranger. Which horn do you take?
