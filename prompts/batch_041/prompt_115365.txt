I'm putting together some notes on welfare economics and I need you to actually argue for a position here, not survey the debate.

The orthodox line is that we never need to get inside anyone's head: utility is whatever their choices reveal. Faced with a menu {A, B} they take A, so A is at least as good for them as B; stack up enough of these and you've got a ranking of outcomes, and that ranking just is the utility function. Clean, testable, no introspection required.

The thing thats been bugging me: the menu is almost never handed down from outside. What you can choose from depends on what you already chose. Your savings determine your credit options, your past consumption determines what you can now stand, your habits determine which alternatives even register as live options. So the menu is an output of the agent's own history rather than an input to it. Let's call the whole thing — the sequence of menus faced and the choices taken from them — the agent's ledger.

What I want from you is the strongest argument you can make for this claim: utility is a property of a ledger, not a ranking over outcomes. The ledger is the primitive; any outcome-ranking is at most a lossy compression of it, and it goes wrong in exactly the cases we care about most — addiction, adaptive preference, habit, the traps where the agent's own past choices are what keep the bad menu in place.

Make it a real argument, not a vibe. In particular I want you to deal head-on with the obvious collapse: someone will say "fine, just make the outcomes richer, let an outcome be a whole history." Say why that isn't the same thing, or concede that it is and then tell me what the ledger framing buys you that a fatter outcome space doesn't. Also give me the strongest objection to your own position and your best reply, and say what conceptual or empirical finding would actually make you give the claim up.
