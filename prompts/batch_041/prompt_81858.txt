At our lab's reading group we discussed a proposal for supervising models that are too strong for us to check directly: put two copies into a debate, have a judge who is weaker than either debater award the win, and trust that the truthful side tends to prevail. The standing objection was the problem of obfuscated arguments — a dishonest debater can assert something false whose refutation costs far more computation than the judge has available, so the judge cannot reliably tell the lie from the truth. Two colleagues then argued about whether the proposal could be patched.

Ana opened: the fix is to force both sides to commit to their full argument tree in advance. The honest debater then does not need to reproduce the whole refutation; she can simply point the judge to the single step where her opponent's tree fails, and the judge checks only that one step.

Ben replied that this merely relocates the cost. The liar will choose a claim whose failure is expensive to see, and pointing at it does not help unless the judge can verify the step being pointed at. Worse, the error can be buried in the *statement* of a step rather than in its derivation, so the judge must assess what the claim means, not merely whether the inference is valid.

Ana: then make the protocol asymmetric. Let any claim the judge cannot check count against whoever asserted it, so a liar who reaches for uncheckable assertions loses by default.

Ben: that penalizes honesty. A model genuinely better than the judge will legitimately rest its answer on reasoning the judge cannot follow, so burden-shifting means the most capable honest debater is the one who loses. And if only judge-checkable arguments can win, we have capped the model at the judge's level, which abandons the entire point of debate.

Ana: not so, because honesty is the cheap strategy. The honest side can give the same answer under every line of questioning, whereas the liar must tailor each answer to the last challenge, and keeping a fabricated tree consistent across challenges is a cost that compounds. Truth is stable; lies are brittle.

Ben: that assumes the judge can track consistency across rounds, which is precisely the resource we agreed she lacks.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: rate each of Ana's and Ben's moves, explain which objections land and which are confused, and say what both of them are missing.
