The question was:

"Freud calls religion an illusion in The Future of an Illusion (1927), and a few years later, in Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), he opens with a discussion of what he calls the oceanic feeling. Explain what he means by calling religion an illusion, give his account of the psychical origin of the religious attitude, and assess whether he takes religious doctrines to be false. If the two books seem to give different answers about where religion comes from, say so and adjudicate between them."

And here's what the student handed in (transcribed as written):

"Freud's claim that religion is an illusion is often taken as a straightforward debunking, but he is making a narrower and more careful point than that. An illusion, for him, is not the same as an error: what defines it is that a wish-fulfilment is a prominent factor in its motivation. He even insists that illusions need not be false — the middle-class girl who is certain a prince will come and marry her is not making a factual mistake so much as letting her wishes do the believing. Religion qualifies as an illusion because it answers to the oldest, strongest and most urgent wishes we have: that the terrors of nature be exorcised, that we be reconciled to the cruelty of fate and especially of death, and that we be compensated for the privations civilization imposes on us.

Where does the religious attitude come from? Freud's answer, I think, is the oceanic feeling — the sense of an indissoluble bond with the external world, of being one with the world as a whole — which he describes in the opening pages of Civilization and Its Discontents. This feeling is, for him, the source of religious energy: it is a survival of the infant's earliest state, before the ego has been separated off from the world around it, and so religion is at bottom the memory of a fusion with the mother that we can never quite give up. The longing for the father's protection belongs to the same story, but the oceanic feeling is the deeper root.

This is also why the comparison with obsessional neurosis does so much work for him. Religious ritual, with its exacting observances and its guilt when they are botched, is structurally like the ceremonials of the obsessional neurotic, except that in the one case the neurosis is individual and in the other it is universal — religion as the obsessional neurosis of the human species. Freud is also careful to grant that religion has done real civilizing work, helping to tame the asocial drives that would otherwise make social life impossible.

As for whether the doctrines are false: Freud never claims to have refuted them. He concedes that he cannot prove that God does not exist any more than the believer can prove that he does, so his point is about the pedigree of the belief rather than its truth value — which is why he treats religious doctrines as neither true nor false, but simply as expressions of human wishes. What he does predict is that as the intellect matures, religion will be given up the way an individual gives up a childhood neurosis he has outgrown, which is why he calls for an education to reality rather than for religion to be abolished by decree."
