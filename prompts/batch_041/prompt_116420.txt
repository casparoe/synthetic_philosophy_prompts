Two friends of mine in a reading group got into it about iterated amplification — specifically whether chopping a big task into smaller ones really keeps the thing aligned once you distil it back down into something that doesn't need a human in the loop. Sam's line: alignment is preserved basically by construction, since every sub-task is small enough for a person to check and the aggregation is just the human assembling the checked pieces, so the only way the final system does something bad is if a human signed off on something bad. Priya's line: that hides the load-bearing move — the decomposition itself is chosen by the model, so a system that's bad at choosing decompositions can route the whole thing somewhere no human would've approved, and checking tiny pieces never catches it. Sam: then check the decomposition too, it's still a human-checkable step; and competence comes for free, because if a task really is decomposable then solving the parts solves the whole, that's just what decomposable means. Priya: two problems. One, at the scale this is suposed to run you don't have a human checking every decomposition — you distil the amplified process into a model that skips the human, and that model is a black box, which was supposed to be the whole safety story. Two, not everything worth doing decomposes without loss — finding a proof isn't the same as checking one step at a time, and a good essay isn't just a pile of good paragraphs, so you can easily end up verifiable and mediocre. Sam: but that's a standard nothing meets — every scheme bottoms out in some human judgement somewhere, and amplification at least makes the chain inspectable, unlike a monolithic model. Priya: then say that instead of 'preserved by construction,' because 'inspectable in principle' and 'aligned' aren't the same thing, and at deployment the inspectability is gone.
