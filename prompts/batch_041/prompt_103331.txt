I've been roped into giving the after-dinner speech at a small workshop on imprecise probabilities next month, and I'm in a bit of a state about it. My old mate and former colleague — he spent the last twenty years of his career on the Judy Benjamin problem — is retiring, and his wife asked me to say a few words because I'm the only person she knows who isn't a probabilist. I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, by the way, and I only got into philosphy after I stopped doing science, so I'm well out of my depth. The room will be about half specialists (people who know the Benjamin problem inside out and will wince if I get it wrong) and half family and friends who've never heard of it and will glaze over if I go on too long.

What I want is a five-minute speech that uses the actual structure of the Judy Benjamin problem — the thing where Judy parachutes into a field split into four quadrants by two roads, and the radio operator can't tell her whether she's in the red area, only that the chance she's in the red area given she's in the second company's area is something like a half — as a way of talking about my friend's career and about what it means to give a retirement toast when you only have partial information and can't just conditionalise on a nice clean fact. The philosophical point needs to do real structural work: I don't want to name-drop the problem and then rattle off anecdotes. Ideally the speech should make the non-experts feel something about the difficulty of updating on conditional information without lying to the experts, and it should land as affection rather than as a lecture. Can you write it?
