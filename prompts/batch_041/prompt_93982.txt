I teach a ninety-minute session of an upper-level seminar on practical rationality, sixteen students, and I want them to feel a money pump happen to them rather than hear one described. What I want them to come away seeing is this: what it is about a certain structure of preferences that makes an agent exploitable, and why the obvious fix — simply refusing to trade — is itself a substantive commitment with a price rather than a free move.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though I want you to design the actual mechanics. Each participant is assigned a small set of preferences over three or four items, with a small fee charged for each pairwise trade, and the preferences arranged so that a participant who sincerely follows her current preference at each step can be cycled back to where she began, minus the fees, with nothing to show for it. I am content for the preferences to be induced by an instruction card rather than discovered — the point is the structure, not the autobiography. The items and fees should be trivial: tokens, pieces of candy, points on a scoreboard, no real money.

Then I want a second stage, and this is the part I care most about. Once the group has watched or suffered the pump, give them a chance to do something about it: before play resumes, they may adopt a rule for how they will choose over the whole sequence. Some will want to adopt a policy and hold to it even when their present preference points the other way; some will want to anticipate their own future choices and reason backwards; some will want to keep choosing freely step by step. I want this stage to make each of those a real position with a real cost rather than a slogan — the committed agent has to act against what she now wants and may have mispredicted her future self; the anticipating agent may find that foreseeing the trap is not the same as escaping it; the step-by-step agent may find herself pumped a second time.

Two constraints on the design. First, the mechanism, not the facilitator, must do the work: participants should run into the trap through the rules and incentives before anyone names it, and the debrief should come only after they have already felt it. Second, I want the pump to be recognizably about diachronic rationality and not only about intransitivity. Ideally the structure that generates it should turn on a preference that is unstable across the stages of the game, so that the problem is about an agent whose choices at different times pull against one another. If you can build a single activity that exhibits both the synchronic and the diachronic version, better still; if not, choose the diachronic one and say why.

Please give me the full rules and roles; the materials; the payoff or fee scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and the debrief, which should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea they are meant to carry — where the cycle bites, where the first person tries to step outside it, where the group's chosen rule either holds or breaks — and which should price the alternatives honestly rather than recommend one. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I expect at least some participants to refuse to trade at all from the outset, and others to treat the whole thing as a puzzle about the rules rather than about their own preferences; I want to know how to respond without tipping the point. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words. I suspect it is one of two things: the case of an agent whose preferences are fully stable and well behaved, who — if the arguments are right — simply cannot be pumped; or the felt grip of preferences that are genuinely one's own rather than handed over on a card. Tell me which, and say what I would have to do about it.
