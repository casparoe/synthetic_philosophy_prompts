I'm in a seminar on the history of economic thought and a friend from the econ side has an argument I can't shake but also can't accept, and I've got a hunch it proves way too much. Here's his reconstruction, roughly. (1) A theory earns its keep by forbidding things — it tells you which observations can't happen. (2) Malthus's theory forbids this: whenever population grows, living standards get pushed back down toward bare subsistence. (3) The last two hundred years are exactly the observation it forbade — population exploded and living standards rose along with it instead of collapsing. (4) Malthusians reply that the mechanism was right and is still right, it just got outrun; agricultural improvements, then fossil fuels, then the demographic transition showed up before the ceiling did. (5) But there's no outcome a clever defender couldn't say that about: if things had gone badly, that's the mechanism working; if they went well, some masking factor got there first. The theory absorbs everything. (6) A theory that absorbs every outcome forbids nothing. (7) So "right but early" isn't a defense of Malthus, it's the confession that he was never making a claim about the world, just expressing a mood. My problem is that the recipe seems to eat everything. Run it on Darwin: nobody has watched a new species form in a human lifetime, and the reply is always that the timescales are longer than the record — is natural selection a mood too? Plate tectonics spent decades being right but early while the seafloor data was still coming in. So I won't give up (6), but I also don't want to say every rescue is legitimate, because then the thing really is unfalsifiable and my friend wins.

I feel like the word "absorbs" is doing something shady between (5) and (6) and I can't put my finger on it. Don't just tell me it's the Duhem-Quine thing with auxiliary hypotheses — I've heard that and it feels like a label, not a diagnosis. Tell me which step or steps to give up, why they looked so plausible in the first place, and what the fix costs. I especially want to know what a principled version of "right but early" would have to demand of the defender, and whether Malthus's version can actually meet that bar. I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (6), so price that trade for me. One more wrinkle that might matter: he also likes to say a Malthusian who pleads right but early is like a doctor saying the drug works, the patient just came in too late — I can't tell whether that analogy helps him or hurts him. Max 600 words, and I want it dense, not padded.
