I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about what a person should do when she is unsure which moral view is right, and I have written a short essay setting out my own way of handling it. I would like your comments.

The essay:

When I am unsure whether an action is right, I try to do whatever is most likely to be right, weighing each moral view I take seriously by how confident I am in it. If I am sixty percent confident in a view on which I ought to give the money away and forty percent confident in a view on which I ought to keep it, I give it away. This is just betting, with moral stakes instead of money.

But I have come to think this simple picture double-counts. The moral views I take seriously are not independent of one another; many of them share assumptions, and so they tend to agree with one another in the same cases. Suppose I have twenty variations of a view that treats consequences as the only thing that matters, differing only over which consequences count, and one view of an entirely different kind. Counting heads, the consequentialist family wins twenty to one. But those twenty views are not twenty independent attempts to get at the truth; they are twenty variations on a single idea, and they will tend to be wrong together. So it seems to me the family should get one vote, not twenty, with that vote divided among its members according to how confident I am in each of them. I call this the family discount.

The trouble is that this makes my answer depend on how I sort the views into families. If the twenty consequentialist views form one family and the other view sits alone, the other view can win. If I split the twenty into two families of ten, the arithmetic changes again, even though my confidence in each individual view has not changed at all. In one sorting I give the money away; in another I keep it. So the answer seems to depend on a decision I make about how to carve things up rather than on anything about the moral facts. I call this the carving problem, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine objection to my idea or merely an artifact of how I keep the books.

Please comment on whether my distinction between counting views and counting families is coherent; on whether the carving problem is fatal to the family discount or something I can manage, and if so, how; and on whether I am quietly assuming something that a careful thinker would reject. I am also unsure whether my reasoning proves too much, since the same move would seem to let me discount a moral view merely because many people happen to hold it. Be explicit about which of your points are settled among people who have thought hard about this and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.

After your comments, give me a table with these columns: Point in my essay (a few words identifying it); Verdict (sound, needs qualification, or mistaken); Settled or contested (and, if contested, who holds which side); Why (your reasoning); Suggested revision. Use one row per point, no more than eight rows, and finish with a one-sentence bottom line.
