My oldest friend is getting married in three weeks and I'm giving the toast, and I've gotten myself into a mess. She and her fiancé are both philosophers — they met in grad school when they got assigned to comment on each other's seminar papers, and the thing they were fighting about that day is the thing they have not stopped fighting about since: what you're supposed to do when somebody you've got every reason to think is just as careful and just as smart as you are tells you that you're wrong. She thinks you have to move a long way toward them — that if you've got no independent reason to think you're the one who's right, then digging in is just stubbornness wearing a nice outfit. He thinks that's spineless, that after you've actually done the work you're allowed to stand your ground, and he's got a whole thing about how if you always cave to whoever pushes hardest you end up with nobody's real view, just a mush. It's gotten recursive too — lately they've been argueing about whether the fact that they keep arguing about it is itself a reason for either of them to move, which I gather is either the deepest part of the problem or a sign they need a hobby. He also keeps a spreadsheet of every disagreement they've ever had and who turned out to be right, and he thinks the numbers are on his side, which she says is exactly the kind of thing a person says once they've stopped listening.

Here's the part that's making me sweat: they've asked me to settle it. In the toast. In earnest — it was in the group chat, no jokes, and they say whichever way I call it they'll take it as final. Half the room is going to be philosophers who know this fight backwards and will be listening to see whether I fudge it or actually say something; the other half is her family from Ohio, lovely people, none of whom have heard of any of this, and who mostly just want to hear that the couple is happy. I'm a nurse. I took one philosophy class, I got a B, but I've heard this argument more times than I've heard my own name, so I'm not as clueless as I sound.

So write me the toast — about five minutes, warm, funny, the kind of thing that gets a real laugh and then a real silence. And it has to actually do the thing they asked: either come down on one side and say why, or explain, for real, why I'm the wrong person to call it — but if it's the second one I want the actual reason, not some polite dodge about it being their day. You can use the technical words once or twice so the philosophers know I'm not bluffing, but everything that matters has to land for the Ohio relatives too. And don't let it turn into a lecture — if the philosophy doesn't come out sounding like something a person would actually say at a wedding, it isn't working.
