I got into an argument in the pub the other night about whether economic growth can keep going forever, and my friend kept bringing up Malthus. I said the whole point is that the collapse he predicted never arrived — population went up, food went up faster, no catastrophe — and he said "yeah, but Malthus wasn't wrong, he was just early", as if that settled it. When I pushed him on it he said the Green Revolution was only ever a temporary reprieve, that eventually the arithmetic has to bite, and that the fact we're all still here isn't evidence against him at all, because the prediction was about the long run. I'm writing a short response paper for a philosophy of science module on failed predictions, so this is doing double duty, and I want to take his move apart properly instead of just saying "that's unfalsifiable" and feeling smug — because honestly some of what he says sounds reasonable and I can't tell which bits.

So what I want is a menu of the different precise things "he wasn't wrong, he was just early" could be claiming, running from the almost vacuous to the quite radical, with a verdict on each. The candidates I can see, and I'm sure I've missed some and got the boundries between them wrong, are roughly: it's a claim that the prediction was conditional, so the antecedent simply never obtained; or that the theory asserts a tendency that is always operative but masked by counteracting causes; or that the prediction was indexed to a horizon that hasn't elapsed yet; or that he was right about the mechanism and wrong only about the aggregate; or that what he got right was which variables matter, not what would actually happen; or that it's just a device for never letting any evidence count against the theory at all. For each one say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false, and then say which of them my friend would actually need in order to make his move work — because I suspect he needs one particular reading while quietly borrowing the credibility of a more respectable one.

Also tell me what would have to be added to the phrase (a date, a mechanism, a counterfactual, some specified horizon and a rate of approach to it) to turn it from a rescue into something that could actually be tested, and whether the "temporary reprieve" line is the same move over again or a different one. Don't worry about sources or citations, I'm not footnoting this. Write it as continuous prose please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, I'll be reading it on my phone on the bus.
