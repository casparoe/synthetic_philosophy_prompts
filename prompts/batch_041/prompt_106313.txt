I'm a journalist — I write a weekly column about science and ideas, mostly history and sociology of science, and I keep bumping into a phrase that I think is doing way more work than the people using it realize: 'ideas have consequences.' I first clocked it at a two-day workshop I was covering on why industrial capitalism showed up where and when it did. A very confident economic historian on the closing panel used it to wave away a question about coal, colonies and factor prices — the gist being, sure, the material stuff was lying around, but the thing that mattered was that people had rethought how to live, so there. Then at dinner a friend of mine, Dan, who's a recovering political theorist, said fine, but interests are the engine and ideas are only the switchmen, which he claims is the sober version of the whole Weber business, and that once you see it that way you can't say culture caused anything at all. Both of them acted like their line was obviously true and the other guy's was obviously confused, and I couldnt tell whether they were disagreeing about a fact or about what the words even mean. I have to file something on this and I don't want to write the usual thing where I quote both and shrug.

So here's what I want. Act like a philosopher of the social sciences who knows the Weber debates inside out and is allergic to hand-waving. Before you answer anything, brainstorm — give yourself room to list every distinct thing 'ideas have consequences' could be claiming, from the trivially true up to the genuinely radical, and then sort them: which are platitudes nobody would deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably just false, and which one my historian was actually leaning on when he waved that question away — and if none of them gets him his conclusion, say so plainly. Do the same for Dan's engine-and-switchmen line, at least far enough to tell me whether it genuinely defeats any of those readings or just relabels them (alot of these dinner-table formulas feel like they win by re-describing). Then tell me what a serious version of 'culture can be a cause' would have to commit to — what kind of cause, a nudge or a push, a necessary condition or an autonomous force — and whether the thing everyone calls the Weber thesis needs the strong reading or can get by on a weak one, since I suspect the people invoking it in my piece haven't thought about that at all. Please, no bibliography and no citations; I'm drafting this on my phone on a train and I don't need a reading list, I need the menu of precise claims with your verdict on each one, and an honest flag wherever you're just not sure.
