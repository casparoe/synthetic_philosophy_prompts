Last week I spent three hours arguing with a friend — a neurologist who works with patients recovering from insular strokes — about what pain asymbolia shows, and I came away more committed than ever to a claim I now have to defend in public: that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation but is carried by a separable affective-motivational state, so that a creature can be in genuine pain and have nothing be bad for it. My argument is that asymbolic patients can localize a noxious stimulus, rate its intensity, and describe its quality, while showing none of the usual distress and making no effort to escape it; if the badness were a property of the sensation itself, that combination should be impossible, and the fact that the two come apart tells us where the badness lives. I'm giving this as a thirty-minute paper next month to a mixed audience: about half analytic philosophers of mind, most of them representationalists about pain; two clinical pain researchers who suspect asymbolia is just a lesion-induced attentional dissociation; and one person who thinks the entire literature is over-interpreted case reports. No citations or reading lists — just the objections and what I say back.
