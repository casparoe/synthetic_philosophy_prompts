I've been trying to work out, on my own, whether there's a genuine moral difference between lying to someone and merely misleading them, and I've written a rough draft of an essay I'd like you to comment on. I don't have any training in philosophy, so please tell me where I've gone wrong rather than being polite about it.

Here is the draft.

Almost everyone treats lying and misleading as morally different, and I used to as well. A lie is a statement the speaker believes false and offers as true. Misleading is different: everything you actually say is true, but you arrange it — through omission, emphasis, or by answering a question adjacent to the one asked — so that the other person draws a false conclusion. The clean case is the answer that is true but not an answer to what was asked.

I want to argue that the moral difference people feel here is really about who does the inferring, and that this is the wrong thing to care about. When I lie, I hand you a false belief and you take it in. When I mislead, you build the false belief yourself out of true materials I supplied. That is why the misleader can always say, I never told you anything false — you concluded that — and why we half-agree, since the inference really was yours.

My claim is that the thing that makes deception wrong is that it recruits the other person's trust against their own interests. On that view, misleading isn't a milder version of lying; it can be worse, because it makes the victim a participant in their own deception and leaves them with nothing to point to afterward — no false sentence, only their own reasoning, which they now have to disown. It launders responsibility through the victim's mind.

The objection I can't get past: if hearers are responsible for their own inferences, then maybe the misleader really is less culpable, since a more cautious hearer would have asked again or noticed the dodge. So everything seems to hinge on how much we're entitled to assume about how cooperative and literal ordinary speech is — whether there is a standing expectation that people answer the question actually asked.

Could you give me comments on this? I'm most interested in three things: (a) whether the central claim — that the trust-recruitment view makes misleading at least as bad as lying — is actually defensible, or whether it proves too much, for instance by turning every ordinary implication of a conversation into a small crime; (b) whether my who-does-the-inferring framing misdescribes the real philosophy of the distinction; and (c) whether the thesis is genuinely a position or just something obvious dressed up.
