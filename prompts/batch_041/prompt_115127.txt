Q3. Consider the argument: (P1) If I stop buying animal products, the number of animals raised and killed will not go down by even one. (P2) If my act makes no difference to the outcome, it can't be wrong in virtue of that outcome. (C) So I have no moral reason to stop buying animal products.

(a) Reconstruct the argument and say which premise a defender of (C) is most vulnerable on.
(b) Explain the difference between an act's making no difference to an outcome and its not being a cause of that outcome, and say why the difference is supposed to matter here.
(c) Now suppose the market is a threshold case: the store only places its weekly order of 500 chickens if demand for that week crosses some line, so the order is either placed or not placed. Does the argument still go through in that version? Say exactly what your answer turns on.
(d) Briefly: does anything change if the boundary is imperceptible rather than sharp?

Their answer:

The argument is a valid modus ponens with one empirical premise and one moral premise. P1 is the empirical one: my individual consumption doesn't move the number of animals killed. That's plausible because supply adjusts at the level of whole barns and truckloads. A slaughterhouse schedules in batches, and a retailer's order doesn't shrink because one customer stopped buying; it gets absorbed as waste, or as stock somebody else buys. P2 is the moral premise and it's the one the conclusion really leans on, so that's where the reply has to go.

The reply says P2 trades on a confusion between making a difference and being a cause, and those come apart. A cause can be one of a very large number of partial contributors, each individually undetectable, and an act can be a cause of an outcome without being a difference-maker with respect to it. Difference-making is counterfactual and act-sized: my act made a difference if, without it, the outcome would not have occurred, or would have been different. Causation isn't act-sized in that way; it can hold between a huge set of contributions and a single outcome. The harmless torturers case from the readings is the standard illustration: each of the thousand torturers turns a dial by an amount that makes no detectable difference to the victim, and the victim's agony is nonetheless the sum of those turns. So the case is there to show that P2's inference is the one we should give up, not the one we should keep.

For (c), the threshold version changes the picture in an interesting way. Here my purchase isn't a drop in the bucket, it's potentially the last straw. The order is discrete: the line is either crossed or it isn't. Since demand moves continuously and the threshold is a sharp line, some purchase has to be the one that crosses it, and there's no reason to think it won't be mine. So in the threshold version P1 comes out false: my purchase can be the difference between 500 chickens being ordered and none being ordered, and the argument doesn't go through.

(d) Nothing changes if the boundary is imperceptible rather than sharp. Imperceptibility is a fact about our measurement, not about the causal structure underneath. The threshold case is really just the torturers case with a sharper edge, and the same reply covers both.
