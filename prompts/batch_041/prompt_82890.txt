I am a novelist. My work is ensemble fiction with rotating third-person limited point of view, and I hold to a strict formal rule: no chapter may contain more than one consciousness, and every death must be either dramatized in scene or reported in a single flat line. I am now writing a sequence in which a rescuer can save either one character or five, and I want to understand what Taurek's position—his challenge to the aggregative view that when one can save either one person or five, the fact that five is more than one by itself gives a stronger reason to save the five—looks like when restated in the vocabulary I actually use. Please translate that position, as faithfully as you can, into the terms of narrative craft: focalization, narrative distance, scene and summary, character arc, dramatic need, stakes, the reader's attachment, the difference between a death we witness and a death we are told about, the moral weight a novel confers simply by granting interiority. Do not smuggle in the philosopher's assumptions and call them translation; keep the translation honest. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Taurek's argument map cleanly onto narrative terms? Which parts translate only with distortion, and what exactly is lost or added? Which parts have no counterpart in the novelist's vocabulary at all? Finally, tell me what the residue suggests: is it a defect of narrative craft—something novels cannot say—or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked? And does the translation make Taurek's position look like a claim about narrative justice, about whose story gets the camera, or does that reading miss the point?
