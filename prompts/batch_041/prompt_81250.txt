Ok, this is a slightly weird request so let me explain where I'm coming from. I'm a novelist — three books, the most recent one about a woman who discovers she has an exact double, which is why any of this is on my radar — and I got talked into teaching a senior seminar this term in the philosophy dept because they needed a warm body and I have a philosophy BA from twenty years ago. The last unit is on identity and indiscernibility, and I assigned Max Black's 1952 paper with the two spheres in it. Big mistake, because now my students think they own it. They've got it down to a slogan: two identical iron spheres in a perfectly symmetric universe, nothing to tell them apart, therefore the identity of indiscernibles is dead, can we leave early. I know that's not the whole story and I want the final to prove it.

So here's what I need: one essay question for the final, 50 minutes, closed book, that can't be answered by either of the two lazy moves — "well, the spheres are in different places, so they're discernible after all" and "they're indiscernible, so the principle is refuted." Both of those should be a C at best. The crux I want them to actually hit is that the principle only does any work if you restrict it to a certain kind of property, and that once you do that, the whole thing turns into a fight about what a thing is made of: is an object just its properties, in which case I'd like them to notice that you maybe can't even describe Black's universe without already assuming the answer, or is there some bare thisness underneath that makes each sphere itself and not the other? I also want them to say what kind of claim the principle is supposed to be — necessary, analytic, empirical, whatever — and to notice that Black's case at most bears on one direction of the Leibnizian biconditional and not the other. And I want the question phrased so it can be printed on the exam as-is, no preamble from me.

Along with the question, since I'll be grading thirty of these at the kitchen table while I should be revising chapter nine, give me a model answer of about 700 words that I can hold up as the standard, and a marking scheme — let's say 25 points — broken into the specific moves a strong answer has to make, with a one-line note for each on how students usually get it wrong. Please dont put any marks on naming philosophers, works, or dates; a third of the class are English majors who've never opened Leibniz and I don't want them rewarded for dropping names they googled.
