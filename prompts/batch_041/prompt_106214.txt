ok so I'm building a set of prompts and I need one more, and this one's for a fake document. Here's the brief.

A museum's conservation department has to write a treatment report on a painting whose maker did something monstrous — genuinely monstrous, not just rude on twitter — and the museum has decided to keep it on display. The joke engine is that conservators are the one kind of person who cant just say they separate the art from the artist and move on: their job is to locate what's wrong with a picture in the picture. Varnish, ground layer, canvas, stretcher. So the report has to attempt the moral housekeeping with the instruments and the vocabulary of painting conservation — deadpan, no winking, in the exact register of those reports ('the blanching is confined to the upper right quadrant; the signature is intact'). The thing being satirised is the reflex of treating moral taint as a substance you can locate in an object and quarantine, plus the house style of institutions doing ethics as paperwork.

600-700 words. Headings, roughly: Object and Provenance / Condition / Examination and Analysis / Treatment Proposal / Risk Assessment / Recommendation. Invent the painting, the artist, the museum. No real ones, no real people.

What I actually want it to get right, since the humour has to come out of the content and not out of jokes bolted on:

- The analysis has to be honest about the fork. Either the fault is a property of the object, in which case you can treat it, but then you have to say what's lost by treating it — and if the fault turns out to be load-bearing, you're now saying the work is worse as art because of the moral thing, which is a real position and not a scandal. Or the fault is a property of the maker, in which case the instruments find nothing and the report has to say so the way a lab report says a test came back negative. Dont fudge this. The piece dies if the conservator magically finds the soul in the pigment.
- Provenance is where the meaning question lives: the same canvas is a different work depending on who we think made it, so a reattribution is a conservation event with aesthetic consequences. Play that out concretely, with a second object in the show if it helps.
- Somewhere in there, the gap between judging the painting and deciding to keep showing it. Those come apart and the report should keep them apart — probably in the Recommendation, where the curator's covering note can be clear that a display decision is not an aesthetic verdict.
- Two appended minority notes, and they have to be real arguments, not strawmen. One from a curator saying the horror is part of the achievement, that the thing is better — more powerful, more honest about what people are — because of what the artist was. One from someone saying the opposite: that the flaw is internal, that the painting asks you to go along with something you cant go along with, and that this is an artistic defect and not merely a moral one. Also worth a line on the viewer who can't take up the perspective the picture demands, and whether that difficulty is a defect in the picture or in the viewer. Leave all of it unresolved.
- It should be funny to someone who's spent time on the moral-value-and-aesthetic-value literature and readable to someone who hasnt. No footnotes, no citations, obviously.

Deadpan the whole way through. The comedy is a conservator measuring something that can't be measured and filing the results anyway.
