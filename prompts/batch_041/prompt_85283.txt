I am a retired geophysicist — thirty-one years in crustal deformation and seismic hazard, and then, after my wife handed me a secondhand paperback of Thomas Nagel's essays and I failed to put it down, an increasingly serious amateur of philosophy. I now sit as one of two non-clinician members on the Data and Safety Monitoring Board of a multi-site randomized trial, appointed, I suspect, because the sponsor wanted someone who could read a covariance matrix and would not be intimidated by the principal investigators. We meet in closed session on Thursday, and I am expected to circulate a short written opinion beforehand. The question before us is whether to recommend that the sponsor stop enrollment and unblind early — the interim analysis is strikingly favorable — or to recommend continuing with a tightened monitoring plan for another six months while a new, mild safety signal is characterized. I need a recommendation with an actual bottom line and a time frame, not a survey of considerations.

My difficulty is this, and I would like you to help me think it through rather than reassure me. Our charter, drafted by lawyers who I believe were unconsciously paraphrasing a philosopher, permits the Board to certify a finding as 'established for the purposes of a recommendation' only if it passes what §4.2 calls the counterfactual test: had the finding been false, the Board's evidence would have been different. A friend in the philosophy department tells me this is more or less Nozick's sensitivity condition, that it is only half of Nozick's tracking account (the other half, which our charter omits, runs in the opposite direction), and that accepting it forces us to abandon the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment. I see the appeal: the test is aimed precisely at the case we all lie awake over, in which one site's data have been quietly fabricated or a randomization file silently corrupted, and the numbers would have looked identical either way. But I also see that we can pass §4.2 for the narrow claim that the observed between-arm difference is not attributable to chance, while failing it for the claim that the drug works — even though the second follows from the first together with premises we all accept. And if we insist on the broad claim, I cannot see how we could ever certify anything, since for any story of undetected corruption the evidence would have been exactly as it is. There is a further wrinkle: fourteen sites were each individually audited, and while I am confident of each audit, I am not certain of any of them, and it is the conjunction that the recommendation requires — one of my colleagues says this is a familiar puzzle about knowledge and long conjunctions, and another says that because we certified the design at the outset we are now entitled simply to set the new safety signal aside. I do not know whether either is right.

Please take the role of a philosopher of epistemology with a working knowledge of research methodology, think carefully before you write, and begin with a short brainstorming section in which you lay out the competing ways of framing what the Board is being asked to certify — then produce the opinion itself. In the opinion, state a concrete recommendation (stop, continue, or continue with named conditions), give the reasoning, specify which proposition the Board should treat itself as certifying and why the narrower and broader candidates differ in what they license, and give the strongest objection to your own recommendation an honest hearing rather than a token one, since two of my colleagues will hold the opposite view and I need to be able to answer them in the room. No citations, references, or reading lists are needed; reason from the case as I have described it.
