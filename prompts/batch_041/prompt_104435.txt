I retired from the lab a few years ago now — thirty-odd years of analytical chemistry, most of it babysitting instruments — and I've been trying to catch up on philosophy ever since. My daughter has me in her book club and next month it's Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and the more I think about it the more I think it's a philosophy book wearing a butler's uniform.

Here's my problem with it. Stevens tells us what he thinks and feels the whole way through, with total confidence, and the reader can see he's wrong. He reports that his feelings for Miss Kenton are strictly professional, that his loyalty to Lord Darlington was just loyalty to a gentleman, that he's pleased to be learning to joke along with his new American employer. He tells us he was perfectly composed the night his father died, and he appears to believe it. He even catches himself partway through a recollection having misremembered who said what, and quietly corrects the record and carrys on as if nothing had happened. So which is it: is he misreading his own mind, or reading it correctly and misreporting it to us and to himself, or reading it and reporting it correctly, with the problem being that the states themselves are a mess? The psychology I've read since retiring says the first kind of failure is normal and easy to produce in a lab — people confidently explain a choice they never made, give reasons for behaviour that couldn't have caused it, defend a memory that's been quietly edited. And the philosphy seems split between people who think introspection is like a sense organ pointed inward, people who think it's really inference, working out your own mind the way you'd work out anyone else's, and a third lot who say you don't look inward at all, you look at the world and read off what you must believe from that.

What I want from you is a reading of the novel through that problem, not a character study of Stevens. Which of those failures is the book actually staging, and where does it push back on the tidy answer? Which scenes should I take to the group, and what does each one show — including any scene where he does get himself right? The two at the end that I keep coming back to are the moment he finally admits to himself that his heart was breaking, and then the bench on the seafront where he tells a stranger that at least Lord Darlington made his own mistakes. Is that honesty at last, or just fatigue? Where does the novel refuse the philosophical reading — if it turns out to be a clean illustration of a point about self-knowledge I'll trust it less. And what does fiction give us here that the experiments can't, seeing as we get his account and the evidence against it at the same time — and what do the philosophers see that the novel can't, so I don't over-read it.

No reading list please, I'm not going to chase citations. I've got the book.
