I'm designing a seminar session on guilt and shame, and I've noticed that I don't yet have a clear sense of what an adequate account of the distinction would even have to deliver — I keep encountering accounts that look persuasive until I test them against a case they were plainly not built for, and then I can't tell whether the account is wrong or whether I was asking it for something no account of this kind owes anyone. So before I commit to any particular view, I want the specification: the full list of requirements that an account of guilt and shame must satisfy to be worth adopting, each with the case or consideration that motivates it.

Some of what I expect the list to contain, though I don't want to pre-empt it: the account must handle shame that arises with no audience whatsoever, as in the person alone at three in the morning, ashamed of something no one else will ever learn, and it must handle guilt that arises with no victim and no possibility of repair, as in a promise broken to someone who is now dead. It must keep shame apart from embarrassment and guilt apart from regret without defining either emotion purely by contrast with the other. It must accommodate a single act that produces guilt in one person and shame in another, and the case where both emotions arrive together in the same person about the same act. It must say something about vicarious or group shame, where one is ashamed of what one did not do. It must not quietly build the verdict into the description — for instance by treating shame as nothing but an internalised social sanction, which makes guilt the authentically moral emotion by construction, or by treating guilt as nothing but a cognitive appraisal of a violated norm, which does the same trick in reverse. And it must survive the awkward fact that the vocabulary and the phenomena come apart across languages and historical periods, so that any account resting on the distinctions available in a single language is under suspicion from the start.

A second layer of requirements, which I suspect is where the difficulty actually lies: the account has to separate the emotion from the social practice — being ashamed from being shamed — and it has to be explicit about levels of analysis, since I am ultimately interested in whether a culture can choose which of the two emotions to run its moral life on, and that question seems to require an account of individual episodes, an account of a culture's moral economy, and a story about how the second could shape the first. It should also be the sort of account that evidence could count against, rather than a conceptual scheme that no finding about how people actually feel could touch. Given all that, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the candidate accounts currently on offer, and — the part I care about most — which pair of these requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list is internally inconsistent that is itself the result. And flag anything in my list that looks less like a genuine requirement than a prejudice of one camp, including mine.
