I'm a novelist — two books out, a third that's been stalled for about a year — and a while back the guidebook people asked me to write the local-etiquette chapter for their new city series, mostly I think because I've lived in four countries and I'm cheap. The chapter has turned into a problem I can't solve, and I've started getting reader mail about it, so I need something better than my current method, which is to answer each letter by vibe and then feel bad about it later. What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, in maybe ten minutes, on a reader's question about wether they have to follow a local convention while visiting somewhere: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a clear no (or a clear yes, you must), and where it stops and hands the thing back to the person as a judgment call rather than a fact. I want it grounded in whatever the philosophy of convention and of obligation actually says that's usable — conventions as solutions to repeated coordination problems, why a convention isn't automatically a moral requirement, fair play and reciprocity, the difference between what a practice demands of someone who lives inside it and someone just passing through, and the awkward case where the local convention is itself morally bad, so that following it makes you complicit. Please don't hand me a reading list or a pile of citations; I can't look anything up and I'd rather have the machinery than the names.

Things it has to survive: tipping in a place where leaving money on the table is reportedly read as an insult or as pity; whether a visitor has to queue the way locals queue (or, in the other direction, is a bit of a monster for queuing where nobody queues); jaywalking, which is illegal almost everywhere and normal almost everywhere; and one case where the local custom is something the reader finds genuinely distasteful or wrong, so the procedure has to say something honest about whether the visitor is obliged, merely permitted, or obliged to stay home. Give me the checklist as numbered steps, each with what would count as a sufficient answer, then run it on three of those cases, including at least one where it tells the reader they have to do something they won't like — I suspect the tipping one is that case and I don't love it. Say explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, mark the places where the procedure will misfire (long-term visitors, people who can't afford the convention, conventions that are contested among locals themselves), and end by saying what the procedure simply can't decide. And if the honest answer to part of this is that the philosophy doesn't settle it and the reader just has to pick, say so — I'd rather print that then a fake flowchart.
