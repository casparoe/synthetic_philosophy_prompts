I recently retired from a career in experimental physics and have spent the past few years teaching myself philosophy; next month I have been asked to lead a two-hour session on Moore's paradox for a local philosophy reading group whose members are intelligent non-specialists with no training in logic. I do not want the paradox explained to me — I want a plan for how to explain it. Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what: in particular, what the group must grasp about the difference between a proposition being self-contradictory and an assertion being absurd, and what turns on the question whether the words I believe that p function as a description of a mental state or as an expression of commitment, before the paradox can be presented as a genuine puzzle rather than a trick. Choose the single example that should carry the whole session, and do not use the raining sentence — it has hardened into a slogan and I want something fresh; tell me what properties that carrying example must have, and must lack, for the paradox to come out cleanly, and why the obvious candidates fail. I expect the group to arrive with several misconceptions — that the sentence is a plain contradiction, that the speaker is lying or confused, that the oddity would survive in the third person or the past tense, and that the belief version and the knowledge version are the same puzzle — and I want to know exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Close with one question I can put to the group at the end that would reveal whether they have understood where the absurdity actually lives, and tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level.
