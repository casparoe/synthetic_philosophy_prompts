For most of my working life I was a health physicist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline. There is a puzzle I kept running into professionally, and it has followed me into my reading, so I should like your help in working out what it amounts to.

Here is the puzzle. In radiological protection, the governing idea, as the International Commission on Radiological Protection sets it out, is optimisation: exposures are to be kept as low as reasonably achievable, with economic and social considerations in view, and subject to dose limits and constraints. To my ear that reads like a cost–benefit exercise with a ceiling. In the general safety regime of the United Kingdom, by contrast, the operative rule is different, and is known as ALARP — as low as reasonably practicable. On that rule a risk has been reduced far enough only when the cost of reducing it further would be grossly disproportionate to the benefit thereby gained. The word grossly is doing a great deal of work: it means the assessment is deliberately weighted in favour of the safety improvement, so that it is explicitly not a straightforward cost–benefit comparison. And this rule sits inside a larger picture in which risks are sorted into three bands — an upper band of intolerable risks that must be reduced whatever the cost, a middle band of tolerable risks that must be kept ALARP, and a lower band of broadly acceptable risks for which no further action is normally required. I sat for years on the sort of committee that had to make these judgements, and I never once heard anyone ask what conception of rational choice we were operating.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature, but a reconstruction of the conception of practical rationality that these arrangements presuppose. Please work through the following, distinguishing as you go between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with.

First, does the ALARP rule presuppose a satisficing conception of rational choice — a search that halts once an option clears some aspiration level, rather than a maximising one that seeks the best available option? If it does, say precisely what the aspiration level is and what fixes it. If it does not, say what conception it does presuppose.

Second, the gross-disproportion bias is the crux, and I should like you to press on it. What does it presuppose about the agent who is to apply it, and about the regulator who imposes it? Does it assume that the duty-holder cannot, or will not, perform an unbiased cost–benefit calculation, so that the regulator must tilt the scales? Does it assume something about the agent's limited capacity even to enumerate the available options — so that reasonably practicable is a statement about the cost of search as much as about the cost of hardware?

Third, which rival conceptions does the practice quietly rule out? Consider at least these three: expected-value maximisation; a strict precautionary principle that ignores cost altogether; and a purely deontological duty to eliminate risk regardless of sacrifice. For each, say whether the practice excludes it or merely sits awkwardly alongside it.

Fourth, the three-band framework seems to me to mix registers. The intolerable band looks deontological — a limit that no cost can override. The ALARP band looks satisficing. The broadly acceptable band looks like a threshold below which the question is simply not asked. Are these three bands consistent with a single conception of rationality, or does the practice presuppose two or three that cannot be reconciled? If they cannot be reconciled, is that a defect, or is the inconsistency doing necessary work?

Fifth, is the practice committed to satisficing at all? Or would a constrained-optimisation view of the sort the radiation-protection principle of optimisation suggests license exactly the same practice, leaving ALARP philosophically underdetermined? I want to know where the practice is genuinely committed and where several conceptions of rationality would license it equally well.

Finally, suppose one replaced the satisficing element with a genuinely optimising one: a duty-holder required to minimise total expected harm, weighing safety against cost without bias. Tell me what would have to change. Would the gross-disproportion test survive such a substitution? Would the three-band structure survive it? And would anything be lost that the present arrangement protects?

Please write in British English. And please be willing to conclude that the practice has no single coherent philosophy behind it: if that is the honest answer, say so, and say what it is instead.
