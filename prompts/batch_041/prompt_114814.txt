I teach a 75-minute session to about twenty students in an upper-level decision theory course, and I want a runnable activity that makes the independence axiom feel like a constraint they are already under rather than a definition they can recite. Build it around a physical lottery booth: participants first commit privately, on paper, to a choice in each of the two Allais pairs (a certain moderate prize against a gamble that is mostly that same prize with a chance of a larger one; then a small chance of the moderate prize against a slightly smaller chance of the larger one), and the tally will show the familiar pattern of sure-thing-plus-gamble. Then restage both pairs as two-stage trees and, before either first stage is resolved, ask each participant which option they would take at the second stage if they reached it; since the second stages of the two trees are the same choice, anyone who took the sure thing in the first pair and the gamble in the second has just given two different answers to one question, and I want them to run into that contradiction themselves, with the two trees drawn side by side, before anyone says "independence" or "sure-thing principle." Give me the exact prizes and probabilities (no real money — tokens or course points, with one small real prize drawn at the end), the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps each moment onto the axiom and onto the reconstruction of an ex-ante choice as a plan for its two-stage version, and one short variant in which the pairs are altered so that no option is certain, so participants can tell whether what they felt was about the shared branch or merely about certainty. Open with a single sentence stating the activity's core mechanic and the experience it is meant to produce, then give the rest, including what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be frank about the one thing the activity cannot do: it can force the inconsistency into the open, but it cannot by itself show whether the axiom or their choices are what should give way.
