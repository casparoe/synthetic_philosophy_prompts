I'm in the second year of a master's in quantitative social science, and for the past few months I've been an RA on a project that estimates the heritability of educational outcomes from a twin and adoption registry. What unsettles me is not the estimation but the way the number travels. A colleague can produce a perfectly defensible figure on a Tuesday afternoon, and by Wednesday morning it has hardened into a claim about what children are, what parents can do, and whether money spent on schools is wasted. I know the standard corrections well enough to recite them, and I have never once seen them made vivid, which is why I would rather have a story than another review article. My reading group meets in a fortnight; write me something I can hand them.

Here is the setting I have in mind. The island republic of Halloran, a small North Atlantic country of roughly two million people, has kept a national twin and adoption registry for forty years. Its flagship study has been run twice, a generation apart, and both waves estimated the heritability of school attainment. The second-wave figure is markedly higher than the first, and the reason is that in the intervening years Halloran did something unglamorous and effective: it equalized school funding across its regions and eliminated childhood malnutrition in the poorer inland districts. A national newspaper reads the rise as proof that attainment is a matter of genes, and that further school spending is money thrown away. Your protagonist is the junior analyst who computed the second-wave figure, and who is the only person in the room able to feel that the headline is wrong without yet being able to say why. I want the story to turn on the fact that the number went up because the country succeeded rather than because it failed, and I want her to arrive at that through a concrete human situation rather than a revelation in a seminar room. Give her a stake in it — a twin sister, a teaching post in the inland districts, a child of her own whom the registry's tidy categories cannot accommodate.

Let the story also dramatize, without any character ever delivering a lecture, at least three of the following: that the estimate is a property of a population in a particular range of environments and not a property of any individual; that equalizing environments tends to push heritability up, so a rising figure can be a mark of environmental success; that high heritability does not mean a trait is immune to intervention; that a large heritability within each of two groups is perfectly consistent with the entire difference between those groups being environmental; and that many supposedly environmental variables — how much a parent reads to a child, for instance — are themselves partly heritable, so that the causal story is knotted in ways the standard twin design cannot untie. The reader should be able to see the misreading before the characters do, and I would rather the story end unresolved than with a tidy moral; if someone turns out to be right for the wrong reasons, or right at a cost, so much the better. Please don't put the names of real scientists or real studies into anyone's mouth — the point is that these people are reasoning from their own data — and aim for somewhere between 2,500 and 4,000 words.
