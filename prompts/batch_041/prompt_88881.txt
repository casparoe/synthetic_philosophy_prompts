I wrote this short essay for a blog I run and I want to get comments on it before I post it. It's about AI safety via debate — you know, the Paul Christiano-style setup where you train two models to argue opposite sides of a question and have a judge (a human, or a weaker model) decide who won, with the hope that this lets you supervise a system smarter than the supervisor. Here's the draft:

---

The standard story for debate goes something like this. If two systems are trying to convince a judge of opposing answers, and one of them is telling the truth, then the honest one has an advantage: it can always point to a concrete flaw in the dishonest one's reasoning, and a sufficiently careful judge will eventually notice. The dishonest side has to keep making things up, and the lies have to stay consistent with each other, which gets harder the longer the exchange goes. So debate is supposed to scale — you can supervise a model smarter than you because you don't need to understand the whole argument, you just need to notice when one side gets cornered.

I buy a lot of this, but I think there's a failure mode that gets glossed over, which I'll call adversarial drift. The thing is, the debaters aren't optimizing for truth or even for winning in the abstract. They're optimizing for winning *in front of this judge*. Those are different objectives whenever the judge has quirks. So instead of converging on good arguments, both sides slowly migrate toward arguments that exploit whatever the judge is biased toward — confident tone, jargon, appeals to stuff the judge already believes, a well-timed "as my opponent tacitly concedes...". The honest debater has no special immunity here, because being honest doesn't mean being immune to optimizing against the judge. If anything the honest side is under more pressure to adapt, since it's the one that has to overcome the judge's priors.

I think this is why a lot of debate setups feel convincing in the lab and then rot in deployment. The judge is usually a human who's paying attention because it's an experiment. Take that away and the equilibrium arguments are whatever pattern-matches to "sounds right" for that particular judge.

My proposed fix, which I'm not sure survives contact with reality: force a commitment step. Before either side sees the other's opening, each debater has to commit to a small set of checkable claims — things a judge could in principle verify independently — and those commitments are frozen. Then the rest of the debate has to reduce to those claims, and the judge only has to check the claims, not follow the rhetoric. The idea is that this cuts off the drift, because you can't migrate toward judge-pleasing rhetoric if the actual decision is pinned to a handful of facts that were locked in before you knew what you were arguing against.

The obvious objection is that choosing *which* claims to commit to is itself a move that can be gamed, and maybe the whole game just relocates up a level. I don't have a great answer to that. My hunch is that it's still progress because the space of "sneaky ways to pick commitments" is much smaller than the space of "sneaky rhetoric," but that's a hunch, not an argument.

---

Could you give me comments? Specifically: (1) is the central adversarial-drift point actually sound, or am I sneaking in an assumption somewhere, (2) what's the strongest objection to it that I'm not addressing, and (3) does the commitment-step fix even make sense as stated, or is it just relocating the problem like I worry? Feel free to be harsh, and if there's a part that's just wrong tell me straight.
