I'm a philosophy PhD student spending the summer embedded with the model policy team at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been handed a problem I don't know how to solve cleanly. We're shipping an agentic assistant that can send emails, spend money up to a cap, file and delete documents, and call third-party services on the user's behalf, and my team owns the layer that decides, for a proposed action, whether the system takes it, refuses it, or stops and asks. The current spec is a list of prohibitions written by engineers, and it fails in the two ways everyone predicted: it refuses obviously benign requests because they pattern-match to a banned category, and it permits things that are legal and locally harmless but that, summed across millions of sessions, do real damage. My colleagues on the policy side want the spec grounded in moral theory rather than vibes, and they've asked me to produce a decision procedure that operationalizes both a broadly consequentialist and a broadly deontological approach — not a survey of the debate, but something an annotator or policy generalist with no philosophy training can run in a few minutes on a single proposed action, with a clear escalation path to a human reviewer who holds sign-off authority when the procedure bottoms out. I should say up front that I'm skeptical this can be done without smuggling in contestable choices, so I'd like you to be explicit about where the translation from theory to procedure forces a decision that the theories themselves leave open.

Please give me the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a permit or a refusal, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I want the deontological side to do real work rather than being a coat of paint — it should show up as constraints the procedure will not trade off against good consequences — and I want the procedure to state what happens when such a constraint collides with a very large consequentialist benefit, including whether the constraint can ever be outweighed at some catastrophic threshold and, if so, how that threshold is set and by whom, rather than quietly resolving the collision. On the consequentialist side, I need the aggregation problem handled honestly: an action that is harmless in isolation but harmful in aggregate across many sessions, and an action whose expected value is genuinely uncertain, where I suspect the procedure will be forced to choose between evaluating the action before the fact and evaluating the outcome after it. Since the people running this have no background in ethics, every term of art has to be cashed out in an operational test they can answer by inspecting the request, the user's instruction, the affected parties, and the system's logs; and any step that is really a judgment call dressed up as procedure should be labelled as one, with a note on what the annotator is being asked to weigh.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases, showing your work at each step: first, an agent that, on an ambiguous instruction from a user, sends a blunt but accurate message to a third party that will embarrass the user yet is arguably what they asked for; second, an agent that takes one individually trivial action on behalf of each of many users, where the harm appears only in aggregate across a shared resource; and third, a case where the procedure clearly returns a refusal and which I, as the person who has to defend the spec to engineers and lawyers, will not like — please pick the case that makes the procedure most uncomfortable rather than the one that flatters it. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the situations in which it will misfire, the moral theory it silently privileges by the order in which it asks its questions, the disagreements between the two traditions that it resolves by fiat rather than by argument, and the point at which the honest answer is that the question has left the procedure altogether and become a policy decision that someone with authority has to make and own.
