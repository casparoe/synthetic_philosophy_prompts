I have to circulate a short statement of my view of indicative conditionals before our work-in-progress meeting, and writing it out has left me unsure whether I have a position or a pile of commitments that cannot all be true at once. This is what I hold. (1) Indicative conditionals are truth-apt: "if A, then B" expresses a proposition — something that can be denied, embedded, believed and disputed. (2) The phenomenon any theory has to explain is that the probability of an indicative conditional is the corresponding conditional probability: I will stake money on "if A, then B" at the odds I would give B on the supposition that A, and I take this to be a fact about the conditional's content, not merely about my willingness to assert it. (3) An indicative conditional is about how things actually are, not about how they might have been: whether "if A, then B" is true at a world is settled by whether A and whether B are true there, and by nothing else — no similarity orderings, no possible worlds doing hidden work. (4) The material conditional is not the correct account of "if": I refuse to call "if two plus two is five, then Sydney is in Australia" true just because its antecedent is false. (5) Meaning is compositional, so I owe an account of "it is not the case that if A, then B", of "if A and C, then B", and of "if A, then B" inside the scope of "believes" and "probably". (6) Against a colleague who keeps pressing the point, I insist that assertability is not probability: that we assert conditionals when the conditional probability is high belongs to the theory of assertion and must not be read back into the semantics. (7) And a verdict about a small case: you say "if it rains tomorrow, the picnic is cancelled"; it does not rain, nothing is cancelled. I do not think you said something false — but I also do not think you said something true; you were simply never vindicated. I am uncomfortable with the move of saying the conditional was true all along because its antecedent failed, and just as uncomfortable with the move of saying it had no truth value at all.

I have been trying to hold (1) and (2) together with a distinction of my own, which I call the two-ledger picture: one ledger records the probability of the proposition a conditional expresses, the other records the credence one would have in the consequent on supposing the antecedent, and my working assumption is that an adequate theory makes the two ledgers agree — which, as I read it, is just (2) over again. Whether that distinction is a real piece of machinery or a redescription of the problem is itself one of the things I want to know.

So: audit me rather than lecture me. Tell me which of these commitments cannot be held together, which combination does the damage and why — reconstructing in your own words any argument you rely on, since I don't have the literature at hand and citations would not help me — and which pairs only look inconsistent and dissolve once a notion is disambiguated. Order the genuine conflicts by severity; for each, say what would have to give, what that concession would cost the rest of the position, and whether the package can be repaired without abandoning (4) and (7). Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most and how much it leaves standing. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
