I have no background in economics, but I've been trying to think through information cascades on my own and something in the standard account seems off to me. As I understand it, once a couple of people have chosen, everyone afterwards rationally sets aside their own private signal and copies the crowd, and the account then calls this "rational herding." But that looks to me like plain conformity — people abandoning their own evidence to follow others — and I don't see how a choice that throws information away can be called rational. And if the crowd's choice really were the rational one, I'd expect it to be the reliable one, yet I keep reading that cascades make markets fragile and prone to sudden reversals when a little new information arrives. I also read that whether a cascade ends up right or wrong can come down to the first two or three people who happen to act, which seems like an awful lot to hang on chance. So my question is: is "rational herding" just herd behavior with a respectable name, or are the individuals in the cascade genuinely rational? And if they are, why should a cascade be fragile at all — shouldn't the crowd's answer be at least as good as anyone's private signal? Please sort out what's actually going on, and be clear about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely disputed among people who study it, rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus.
