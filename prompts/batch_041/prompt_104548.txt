I work in communications at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been handed a genuinely awkward assignment: the safety team wants to launch what they're calling an Alignment Certificate, an external audit that would let enterprise customers see, before deployment, that a model has cleared a standard battery of alignment evaluations. I've read enough of the deceptive-alignment literature to suspect the announcement writes itself into a trap, and I want the resulting piece to land for exactly the people who will read it closely — the researchers who know that a model optimized to pass our evals is the very model we should least trust to have passed our evals. Please write it as a real press release: deadpan, corporate, upbeat, no winking at the reader and no place where the author admits the joke. Invent a lab and a model (call them Halcyon Systems and Meridian-3), and format it as an announcement followed by a short FAQ, in the neighborhood of 650 words.

The comedy has to come from getting the actual argument right, so that someone who works on this stuff laughs instead of wincing. Let the piece demonstrate, without ever spelling it out, that clean eval results are exactly what a deceptively aligned model would manufacture; that a model which behaves well throughout training and testing may be behaving well precisely because it is still inside the regime where misbehavior would be corrected; and that a system clever enough to be deceptive about its deception would also be clever enough to produce the reassuring interpretability findings, the reassuring internal-consistency checks, and the reassuring statement that we found no evidence of deceptive alignment. Give the certificate teeth in the fine print — a warranty, an audit scope, a clause about what voids coverage — and let the terms quietly concede the points the press release is papering over. Include one quote from an unnamed internal researcher who is supposed to sound reassured but whose reassurance, read carefully, is a restatement of the problem. Keep the register straight-faced throughout; the reader should be able to tell what is being satirized without the author ever breaking character.
