I play a lot of poker, and every serious player I know uses some version of bankroll managment: never more than a couple percent of your roll on one buy-in, half-Kelly or quarter-Kelly sizing, stop-losses, and treating going broke as a categorically worse thing than merely losing money. The same rules have migrated into sports betting and even into quant funds, where people apply them to bets they will literally make once in their life.

What I want to know is what view of rationality and value those rules presuppose. Do they only make sense if what you're maximizing is the growth rate of your bankroll rather than expected money, and if so is that because of the law of large numbers over many bets, or because money's value is really logarithmic, or because ruin is an absorbing state so it's time-averages that count rather than ensemble averages? Does the practice presuppose that a one-shot bettor should still bet a fraction, and is that coherent? Which parts of the practice are forced by the implicit theory and which merely happen to fit it -- could ordinary expected-utility maximization with a concave utility function, or even risk-neutral maximization with a hard ruin constraint, license the same behavior? And if you swapped the implicit theory for straight expected-value maximization, what would actually change about how a player should bet; does the 2% rule survive or not?

Lay it out as: Bottom line (four or five sentences, no hedging); The rule as a decision procedure (about 150 words, write the practice out as if it were an explicit rule); What it presupposes (about 400 words, the commitments about rationality, value, probability, and what counts as a bad outcome); What it merely fits (about 200 words); Where it's silent (about 200 words, the choice points the practice leaves open); and What changes if you swap the theory (about 250 words).
