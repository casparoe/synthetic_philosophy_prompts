So I lost most of last night to an argument with my friend Devi, who works in machine learning, about the old evidence problem in Bayesian confirmation theory, and by the end I wasn't sure we were even having the same disagreement, so I want a diagnosis rather than a winner. Here's the setup as I understand it. Bayesian confirmation says evidence confirms a hypothesis when learning the evidence raises the probability of the hypothesis. But the cases scientists themselves treat as the crowning confirmations are usually things that were already known: the perihelion of Mercury was a known anomaly long before general relativity accounted for it, and Darwin built huge chunks of the Origin out of facts about biogeography and embryology that everyone already had on the books. If the evidence is already known, its probability is 1, and conditionalizing on something with probability 1 leaves your credences exactly where they were. So the most celebrated confirmations in the history of science come out as confirming nothing. There's a fix I've seen credited to Garber where instead of conditionalizing on the evidence you conditionalize on the fact that the hypothesis entails the evidence, which may be news to the agent even if the evidence isn't. Then comes the standard complaint: that only works for an agent who has already taken account of every logical consequence of everything she believes, i.e. a logically omniscient one, which is arguably the very idealization that created the trouble. There's a related move where you ask what the agent's credence in the hypothesis would have been had she not known the evidence and count the difference as the confirmation, but I gather people think that counterfactual is ill-defined. Devi's take is blunter: she says this is just data hygiene, the train/test split, don't double dip. You can't count data you used to build or select the model as evidence for the model, evidence has to be out of sample, and the philosophical literature is a few decades of people failing to notice a rule that any statistician learns in week one.

My own suspicion, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that the impasse is at least half verbal in a way neither side admits. The word confirmation seems to do two jobs: sometimes its a synchronic relation between a hypothesis and a body of evidence, how much does this evidence support this hypothesis given everything else we know, and sometimes it's a diachronic story about an agent learning something. The old evidence problem looks fatal on the second reading and like a non-problem on the first, and both sides seem to assume they're talking about the same thing. But I don't want to just declare it verbal and go home, partly because the two readings are obviously connected and someone might be able to show they coincide for agents with the right priors, and partly because Devi's rule doesn't obviously fit either reading: it sounds like a claim about which data you're permitted to count as evidence, which is a different kind of claim from a claim about what confirmation is. And there's a shadow argument I keep bumping into about novel prediction, where people insist a theory is better confirmed by facts it predicted than by facts it merely accommodated. Is that the old evidence problem with the sign flipped, or a genuinely separate issue about the epistemology of theory choice?

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side is committed to rather than a survey of who said what. Is it verbal, substantive, a hidden formal problem about probability spaces and entailment, or several questions wearing one heading? Then be honest about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or the conclusion that this is a pseudo-problem and everyone should stop. If Devi is right that it's all just data hygiene in fancier clothes, I want to be told that, because I'd rather lose the argument than keep having it.
