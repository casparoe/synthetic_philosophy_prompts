I sit on the oversight board for a small university forecasting cooperative — we run a play-money prediction market that the provost's office actually uses when planning stuff like whether a flu surge will hit before finals, and we give a cash prize each term to the most accurate forecaster. Last month one undergrad team, call them the Bayesians, submitted a set of probabilites for a bunch of logically related questions about a tuition strike. Their headline forecasts were great, best Brier score in the cohort, but a grad student on our board ran them through a script and found they were incoherent: they had 0.80 on the strike happening by March, 0.75 on the strike happening by April, and 0.90 on the strike happening by March and then continuing into April. That last one can't be more likely than the first two, since it entails them, so there's a sure-loss portfolio sitting right there. The board is split. One member says disqualify them and adopt a rule that any submitted distribution has to be probabilistically coherent or it gets thrown out. Another says the Dutch book only bites if someone can actually book bets against them, and in a play-money market nobody can force them to take the other side, so we'd be punishing a team whose forecasts were more accurate than everyone else's. A third wants a middle path: a coherence audit that solves a small linear program to find the maximum guaranteed loss a bookie could extract from a forecaster's posted odds, then either fines the forecaster in prize points or pays that amount as a rebate to the other users who took the opposite side, with the penalty capped at the sure-loss amount and only affecting the cash prize, not market standing. I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

Please draft that opinion: recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the audit, reject it, or adopt it with conditions — and say what we should do about the Bayesians this term. Give me a clear bottom line and a time frame for implementation. Engage the strongest version of the losing side, especially the point that accuracy and coherence might come apart and that play-money stakes may make the Dutch book argument less relevant, and don't just restate the Dutch book theorem in the abstract; tell us how it should cash out for a small campus market. Also say whether the rule should be retroactive, and how it should treat conditional forecasts and logically related questions that aren't simple conjunctions. Write it in American English, and don't bother with citations or reading lists — I want the actual reasoning, not a bibliography.
