I am a graduate student in philosophy preparing a position paper for a seminar on distributive ethics, and before I commit to a draft I want to run my current set of commitments through a consistency audit. I have ended up with a package that feels right to me but that I suspect may not hang together, and I would rather find the fault lines now than have a commentator find them later. Here is the package. (1) I accept the anti-aggregationist claim associated with Taurek: in a rescue case where I can save either one stranger or five strangers, and no other differences matter, the bare fact that there are five rather than one is not itself a moral reason to save the five. Each person's life is equally valuable, and the fundamental moral units are individual claims, not sums. (2) I nonetheless think it is permissible to save the five in that case: if I can save more people without violating anyone's claim, I may do so, and the one who is not saved cannot complain that I saved five instead of him, since he had no stronger claim than any of them. (3) I think equal respect for each person in the one-versus-five case requires giving each person an equal chance, by lottery or coin flip, rather than simply saving the greater number. (4) I think that from an ex ante point of view, behind a veil of ignorance, I would choose a rule that saves the greater number, because I would have a five-sixths chance of being saved under that rule and only a one-sixth chance under the lottery. (5) I think contractualism is true: an act is wrong if it is disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject, and I think the lone person could not reasonably reject a principle permitting the five to be saved, because he would have accepted that principle ex ante. (6) I think agent-relative permissions are real and important: I may save my own child even when I could save two strangers, and I am not required to be impartial; but I also think that in public decisions, such as a rescue coordinator's, agent-neutral reasons should govern. (7) I think expected utility theory is the correct theory of rational choice under risk, and that saving five maximizes expected lives saved, so it is the rationally required choice. (8) I think doing harm is worse than allowing harm, so I may not kill one person to save five in a transplant case, but I may save five rather than one in a rescue case, and the doing/allowing distinction explains the difference. (9) I think there is a difference between a claim being defeated and a claim being outweighed, and in the one-versus-five case no one's claim is either defeated or outweighed; the situation is one in which the claims simply do not determine a unique answer. I have been calling this last idea claim-pluralism.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but an audit of this package. Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve once the right distinctions are made, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency in each genuine case? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what exactly would have to give and what would be lost by giving it, and finish by identifying the single commitment that is doing the most damage to the coherence of the whole. I do not want every pair flagged as a tension; I want the real ones separated from the merely apparent ones, and I want the audit to be specific about the reasoning rather than gesturing at broad positions.
