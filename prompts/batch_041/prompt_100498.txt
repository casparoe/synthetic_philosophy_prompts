You are an expert in the philosophy of technology and the history of science, specializing in path dependence — how early, often arbitrary decisions harden into structures that later look inevitable, and how one can tell genuine constraints apart from historical accident. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I chair the Architecture Review Board at a mid-sized company that provides scheduling and billing software to regional hospital networks. I need you to draft a formal written opinion that I can circulate before our next meeting.

Here is the situation. Our core platform is a twenty-year-old monolith built on a stack nobody would choose today: one shared relational database, a homegrown event bus, and a batch scheduler that a long-departed engineer originally wrote for a different line of business. Last year we hired a consulting firm to do what it called a path dependence audit. Its claim is that most of what looks like necessity in our architecture is actually contingency — a 2009 acquisition that forced two systems to merge badly, a 2013 pivot toward a market we later abandoned, and a two-day hackathon in 2016 whose shortcut hardened into a load-bearing interface. None of those were inevitable, the audit argues; had any one of them gone differently, we would be running something else today. A rewrite proposal now sits on the table: replace the monolith with microservices over eighteen months.

The Board is split. One faction says the audit proves the architecture is a historical accident and that a clean rewrite is the only way to escape the path. Another says the audit proves nothing: every system that lasts twenty years accretes quirks, and a rewrite would simply restart the same process and land us somewhere equally contingent, so we should modernize incrementally and stop pretending we can step outside history. A third member keeps invoking the idea of lock-in and the familiar story about how an inefficient standard can beat a better one, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that analogy applies to us or is just a bedtime story for engineers.

Think carefully about the underlying issues before you write, and begin with a brief brainstorming section — a few short paragraphs — in which you lay out the competing interpretations of our situation and identify what is actually at stake. Then draft the memo itself.

The memo should:

- Open with a header and a one-paragraph recommendation, then give the reasoning.
- Recommend a concrete course of action, with a time frame, that the Board can vote on.
- Make the reasoning followable by a non-philosopher, without leaning on jargon or on any literature I would not have read.
- Address the central question directly: is our current architecture the product of genuine constraints or of contingent choices, and how could we tell the difference from the inside?
- Explain how, given that we have only one actual history, anyone could responsibly assess the counterfactual — whether a different early choice would really have produced a different outcome — and where that kind of reasoning is most likely to fool us.
- Take seriously the strongest version of the losing side's case, including the argument that a rewrite would not escape path dependence but merely relocate it, and say honestly what it would take to change your recommendation.
- State what evidence or events over the next year should lead the Board to revisit the decision.
- Be about two pages, and be written in American English.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need a decision document, not a reading list.
