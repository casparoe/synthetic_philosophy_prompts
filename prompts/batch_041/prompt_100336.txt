I am a retired field biologist — forty years of it, mostly on the behavioural ecology of birds — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of science, and I am trying to determine whether the position I have drifted into actually hangs together. What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial: which of the following are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and, for the real ones, how severe the tension is and what the cheapest repair would cost me.

Here is where I have landed.

(1) Demarcation. I take the line between science and non-science to run through testability: a claim is scientific only to the extent that it rules something out — that it forbids some state of affairs we could in principle observe. I am content to treat this as a regulative ideal rather than a boundary sharp enough to classify every borderline case, but I do think the testability requirement does real work and is not decoration.

(2) I regard Darwinian natural selection as the indispensable organising principle of biology and as a genuine science, not merely a useful heuristic or a way of talking.

(3) Yet I have become convinced that the bare principle, stated at full generality, forbids nothing. Whatever we find in nature, a selection story can be told for it after the fact; the survivors are, by definition, the ones that survived. Taken by itself, the principle appears to exclude no possible world. I have a dim recollection that Popper said something along these lines about natural selection and then retreated from it; I have never managed to pin down what he finally held, and I would rather not rest anything on my memory of it.

(4) I do not conclude from (3) that evolutionary biology is unscientific. I think the specific hypotheses we actually work with are testable, because each commits us to something about ancestral conditions and about how a trait should be distributed across related lineages, and those commitments could come out false.

(5) Here is a criterion I have been trying out on my own work and would like you to stress-test. A selectionist hypothesis earns its keep only if it does three things at once: it posits an ancestral population in which the trait was absent, it identifies a selection pressure, and it entails a pattern the trait should show across the relevant phylogeny — so that the hypothesis is answerable to comparative data and is refuted if the pattern comes out otherwise.

(6) I am also convinced that genetic drift and developmental constraint are every bit as fundamental as selection. I do not believe every trait is an adaptation, and I distrust the reflex of reading one into every structure.

(7) I think the historical sciences — palaeontology, phylogenetics, the reconstruction of ancestral states — are genuinely testable and genuinely progressive, even though they cannot run controlled experiments.

(8) I have never been able to make much of the complaint that natural selection is a tautology, because fitness is fixed by actual survival and reproduction, which is a plain empirical matter and not something we can read off in advance of the data.

What I would like from you: go through these and tell me which pairs actually conflict, which are only apparently in conflict, and how serious each real conflict is. For the genuine ones, say what would have to give — the least costly revision, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Do not assume you know which commitment I would be most willing to surrender; I genuinely do not know where the trouble lies, which is precisely why I am asking.
