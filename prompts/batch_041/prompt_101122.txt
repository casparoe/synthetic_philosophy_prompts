I am a philosopher of mind preparing a short piece for a workshop volume on the science of consciousness, and I have drafted the essay below in deliberately compressed form; before I expand it into a full chapter I should be grateful for a candid set of comments. The background is a recurring frustration in my graduate seminar: students arrive able to recite that access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness are distinct, and able to recite that global workspace theory is a theory of the former, yet they cannot say what would count as evidence for or against the further claim that a broadcast makes a state phenomenal. I want the essay to force that issue by inventing its own cases rather than leaning on the stock examples, and I need to know whether the cases earn their keep or whether I have merely dressed up an old worry in new vocabulary.

The essay's argument runs roughly as follows. Global workspace theory, as originally proposed by Baars and later given a neuronal implementation by Dehaene and his collaborators, holds that a representation becomes conscious when it is broadcast to a wide coalition of processors, an event usually described by way of the ignition metaphor. I distinguish two readings of the theory. On the modest reading, it identifies a mechanism that reliably accompanies phenomenal consciousness while claiming nothing about constitution; on this reading the theory is silent about why broadcast should feel like anything at all, and its explanatory ambitions are exhausted by access. On the ambitious reading, broadcast constitutes phenomenal consciousness, and the theory is offered as an answer to the hard problem rather than as a mere correlate. I then argue that the ambitious reading carries two commitments: first, that nothing is phenomenally present unless it is globally available, and second, that nothing is globally available for reasoning, report and the flexible guidance of action without being phenomenally present. I introduce two imagined cases against these commitments. The first, the Silent Fringe, is whatever phenomenal character attaches to material that never enters the workspace — the felt shape of the unattended periphery, the peculiar presence of a word on the tip of one's tongue — and I use it to press the first commitment. The second, the Co-pilot Case, is a hypothetical subject who can accurately report a stimulus, reason with it and act on it flexibly, and who does all of this with ordinary confidence, yet who sincerely and stably denies that there is anything it is like for her to undergo it; if that case is coherent, the ambitious reading is hostage to a first-person report, and keeping it alive requires treating the subject's denial as authoritative, which makes the theory's central claim depend on precisely the reports it was meant to explain. The essay concludes that the theory is best defended in its modest form, and that the constitution claim, if retained at all, must be detached from the theory's empirical successes and defended on independent metaphysical grounds.

I should like comments of three kinds. First, on accuracy: is my characterisation of the access and phenomenal distinction, and of the modest and ambitious readings of the theory, fair, or have I manufactured a position that nobody actually holds? I am especially unsure whether the ambitious reading is a genuine commitment of the theory's proponents or a straw man assembled out of their rhetoric, and whether my use of Ned Block's distinction is faithful to its purpose. Second, on whether the piece is interesting: is the dichotomy between the modest and ambitious readings exhaustive, or is there a third option — say, a reading on which broadcast makes a state available to a self-model, and consciousness consists in that availability rather than in the broadcast itself — that would make my dilemma evaporate? Third, on the two invented cases: is the Co-pilot Case genuinely conceivable, or does the subject's reporting, reasoning and acting as she does render her denial of phenomenality incoherent rather than merely surprising? And does the Silent Fringe tell against the ambitious reading at all, or can its proponent simply redescribe the fringe as weakly broadcast and so absorb the case? Please be direct about where the argument equivocates, particularly if I have slid between access as availability to report and access as availability to control. I would also welcome the strongest objection I have not anticipated, and a verdict on whether the essay is worth expanding or ought to be folded into a footnote.
