I have fallen into the habit of saying that a volcano or an epidemic was a historical actor in just the way a general or a minister is: it decided how a war ended, it emptied cities, it brought down a government, and it did all of this without anyone's leave. My friends tell me this is environmental determinism wearing a new coat, and that calling a natural event an actor quietly hands it a purpose and a will that it does not have. What I want is an audit of the comparison rather than a general verdict on whether nature shapes history. Take a human actor as the source and tell me what corresponds to what in the case of an eruption or a plague — what stands in for intention, deliberation, choice, and responsibility — then say where the correspondence fails, and which parts of the actor picture are doing real work in my claim rather than merely travelling with it. One suspicion of mine, which I would like tested, is that we reach for actor talk precisely when the causal path is opaque to us, so that it confesses ignorance rather than describing agency. Above all, tell me whether the conclusion I actually want — that a natural event can carry as much explanatory weight as a human decision, and that a history which leaves it out is as incomplete as one that leaves out a statesman — can stand on its own without the actor talk. If it can, give me a better way of putting the point; if it cannot, say what the actor talk buys that plain causal language does not.
