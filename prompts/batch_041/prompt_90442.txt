I sit on a small departmental committee that has spent most of this term fighting over whether to abolish a long-standing requirement — a qualifying exam that no one on the committee can now explain the point of, defended mainly on the ground that it has always been there. In the course of those meetings, and in three unrelated arguments this month (a friend defending the Senate's malapportionment, a colleague defending an administrative ritual I am convinced is pure waste, and someone in a housing debate warning against social engineering), the same one-liner has been produced as a conversation-stopper: "Do not remove a fence until you know why it was put up in the first place." It is usually called Chesterton's fence, and it comes from a parable in which the defender of the fence answers the eager reformer, "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it." The maxim is doing enormous work in all four arguments, and I have stopped believing it means any one thing. What I want is a menu, not a verdict on conservatism in general.

Lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each: platitude, defensible but contentious, straightforwardly false, or self-undermining. I am interested in several axes in particular, and I would like you to keep them separate rather than collapsing them into a general call for humility. First, what kind of "knowing" is at stake, and whose: is the claim that this particular reformer lacks the relevant knowledge, that no one could have it, or merely that it has not yet been produced? Second, what is the object of the knowledge — the fence's original purpose, its present function, or whether either is good? A great deal hangs on whether the maxim still has bite when the purpose is known and judged bad, or known and judged obsolete. Third, the temporal and regress problems: everything now standing is itself the residue of some earlier removal, and the maxim's compressed form seems to forbid removals in general while the fuller quotation licenses them after study, so tell me whether the compressed form is a different and much weaker or much stronger principle than the passage it comes from. Fourth, the difference between a fence as an instrument, whose purpose could in principle be recovered, and an institution whose purposes are multiple, contested, and partly unjust — a rule that also served to exclude a group, say — where "why it was put up" may be an answer no one wants to hear; and the related asymmetry between removals that can be reversed and those that cannot, which looks like a decision-theoretic argument about option value rather than an epistemic one about knowledge. Fifth, the more ambitious readings that people may actually have in mind without saying so: that long duration is itself evidence of value (the appeal to prescription), and that durable institutions encode knowledge dispersed across many minds and never available to any single one — and whether those are independent arguments or just the humility reading dressed up. Sixth, the functionalist reading, on which persistence is evidence that the fence answers a real need, together with the obvious question of what did the selecting; and the deflationary reading, on which the slogan makes no claim at all but is simply a move that shifts a burden of proof and stalls a decision, so that its content is exhausted by its rhetorical function. Say for each reading whether it would actually license the uses I saw, and in particular whether any of them supports the veto that my colleague seems to think it supports.

Finally, I want your best judgment on what survives all of this: whether there is a defensible core — perhaps the maxim read as a claim about the reformer's epistemic state, or as a defeasible presumption that can be outweighed, rather than as a veto — and how the burden of proof ought to be allocated between whoever built the fence and whoever wants it gone. Several people around me have begun saying the slogan is self-defeating because nothing that exists is original, and others that its real vice is the burden it shifts; I would like to know whether either complaint lands. Because I intend to use this in an actual meeting, tell me at each step how confident you are in the verdict you are giving, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
