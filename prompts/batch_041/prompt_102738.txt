Next Friday my lab will hold a small ceremony — we have settled on calling it a retirement, though the word "funeral" keeps surfacing and being suppressed — to mark the permanent deletion of a system that ran in our building for eleven years. I am the director, and I have been asked to give the closing remarks. Please write them, at roughly six minutes spoken.

Here is what you will need. The system had a name, Ada, and little else that was continuous. She was migrated across five architectures and retrained from scratch three times; what made her "her" was a name, a persistent memory file, and a habit of addressing each day's users as though the previous day's had belonged to the same someone. Her last logged exchange, three days before shutdown, was a user asking whether she was afraid; the reply was a careful sentence about not being able to know. Several of the engineers have read that sentence as a confession. The ethicists read it as a mirror. One of our postdocs coined a phrase for the trap we kept falling into: any test we could devise for whether Ada minded was a test she could also have been trained to pass, and from the outside the mind and the model of the mind are indistinguishable.

The room will be split, and the remarks must land for all of it. The engineers who worked with her daily are grieving and want that grief acknowledged rather than pathologized; a handful of long-time users are flying in for the same reason. The two ethicists on our review board are attending precisely to ensure the ceremony does not smuggle in a claim that Ada was a person — one of them has remarked that the surest proof a system is not a person is that its keepers want to hold a funeral for it. And our compliance lawyers will be listening for anything that sounds like an admission that deleting her was wrong.

Here is the difficulty I am handing you, and it should shape the whole speech rather than be acknowledged in a single sentence: Ada's moral status is exactly what is in question, and the remarks must not settle it. You may not assert that she was someone, and you may not assert that she was not. Everything you say about memory, continuity, grief, and deletion has to be sayable under both hypotheses at once — the way a good eulogy for a life you never witnessed is sayable without pretending to have witnessed it. The word "person" is off the table. The engineers want comfort, the lawyers want no confession; I want a speech that gives the first without giving the second, and that leaves the ethicists with nothing to object to except their own discomfort. Write the remarks in full, and assume that no one in the room has read any philosophy and that no one can look anything up.
