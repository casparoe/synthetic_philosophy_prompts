You're a philosopher of mathematics who designs in-class games, so think carefully before committing — sketch two or three candidate activities first and pick the one where participants run into the problem before anyone names it.

I teach a 300-level seminar on games, proofs and rationality, 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want an activity where they feel the whole force of the strategy-stealing argument: the moment you learn that a winning strategy must exist and simultaneously learn that the proof hands you nothing you can play. They'll play Hex in pairs on paper 7x7 grids (I have pre-printed sheets and two colours of sticky dots), and by the end of the hour I want them to have convinced themselves out of their own play that the first player wins, that the second player cant have a winning strategy, and that nobody in the room can say how to win as first player.

Build it in stages so it happens to them: a round where they play and then have to write down a first-player strategy and fail; a round where they test the load-bearing assumption — that an extra stone never hurts — and then have to construct a small symmetric game where an extra stone does hurt (seed zugzwang in chess as a hint if they stall); and a staged theft round with roles, where one student has to actually produce a second-player winning strategy on paper, one has to steal it, and one referees whether the thief's procedure can even be executed. Give me materials, timings, the wording of the roles, and a debrief that maps each round onto a piece of the argument: symmetry, the extra-move assumption, the impossibility of draws, excluded middle.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect the theft round to stall in an interesting way, and someone will insist the argument tells them which opening move to play, so what do I do with that? — and be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply from the front of the room.
