I'm in a fortnightly reading group that's been working through On Liberty, and we keep hitting the same wall: everyone in the room calls themselves a Millian, and then we disagree about basically every hard case. One person thinks the principle was addressed to legal penalties only and that a platform banning a user, or a boss firing someone for a tweet, is just private life and none of Mill's business; another thinks Mill's whole point was that the pressure of public opinion is one of the forms of coercion the principle is supposed to discipline, so the same standard has to cover ostracism, no-platforming, and moderation queues. Someone else keeps saying harm to others is necessary but not sufficient — that Mill still leaves room for time, place, and manner rules, and for the corn dealer case where the identical opinion about corn dealers is one thing in a printed essay and another thing shouted to a hungry crowd outside a dealer's house — and someone else insists that if we're allowed to slide from content to context like that, the principle does no work at all and we should just admit the whole thing is a balancing test with a Mill-shaped sticker on it. And then there's the person who thinks the interesting question now is aggregate or systemic harm: a single piece of disinformation or a single dehumanizing joke doesn't set back anyone's interests in a way you could prove in court, but a steady diet of it can corrode the shared factual and deliberative basis everyone needs in order to disagree productively, and if that counts as harm to others then the principle starts restricting a lot more than anyone signed up for.

What I want is a decision tree I can hand out at the next session — not an essay about Mill, an actual branching instrument. Build it so each branch node is a single question a person can answer without already knowing which position it lands them in, and phrase each one as a concrete case or a concrete claim about a case rather than as a label question (so nothing like "do you accept the harm principle?" — obviously everyone will say yes). I need the scope question in there (state coercion only, versus state coercion plus informal social pressure plus private platform governance), the question of what counts as a harm (only setbacks to rights or vital interests, versus any welfare setback, versus injuries to dignity or standing that some people want to call harm and others want to call offense and handle under a separate principle with its own test), the necessity/sufficiency question, and the attribution question — whether speech has to be a proximate cause of a specific harm or whether predictable cumulative contribution to a degraded deliberative environment is enough. There should also be a branch about who gets to apply the standard, since a lot of my group's disagreements turn out to be about courts versus platforms versus the court of public opinion rather than about the standard itself.

Aim for eight to ten nodes and somewhere around six to eight leaves. Give each leaf a short name for the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders have to swallow. Where two leaves end up making the same practical recommendations across your cases, say so and explain why the tree can't pull them apart rather than inventing a fake difference. And if any combination of answers along the way is a position nobody actually holds, mark it as unoccupied and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, because it's stable but repulsive, or just because nobody has bothered to defend it. For the test cases, please use the corn dealer-style case (same content, different medium and audience), a platform moderation case, and a case where the harm is diffuse and cumulative rather than traceable to one speaker, since those are the three we keep getting stuck on. Don't send me to any secondary literature, I just need the instrument.
