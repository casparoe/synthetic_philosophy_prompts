My decision theory reading group has a member who has begun settling arguments with the maxim "you can't be worse off with more information," and he wields it as a theorem rather than a motto. His most recent use of it was to argue that someone who declines to learn a predictive fact about themselves — a genetic risk score, a forecast of how they will behave in a situation they are about to enter, the result of a test whose outcome they cannot change — is being straightforwardly irrational, on the grounds that no rational agent could prefer ignorance. I suspect he is right under one reading of that sentence, wrong under several others, and that the argument he actually needs is much narrower than the one he gave. What I want from you is a menu of the precise claims "you can't be worse off with more information" could be making, each with a verdict, so that I can tell him exactly which reading his conclusion depends on.

For context, the result I take him to have half-remembered is the non-negativity of the expected value of information in Bayesian decision theory (sometimes called Good's theorem): the maximum expected utility available to a decision-maker who acts after receiving a signal, computed under her prior, is never less than the maximum expected utility available to her before receiving it, on the reasoning that she could always act as though she had not seen the signal. I want the readings arranged from the nearly empty to the nearly false, and I want the following distinctions respected in the process:

- Whether a reading is about expected value measured *before* the information arrives or about the outcome actually realized *after* it arrives. (Bad news can be bad news.)
- What the reading assumes about the agent: that she maximizes expected utility at all; that her preferences are fixed and not themselves altered by what she learns; that she knows the likelihoods; that she is unbounded in time or computation; that she is able to refrain from updating on evidence once she has seen it, rather than being compelled to conditionalize.
- Whether the information is free, and whether the reading is a claim about value or a claim about what one is obligated to acquire.
- Whether the reading survives when the information concerns the agent's own future choice, or is merely correlated with it, so that learning it rationally shifts what she does.
- Whether the reading is about information that only moves her beliefs, or also about information that moves her preferences, her reputation in others' eyes, or the payoffs themselves.

Then, at the end, do two things. First, identify which single reading my colleague's "therefore refusing to learn is irrational" conclusion actually requires, and say whether that reading is true, and whether it licenses the conclusion or only something weaker (a claim about willingness to pay, say, rather than about rationality). Second, name the reading under which refusing the information could be rational, and say what the theorem would have to assume in order to rule it out. Lay the readings out as a numbered list I can bring to the meeting.
