I am a PhD student in metaphysics, and I need to anticipate a verdict from my supervisor before I commit to a line of argument in a paper. She is a realist about universals: she holds that numerically distinct particulars can literally share one and the same entity, a repeatable property, and that such sharing is not a matter of mere resemblance and not merely a matter of our classifying things together. Over the years — in seminars, in marginal comments, and in referee reports she has let me read — she has committed herself to verdicts on a considerable range of candidate properties. What I have recorded is the following.

— Being negatively charged: a universal.
— Having a mass of exactly one kilogram: a universal.
— Being at a temperature of exactly 300 kelvin: a universal.
— Being red: not a universal.
— Being a game: not a universal.
— Being a tiger: not a universal.
— Being a dollar bill: not a universal.
— Being such that Vienna lies to its south, i.e. a property one has only in virtue of how one stands to some particular individual: not a universal.
— Being a shadow: not a universal.
— Being both negatively charged and exactly one kilogram in mass: not a universal.

The question I am writing about is whether being alive is a universal — whether organisms literally share one and the same repeatable entity, life, rather than sharing nothing beyond a disjunction of more specific properties or a resemblance among them. She will be refereeing the paper in which I take a position on this, so I would like to predict her verdict rather than guess it, and I would rather understand the rule she is running than merely guess her answer.

So please do the following. First, set out the candidate rules that fit her recorded verdicts — I expect there to be more than one — and state explicitly what each of them predicts about being alive. Second, say which rule you take her to be running in fact, and identify which of her earlier verdicts do the real work in that diagnosis; where a verdict has to be read in one way rather than another for the diagnosis to go through, say so, and say why that reading is the better one. Third, name the further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart, and say what verdict in that case would settle the matter. If her recorded verdicts genuinely leave the question open, I would rather be told that than given a confident answer.
