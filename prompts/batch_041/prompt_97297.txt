I'm working on a book arguing that the sunk cost fallacy is oversold -- that in a lot of real cases, "honoring" sunk costs is either the rational move or at least not obviously irrational. I've got roughly a year of writting time and I want to spend it on the right chapters.

Here's what's bugging me. Everyone talks about "the sunk cost fallacy" like it's one phenomenon, but I think there's at least three seperate things getting mashed together: (a) the textbook case, where the money or time is genuinely gone and can't affect the payoff of continuing; (b) cases where the past investment is itself evidence about the future -- you've sunk a ton into a project that keeps not working, and that fact tells you something about whether continuing is worth it, so sticking with it can be a rational inference from information rather than a bias; and (c) cases where quitting has costs -- reputation, credibility, commitment, team morale, whatever -- so continuing is rational for strategic reasons even though the sunk resources themselves are irrelevant.
