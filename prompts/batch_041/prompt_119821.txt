An essay I read summarizes accuracy-first epistemology roughly like this: "Its central claim is that the only epistemic good is having true beliefs and avoiding false ones. On that basis it argues that any credence function that violates the axioms of probability is guaranteed to be strictly less accurate than some alternative, whatever the world turns out to be, so that the probability axioms are forced on anyone who cares about the truth. The same style of argument is then extended to conditionalization, the Principal Principle, and other norms, leaving the picture that a rational agent is simply one who does whatever maximizes her accuracy. The essay concludes that the program merely redescribes Bayesianism in the vocabulary of scoring rules, and that because the 'epistemic good' it invokes is defined by those very scoring rules, the argument is circular and the view is refuted."

I know enough Bayesian epistemology to suspect this is unfair, but not enough to say precisely where. Please audit the summary claim by claim: what would a defender of accuracy-first epistemology accept, what is a distortion, and what is simply false? In particular, is it true that an incoherent credence function is strictly less accurate whatever the world is like, and does the circularity complaint refute the view or merely relocate where the explanatory burden lies?
