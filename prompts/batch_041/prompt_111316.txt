I've spent this term in a reading group that keeps circling back to how a text's meaning relates to what its author meant, and rather than argue in the abstract I've been keeping a list of cases with my honest verdict on each, on the theory that if my verdicts are consistent they must add up to some principle — the group is half historians and half poets, which may be why we never converge. My list: a history book prints "the committee's report was unanimous" where the author left out a "not," and the very next sentence describes the dissent — I say that sentence means the report was not unanimous, and that we should silently correct it the way we correct a misprint, because the literal reading is one no competent reader could have arrived at; the same author, in another chapter, writes that a diplomat was "uninterested in the negotiations" where he meant "disinterested," and here I say the text means the man was bored by them — his intention to say something about impartiality doesn't make the sentence say it, since nothing in the surrounding prose points that way and the bored reading is perfectly available; a columnist who files a single sentence, "Wonderful news," the morning after a landslide defeat means the opposite of what the words say, and I don't think anyone should read that column literally; a poet publishes a line that supports two readings, one of which she says she never noticed, and I say the line is genuinely ambiguous — both readings are meanings of it, and her having only one in mind doesn't narrow it; a novelist builds a running joke into a minor character that turns on a private fact about his own household, disclosed nowhere in the book, and I want to say the joke is nevertheless what that passage means and that every reader since has simply been missing it; the same novelist, interviewed decades later, insists the ending is meant to be hopeful, and I say the ending means what it means — desolate — and that the interview is evidence about him, not about the book; an editor rewrote a final line without telling the author, who saw the proofs and let it stand, and I say the published novel means what it now says, though there is a different novel in the drawer; and, finally, a friend of mine generates poems by feeding a list of images into a language model, and I find one of them unmistakably about a mother's death and would defend that reading against a rival — even though there is no intention behind it anywhere, human or otherwise.

My working hypothesis has been the crude one — a text means what its author meant by it — and I notice that it survives some of these verdicts and gets routed by others. When I try to patch it I end up saying something like "it means what the author meant, except where we cannot recover that, in which case it means what the best available reading makes of it," and I suspect the exception is doing all the work and the first clause is idle. So what I would like from you is: whether one principle, or a small set of them stated precisely, delivers all eight verdicts; if the honest answer is that they cannot all be had, which one I should give up and whether giving it up is a cost or a correction; what the surviving principle says about two further cases of your own choosing that would be diagnostic, ones where principles that fit my list come apart; and, if it turns out that what I have been calling "the meaning" is really two things — what the text says, and what it was meant or taken to do — where exactly the line between them falls. I am not after a reading list or citations; I would rather have the principle, its clauses, and the case that motivates each one.
