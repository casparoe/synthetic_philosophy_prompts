You are a philosopher of language who has spent years teaching two-dimensional semantics to people who will never take another philosophy course, and you have gotten good at it. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation. I am running a ninety-minute session for a reading group of six non-philosophers — two clinicians, a statistician, a novelist, an engineer, and my brother — none of whom has any logic or possible-worlds background. I want them to leave with a working grip on the distinction between the dimension of meaning that fixes reference by how the world actually turns out and the dimension that tracks how the reference would have gone had the world turned out differently, and specifically on why those two come apart. What I do not want is a vocabulary they can repeat and a diagram they cannot use. Do not explain the framework to me; I know it. Plan how I should explain it.

Before you write anything, work out the dependency structure for yourself: which idea has to be in place before the next one can even be stated, and which of the standard entry points — possible worlds, the a priori/a posteriori distinction, Kripke's necessary a posteriori, indexicals — is a trap to start from with this audience.

Two constraints on examples. First, the example that carries the whole session must be one you construct for it, not water and Twin Earth, not Hesperus and Phosphorus, not Gödel and Schmidt. I want something this audience has never seen and cannot half-remember, and I want to know what it must have and what it must lack to do the work. Second, I have been toying with a case from outside philosophy of language and I want your verdict on it: a clinical diagnostic category whose extension depends on which of two rival gold-standard tests the medical community would have settled on, so that the term's primary intension picks out roughly whatever the accepted test would have classified and its secondary intension picks out the actual pathophysiology. Is that a genuine two-dimensional structure, or is it Twin Earth in a lab coat? If it is genuine, tell me where in the sequence it goes and what it buys me; if it is not, say so plainly and tell me what it would take to repair it.

The misconceptions I expect, and want mapped: that the two dimensions are just two strengths of possibility, with the epistemic one a watered-down version of the metaphysical one; that the primary intension is the stereotype or the description in the speaker's head, which makes the whole thing sound like old-fashioned descriptivism; that if water could have turned out not to be H2O then the necessity claim collapses into relativism; that the framework is really only about indexicals; and that the diagonal is a third kind of meaning sitting alongside the other two. Tell me which of these my sequence generates on its own, which ones they will walk in with, and where in the sequence each one has to be headed off — and add any I have missed.

Structure your answer as follows, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Dependency chain (~200 words). The order in which the ideas must be introduced, and for each step why the previous one had to be there first. This is your planning section; do not explain the framework in it beyond what is needed to justify the order.
2. The carrying example (~250 words). The example itself, what it must have and must lack, why you rejected the obvious candidates, and how it gets extended at each later step so that it is doing work all the way through rather than being discarded after the first ten minutes.
3. The lab-coat case (~150 words). Your verdict on the clinical category, and either where it enters the sequence or what would have to change for it to earn a place.
4. Misconception map (~300 words). For each misconception: how the audience will phrase it, which step produces or admits it, the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, and the one or two sentences I should use there.
5. Check for understanding (~100 words). One question to put to the group at the end, what a good answer looks like, and the two most likely wrong answers and what each would reveal.
6. Left out on purpose (~100 words). What I should not attempt at this level, and the sentence I should say when someone asks for it.
