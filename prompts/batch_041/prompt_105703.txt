I host a small philosophy-of-science podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent several papers arguing that the raven paradox is misfiled. Her line is that it is not fundamentally a problem about ravens, blackness, or even the equivalence condition taken on its own, but a special case of the irrelevant-conjunction problem: the same failure that lets any evidence for a hypothesis H confirm H conjoined with an arbitrary unrelated claim X. Her positive proposal is to treat confirmation as a three-place relation among the evidence, the hypothesis, and the contrast against which the hypothesis is being tested, so that the observation of a white shoe comes out as not confirming that all ravens are black, and does so without any appeal to the relative sizes of the raven and non-black-object populations. I want the interview to press the view rather than let her restate it, since a Bayesian guest on an earlier episode gave the standard answer that the shoe does confirm, just by a negligible amount, and I found that answer evasive -- it seemed to change the subject rather than resolve it.

Please draft eight to ten questions. Include at least one on why she takes the equivalence condition, rather than Nicod's criterion, to be the thing worth giving up; at least one on how her contrastive machinery secures the white-shoe verdict without smuggling base rates back in, and how that differs from the Bayesian answer rather than merely rejecting it; at least one on what her account says about a hypothesis with a disjunctive tail, where the evidence plainly bears on one disjunct and not the other; and at least one on whether making confirmation contrast-relative makes it too easy to gerrymander the contrast after seeing the data. For each of those four, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give -- for example, if she says the contrast is fixed by the scientist's antecedent question, the follow-up should ask what stops a scientist from asking a different question once the data are in. No biographical or scene-setting questions. Keep the whole set under 500 words.
