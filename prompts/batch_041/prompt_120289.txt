I'm building materials for a grad seminar on Kant's political philosophy and I want to check how a model handles a particular exercise: taking the machinery from the perpetual peace essay and running it on problems that didn't exist when Kant wrote it. So, predict Kant's views, but do it under discipline.

Here's the setup, and please take it as stipulated rather than looking for it anywhere — the taxonomy below is mine, not Kant's, and it's not in the literature, so don't treat it as something to recall.

I've been kicking around a little scheme for the shapes a peace can take:

1. Settlement — a bargained agreement that ends a war, held together by the parties' interest in not reopening it. There's a document, there are signatures.
2. Standoff — no agreement at all, just mutual incapacity or unwillingness to strike. An equilibrium nobody signed.
3. Condominium — joint institutions with real authority over the parties, so peace is administered rather than merely promised.

And one more thing I want tracked seperately, because I think it's where the action is: peace debt. By that I mean obligations baked into a peace arrangement that get paid by people who weren't at the table — later generations, non-signatories, outsiders who bear the costs. A settlement that buys thirty years of quiet by mortgaging the next century is carrying peace debt.

Now the cases. For each of these post-Kantian situations, tell me what Kant would have thought:

- nuclear deterrence between two hostile powers
- the UN Security Council veto
- humanitarian military intervention in a state that isn't attacking anyone
- autonomous weapons and machine-speed targeting decisions
- climate change as a threat to peace
- the EU, as against both a federation of states and a single world republic
- unilateral secession by a region
- an international criminal court trying heads of state
- mass migration and the limits of hospitality
- economic sanctions that mainly hurt civilians
- cyber operations that fall short of armed attack

Don't just hand me verdicts. For each case I want to know which parts of Kant's own framework are actually doing the work — the difference between a peace treaty and a peace alliance, the republican-constitution condition, the hospitality limit, publicity as the test of right in the appendix, nature's guarantee functioning as a supplement rather than a proof — and, more importantly, which parts of your answer are extrapolation where the text simply doesn't settle anything. If the machinery gives no determinate answer for a case, say that instead of manufacturing one. And flag any case where Kant's own system seems to push against the verdict you're giving.

Output it as JSON. One object per case, in the order I listed them, with these fields:

- case: the case, short
- shape: "settlement" | "standoff" | "condominium" | "none of the three" — plus a couple of words on why
- peace_debt: one sentence, or "none" if you don't think any accrues
- verdict: "would endorse" | "would reject" | "would endorse with conditions" | "no determinate answer"
- reasoning: a few sentences, the actual argument
- machinery: array of the specific Kantian elements carrying the weight here
- extrapolation: "licensed" | "mixed" | "speculative"
- counterpressure: what in Kant's system pushes the other way
- confidence: "high" | "medium" | "low"

Then a final object with three fields: "cross_case_pattern" (anything that shows up across the eleven), "un_kantian_taxonomy" (the single biggest way my settlement/standoff/condominium scheme is un-Kantian — I want the taxonomy itself criticised, not just the cases), and "textual_gaps" (where you'd most want to check the actual text before trusting your own answer).

No citations, I'll handle those. And no quoting Kant at me — paraphrase.
