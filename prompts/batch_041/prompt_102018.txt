A bit of background first. I spent 35 years as an entomologist — ants, mostly, colony-level behaviour, a bit of modelling — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, which has been a humbling experience. Anyway, I've somehow ended up as the "biology person" on a small advisory panel our old institute runs. We review research proposals and museum/outreach stuff that make claims about animal minds, and increasingly the proposals talk about collectives: "the colony decides", "the hive remembers", "the mycelium is aware", "the slime mould works out the shortest path". I need to be able to say something defensible instead of waving my hands. These come up maybe a dozen times a year, the verdicts go into minutes that the funding committee actually reads, and I can't spend a week on each one, so I need something I can run in a meeting.

So here's what I want. Take on the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows the biology, and think this through carefully before you answer — start by brainstorming the candidate criteria out loud, then throw out the ones that don't survive scrutiny, and only then hand me the thing itself. What I want is a decision procedure: a checklist I can literally run, in order, on a claim of the form "entity X has a mind / is an agent". The questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because the question has turned into a values question rather than a factual one.

Keep the actual philosophical content — the stuff about the intentional stance being a predictive strategy rather than a discovery, about whether the group-level description earns its keep over the description of the parts, about how the colony's "beliefs" needn't be beliefs of any ant, about multiple realizability, about the difference between a system that behaves as if it computes something and one that represents it, and the Aunt Hillary / Ant Fugue business about whether there's anybody home — but translate it so I can use it without a glossary.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (1) a leafcutter ant colony doing the usual impressive colony-level things; (2) a slime mould navigating a maze; (3) one case of your own choosing where I probably won't like the verdict — I'd rather find out where my instintcs are wrong than be flattered. And tell me straight what the procedure can't do, where it will misfire or where the answer just depends on a prior commitment I've smuggled in. I don't want citations or a reading list, just the procedure and the reasoning, and honsetly write it like you'd explain it to a colleague in the tea room, not like a journal article.
