My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. I said no; she said the structural conditions — the settlement at Versailles, the Depression, the fragmentation of the Weimar parties, the readiness of conservative elites to do business with the radical right — would have produced something very like it under somebody else, and that the man was less a cause than a beneficiary. We got nowhere, and I am not asking you to settle it. What unsettles me is that our argument seems to bear on a practice I otherwise admire: the practice, inaugurated at Nuremberg and carried on by the international criminal tribunals, of answering state-organised atrocity by prosecuting individual leaders — the man at the desk rather than the machinery around him — for planning, ordering, and directing the crimes. That practice appears to take my side. It treats the individual as the author of the catastrophe, it rejects the plea that one was merely following orders, and it holds one person answerable for events that required an army, a civil service, and a population's acquiescence. Yet the same law hedges. To make out a crime against humanity, a prosecutor must show that the particular act belonged to a widespread or systematic attack directed against a civilian population — which reads like a concession that the act is criminal only because of the structure surrounding it.

So: what philosophical commitments about historical causation and individual responsibility does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to something like the great-man theory of history, on which the leader is the difference-maker, the cause without which the catastrophe would not have occurred? Or can a thoroughly structural account of atrocity accommodate individual prosecution, casting the leader as a trigger, a catalyst, or merely the point at which the system becomes visible? Which parts of the practice strictly require such commitments and which merely sit comfortably with them — in other words, where is the practice underdetermined, so that rival theories of history would each license it? And if we gave up the presupposed view for a frankly structural one, what would have to change: would personal prosecution have to yield to measures aimed at institutions and arrangements, or is there a way of holding individuals answerable that survives the thought that any one of them was replaceable?
