I am preparing a case for a graduate seminar on whether culture can be a cause, with the dispute over the Protestant ethic as the backdrop, and I want a thought experiment built to my specifications rather than a summary of the dispute. The case is for a single session whose question is whether explanatory relevance and causal relevance can come apart; a scenario that simply declares that the value is, or is not, a cause will not do, because the whole point is to see the verdicts fall out of the structure of the case.

Please construct the case and then demonstrate that it meets each specification.

The specifications. The setting must be realistic — a congregation, a guild, a merchant family, a village, a firm — with no science fiction, no time travel, and no appeals to supernatural sanction as a mechanism. The scenario itself must be no longer than one paragraph. It must feature a shared value that a group holds and that is at least prima facie relevant to explaining a distinctive pattern of economic behaviour the group displays. Two further constraints are meant to create the difficulty: the behaviour must persist for at least a generation after the value has ceased to be widely held, and the behaviour need not be displayed only by those who hold the value. The case must be engineered so that three recognisable stances deliver three different verdicts on whether the value did any causal work, while agreeing on what happened. First, a causal-mechanism or rational-choice stance, on which the value earns its place by entering the agents' preferences and thereby their choices. Second, an interpretive stance in the Weberian lineage, on which the value renders the behaviour intelligible — the agents acted for reasons — and it is a further and contestable step to count those reasons as causes in the same sense as pushings and pullings. Third, a materialist or practice-theoretic stance, on which the value is a downstream rationalisation of a habitus and does no explanatory work at all.

Then the response, in this order and with roughly these lengths:

The case — one paragraph, no more than 150 words.

The three verdicts — one short paragraph for each stance, about 120 words each, saying what it says about whether the value caused the behaviour, and why.

What is doing the work — about 100 words naming the single feature of the case on which the disagreement turns, and why that feature rather than the others.

Stripping the feature out — about 100 words on what happens to the three verdicts if that feature is removed, and whether the case then collapses into something more familiar.

The pushback — about 150 words, in the voice of the best defender of whichever stance comes off worst, attacking the case and explaining why the disagreement is an artefact of how I set it up.

Bottom line — two sentences on whether the case shows that a value can explain without causing, or the reverse.

Write in British English.
