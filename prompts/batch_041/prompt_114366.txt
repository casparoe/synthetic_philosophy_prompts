Hi — novelist here (three books, currently stuck on teh fourth), and I've somehow ended up writing an essay, which is going badly. I'd love your comments on it. Below are the notes; treat them as the essay, since the actual prose is a mess.

The thing I keep circling: every novel I love stands on a floor of stuff it never mentions and never lets you question. In a realist novel nobody says that memory works, that other people have interiors, that the narrator isn't simply lying, that tomorrow will resemble today. Say any of those out loud and the book becomes about them instead of about the marriage or the murder. They aren't premises the story argues from; they're more like the thing that has to stay put for arguing to be possible at all. That's roughly how I read the hinge material in On Certainty — certainties that aren't known, just held, and that make doubt possible rather than being a possible object of it. Please tell me if I've mangled that; I'm going off a couple of readings and a bad memory.

My move, and the part I actually want judged, is to split it three ways. Hinges the author relies on (genre, physics, the fact that sentences mean things). Hinges the characters rely on — the things a character cannot doubt without becoming a different character; doubt one of those and you've written a different book. And hinges the reader brings and can't be argued into, which is where I think most failed novels actually fail. The claim I want to make is that these three have to be kept apart because they can conflict: a character doubting a character-hinge is drama, a narrator stating an author-hinge is death.

Then the bit I'm least sure of. In life, hinges look grown rather than built — the riverbed image, sediment settling over years, communities agreeing without agreeing. In a novel they're installed, in one sitting, by one person. So the analogy might be backwards: fiction doesn't have a riverbed, it has a poured foundation. Is that a real disanalogy or am I just redescribing authorship? And if it's real, is it the interesting thing, or a red herring I should cut?

Also — is this just suspension of disbelief with more steps? I keep suspecting it is and I can't tell. If it is, say so plainly; I'd rather cut the essay then publish a long way of saying something everyone already knows.

Comments on accuracy, on whether the three-way split earns its keep, and on what to cut. No reading lists please — I won't read them, and I'd rather have your objections.
