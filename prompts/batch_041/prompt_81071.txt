For the past two years, since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading philosophy in a somewhat systematic way, and I have arrived at Aristotle's treatment of exchange in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics. I can follow the general shape of the argument, but its central stretch defeats me, and I would be grateful for assistance in separating what is being asserted from what is being taken for granted. Here is the passage, in an older English translation:

"Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. As therefore a builder is to a shoemaker, so must such and such a number of shoes be to a house; for without this reciprocal proportion, there can be no exchange and no association; and it cannot be secured unless the commodities in question be equal in a sense. It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will. There will therefore be reciprocal proportion when the products have been equated, so that as farmer is to shoemaker, so may the shoemaker's product be to the farmer's product. And when they exchange their products they must reduce them to the form of a proportion, otherwise one of the two extremes will have both the excesses; whereas when they have their own, they then are equal, and can form an association together, because equality in this sense can be established in their case (farmer A, food C, shoemaker B, shoemaker's product equalized D); whereas if it were impossible for reciprocal proportion to be effected in this way, there could be no association between them. That it is demand which, by serving as a single standard, holds such an association together, is shown by the fact that, when there is no demand for mutual service on the part of both or at least of one of the parties, no exchange takes place between them. This inequality of demand has therefore to be equalized. Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

Three difficulties, of which the first is the one I most want addressed.

First, I cannot tell what the convention is supposed to accomplish and what it is supposed merely to record. We are told that the standard of measurement is, "in reality," demand, and that demand is then "conventionally represented" by money. That sounds to me like the situation in my old field, where the choice of a unit is arbitrary but the relations expressed in it are not: change the unit and the numbers change while the ratios survive. But the passage ends by conceding that things so different cannot be commensurable in the strict sense, and that demand supplies only a measure accurate enough for practical purposes. Is Aristotle saying that the goods really do stand in determinate value relations to one another, which money merely registers in a cumbrous and variable notation, or is he saying that the equality is manufactured by the convention, so that the whole edifice of exchange and hence of political association rests on something he has just admitted to be, strictly speaking, untrue? It has been suggested to me that the argument is circular, since commensurability is needed to explain exchange and money is needed to explain commensurability; I would like to know whether the appeal to demand genuinely breaks the circle or merely relocates it.

Second, earlier in the same chapter it is said that "proportionate requital is effected by diagonal conjunction," and then builders, shoemakers, houses, and shoes are arranged in what looks like a proportion with four terms. Should I read this as a definite mathematical operation — something like matching the diagonal terms of a proportion — or only as a picturesque way of saying that each party receives what the other's product is worth? What work does the construction actually do in the argument, and does anything later in the chapter depend on it?

Third, money is described both as a guarantee that exchange will remain possible in the future, given that it can be held while one's needs are in abeyance, and as something that "can be altered and rendered useless at will." Does Aristotle's account require money to have some value of its own, or is its role purely that of a token standing for unmet demand? And does he notice that the fluctuation of money's own purchasing power threatens the measuring function he assigns to it, or does he take the "comparative constancy" of money to be enough?

What I am after is not a paraphrase of the passage but an account of how its parts are meant to hang together: which of these claims is doing explanatory work, which is doing merely expository work, and where the argument is genuinely exposed to objection rather than relying on a stipulation I have failed to notice.
