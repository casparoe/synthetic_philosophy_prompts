Construct a thought experiment to order, for a graduate seminar in ethics, and then perform the analysis the seminar would perform on it.

The target is the cluster of intuitions that surround the standard experience-machine case. That case, as usually told, varies two things at once: whether the subject's experiences are caused by real states of affairs, and whether the subject knows which. Build a single device whose settings vary these two things independently, so that a life can be (1) real and known to be real, (2) real but believed to be simulated, (3) simulated and known to be simulated, or (4) simulated but believed to be real — with the subject's stream of experience held as constant as the setup allows across all four settings. Constraints: no supernatural agency, no perfect predictors, no technology so distant that the reader cannot say what would be true if it existed; the scenario itself in under 400 words; and no verdict about anyone's well-being written into the setup, since the verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than be stipulated.

Then supply the analysis:

- For each of the four settings, the verdict of (a) hedonism, (b) actual-desire satisfaction, (c) an objective-list theory, and (d) an achievement- or reality-contact perfectionism, with the reasoning for each.
- A two-by-two table of where the four theories agree and where they come apart, and an explicit statement of which of the two variables, if either, moves any theory's verdict, and which theory, if any, is sensitive to both.
- The single feature of the case doing the real work. Say what happens to the pattern of disagreement if that feature is deleted, and whether the case then collapses back into the ordinary experience-machine case.
- An honest check on whether the four theories are genuinely four here, or whether two of them coincide once the subject's desires are exhausted by experience; if they coincide, show exactly where and why.
- Finally, the strongest reply available to whichever theory fares worst. State whether that reply is that the case is unrealistic, that it smuggles a non-welfare value such as autonomy, truth, or authenticity into the welfare verdict, or that the device's settings cannot in fact be varied independently, and assess whether the reply succeeds on its own terms.

The purpose of the case, so that you can tell what would count as cheating: it is meant to decide whether the pull of the anti-machine intuition comes from the deception (the false belief about one's situation) or from the disconnection (the unreality of what one's experience answers to), since those two are precisely what the standard telling confounds.
