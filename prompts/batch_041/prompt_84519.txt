I am drafting a paper for a value-theory seminar and would like your comments on the argument before I commit to it, since I suspect it is either a genuine contribution or a confusion I cannot see from the inside. The background is a familiar puzzle about reasons for attitudes. Call an attitude — admiration, desire, contempt, a credence — fitting when it is correct given its object, and beneficial when having it tends to go well for the person who has it. The two come apart. If a powerful being credibly threatens to ruin my life unless I admire him, I have an excellent reason to admire him, yet the reason appears to have nothing to do with whether he is admirable; and if such reasons counted, any threat-backed attitude would certify its own object as valuable. The usual diagnosis is that talk of a reason to admire is ambiguous between a reason bearing on the fittingness of the admiration and a reason bearing on the desirability of having it, and that only the first sort can support a judgement about the object's value. Pressure to add further categories comes from reasons that seem to be neither, such as the reason I have to admire a colleague's book because she spent years on it and I owe her the courtesy of engaging with it seriously: that looks grounded in a relation I stand in rather than in the book's merits or in the benefits of admiring it.

My draft turns the usual diagnosis against itself. Ask, of a particular reason, whether it belongs to the fittingness-conferring or the merely benefit-conferring class. Any answer will be backed by some consideration — for instance, that the reason concerns features of the object, and that fittingness tracks features of the object. But that consideration is itself a reason for adopting a classification, and the original question arises for it: does it bear on the correctness of classifying that way, or only on the benefits, such as theoretical tidiness or the avoidance of paradox, of classifying that way? If correctness, we need a further criterion to explain why considerations about object-features are correctness-relevant, and the regress is under way; if benefits, the distinction looks like a pragmatic recommendation dressed up as a discovery about reasons. My tentative escape is that the fittingness/benefit distinction is not itself a piece of value theory but a division of labour within a practice — the practice of holding one another answerable for attitudes — and that the criteria for drawing it answer to the coherence of that practice rather than to any further reason. That dissolves the regress, I claim, because the regress only starts if one demands that every normative distinction be underwritten by a reason of the same kind it introduces.

What I would like from you: first, whether the regress is real or whether I have manufactured it by running together a reason for a classification with a reason for an attitude; second, whether the practice-based escape is a genuine dissolution or a relabelling of the problem; third, whether the argument, if sound, generalizes in a way that helps or hurts it — in particular, whether the same self-application would threaten the parallel distinction between reasons to believe that bear on truth and pragmatic reasons to believe. I would also value a blunt assessment of how interesting this would be to someone who knows the area well: is the self-application of the puzzle a fresh move, or is it the kind of thing that has already been dismissed for a reason I am not seeing? Please be specific about the weakest step in the argument.
