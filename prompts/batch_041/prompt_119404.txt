For the past three years, since retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading metaphysics in an attempt to work out which of its puzzles are genuine. One has stopped me cold: Bradley's regress, and the associated problem of the unity of the proposition. I should like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between the two camps in that dispute would produce.

The two parties are as follows. The first holds that the unity of a proposition, and equally the unity of a relational fact, is a genuine explanatory problem, and that Bradley's regress demonstrates it cannot be solved by adding relational constituents, since any such constituent must in turn be tied to the items it was introduced to relate. The second holds that the regress is an artefact of a particular picture of unity — one on which whatever unifies a plurality is itself one more item in that plurality — and that once the picture is dropped there is no explanatory debt left to pay. Both parties should be treated as competent and as having thought hard about the matter; I want neither of them caricatured, and I want them to answer each other's actual formulations rather than talk past one another.

Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. This should include the regress itself written out as an explicit derivation with numbered steps, with a note marking the step at which the two readings of the argument part company; what both concede (for instance, that the series is infinite, and that infinite length is not by itself the objection); and an inventory of the assumptions the argument needs — such as that a relation is an entity alongside its terms, that the terms are prior to the whole, and that unity is something added to constituents rather than constitutive of them.

2. A disagreements table. For each crux: the question stated in wording neutral between the parties; each side's answer, phrased so that side would accept the phrasing as its own; and what, if anything, each side would count as moving it. The cruxes I suspect are in play — confirm, revise or replace them: whether the nexus that relates is itself a relation; whether this is one regress or three, arising separately for relations among objects, for properties inhering in a substance, and for the copula in a proposition; what the mark of a vicious as opposed to a merely long regress is; whether the regress, if sound, tells only against relational accounts of unity or against every account on which a proposition is a structured complex of constituents, contemporary ones included; and whether the unity of a proposition is even the same problem as the unity of a fact.

3. Concrete cases that both sides agree would bear on those cruxes, with each side's prediction for each case recorded in advance. I would suggest at least the following: the difference between a mere list of constituents — the name Socrates, the predicate wise — and the proposition that Socrates is wise; a non-symmetric relation, where the same two objects must occur in one order rather than the reverse; the composition of a whole out of parts, and whether the same regress arises there; and the false proposition.

4. A closing note on which parts of the disagreement are verbal rather than substantive, and why.

One further request, arising from my own training. In chemistry a bond is not a further atom that itself requires bonding; I should like the statement to say explicitly whether the relating nexus is meant to be more like a bond or more like an extra atom, and what turns on the answer.

Please do not rest any of the claims on the authority of a named historical author, since I have no way to check attributions, and write the whole document in British English.
