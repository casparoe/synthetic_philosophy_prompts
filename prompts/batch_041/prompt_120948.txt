Start the tree from a commitment I want readers to be able to accept before they know any of the labels: some of the choices that matter most to me — having a child, converting to a religion, transitioning, emigrating, enlisting, going blind — will change not just what I know but what I care about, so the values I have right now can't rank them. I'm making a handout for a grad seminar session that opens with L.A. Paul's framing of transformative experience, and what I want is a decision tree, not an essay. Each branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads (no node like 'do you accept the aspiration view?'), each leaf a named position on whether these choices can be made rationally and on what basis, plus one sentence on the commitment that leaf has the hardest time keeping.

The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order I'd put them on the tree: whether the obstacle is that you can't know what the experience is like, or that you can't know what you'll care about afterwards — or whether only one of those is a real problem and the other is a red herring; whether there's still a fact of the matter about which option is better for you, fixed by something other than your preferences; whether you can borrow the missing knowledge from people who've already done it; whether you can rationally aim at a value you don't yet grasp, with reasons that point forward at the value you're growing into rather than at anything you currently want; whether a second-order preference about what kind of person you want to become can do the work instead; whether this is a special problem at all, or just ordinary decision-making under uncertainty that people dramatize; and — because half the class works on disability and bioethics — what changes when the choice is made for someone else, like a parent deciding on a cochlear implant for a deaf kid or raising a child inside a religion.

Build the tree from the questions first and drop the names in afterwards, and tell me which combinations of answers nobody actually occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). If two leaves can't really be seperated by your questions, say so instead of fudging it. Eight-ish leaves is fine; I'd definately rather have a slightly ugly tree that's honest about its gaps than a tidy one that hides them. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall shape of the tree and which single branch or leaf you're least sure of — the one where someone who works on this would most likely push back.
