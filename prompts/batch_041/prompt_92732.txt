I'm in a phil of mind seminar and we just did the color phi phenomenon, which I'd somehow never heard of before. The setup is: you flash a dot of one color on the left, then a dot of a different color on the right, close together in time, and at the right spacing people report a single dot that travels across and switches color about halfway along the path. The catch is the "halfway": subjects say they saw the color change before the second dot had even appeared, which can't literally be right. Our prof used it to bring up Daniel Dennett's distinction (from Consciousness Explained) between two ways the brain could be pulling this off — a "Stalinesque" revision, where the experience itself is fudged before it ever reaches you (like a show trial with the verdict decided in advance), and an "Orwellian" one, where you have the experience and then your memory of it gets quietly rewritten (like Winston rewriting old newspaper records). Dennett's point, as I understood it, is that you can't in principle tell these two apart, because there's no moment where the brain is either fudging or rewriting — so maybe "when did the experience happen" just doesn't have a determinate answer.

Anyway a guy in my seminar claims you can push this alot further, and he wrote up an argument that I cant tell if its clever or just confused. Roughly:

(1) When she watches the two dots, she reports a single dot moving across that turns green about halfway. (2) That report describes what she experienced, not an interpretation or a guess. (3) So her experience represents the color change as happening at the midpoint — i.e., before the second dot was shown. (4) A state can't encode information before the information exists, so the color-change part of her experience couldn't have been in place until after the second dot was shown. (5) So the experience she reports — a dot that moves and changes color — was not present during the stretch of motion it describes; it must have been assembled afterward. (6) Something that gets assembled after the events it describes is a memory, not a perception. (7) So what she took to be a perception was really a memory. (8) And nothing here is special to phi: any experience that has to combine information arriving over an interval must wait for that information and then assemble. (9) But all experience combines information arriving over an interval. (10) So there's no perception, only memory. (11) Which is absurd.

He says the upshot is that the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction isn't just undecidable, it's incoherent, and that any talk of an experience having a "time" is confused. I'd like to keep (1)-(3) if I can, since the phenomenon really does look like the experience reaching backward, and I want to keep Dennett's framing because I think the distinction is doing real work. So: which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I have to give up to reject it?
