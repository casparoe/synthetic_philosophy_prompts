OK so I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book where a small town talks itself into selling everything off to a developer, one family at a time, and the whole middle section is just people going along with whatever the family before them did. Writing it has got me obsessed with when that's dumb and when it's actually the smart thing, and I keep flip-flopping, so I want to lay out my gut reactions to a bunch of little cases and see if there's one rule underneath them or if I'm kidding myself.

Here's what I've got:

My neighbor says the new bakery is great. Then my other neighbor says so too, seperately, never having talked to the first one. I go. Obviously smart -- two people I trust agreeing.

Same scene, but the two neighbors are married, do everything together, and both actually got it from the same third cousin. I still go, but now I feel like a chump, even though it's the exact same two people saying the exact same words.

Walking down a street, two restaurants, one packed, one empty. I pick the packed one. Feels fine but a little shameful, like I'm being led around by the nose.

At a show the crowd stands up, so I stand up. No deliberation at all. Feels completely fine, not stupid in the slightest.

A stock I own starts climbing because everybody's piling in, so I buy more. This feels like the definition of being a sucker, and if a character of mine did it I'd want the reader to wince.

A jar of jellybeans, prize for closest guess. Five people ahead of me call out numbers around a thousand. I actually looked at the jar and my honest read is two thousand. I say a thousand anyway. I think this is a genuine mistake and I'd feel bad afterward.

So my tentative rule is: follow the crowd when you've got nothing of your own to go on, ignore it when you do. But that rule seems to make the restaurant smart (I've got nothing, so I follow) and the concert smart (same), while the jellybean case is the one I'm dead sure is a mistake, and the rule only catches it because I *did* have my own info there. Meanwhile the restaurant and the concert look structurally identical to the stock, which I hate, and I can't tell you why standing up at a show is fine and buying the climbing stock isn't, except that one's about money and one isn't, which feels like a total cop-out.

Can you tell me what principle actually fits these six, or if there isn't one, which of my verdicts I should give up? And please say what it predicts for the case I'm actually writing -- the town, where each family sells because the family before them did, nobody checks the deal independently, and by the end even the old man who swore he'd never sell caves, and nobody ever finds out if it was a good price.
