I teach a graduate seminar on historical materialism, and every year the same thing happens: students arrive able to recite the base/superstructure metaphor and the formula about determination in the last instance, and unable to say what they actually think about either. I want an instrument that sorts them by what they are willing to say about cases rather than by the label they prefer. Please build me a decision tree.

The root should be the commitment almost nobody in the room disputes — that a society's economic structure has some explanatory priority with respect to its legal, political, religious, and intellectual life — and the branches should be the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart. I want at least these five distinctions to appear as branch nodes, phrased as questions a student could answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to:

1. Does the economic structure only constrain what the rest of society can do, or does it also fix the content of what the rest of society says?
2. Does it select among ideas and institutions that are generated independently of it, or does it produce them?
3. Is the bearer of the explanatory priority the development of the productive forces, the relations of production and the class struggle they encode, or the competitive pressure of markets on units that must survive?
4. Is the claim a universal law of history or a claim about capitalism alone — and if universal, what does it predict for societies that never developed capitalism on their own?
5. Is determination in the last instance an empirical claim about how often the economy actually wins, or a formal reservation that costs the theory nothing? This one matters most to me; the tree should make plain that the two answers lead to different places.

Locate the leaves in that space: seven to ten of them, each labeled with the name of the position — the standard name where there is one, a descriptive label where there isn't, and "unnamed" where you can find nobody who holds it — plus one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending. Do not give me a reading list. I want no titles, dates, or citations, and I would rather have a descriptive label than a guessed attribution. I also don't want the space to collapse into crude economic determinism, relative autonomy, and rejection; the intermediate positions that keep the priority claim while denying content-determination have to be distinguishable from one another.

Build the branch questions out of concrete cases rather than abstractions. At least four nodes should be answerable only by taking a stand on a case: the transition from feudalism to capitalism in England and the question of why comparable pressures produced different outcomes elsewhere; the abolition of slavery in the Atlantic world, where moral and religious argument appears to do independent work; rapid state-directed industrialization in a society that imported its technology rather than developing it; and the persistence of religious belief in an advanced capitalist society. None of the cases should require the student to know a historiography.

Tell me which leaves the tree cannot separate, and why — where two positions give the same answer at every node, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction. For the cells of the space that no position occupies, commit to a diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, with your reason.

Finally, take a position yourself. Which leaf would you defend if you had to, and what does your tree get right that the standard textbook rendering of the metaphor gets wrong? Give me your reasons rather than a survey of options; where the considerations are genuinely divided, say so and still pick. Deliver the tree with numbered nodes and a table of leaves, followed by a short key for reading a path from the root to a leaf.
