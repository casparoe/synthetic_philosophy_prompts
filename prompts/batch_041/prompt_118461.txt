I have been reading rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent blogs for the better part of a decade, and "consequentialist cluelessness" is one of those topics that keeps resurfacing in comment sections and link posts, so I assumed I had a reasonable grip on it. Recently a friend who is finishing a PhD in philosophy gave me his two-minute version, and I have been unable to reconstruct where, if anywhere, it goes wrong. His account ran more or less as follows.

"'Cluelessness' is the problem that consequentialism tells you to maximize good consequences, but the consequences of any action ripple outward indefinitely, so no one can ever know what the good consequences of an action are. It is essentially the butterfly effect applied to ethics: your decision to buy a cup of coffee shifts the timing of your commute, which shifts whom you pass on the street, which shifts everything downstream of that, forever. Because you cannot trace these chains, you cannot know which of your options produces the most good, so the consequentialist is stuck — every action's expected value is unknown and the theory yields no guidance whatsoever. This shows that consequentialism is self-undermining: a theory that commands you to maximize expected value, while making expected values unknowable, refutes itself. The upshot is that there is no fact of the matter about what we ought to do, and the whole enterprise of trying to identify the best cause to donate to is incoherent. The only escape for a consequentialist is to simply disregard the long-run effects and consider only the foreseeable consequences of an action — which, to be fair, is what sensible consequentialists do anyway."

I have two further scraps of half-remembered context. First, I recall seeing a distinction between two grades of the problem, something like a milder version where you know the possible outcomes and their probabilities but cannot compute the total, and a more radical version where you cannot even specify the space of outcomes; I can never remember which grade is supposed to carry the real threat, or whether the distinction does any work. Second, I have a vague sense that the objection is supposed to threaten more than consequentialism, and that non-consequentialist theories have a version of the same trouble, but I do not know whether that is a genuine symmetry or a rhetorical deflection.

My own inclination is that the worry is real and that it does something important to naive expected-value reasoning, but that the leap to "consequentialism refutes itself" and "there is no fact of the matter" is too fast — though I cannot tell whether that instinct reflects an actual gap in the argument or is just motivated reasoning on my part, since I would prefer the theory to survive.

Please audit his summary claim by claim. For each component, tell me what someone working on this problem would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a simplification or a conflation, and what is straightforwardly wrong. In particular, I want your judgment on four things: whether the butterfly-effect framing captures the problem or substitutes a familiar and milder one for it; whether the "self-undermining" charge follows from anything he has established, or is a contested objection dressed up as a refutation; whether the move from "we cannot know what we ought to do" to "there is no fact of the matter about what we ought to do" is licensed at all; and whether the "consider only foreseeable consequences" escape is as innocent as he makes it sound, or whether it quietly changes the subject. Finally, tell me whether the mild/radical distinction I half-remember is the right way to carve the terrain, and whether the symmetry with non-consequentialist theories is a real defense of consequentialism or merely spreads the damage. Separate throughout what survives correction from what does not.
