I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I'm recording a long-form interview with a philosopher whose forthcoming book attempts to dissolve the paradox of tragedy — the puzzle of why we voluntarily seek out and take pleasure in artworks that make us sad, frightened, or bereaved.

Her position is more specific than the usual survey of answers, and I want the conversation to engage it rather than replay the classics. She sets aside both the "conversion" tradition, in which some other pleasure (elegance of form, beauty of language, narrative mastery) outweighs or transmutes the painful affect, and the "meta-response" tradition, in which what we enjoy is not the sadness but our own compassionate reaction to it. On her account the sadness is real and genuinely unpleasant, and we do not enjoy it; what we enjoy is complying with a demand. A tragic work, she argues, prescribes how the audience ought to feel from moment to moment — grief here, dread there, relief at the close — and the pleasure peculiar to tragedy is the pleasure of getting the prescription right, of finding one's emotional response fitting. She calls this the fittingness account and insists it is not covert self-congratulation, since the standard of correctness comes from the work and not from the viewer's self-image.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want the questions to press on the account's weak points rather than let her restate it, and I'd like at least one question each on: how a work can be said to prescribe a feeling when audiences disagree or the prescription seems indeterminate; why the pleasure does not fade — and may even grow — on a second or tenth encounter, once the demanded response has already been delivered; how the account avoids making tragic pleasure depend on a flattering view of oneself; whether non-narrative sad art (a lament, a photograph) issues any prescription at all; and what to say about the viewer who simply cannot produce the feeling the work demands, so that the fault might lie with her rather than with the work. Close with a question that forces her to confront the strongest version of the rival she has set aside — the idea that we are enjoying something other than our own correctness.
