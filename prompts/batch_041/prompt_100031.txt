I'm the general counsel for a mid-sized city (about 90,000 people, four lawyers total in our office) and my boss, the city manager, has asked me to write a short internal guide for our licensing and code-enforcement staff. The problem is that our ordinances were drafted by people who died before I was born and then amended piecemeal ever since, so we get a steady stream of calls where the text doesn't obviously answer the question and the clerk just wants someone to tell them yes or no. Right now they email me, I read the section, and I write back something like "I think it covers it," and honestly the way I get there is: I read the words, I try to reconstruct what the council was trying to accomplish, and I split the difference between the two. Thats not a method, thats a mood. They're making maybe thirty of these calls a month, some of them (food trucks, short-term rentals, the e-bike thing on the riverfront trail) have real money and real angry people behind them, and my name goes on the guidance letter when it blows up.

So: I want you to act as an expert in the philosophy of law and the philosophy of language who actually knows the statutory-interpretation literature — textualism, purposivism, intentionalism, the old mischief rule, the absurdity doctrine, canons like ejusdem generis and expressio unius, and the distinction people draw between working out what a text means and deciding how to apply it to something the drafters never imagined — and turn all that into a decision procedure my clerks can run. Two things I care about. First, dont hand me a list of canons in a trench coat. It has to be a sequence they can actually walk through: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates in a clean "covered" or "not covered," and where it hands the call back to a person because whats left is a question about values, or about what we wish the law said rather than what it does. Second, be honest about the theory, because this is the part I actually need: every step in a procedure like this quietly commits you to some view about what it even is for a text to have a meaning. So for each step, tell me which view it presupposes, and what that step would look like under the rival view — if you thought the point was the drafters' intentions, or the purpose, rather than the words on the page. I'd rather have a procedure with a visible seam down the middle than one that pretends the disagreement isn't there. Practical constraints: no legal training, one of my clerks is working in her second language, someone is standing at the counter waiting, so keep it to a page, no 1-to-5 factor weighing, and flag the places where the procedure is just my gut dressed up as a rule.

Then run it on three of our actual messes. The main ordinance, passed 1974, amended twice: "It shall be unlawful to sell or offer for sale any food, beverage, or other merchandise from a vehicle parked on any public street or public sidewalk within the Downtown Overlay District, except during a special event permitted under Chapter 9." Case one: a food truck parked in a private parking lot inside the district, with customers ordering at the window and then standing on the public sidewalk to eat. Case two: an ice cream truck that never really parks — it rolls slowly down the street and stops for two or three minutes at a time with the engine running. Case three: a woman selling bottled water off a card table on the sidewalk, no vehicle at all. Before you write the procedure, spend a few sentences figuring out where the genuinely hard cases are and which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which ones I can afford to lose. And please include at least one result I wont like — my instinct is always to do whatever seems sensible, and I have a suspicion your procedure is going to tell me the sensible thing is not what the ordinance says.
