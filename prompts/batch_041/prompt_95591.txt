I teach an upper-level seminar on medieval political thought — mostly third- and fourth-years, a couple of MA students — and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Aquinas and tyrannicide that can't be answered by reciting the usual two-tyranny line (the usurper vs. the legitimate ruler who goes bad, only the first is fair game). Here's the knot I want them working on.

In the sedition question in the Secunda Secundae, he argues that a tyrannical government isn't just, because it's ordered to the ruler's private good rather than the common good — so disturbing it isn't sedition at all. That reads like a criterion that turns entirely on what the goverment is *for*, not on who's doing the resisting or how the tyrant got there. Then there's the proviso he tacks on, that it isn't sedition to disturb a tyrant's rule unless the upheaval leaves the subjects worse off than the tyranny did — which is a consequence-based qualification and doesn't sit comfortably with the clean common-good criterion. Meanwhile in On Kingship, in the bit where he asks whether it's a great and manly deed to kill the tyrant, the answer seems to turn on something else entirely: a private individual has no business doing it, if there's a superior authority the appeal goes to him, and he throws in the prudential worry that you may trade one tyrant for a worse one. And in his early Sentences commentary he sounds a good deal friendlier to killing the tyrant than he does later, which at least raises the question of whether we're looking at one doctrine or a change of mind.

A colleague has a tidy reading she wants me to build the question around: she says the common-good language is doing no independent work, and that Aquinas's one constant criterion is who holds public authority to act on the community's behalf. She calls it the 'borrowed criterion' reading. I'm not sure I buy it, but its a nice stick to beat them with.

So: write me the essay question (closed book, 90 minutes, no footnotes needed — they can't cite anything anyway), a model answer of about 800 words that actually adjudicates between the readings instead of listing them side by side, and a marking scheme out of 20. In the scheme, name the specific moves a top answer has to make, what each is worth, and for each one a line on how students usually get it wrong. I'd also like it to be clear from the scheme that an answer which just tells me about the two kinds of tyranny tops out around a C, no matter how well written it is.
