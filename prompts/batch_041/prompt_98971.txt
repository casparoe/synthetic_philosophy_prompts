I teach a 90-minute session for about 18 final-year undergrads on a philosophy-and-economics module. They've had a bit of decision theory and basic probability but no formal economics, and most of them assume 'efficient market' just means 'sensible'. I want them to feel the force of the no-trade theorem — the claim that if everyone starts from the same prior, the starting allocation is already efficient, and it's common knowledge that everyone is rational, then there's no speculative trade to be had, so any trade you actually observe means somebody has just been mugged by the person on the other side of it. On the page it reads like a technicality about a corner case. I want them to run into it with their hands.

Design the activity. Rough sketch, ignore it if you've got something better: everyone gets a private signal about the value of some asset, points instead of money, a few minutes to trade, and then we work out why the theorem says they should all have folded their arms. The important bit is that the rules should do the work — by the time I say the word 'theorem' they should already have felt the squeeze, and ideally one or two of them should be annoyed. Then I want a second stage where I quietly hand out different priors (different stories about how the asset is generated) so trade comes roaring back and they can see for themselves which single assumption was carrying all the weight; the debrief should land on the agreeing-to-disagree point here — with a shared prior, two rational agents can't end up with different posteriors, and once you drop the shared prior that whole constraint evaporates. One more wrinkle I'd like somewhere, even if only in the debrief: the result is about speculative trade, not trade in general, so a pure hedging or liquidity motive is a genuine escape hatch, and they should be able to say which of the trades they actually made were of that kind rather than bets.

Give me: rules, materials (nothing that needs real money or a computer lab), the payoff scheme in points, roles if any, a minute-by-minute-ish timeline that fits 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the argument. Then tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it — the confusions, the exploits, the ways it fizzles — and what to do about each. And be honest about which feature of the result the game cannot reproduce and that I'll just have to supply from the front of the room. Keep the whole thing practical enough that I can set it up seperately from my lecture notes and run it cold.

British English throughout, please, and finish with a final line \answer{...} holding a short phrase naming the one assumption the whole activity turns on — the thing that, if I botched it, would leave them with the opposite lesson.
