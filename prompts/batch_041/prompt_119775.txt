I run a small documentation team at a human rights NGO; we interview survivors and witnesses, usually through interpreters, and write the accounts up for lawyers who'll use them in asylum and criminal cases. Our recurring headache is what to do with an account that doesn't hang together — a witness says something we'd call plainly false, or uses a word in a way that makes his next three sentences contradict each other, or insists on something our background theory says can't happen. Right now my interviewers either quietly smooth it over or flag it as an inconsistency that damages credibility, and neither is a decision I can defend to a tribunal.

I want a procedure built on Davidson's picture of radical interpretation — roughly, that you can't fix what someone means separately from attributing beliefs to them, and that interpretation has to start from a large background of agreement. Turn that into an ordered checklist an interviewer with no philosophy training can run on a transcript in under an hour. It needs to cover: the difference between revising the lexicon, revising the logic, and revising what we assume the speaker wants, plus a rule about how many of those three you're allowed to move at once, since in Davidson everything hangs together and I dont want a staffer rewriting a whole account to rescue one sentence; why attributing a false belief should be cheaper than attributing a broken inference; the competing thought that you should attribute the beliefs a speaker could reasonably have reached from her evidence rather than the ones that are true, and which of the two my team should use when they conflict; and how to break the circle of using what someone says to fix what they mean in the first place. It also has to say when to stop and leave something marked as residue instead of interpreting it away, and to write the cost down — every charitable reading costs us something in our own background theory about how the police, the state and the neighbours actually work, and I want that on the page, not hidden.

Then run it on three cases: a witness who says the police came at night when every document says dawn, and who is otherwise consistent; a woman whose word for a kinship relation makes three of her statements mutually inconsistent; and a case where I expect the procedure to tell me to record something as genuine incomprehension when my lawyers would much rather have a clean inconsistency they can attack. Finish with what the procedure can't do and where a person just has to decide.
