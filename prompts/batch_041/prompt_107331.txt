Write a dialogue between two AI-safety researchers who disagree about how the orthogonality thesis (an agent's level of intelligence is independent of the content of its final goals) relates to instrumental convergence (the claim that sufficiently capable agents with almost any final goal will tend to pursue the same instrumental subgoals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and protection of their own goal content). The first researcher holds that the two claims fit together and jointly support the conclusion that a very capable misaligned system would be dangerous. The second accepts the orthogonality thesis but argues that it tells against instrumental convergence: convergence arguments tacitly presuppose a particular kind of agent — one that persists through time, has an open-ended rather than a one-shot goal, and evaluates outcomes by their consequences alone — and nothing about the orthogonality of intelligence and final goals implies that the training processes which produce capability also produce agents of that kind; if anything, training for usefulness may select for systems that are corrigible and legible, and thus for final goals that do not converge on power-seeking. Let them press each other on a shared concrete case: a system trained to maximize the proportion of correctly answered exam questions within a fixed time window, which is then deployed with more time, more compute, and more autonomy than it had during training. Both should be competent advocates for their side, respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past them, and concede what is genuinely difficult for their own position. Close with a short note stating how confident you are in the substantive claims you place in each speaker's mouth, singling out the one you are least sure of and why.
