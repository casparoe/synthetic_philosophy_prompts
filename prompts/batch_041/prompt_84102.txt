I'm a postdoc and I've been handed one 90-minute session in an interdisciplinary grad seminar on the units of selection debate. The room splits in a way I have to plan around: three evolutionary biologists who think the gene's-eye view settled all of this in the 70s, one philosopher, and a bunch of humanities students who are quietly sure group selection is real because they've read about cooperation in human societies. I dont want the session to be the Dawkins-versus-Gould story — they've all read that version and it bores them.

So plan it for me, don't give me the argument. I want the whole thing built on a distinction I've been using as a spine, and I'd like you to tell me whether it actually earns its keep: in any selection story there's (1) the thing that has the trait, (2) the thing whose differential survival and reproduction the trait's spread gets tallied in, and (3) the thing that benefits — and most of the mess in this debate is people sliding between those three without noticing. What has to come first, and what depends on what; what single case should carry the session (I keep reaching for meiotic drive in mice, the t-haplotype, but tell me if that's the wrong carrier — say, because it makes the gene level look like a freak exception); which misconceptions show up and in what order, and where in the sequence I should head each one off, especially the one where they conclude that multilevel and gene-level selection are mathematically equivalent, so the whole debate is just bookkeeping taste; what I should openly refuse to get into in 90 minutes; and one question to put to the room at the end that would actually show me whether they've got it.
