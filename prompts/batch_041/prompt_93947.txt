I sit on the committee of a residents' association that manages a shared access lane, the refuse arrangements and, for the past decade, a levy for coastal defences along our stretch of seafront. Until now membership has been confined to the freeholders of the original nineteen houses, on the strength of a clause in a founding deed that few of us have read, and that deed requires a two-thirds majority of existing members to admit anyone new. Two things have now put the question of who properly belongs to us in play. A block of twelve flats has been built on what was once our communal garden: the occupiers are leaseholders rather than freeholders, they use the lane, and they are liable for the defence levy, yet they have no vote. Meanwhile a small group of houseboat owners has moored more or less permanently along the shore in front of the terrace; they are not ratepayers at all, but the proposed sea wall would either shelter their moorings or, under the engineers' alternative design, leave them exposed to precisely the flooding the wall is meant to hold back.

The committee is now deadlocked over that sea wall, which would protect the properties at the northern end — mine among them — while, on the engineers' preferred design, worsening erosion at the southern end and spoiling the view from the flats. The membership is split almost evenly, and we can all do the arithmetic: admitting the flat-dwellers, who overwhelmingly favour the rival design, would carry the day against the northern end, and admitting the houseboat owners would tilt it further still. Some of my neighbours therefore insist that the membership question must be settled first, by a vote of the existing members, before the sea wall is put to any vote at all; others retort that people who pay the levy and live with the consequences are being taxed without a say, and that a body which simply defines its own membership can never be trusted to decide who is affected by what it does. I would like your advice on how the committee ought to proceed — who, if anyone, has a claim to a vote, whether it matters that the newcomers would be voting on a dispute that arose before most of them arrived, and how far I should discount my own judgment given that my house is one of those the northern design would save. I am less interested in a general sermon about inclusion than in how these particular claims sort out, and what would be the fairest way to constitute the body that is to decide.
