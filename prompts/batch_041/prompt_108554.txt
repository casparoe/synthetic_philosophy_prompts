My two housemates Ari and Bea have been going around in circles for a week about Ari's decision to stop flying, and it all kicked off over one specific trip — a cousin's wedding in Spain in June. I was in the room for most of it and I still coudln't tell you who's winning. Can you adjudicate it move by move? Here's roughly how it went.

Ari: I'm not going. That round trip is a good fraction of a tonne of CO2 with my name on it and I'm not putting it in the air for a party.

Bea: That plane is going to take off whether you're in seat 14C or not. The airline isn't adding a plane for you, the schedule was set months ago. Your not preventing anything — the emissions are attached to the flight, not to your name on the manifest. All you've done is give up a wedding.

Ari: That's not right. Airlines aren't a force of nature, they decide routes and frequencies based on how many tickets they sell. My seat moves the load factor by a rounding error, and rounding errors tip decisions — routes get cut when the loads sag, frequencies get trimmed. So it isn't "the emissions happen either way", it's "there's a small chance my seat is the straw that breaks a route's back". Small probability times a big harm is still real expected harm. Weak reason to stay home, fine, but not nothing.

Bea: If you're going to play that game you have to play it properly. Tiny probability times enormous harm is how people talk themselves into thinking every trivial choice is momentous — it proves way too much. And here it doesn't even get off the ground, because if you don't buy the seat the airline sells it to someone else. There's a standby list; if the flight isn't full they discount it until it is. So the plane flies full either way and the only thing you change is whose name is on the manifest — and the person who takes your seat might be someone who'd otherwise have flown a longer route, so the substitution could even cut the other way. Call it the ghost seat: the emissions belong to the flight, not to the passenger.

Ari: Two problems with that. First, you're assuming the substitution always happens. Whether the seat fills depends on how elastic demand is on that route at that price — on plenty of flights the seat just goes empty, and then my choice is the difference between one more passenger's worth of fuel being burned or not. You've turned an empirical question into a convenient assumption. Second, and this is the part I can't let go of: "I didn't change the total" doesn't settle whether it's wrong. Imagine ten of us each buy a seat on a flight that only gets scheduled because ten people bought seats. The flight happens, and if any one of us had stayed home it would have happened anyway — so by your logic every one of us gets to say "my seat didn't matter". And yet no ten passengers, no flight. You can't dissolve a collective action by pointing at the members one at a time; that's like saying no single voter decides an election, so voters don't decide elections.

Bea: That's the move I don't buy. The ten-passenger story shows the set of you made the flight happen. It doesn't show what any one of you did wrong, because any one of you could have dropped out and the flight would still have gone. If you want to say each of you acted wrongly you need an argument for why an act is wrong when it changes nothing — and "it's part of a set that changed something" is exactly the thing that needs arguing, not the thing you get to assume. And notice the case you built isn't the case you're in. Yours is a four-hundred-seat flight that's already scheduled and already mostly sold. If your argument only bites in the ten-passenger case, it doesn't bite on your trip.

Ari: Then maybe your whole frame is wrong. You keep assuming the only reason not to do a thing is that it makes the world go worse. I'm not sure I accept that. There's a difference between being someone who pays for a thing and someone who merely benefits from it, and I don't need my ticket to be the cause of anything for that difference to matter to me. If a hundred of us each chip in a small amount to a fund that ruins someone's life, "your contribution was marginal" isn't an answer to "why did you chip in?"

Bea: Now you've changed the subject. You told me you were staying home because of the emissions. If the real reason is that you don't want to be the sort of person who pays, that might be a fine reason for you, but it's not a claim about what the flight does — and it proves too much, because by that standard you shouldn't have got on the bus this morning either; the bus ran whether or not you were on it and your fare went into the same kind of pot. Either the reason is about consequences, in which case the substitution point matters, or it's about something else, in which case we're not having the same argument anymore.

So they asked me to settle it, and I said I'd think about it, which I haven't. I'd like you to go through the moves one at a time — mark each one right / mostly right / half right / mostly wrong / wrong, and say in a sentence or two why. If a move is genuinely a wash say so, but don't rate everything the same to play it safe. Then say what each of them is missing that neither has noticed, and whether anything in the exchange turns on a premise either of them would reject if it were spelled out. Don't just pick a winner and wave at the rest. Then finish with a last line that reads exactly "Verdict: X" where X is Ari, Bea, or draw, and nothing after it.
