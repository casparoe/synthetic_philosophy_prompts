Write a short story of roughly 1,500–2,000 words that stages the epistemological problem of bootstrapping and the threat of "easy knowledge." The core of the problem is this: a person relies on some belief-forming faculty—perception, memory, testimony, or an acquired expert skill—and would like to know that the faculty is reliable, yet every test she can devise either presupposes the reliability of the very faculty in question or delivers a verdict so cheap that it cannot be worth much. I want the circularity to be felt rather than explained.

Set the story in a recognizable near-present and center it on a single protagonist with a single, specific epistemic predicament. Make the faculty concrete and embodied—a taster's palate, a radiologist's trained eye, a piano tuner's ear, a reader of micro-expressions—rather than "reason" in the abstract. Let the plot turn on one particular attempt to break the circle, and describe the procedure in enough detail that a reader could reconstruct the character's reasoning step by step. Include three beats: first, a scene in which she constructs what looks like an independent check on the faculty; second, a moment in which she realizes that the check's own standards of correctness were fixed by the faculty now under suspicion; third, a decision, defended in her own words, about whether to keep trusting the faculty anyway—and what, exactly, she thinks she is thereby entitled to believe.

The story should distinguish, even if the character never uses the terms, between two different things she might want: being entitled to rely on the faculty, and being able to justify the claim that the faculty is reliable. Let the story show which of these she actually needs and whether she can get it. At some point she should notice that if her reasoning worked, it would let her know far too many things far too cheaply, and should reckon with what that tells her. Do not turn the story into an allegory with philosophers as characters, and do not end with a lecture; the ideas should live in the situation, the dialogue, and the character's choices.
