I sit on the working group that revises our national diagnostic manual, and one of our standing advisers is a philosopher of psychiatry — call her Dr Aldworth — whose published verdicts on individual conditions I have followed for years. The question she poses in each case is whether the condition is an interactive, looping kind in the sense of Ian Hacking's work on how classifying people can remake the people classified, who then remake the classification in turn, or an indifferent kind, one that records a fact about people without becoming a moving target. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it, runs as follows.

- A possession-and-trance syndrome that spread through several communities after heavy media coverage, with sufferers enacting the symptoms they had read about: interactive.
- A common, non-stigmatised physiological trait, stable in prevalence across cultures, that people neither notice nor organise around: indifferent.
- A heavily stigmatised condition whose sufferers built a patient movement, reclaimed the label, and negotiated the diagnostic criteria with clinicians: interactive.
- A neutral trait, neither prized nor shamed, that individuals nonetheless know about and actively plan their lives around, but around which no collective identity has ever formed: indifferent.
- A chronic condition that carries no evident evaluative charge and that its sufferers mostly ignore, with no community and no advocacy: indifferent.

There is now a proposal before us for a new category. The candidate condition is a syndrome of severe distress and incapacity that follows an ordinary misfortune — protracted unemployment, a humiliating professional failure, the collapse of a business — rather than any injury or bereavement. It is normatively charged from every direction: clinicians and the public alike read it as a failure of character, sufferers are suspected of malingering or of lacking grit, and the diagnosis is contested precisely because it appears to excuse what many believe should be blamed. Sufferers are acutely aware of the label, but they conceal it; the condition is isolating and shameful, and no patient movement, advocacy organisation, or shared identity has formed around it, nor have the criteria been negotiated with any such group.

Predict Dr Aldworth's verdict on this new case. I am far less interested in the verdict than in the rule she appears to be running: set out the candidate rules that fit her five earlier verdicts equally well, state what each predicts here, commit to the one you think she is genuinely using and explain why, and name the further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. If her track record truly underdetermines the verdict, say so plainly, and tell me which rule our working group would be adopting if we simply follow her.

Give me a committed answer: a definite prediction and a definite choice of rule, defended, not a survey of the options with the decision left to me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the predicted verdict and the rule you take her to be running — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
