I am drafting a short position paper on the ethics of panpsychism and would like a consistency audit before I circulate it to a reading group. Here is where I have landed, stated as flatly as I can.

1. Experiential properties are fundamental and close to ubiquitous. Whatever intrinsic nature realizes the physical, it is phenomenal in character, so any physical system organized in the right way has some degree of experience. I hold this in the spirit of Russellian monism, not as the claim that electrons deliberate.

2. The badness of suffering is intrinsic to its felt character. It does not derive from the sophistication, the self-awareness, or even the unity of the being that has it. A brief, mindless ache is bad in proportion to its intensity and duration, and for no further reason.

3. Only subjects can be harmed. For a thing to be made worse off, it must be a unified locus of experience — there must be a single subjective perspective that the thing has, at the time, as one thing.

4. The intrinsic properties that panpsychism assigns to fundamental entities are valence-neutral. Physics tells us about structure and dynamics, and it tells us nothing about whether any given micro-experience is pleasant, painful, or blank.

5. There is no privileged level of description. If the micro-experiences of a system are arranged in the right way, the resulting macro-experience is genuinely there; it needs no further fact to combine the parts, and the micro-experiences need not be aware of one another.

6. I want to be a moral realist. There are facts about which experiences are bad and about how bad they are, and these facts are not merely projections of our attitudes.

What keeps tripping me up, and the reason I am asking, is a case I have been calling the flicker: a single fundamental entity, out in empty space, that undergoes one interaction and — on this picture — one momentary scrap of negatively valenced experience, and then never figures in any larger organized system. My gut delivers two verdicts I cannot square. The first is that if the flicker really is negatively valenced, then it is bad, full stop. The second is that it is nonetheless nothing: not a wrong, not a harm, not something any moral accounting should register.

Please audit the six commitments. Tell me which ones actually pull against each other and how severely, ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad; tell me which pairs only look like they conflict and why the appearance is misleading; and for each genuine tension say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. I would also like to know which single commitment, if I surrendered it, would resolve the most, and what I would be giving up in surrendering it. Finally, say what the resulting position implies about the flicker — in particular, whether the two verdicts in my gut can both survive, and if not, which one the rest of the position forces me to abandon.
