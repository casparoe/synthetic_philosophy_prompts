Ok, I need you to try to break a view I've ended up defending about Buridan cases. The view is this: when the options are genuinely symetric -- equal in every respect the agent cares about -- there's no rational requirement to pick, and an agent who just stands there isn't irrational. The ass puzzle only looks like a paradox because we smuggle in a cost; deliberating takes time and the agent has an interest in getting on with things. Strip out that cost and the supposed requirement to choose just evaporates.

So: what would count against this? Not the lazy "but then you'd starve" reply, I know that one. I want the sharper stuff. Are there cases where a requirement to choose shows up even with zero deliberation cost and no preference at all? Can you get a genuine requirement out of agency itself -- the thought that being a chooser commits you to choosing, or that the act of choosing is itself a value-relevant event that breaks the tie from the inside -- and if so does that argument actually bite or is it just a pun on the word "commit"? What about the money-pump / Dutch-book arguments that say incomplete preferences leave you open to exploitation: do they actually need the agent to prefer something, or can they run on pure symmetry? And does the view predict anything its rivals don't, or does it just redescribe the case in nicer words?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones a defender like me should just concede and which I can wave off, and be honest about whether the whole thing is definately unfalsifiable -- if the only way to generate a requirement is to add a cost, and real life always has some cost, is the view doing any work at all? The agency line is the one that worries me most, so spend some time on it.
