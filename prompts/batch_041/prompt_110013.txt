I was a bystander to the following disagreement and could not resolve it, so I would like a careful adjudication rather than a verdict on who "won." Please address each of the disputants' claims in turn.

The case. Our reading group was discussing a variant on the familiar Twin Earth thought experiment (the one on which the twins' liquid is XYZ rather than H2O). The variant, which we called the Migration case, runs as follows. A civilization indistinguishable from ours lived for many centuries on a planet — call it Old Earth — whose oceans, lakes, and rain were composed of XYZ, the liquid that is superficially indistinguishable from H2O. Their word "water" was introduced in that setting and was used, generation after generation, for the stuff in the lakes and the rain. Five hundred years ago the entire civilization migrated, with unbroken historical records and no interruption in the transmission of the language, to a new planet — call it New Earth — whose oceans, lakes, and rain are composed of H2O, exactly as on our Earth. The migrants' descendants have lived there ever since. They have never encountered XYZ again; they do not know that their ancestral substance differed from the current one; and their chemistry is correct about the substance in front of them. Suppose further that New Earth already had an indigenous population, which had independently introduced a word, homophonically translated as "water," for the H2O in the very same lakes.

The exchange.

(1) Ana: Meaning is fixed at the point of introduction and then carried along the chain of transmission. "Water" was introduced on Old Earth as a name for XYZ, and nothing in the subsequent history — not migration, not the change of environment — breaks that chain. So when a present-day New Earthling says "water," she means XYZ, even though she has never encountered any and could not distinguish it from H2O. It follows that her beliefs about the liquid in her lakes are, strictly speaking, false, and that she and the indigenous New Earthlings, though they drink from the same lakes and use the same word, do not mean the same thing. Externalism has always had counterintuitive consequences; this is one more.

(2) Ben: That gets the thought experiment backwards. Its lesson was never that reference is bequeathed by history; it was that the extension of a natural kind term is fixed by the actual stuff the community interacts with, through the environment and the division of linguistic labor. On the original Twin Earth, the twins' word meant XYZ because the stuff around them was XYZ. Here the stuff around them is H2O. So present-day New Earthlings mean H2O — the same thing the indigenous population means — and their beliefs are mostly true. On Ana's view, "water" would name a substance that no living speaker, expert or otherwise, has ever encountered or could identify, so the community would be deferring to an authority with nothing to defer to. That is not externalism; that is a word cut loose from the world.

(3) Ana: Your view requires meaning to have changed silently at the instant of migration. No speaker's psychology changed, no one decided anything, no record marks it. If meaning can change without any trace in the minds or practices of speakers, why may it not stay fixed without any trace? And the change you posit is stranger than you allow: a word would migrate from one substance to another while keeping its spelling, its pronunciation, and its entire use, quietly switching referents. At least the historical-chain view can say what did the fixing — an event.

(4) Ben: Silent change is no objection. The original case already makes meaning differ between two people whose psychologies are identical, so externalism never promised that meaning tracks anything introspectively available. And I can say exactly what fixes reference: the causal-functional relation between the community and the stuff it encounters, together with the experts' findings about that stuff. On New Earth the experts examine the lakes and get H2O, and that is what the community's term is answerable to. Your view makes the term answerable to an event five hundred years in the past that no one remembers and that no present practice could correct, so no evidence could ever reveal to these people what their own word means.

(5) Ana: You keep saying the term is answerable to the stuff the community encounters, but the word was not introduced to name the stuff it now encounters; it was introduced to name the stuff in the ancestral lakes, and it has been used throughout under a presupposition of continuity. If your view is right, then a single word, used continuously by a single community, referred to XYZ for centuries and to H2O thereafter, and the community never noticed that one of its central terms had changed its subject matter.

Please adjudicate. I would like you to:

(a) Rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating;
(b) Say whether Ana and Ben are defending the same thesis about reference-fixing or two different ones, and which is the better reading of externalism;
(c) State what present-day New Earthlings mean by "water" on the case as described — XYZ, H2O, neither, or something the description leaves open — and identify exactly which further facts the answer turns on;
(d) Assess whether Ana's "silent change" argument in (3) and (5) is sound, and whether Ben's reply in (4) defeats it;
(e) Say whether the migrants' descendants and the indigenous population mean the same thing, and whether that consequence tells for or against either position;
(f) Explain what, if anything, both parties are missing.

Please write your answer in American English.
