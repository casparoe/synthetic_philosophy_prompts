I direct ethics and grantmaking at a small Buddhist-founded foundation that distributes quarterly grants for humanitarian work. Our board charter commits us to putting compassion first, but it deliberately does not say whether that means maximizing the relief of suffering or holding to the precepts, and our five-person committee is split on the point. The Theravāda members treat the five precepts as near-absolute constraints, and read the tradition's claim that intention is what makes an action karmically decisive as ruling out consequentialist trade-offs; the Mahāyāna members invoke skillful means, and the bodhisattva's willingness to shoulder karmic burden for others' sake, to allow exceptions when the outcome is large enough. None of us is a trained philosopher, we decide in a two-hour meeting, and we have to record a written rationale that both factions will accept as having been applied in good faith. We keep discovering, after the fact, that we have been sliding between two different questions — how much suffering will this relieve, and what will this do to the intentions and dispositions of everyone involved — without noticing.

I want a decision procedure the committee can run on each proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a vote because the question is one of values rather than facts. Please build it around three registers and say explicitly how a proposal can be defeated by each: (1) the outcome register — expected suffering relieved and harm done, including effects far in the future; (2) the karmic register — the effect on the intentions and character of the actors, the foundation's own staff and donors included, since the tradition holds that the state of mind behind an act matters as much as the act's results, and that consequences are said to ripen over spans longer than a lifetime; and (3) the precepts, treated as side-constraints rather than as one more quantity to be traded off. The procedure has to say whether any register has lexical priority, and what to do when the two factions read the precepts differently — in particular whether a disagreement about whether skillful means can license an exception is a factual dispute the procedure can settle or a values dispute it must hand off. It also has to say how to reason at all when the relevant consequences are said to extend across lifetimes no one can estimate, and it should address, at least briefly, whether the no-self teaching undermines the whole exercise of adding up benefits across separate people, or whether conventional-level calculation remains the right thing to do. Keep it usable by people who know the tradition but not the academic literature, and do not ask us to consult sources.

Then run it, step by step, on three proposals: a program with the highest expected suffering-reduction of anything on the table that would require us to spread a deliberately misleading claim about a treatment in order to overcome public hesitation; a contemplative-care program whose benefits we cannot measure at all, which its applicants defend on the ground that it builds the long-run dispositions of everyone it touches; and a partnership that would relieve a great deal of suffering but only by working through a local authority we know will use some of the money coercively. Make the procedure give a clear answer in each case, and let at least one of those answers be one I will not like. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
