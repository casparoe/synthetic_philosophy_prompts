I am a doctoral candidate in cognitive science, and I have been asked to run a session at our department’s annual retreat on the theme of mechanism and agency. My assigned topic is sphexishness—the tendency of an agent to run a fixed routine even after the situation that made the routine appropriate has changed—and it has become unexpectedly personal because my own work on habit learning keeps being summarized by colleagues as showing that human beings are just wasps with more neurons. I want to prepare a classification instrument that will let the group argue about the underlying philosophical positions without first having to agree on labels or on the free will literature.

Please write a decision tree, flowchart, or taxonomy of the positions on whether sphexishness is real, how far it extends, and what it implies for agency and free will. The tree should start from a concrete case—I want the first branch to be the familiar wasp-and-cricket loop, where the wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance to inspect the burrow, and if the cricket is moved while she is inside, retrieves it and repeats the inspection—and then move through questions that a graduate student could answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to. I want the tree built from three separable axes rather than from a list of names. First, the mechanism axis: is the behavior produced by a fixed trigger-response chain, or by an internal model that can be updated when the world changes? Second, the reasons axis: can the agent represent the reason for its behavior and revise the behavior when that reason fails, or is the behavior merely caused by a state that happens to track the reason? Third, the attribution axis: is the behavior attributable to the agent at all, or only to a subpersonal mechanism that the agent is not? Each branch node should be one of these axes or a concrete case that tests it, and each leaf should carry the name of a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I need the tree to be able to reach at least the following positions, though you may add others if the logical space demands it: hard sphexishness, on which there is no agency anywhere, only more or less complex loops; compatibilist reasons-responsiveness, on which flexibility is real but does not require libertarian free will; libertarian agent causation, on which at least some agents originate action in a way no mechanism can; illusionism, on which the experience of flexibility is itself part of the loop; interpretivism, on which agency is a stance we take toward a system rather than a fact about its machinery; and a deflationary scalar view, on which sphexishness comes in degrees and the binary question is the mistake. The instrument should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question should simply ask whether someone believes in free will or whether they are a compatibilist. Please note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and diagnose whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. The audience is a mixed group of cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind who know the wasp story but not the free will literature, and the instrument should be usable in a ninety-minute session, so aim for roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and six to nine leaves. No citations or reading lists are needed; the value is in the diagnostic questions and the map of the logical space.
