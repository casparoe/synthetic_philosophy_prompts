Ok so I'm trying to write a short paper and I keep going in circles on it, wanted to get some comments before I embarass myself. Don't send me reading lists or citations, I know the literature well enough, I need someone to poke holes in the actual argument.

The target is the Principal Principle -- the claim that if you know the objective chance of A is x, your credence in A should be x. I know all the usual surrounding stuff (Humean chances, undermining, the best-system business). What I want to argue is a bit different and I'm not sure it survives a competent reader.

Here's the argument. The PP is a principle of deference. Deferring to something means treating its say-so as a reason to move your own credence. Now every other deference principle we have -- deferring to a better-informed expert, deferring to your future self with more evidence, deferring to the majority of your epistemic peers -- is justified instrumentally, if at all: you defer because the target tends to be right, and in principle you could audit that. Call that thick deference, the authority comes from a track record you could check.

But the PP isn't like that. We don't defer to chance because chancy processes have a good track record of predicting themselves; we defer because chance just is, by stipulation, the thing credence ought to track. So the PP is thin deference: authority by definition, no audit possible. And then the dilemma. Either thin deference is a genuine species of deference, in which case anything at all can be made a proper object of thin deference just by stipulating that credence should track it (defer to the number of letters in your name, defer to whatever the mirror says) -- or thin deference isnt deference at all, and the PP is really a disguised definition of chance, in which case it tells us nothing about what we ought to believe, only about how the word gets used. I can't see a third option.

To make it vivid, heres a case I've been calling the mirror world. In the mirror world the chance of E is defined as whatever credence a perfectly rational agent with exactly your evidence would have in E. The PP holds there trivially and perfectly. So in the mirror world you should defer to chance -- but chance is just your own rational credence, so you're deferring to yourself and the PP is doing no work. I take that to show satisfying the PP is cheap and can't be what makes a chance function special.

What I want from you:

1. Is the thin/thick distinction doing real work, or is it just the familiar instrumental/non-instrumental justification split wearing a new coat? If it collapses, does the dilemma go with it?
2. Is the dilemma exhaustive? The move I keep half-seeing is that the PP is neither a deference principle nor a definition but a constitutive norm -- something you only count as having a credence at all if you'd defer to the chances. Does that dodge the horns or just relabel the second one?
3. Is the mirror world case actually coherent? My worry is that "perfectly rational agent with exactly your evidence" is doing something illicit there, since in the mirror world the fact that the chance equals your rational credence is itself evidence, and maybe that makes the definition unstable or self-undermining in a way that rescues the PP.
4. And the obvious fix I'm suspicious of: someone will say just replace "set your credence equal to the chance" with "condition on the proposition that the chance is x." I have a feeling that changes the subject rather than the substance, but tell me if its a genuine repair.

Be blunt about where this falls apart, I'd rather find out now than in review.

Last line: give me a verdict on whether the dilemma is exhaustive, as a final line of the form \verdict{...} with a short phrase, e.g. \verdict{exhaustive, take horn 2} or \verdict{false dilemma, gap is here}.
