I'm the TA for a fourth-year seminar in normative ethics, and I've been handed the week on promising to run as a discussion rather than a lecture. My trouble is that every handout I can find does the same thing: it lines up the usual suspects — the practice conception, the derivation of ought from is out of the constitutive rules of the speech act, the assurance view, joint commitment, the fidelity and trust views — and the students come away thinking the debate is a beauty contest between six or seven authors rather than a space of live options they might already occupy. A friend I've been arguing with insists the whole thing collapses into the practice conception, and I'd like an instrument that shows him, case by case, exactly where he has to get off that bus. So what I want is a decision tree I can photocopy and hand out, which students work down individually in the first twenty minutes and which routes each of them from a starting commitment to a named position on why promises bind. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the positions located in that space. Aim for between eight and fourteen leaves, and let me know if that range forces you to fuse positions that deserve to be kept apart.

The first-pass questions I would use to carve the space — add, drop, or subdivide as the space itself demands — are roughly these. Does the bindingness of a promise depend on a practice of promising already existing in the community, so that the words I promise, uttered by two people with no such convention between them, would do nothing at all? Is you promised a reason that counts in favour of the promised act whatever the act is, or does it merely add weight to reasons the promisor already had to do that act? Is the obligation owed to the promisee — a claim of hers, which she can waive and which she can invoke even if she is never harmed and never learns of the breach — or is it a reason about her that is owed to no one in particular? Is its ground the promisee's reliance or assurance, the promisor's own authority over her future conduct or her interest in integrity, or something impersonal such as the utility of the practice? And does the promisor's later change of heart about the practice itself count as a release? I care especially about keeping the question of bindingness (does the promise give any reason at all?) separate from the question of directedness (does it give the promisee a claim?), because most textbook treatments blur them and half the apparent disagreements dissolve once they're separated. Whatever tree you build should be able to route someone through at least these cases: the desert-island pair with no convention of promising who say the words anyway; the secret promise broken harmlessly and never discovered; the promise to do something the promisor had no antecedent reason to want done at all, which I take to be the sharpest test of content-independence; the promisee who releases the promisor, or dies, or forgets; the speaker who says I promise while explicitly disavowing the practice; and the promise that conflicts with a serious but not catastrophic good, so we can see whether the position treats the obligation as one reason among others or as something with a special resistance to being outweighed.

Please phrase every branch node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to — no philosopher's names and no -isms in the branch labels — and name each leaf with whatever label is standard if one exists, or a plain descriptive phrase if not; I don't need citations or a reading list, that isn't the point. Finish with two short sections. The first should flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate and say why, rather than papering over the overlap. The second should list the answer combinations that turn out to be unoccupied, with a one-line diagnosis of each — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered? If the tree leaves at least two cells empty that someone could actually defend, sketch what a defender of each would have to say about the secret harmless breach, and label those leaves as unnamed.
