Last week in a seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, a colleague shut down a discussion by observing that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree'. The occasion was two forecasters who had seen the same data, shared the same background theory, and talked the matter through at length, yet continued to diverge; my colleague took the slogan to show that at least one of them must be irrational, and that there was consequently nothing further to explain. I doubt the slogan can bear that weight, but I cannot put my finger on where it slips. Give me a menu of the precise claims that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree' could be making, ordered from the platitudinous through the substantive to the false, and for each say whether it is defensible and whether it would actually license the inference my colleague drew. I am especially interested in the edges of the claim: whether it is a claim about what agents' credences must be, about what they may rationally do, or merely about what they may rationally say; whether it is supposed to bite only once the disagreement has become common knowledge, and what it would take for that condition to be met rather than merely approximated; and what becomes of it when the agents do not begin from a shared prior, or carve up the possibilities differently, or are unsure of one another's reasoning. Where the slogan is a compressed rendering of a real result, spell out the exact conditions under which the result holds, and give me one case that honours the spirit of the slogan without meeting its letter, so that I can work out which reading my colleague was relying on.
