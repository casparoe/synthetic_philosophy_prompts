I'm a second-year PhD student (philosphy, though I work mostly on decision theory and my supevisor keeps dragging me into medical ethics), and I'm trying to write something about a mismatch I can't stop noticing. The background: there's a standard result in Bayesian decision theory, usually credited to I. J. Good, that free information can never hurt you in expectation. The argument is almost embarrassing in how simple it is: whatever you would have done without looking, you can still do after you look, so the option set only grows and your expected utility can only go up or stay flat. It holds as long as the info is genuinely free, you update properly (i.e. you condition), and what you care about is the world -- the state of things and the act you take -- rather than the state of your own head.

Now the practice I'm stuck on. In clinical genetics, when someone gets sequenced, the standard move is to sit them down beforehand and ask whether they want to be told about "incidental" or "secondary" findings -- things nobody went looking for that might suggest a future illness you could maybe do something about. You can say no. You can say don't tell me, and the lab just... won't. There's a name for it, the right not to know, and it shows up in consent forms and declarations and is treated as obviously a good thing. But the same health systems that will bend over backwards not to tell you something will take blood from every newborn in the country and test it for a panel of disorders, and in some places you can't opt out of that at all. And the same institutions now push the other way, insisting that researchers who sequence you have a duty to hand back findings that are "actionable." Meanwhile some countries flat out ban telling parents the sex of the fetus -- not because knowing is bad for the parents, but because knowing leads to choices we don't want them making. And in a lot of places we forbid insurers from using your genetic data, which reads like the opposite verdict: the information is so potent it has to be kept out of the market. So we withhold, we mandate, we forbid, we require -- sometimes about the same information, inside the same system.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher who actually knows both the decision theory and the medical ethics literature, and to take this slowly: brainstorm out loud first about what each practice could possibly presuppose before you commit to anything. Concretely -- what philosophical commitments does the right not to know presuppose? Does it need utility to be belief-dependent, i.e. does it need dread, or peace of mind, or the sheer state of knowing, to be a good or a bad in its own right, so that the theorem's premise quietly fails? Or does it need the agent not to be an expected utility maximizer at all -- someone who'll overreact, spiral, act against their own interest, so that being told leaves them worse off even by their own lights? Or is it something else entirely, like a view that information is property you own rather than a good you want, in which case the justification is autonomy and not welfare at all? Then separate the two things I keep conflating: (i) whether I find out something about myself, and (ii) whether someone else finds out something about me. The theorem is about the first and most of these practices are about the second, and I suspect that's where a lot of my confusion lives. Tell me which commitments the practice strictly needs as opposed to merely fits comfortably with, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where it's underdetermined so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same consent form. And if the presupposed view turned out to be false -- if belief-dependent utility is a mistake, say -- what would actually have to change on the ground? Would the right not to know have to go, or could it survive on autonomy grounds alone? Please don't hand me a reading list, I can find the literature myself; I want the reconstruction and the argument, and I want you to be honest about which parts are your reconstruction and which are things the practitioners would actually say.
