Ok so I'm a third-year PhD in comparative philosophy, mostly Jain epistemology, and I have to circulate a "here's where I stand" doc to my reading group next week. Before I do I want to know if it's a house of cards. Audit it for me. Here's what I hold:

1. Anekāntavāda / syādvāda — every assertion is true only in some sense (syād), so something like "the world is eternal" and "the world is non-eternal" are each conditionally true and neither just wins. I take this to be about reality and not just about how we talk: things really do have endless aspects, and a predication's truth tracks which aspect you're picking out.
2. There are mind-independent facts. The Jains are realists and thats part of the appeal for me, so "conditionally true" is not a sneaky way of saying "up to us" or "just a perspective."
3. The seven-fold schema itself — the machinery that cycles affirmation, denial, and both to spit out the seven predications — isn't up for grabs. The law of non-contradiction in particular is what lets us enumerate those seven options at all, so it has to stay fixed while everything else goes perspectival.
4. Kevala-jñāna: the omniscient being cognizes all aspects at once, and that cognition isn't from a standpoint — it's the one bit of knowledge that isn't partial.
5. Honestly though, some days I think the whole "many-sided" thing is just a polite way of saying everyone's partly right and partly wrong, and the elaborate aspect-metaphysics is decoration.

So where is this actually inconsistent, and how much does each clash cost me? Rank the problems by how bad they really are rather than dumping them all as equally fatal, tell me if any pair is only an apparent clash (I have a hunch one is, but I keep flip-flopping on which), and then give me the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most, plus what I'd be giving up by making it. I don't know which of the five I'd actually drop, so don't assume I'm attached to any of them.
