I've been following the presentism-vs-contextualism fights in history for a couple years now and I'm pretty sure the two people in my reading group who've been argueing about it since forever have mostly been arguing past each other. Write the joint statement they should have produced if they'd done a real adversarial collaboration — the document both of them could actually put their names on. I want it as: a numbered section of agreed claims, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with each side's prediction, then a short note on what (if anything) was just a verbal dispute.

Setup so the two positions are pinned down. The contextualist: you have to understand past people on their own terms and judge them by the standards available to them, and importing our moral and conceptual vocabulary is anachronism at best and self-flattery at worst. The presentist: that's a fantasy — the questions we ask, the categories we carve the past with, and the judgments we make are downstream of where we stand, and pretending otherwise just hides the values in the footnotes. Both are smart, both keep winning individual exchanges, which is the tell. Steelman both, then make them sign the same page.

Specific things I want in there, because I don't want a generic essay about how both sides have a point:

- Split presentism into at least three different things and say, for each, whether the contextualist is really denying it: (a) judging past acts and institutions by present moral standards; (b) choosing which bits of the past to study because of present concerns; (c) using present-day categories to carve up the past at all — is "the economy," "religion," "sexuality," "the state" something they had, or something we're imposing? My hunch is the contextualist happily grants (b), that the loud fight is about (a), and that the real fight is (c). If the hunch is wrong, say so and say why.

- "The standards of their own time" sounds tidy until you notice societies aren't homogeneous. So: whose standards? Handle two cases concretely. First, the dominant practice was condemned by a minority inside that society, using premises basically everyone there accepted — does that make the practice wrong by their own standards, and does the contextualist then have to condemn it? Second, harder: a practice nobody in that society had the conceptual resources to see as a problem at all. Both of them should pre-register what they predict about each case rather than hash it out after the fact.

- The self-undermining / symmetry thing. We're all confident future historians will find something we do now monstrous and obvious. Does the presentist's story about standards being local undercut the confidence in the judgments they want to make? And does the contextualist's view commit them to saying our descendants are making a category error when they condemn us? Each answer phrased so the other side would accept that it's a fair statement of their view.

- A practical test both would agree bears on it: a museum or a public inquiry asks them how to present a past atrocity. Do their methods actually diverge in the advice, or do they converge once it's concrete? Write each side's prediction down in advance.

- Finally the verbal-dispute bit: is any of this two people using the same word for different things, or a fight about tone and rhetoric rather than method? If some chunk is verbal, say which chunk, and what's left once you strip it out.

Rules. Nothing vacuous — "context matters" and "we should be careful" are not agreed claims. Nothing slanted — each side's own wording has to be something that side would actually sign. No citations, no reading lists: neither of them can agree on the literature anyway and I want the argument, not a bibliography, so if you need to name a position just describe it in plain words.
