Compare two conceptions of what it is for one person to bear the punishment that another deserves.

On the first conception, which we may call transfer of penalty, punishment is a suffering that the offender owes by desert, and the substitute simply undergoes that suffering in his place, so that the penalty itself is transferred from the guilty to the innocent. On the second conception, transfer of liability, what the offender owes is a debt or liability, and the substitute discharges it on his behalf, as when one person pays another's fine.

David Lewis once posed a puzzle that turns on the difference between these two. If a burglar has been justly sentenced to imprisonment, or a murderer to death, we would not regard the volunteering of an innocent friend to serve the sentence as any reason at all to spare the offender; the authorities would decline the offer, and were the substitution discovered afterward they would simply carry out the original sentence. Yet when the sentence is a fine, we take it to be perfectly in order that a friend pay it and the offender go free; and a fine, Lewis observed, is no less a punishment than a prison term. He concluded that we are of two minds about penal substitution: we reject it for custodial and capital punishment but accept it for fines, and he pressed the question whether, in the religious case, anyone really believes in penal substitution as an account of the atonement, or whether what is believed is something weaker.

Using the contrast between these two conceptions, and the distinction between punishments that can and cannot be transferred, address the following. First, is the difference between the fine case and the prison case a difference in the nature of the punishment, a difference in the nature of what is owed, or merely a legal convention about who is permitted to discharge an obligation? Second, does the expressive or condemnatory character of punishment -- the thought that punishment is not merely the infliction of suffering but the expression of a community's condemnation of the offender -- explain why penalty resists transfer, and if so, what does that imply for the fine case, where transfer seems unproblematic? Third, if the transfer-of-liability model is the only fully coherent one, what becomes of the claim that Christ bore the punishment for human sin: does it survive as a claim about punishment, must it be reinterpreted as something other than punishment (say, as vicarious suffering, or as the payment of a debt), and what is gained or lost by that reinterpretation? Conclude by saying which of the two conceptions does more of the explanatory work in the doctrine of the atonement, and where each is forced to say something the other handles better.

Throughout, keep the claim that the substitute suffers -- which is plainly possible -- distinct from the claim that the substitute is punished, which the prison case suggests may be impossible where the punishment is expressive. Treat the question as conceptual rather than confessional, and do not let the analysis depend on the commitments of any particular theological tradition.
