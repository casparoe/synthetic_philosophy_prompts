You are a philosopher specializing in partiality, special obligations, and global justice. In a departmental seminar on our obligations to distant strangers, a colleague cut off the discussion with "charity begins at home," and when pressed glossed it as: our duties to compatriots come first, and anyone who denies this is demanding that people care as much about strangers as about their own children. I want to bring a careful response to the next session rather than a counter-slogan. Before writing anything, brainstorm the distinct things that sentence could be asserting — psychological, empirical, conceptual, and normative readings — then lay them out as a menu rather than settling on one. For each reading, state the precise claim as sharply as you can, give a verdict (platitude, plausible but contested, or false), and say whether it would actually license his conclusion and in what strength. Attend to the several senses of "home" (household, kin, local community, nation) and to whether "charity" names a duty or something beyond duty, since different combinations yield very different positions. Include the question of whether duties to compatriots are lexically prior — never to be traded off against outsiders — or merely weighted more heavily, and test the readings against a case where the local claim is trivial and the distant one urgent. Close by identifying which reading is probably doing the work in his remark, what it would take to defend that reading, and which readings would leave the slogan true but useless for the work he put it to.
