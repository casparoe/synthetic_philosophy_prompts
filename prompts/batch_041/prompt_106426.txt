I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of medicine, and I am trying to end a two-year standoff between two colleagues in my department over the concept of disease. One of them is a naturalist in the tradition of Boorse's biostatistical theory: for her, a disease is, roughly, a statistically subnormal deviation from species-typical functional organization, and the concept itself is value-free — whether we happen to dislike a given dysfunction is a separate, practical matter. The other is a normativist: for him, calling something a disease is already an evaluative act, because "dysfunction" cannot be specified without tacitly invoking the goals, interests, or preferred states of the organism (or of whoever is doing the classifying), and the reference class against which "typical" is measured is itself a choice that answers to values.

They have been arguing past each other for long enough that I want the document they should have written together: a joint statement both could put their names to. Please produce it. Include, first, a numbered list of claims they can both accept — about what disease-talk is doing, about the roles of statistical normality, mechanism, and harm, and about what would count as a revision of each view. Second, a precise statement of the residual disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that that side would accept the wording as its own. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect the cruxes include (a) whether "function" can be cashed out without appeal to goals or values, (b) how the reference class is fixed and whether its choice is itself evaluative, and (c) whether the extension of "disease" is fixed by biology alone; if you find others, or find that one of mine collapses into another, say so.

For each crux, give a case, argument, or empirical finding that both parties would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about that case in advance. For each side, also record what it would count as a genuine reason to revise or abandon its own position.

Please test the joint statement against the following cases, which neither of them has considered and which I want classified:

- A condition that is statistically typical within a reference class defined by an environmental exposure, so that removing the exposure would restore the species-typical range, and which those who have it do not experience as a problem.
- A statistically rare condition that its bearers experience as a burden, where no failure of a biological mechanism can be identified, only a pattern of distress.
- A common variation that is harmless in itself but reliably predicts a later, uncontroversially pathological state.

Finally, tell me whether any part of their dispute is verbal — in particular, whether they are using "disease" for two concepts, one descriptive and one evaluative, that could both be legitimate; if so, say which concept each is really analyzing and what would remain a substantive disagreement once the terminology were fixed.

Format: lead with the joint statement itself, beginning in the first sentence with the numbered agreed-claims section, with no preface, no restating of the question, and no commentary; the disagreements table, the cruxes, and the case classifications follow. No citations or reading lists are needed — argue from the positions themselves.
