Design a runnable in-class activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology (20 students, one 75-minute session, six tables of three or four) that makes students feel the force of Bertrand's paradox rather than hear it described: rules, materials, a stake scheme that needs no money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the underlying issue.

The activity must satisfy these constraints. Students are not told the name of the paradox, nor the terms principle of indifference, invariance, or maximum entropy, until the debrief. The activity has three stations, each asking for a single numerical probability under a description that mentions no randomizing mechanism: (a) a chord of a circle is chosen at random — what is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle; (b) a factory produces cubes whose side length lies between 0 and 1 inch — what is the probability that a cube's side is shorter than half an inch; (c) a bottle contains a water–wine mixture whose ratio of water to wine lies between 1 and 2 — what is the probability that this ratio is at most 3/2. Every table posts one number per station, publicly, and stakes chips on it; ranges and hedges are not allowed. Each station is then settled by actually running the procedure: at the chord station, three physical devices the facilitator supplies (for instance, spinning a pointer and drawing the perpendicular chord; marking two points on a hoop and joining them; choosing a point in the disk and drawing the chord through it), whose empirical frequencies will match three different tables' posted numbers; at the other two stations, the corresponding pair of measurement procedures. The point is that every table turns out to be right about its own procedure and no table can claim to be right about the description as given.

Then the debrief, structured as a sequence of questions that gets the group to state what has gone wrong: that a uniform distribution is defined only relative to a parameterization or a symmetry group, that the axioms of probability fix the algebra and leave the measure open, and that a physical mechanism is what makes such a question well-posed. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply — in particular the constructive resolutions, in which a unique measure is derived from a group of transformations or from a maximum-entropy argument, and the fact that in applied work a mechanism is usually given, so the discomfort is rarely felt there.
