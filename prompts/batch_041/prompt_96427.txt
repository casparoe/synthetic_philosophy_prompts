I've been reading rationalist decision-theory writing for a long time, and I can recite the gist of updateless decision theory without being able to say what it actually forbids. My question is about the line between legitimate and illegitimate conditioning. The idea, as I understand it, is that the agent selects a policy — a mapping from observations to actions — and is not permitted to condition on anything downstream of that policy: it may condition on what the world tells it, but not on its own decision, and not on evidence about which algorithm it is running. Please explain how that line is meant to be drawn and why it falls where it does, using the hard cases rather than the easy ones. What happens to an agent that reads its own source code, or that finds itself in a world containing a reliable predictor of its output, or that is simply told by a trusted source what it is about to do? Which of those are ordinary observations and which are illicit self-conditioning, and does the distinction survive once the agent's observations are themselves causally downstream of its own algorithm? I'd like the answer to mark the seams: say which of these verdicts are forced by the core idea and which depend on contested choices about how to model the agent, and please don't present a contested resolution as the consensus.
