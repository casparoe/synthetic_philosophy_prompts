A laboratory has trained a language model on a corpus of text. By every statistical measure the laboratory knows how to construct — token distributions, co-occurrence structure, performance on every benchmark it can devise — the corpus is indistinguishable from one that would have been produced by ordinary human speakers discussing the shared physical environment they inhabit. In fact it was produced by a purely formal process that has never interacted with anything outside itself, and the model's outputs are indistinguishable from those of a twin model trained on genuinely human-produced text. The laboratory now wants to know whether anything its model says can be described as meaningful.

Please describe the most important implications of semantic externalism — the thesis that the meaning of a term is fixed at least in part by facts about the environment and the linguistic community of the speaker, rather than by what is in the speaker's head — for how this laboratory should think about its model. I am interested in the implications that are genuinely forced by externalism as opposed to the ones that merely sound as though they follow from it, so please mark the difference as you go. In particular, address the following:

- Does externalism give any reason to hold that the model's tokens inherit the meanings those tokens carried in the texts it was trained on, or does the sort of causal connection externalism requires break down at precisely this point? Which reading of externalism supports which answer, and is the appeal to linguistic deference — the idea that a speaker can mean by a term what the relevant experts mean by it — available to a system that has no community, only a corpus?
- If two such models produce identical outputs on every possible input, is there any sense in which they can still differ in meaning? Does externalism make that question easier or harder to answer than its denial does, and does the answer change if the two models were trained on corpora drawn from different environments?
- What follows for the ordinary practice of calling a model mistaken rather than merely mismatched to the facts? Does the externalist picture make talk of a model's error a category mistake, or does it underwrite such talk — and does the answer depend on whether we are attributing wide content or only some narrower notion of content?

Then state which of these implications you take to be the most important, and why that one rather than the others.
