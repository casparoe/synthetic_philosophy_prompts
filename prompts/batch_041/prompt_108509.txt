I am rewriting the liberty lectures for my political philosophy course, and a colleague who works on republican theory has spent a term goading me about the gap between the two-concept framework I teach and the verdicts I actually hold. I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. I will set them out as they actually come to me. A tenant whose landlord may evict on thirty days' notice for any reason or none, but who over twenty years has never raised the rent or evicted anyone, and whose building sits in a neighbourhood where moving would mean pulling her children out of school and doubling her costs: less free than a neighbour holding a lifetime, rent-controlled lease, even though nothing whatever is being done to her. A driver stopped at a red light, and a taxpayer whose rate is raised by a government she voted against: neither is unfree in the sense I care about, though both are interfered with against their preferences. A shopkeeper whose business is ruined by a larger rival's lawful price-cutting: not unfree, merely much worse off, even though the rival's choices have left him with far fewer options than before. A man whose savings are placed in a court-supervised trust because of his gambling, over his loud objection: unfree, even though he now has strictly more and better options than he would otherwise have had. The islanders whose only bridge to the mainland is owned by a man who has never charged a toll, never closed the bridge, and never asked them for anything: unfree, and unfree precisely because the bridge is his. A sailor who signs articles with a notoriously harsh captain, at wages she could not match ashore, and who may leave at any port where the ship docks: free, or near enough, though she will be miserable. A novice who has taken a vow of obedience to an abbess, under a rule that obliges the order to release her on request after a year: free, or near enough, and I notice how badly this sits with the next case. A wife in a marriage where the husband has never once overruled her, but where work, money and where they live are organized on the settled understanding that he could overrule her and she would have nowhere to go: unfree, and the fact that he never exercises the power seems to me to make it worse rather than better, because the constraint then operates through her own anticipation of him.

My provisional principle has been roughly this: you are unfree to the degree that what you can do depends on another person's not choosing to change it — on their goodwill, where nothing but their own preferences constrains them. That gets the landlord, the wife, the islanders and the gambler right, and I think it gets the red light and the taxpayer right. But it appears to make the shopkeeper unfree, which I will not accept, and it makes the sailor and the novice unfree in a way I keep resisting. And there is the case I go back and forth on: a tenant whose rent, repairs and eviction are set by a proprietary algorithm that no employee of the management company can explain or override, and which by its own terms treats every unit in her block identically. No one is exercising a will over her at all, so by my principle she is free; but when she tells me that there is no one to appeal to, no one who could make an exception, no one to whom her circumstances are even visible, my confidence wobbles.

So: is there a principle that captures this pattern, or a small set of candidates that divide the labour between them? Please state it precisely, tell me which clause is doing the work in each case above, and say what it predicts for cases I have not put to myself — for instance a worker bound by a non-compete her employer has never once enforced, a tenant whose landlord holds the only key to her flat but has never used it, or an employee whose housing, healthcare and social life are all provided by her employer on terms he could revise tomorrow. I know the labels, so please do not simply tell me that these verdicts are republican rather than liberal and leave it there; I want the principle itself, with the awkward cases actually pressed. Be honest about which of my verdicts the best version has to overturn, and if there is a baroque version that saves all of them, give me that alongside the austere version that does not, so I can see what the extra machinery costs. Keep the question to social freedom — I am not asking about self-mastery or about anyone's true self — and I would rather lose a verdict than buy a principle with epicycles.
