I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about how I ought to respond when someone disagrees with me, and I have arrived at a small set of convictions that I would like you to test against one another. Here they are.

(1) When someone who has looked into a question as carefully as I have ends up on the opposite side, I should genuinely lower my confidence — not merely register the disagreement and carry on as before.

(2) I take the reason to be this: the bare fact that I have reached a conclusion is not by itself any evidence that the conclusion is true. I could be making a mistake I cannot see, and the other person's disagreement is a sign of exactly that possibility.

(3) But I do not move at all when I can point to the precise step at which the other person's reasoning goes wrong. If I have located their error, the fact that they disagree with me tells me nothing new.

(4) What should move me most is how many people disagree with me rather than who they are: if ten careful people land on the other side, I ought to bend further than if a single person does, even if that one person understands the subject far better than all ten of them together.

(5) I am completely certain that 2 + 2 = 4, and that a valid argument cannot have true premises together with a false conclusion, even though I know there are people who deny even these.

(6) I think two people who disagree can both be perfectly reasonable; it need not be that one of them is being irrational.

(7) I think there is a difference between disagreeing about the answer and disagreeing about what even counts as a good argument; in the second case I have no idea how to split the difference, since splitting it seems to require a standard that the two of us already share.

I am not asking for a survey of what others have written about disagreement. I want a check on whether these seven convictions can be held together. Which of them pull against one another, and how badly? Which pairs only look as though they conflict but in fact do not, and why? For each genuine conflict, tell me which conviction would have to give way and what the cheapest repair would be, and then tell me which single change would resolve the most at once. Please order the conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and be concrete about which of my commitments is doing the damage in each case.
