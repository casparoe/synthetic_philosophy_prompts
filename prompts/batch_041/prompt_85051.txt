I convene a fortnightly reading group in the philosophy of mind, and over the past year we have gone round and round the exclusion argument for mental causation — the one associated with Jaegwon Kim — without ever shifting anyone's position. Our group has hardened into two camps. The exclusionists hold that once one accepts the causal closure of the physical, the supervenience of the mental on the physical, and the refusal to treat mind–brain causation as a case of systematic overdetermination, mental properties are squeezed out of causal work: either they are identical with their physical realisers, in which case they inherit the causal powers of those realisers but add nothing of their own, or they are distinct, in which case the realisers do all the work and the mental properties are idle. The non-reductionists in the group accept closure and supervenience too, but deny that exclusion follows; they insist that a higher-level property can be a genuine difference-maker for an effect even when its realiser is by itself sufficient for that effect, and they suspect that the exclusionist is smuggling in a picture of causation as the transfer of a power from cause to effect that no working science requires. After a year of this, I have come to think that a good part of the stand-off is terminological, and that we could settle the rest if we were forced to write something together rather than at each other.

What I want from you is the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Two stipulations are to govern it. First, the whole document is to be written within an interventionist, difference-making account of causation of the sort associated with James Woodward: a cause is a difference-maker for its effect under interventions, and no appeal is to be made at any point to causal production, to the transfer of a power, or to anything of that kind — if a claim cannot be stated in interventionist terms, leave it out and say so. Second, I want both camps to be made to face a constraint I have been pressing on the group for months and which neither has properly addressed, which I call the Realiser-Fixing Constraint: any intervention that varies a mental property is specified as an intervention on that property's physical realiser, so that the question becomes whether variation in the realiser that leaves the mental property fixed is the right contrast class for assessing the mental property's causal status. I also want the document to deal explicitly with the case we cannot settle, which I will call the Redundancy Case. A subject possesses a mental property M on a number of occasions. On some occasions M is realised by a neural property P1, on others by a wholly different neural property P2; there is no single physical property common to the two sets of occasions, and both camps accept the physiology. In every case the same effect E occurs. Does M cause E? Does P1 cause E on the occasions when it occurs? What does an intervention on P1 show, given that M would have been realised by P2 anyway had P1 not occurred? My own suspicion is that the real crux in the whole debate is not supervenience at all but the choice between picking out the most specific difference-maker and picking out the most proportional one, and I should like the document either to bear that suspicion out or to lay it to rest.

Please set the document out as follows: a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about what the disagreement is not — which alleged differences both sides now regard as merely verbal, and why; then a table of the residual disagreements, each side's position written in wording that that side would itself accept, with no formulation that could be read as a concession by the side it describes; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, and for each crux the argument, case, or piece of evidence that both camps agree would bear on it, together with each camp's prediction about how it would come out, recorded in advance; then a short closing note on what would change each camp's mind and whether there is anything that would move both. Write it as a document I could circulate to the group, not as a survey of what has been written on the topic, and do not include citations, reading lists, or appeals to who has said what — the group knows the literature, and what we need is the drafting. Please use British English throughout.
