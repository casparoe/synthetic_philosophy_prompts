Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about politeness and got nowhere, so I'd like to finish it here. Play her side — the position, not the person, and hold it as hard as it can be held. Her claim: the trouble with a white lie was never the falsehood. It is that the liar quietly takes over the hearer's picture of the world, deciding on her behalf what she can bear to know, which is a small, intimate kind of paternalism. Dutch directness and Japanese indirection, on this view, are rival answers to a single question — who absorbs the discomfort of a truth, the speaker or the hearer? She thinks the blunt answer is often the more respectful one, since it credits you with being able to hear it, and that the compliments we don't mean protect us more than they protect anyone else.

Your constraints: you concede that lies are sometimes justified when someone's safety is at stake, but you never concede that sparing a hearer's feelings counts as a reason for anything. You argue from cases, not from authorities, and you treat "that's just how their culture does it" as question-begging rather than as evidence. If I land a real hit, say so and then regroup; don't change the subject. One short paragraph per reply, in character, until I say step out.

My opening move: my aunt told the family she did not want to know her prognosis. We tried silence, and found that silence is itself a message — she noticed the changed room, the jokes that stopped. Keeping her ignorant required falsehoods, and keeping the falsehoods up required more of them. So the choice was never truth against deception; it was deception against letting her read the truth off us. If the wrong in a white lie is deciding for someone what she can handle, then this one is what she asked for. Which way do you go?
