Bit of an odd ask, but here goes. Long-time lurker on the rationalist blogs (old Overcoming Bias days, Slate Star Codex, that whole corner of the internet) and I've somehow ended up as the TA for a final-year seminar on reductionism and emergence. The module is basically Dennett's cranes-vs-skyhooks stuff plus a pile of material on greedy reductionism, and the prof has let me write one of the exam questions. I want it to be a proper separator question — not something a student can answer by reciting the crane/skyhook schtick back at me, and not something that only makes sense if you've read the exact same papers I have.

You're a professional philosopher of science who's taught this at postgrad level and is mildly allergic to students who think quoting a slogan counts as an argument. Before you write anything, do a bit of scratch brainstorming for yourself: what's the actual crux here, what do students who half-get-it say, and where's the trap. Then give me the finished artefacts. I don't need a reading list or references — students aren't allowed notes and I'll bolt the citations on myself if this ever becomes anything. Also don't lean on anything obscure; the difficulty should come from the crux, not from trivia.

Here's the twist I want built in, because otherwise the question is just "define the two terms". I've made up a fictional research outfit for it, the Speculative Cognitive Science Unit, which pitches three programmes:

(a) Consciousness just IS a certain kind of information integration, full stop, and asking for a mechanism beyond that is a category error — the integration is the explanation.
(b) Consciousness is an emergent property that shows up once a system crosses a complexity threshold, and that's the end of the story; no further decomposition is possible or needed.
(c) The system is "just" neurons firing, and all talk of beliefs, desires and content is at best a useful fiction we could in principle delete without explanatory loss.

I want the question to force them to sort those three by whether the skyhook is in the ontology or in the explanation — i.e. to clock that a programme can be perfectly naturalistic about what exists and still be a skyhook in disguise, because it parks an unexplained capacity that quietly does all the work. (a) is the sneaky one; I want them to see that "X just is Y, stop asking" can be either a crane or a skyhook depending on whether anything has actually been explained or only re-labelled. And I want at least one part asking whether the crane/skyhook criterion is itself doing any work, or whether it's just a fancy name for "has a mechanism" — i.e. whether greedy reductionism is a real diagnosis or just an insult you reach for when you don't like the reductionism on offer.

So: one essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, 20 marks, pitched at final-year undergrads who've read the primary stuff but aren't specialists. Then a model answer of roughly 600 words, then a marking scheme: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth adding up to 20, and for each point one line on how candidates typically get it wrong. Think carefully about whether those three programmes really are separable on your criterion before you commit to a marking scheme — if one of them turns out to be a red herring, or to collapse into another, say so and adjust, rather than pretending the taxonomy is tidier than it is.
