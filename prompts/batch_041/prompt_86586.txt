In a debate I keep having about aid and immigration, the lifeboat does all the argumentative work. The rich nations are a boat with room for fifty; the world's poor are a hundred swimmers in the water; taking on even one more swamps everyone aboard. The conclusion drawn is not callousness but triage: refusing to haul people in is required, not merely permitted, and the passengers who want to pull swimmers aboard are sentimentalists who will drown the boat.

I want an audit rather than a refutation. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the boat, its capacity, the passengers, the captain, the swimmers, the sea, the act of hauling someone over the gunwale — then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breakages that matter for the conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. I already suspect two smuggled features, the fixity of capacity and the sharpness of the boundary between aboard and in the water; I want to know which others are being imported without argument, and which of the importations the conclusion actually depends on.

Then say what the image cannot represent at all. Consider: aid that costs the passengers no capacity, like life rings rather than seats; passengers who are aboard because they were hauled in earlier; swimmers who helped build the boat; a capacity that depends on how the cargo is stowed. Does the conclusion survive once these are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if a better image exists — people reach for the shallow pond, or the spaceship, at this point — say what it would have to get right and what it would lose.
