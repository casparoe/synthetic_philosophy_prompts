Alright, I've talked myself into a pretty hardline Braudel-style view and I want you to try to talk me out of it, or at least show me where it's exposed. The view: the real causal action in history lives in the long-run structures -- geography, climate, demography, the slow grind of material life -- and events and individuals are basically surface disturbances, foam on the tide. A reign, a battle, a great man's decision explain almost nothing about the shape of the centuries around them; they're the noise the structure makes. So when someone hands me an individual-level or event-level story about a big historical change, my default is that they've described a symptom and called it a cause.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, ranked by how much it should actually move me, and I'd like you to be honest about which items a committed defender would concede rather than just the ones critics like to throw.

Some things I'm already chewing on, feel free to tell me they're confused:

- The rerun test. If structures are doing the work, then swapping out the individual -- same structural position, different person -- should give you roughly the same outcome. Is there any case where that clearly fails, where a single decision or a short-run contingency plausibly bent a long trajectory and you can't tell the story structurally without smuggling the event back in as a cause? And if there is, is it a rare exception or does it sink the strong version?

- Slow isn't the same as causal. What actually makes a structure an engine rather than just a backdrop? If I can't say, am I just doing correlation at a very large timescale and calling it explanation?

- The unfalsifiability worry, which I definately take seriously. Any event I want to dismiss gets redescribed as 'the structure expressing itself,' and any event that seems to shift the structure gets redescribed as 'the structure finally tipping.' And we only ever get one run of history, so the rerun test is never really available. Is there a version of this view that risks anything at all -- that predicts something its rivals don't, something I could be shown to be wrong about?

- I suspect the view is really two seperate claims glued together: (a) slow structures carry a lot of causal weight, and (b) events and individuals carry near zero. I find (a) easy and (b) hard. Is that split right, and which of my defeaters are actually aimed at (a) versus at (b)?

I don't need a reading list or citations, just the argument and where it bites. And if you think the view only survives by retreating to something weak and unfalsifiable, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that's a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing business at this scale.
