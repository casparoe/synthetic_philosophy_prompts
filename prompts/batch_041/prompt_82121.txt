I'm a backend engineer at a payments company, and next month I'm giving the opening remarks at our two-day internal workshop on model accountability. The audience is around sixty people: ML engineers, a few lawyers from compliance, and a large group from customer support and disputes who have no technical background and are tired of being told the model is just math. I have about twelve minutes, no slides.

I want the talk to do real work. By the end, I want the room to share a usable way of talking about what our models do and don't mean when they flag a transaction, so the later sessions on audit trails and customer appeals aren't starting from zero. There's a specific tension I want the talk to hold: compliance needs the model's output to be describable, in a customer letter or a regulator-facing document, as a determination that a transaction was fraudulent, while every engineer in the room knows it's a score computed from a proxy.

Here is the case I want to build the talk around. Last year our fraud model started flagging a batch of transactions. It turned out the model had latched onto a feature that correlated with fraud in the training data but wasn't fraud itself: the presence of a particular merchant-category code that appeared almost exclusively in a set of confirmed fraud cases from one issuer. It wasn't detecting fraud; it was detecting that code. We retrained, it got better, and everyone moved on. But I keep thinking about a thought experiment I half-remember from a podcast: a vending machine that accepts quarters by checking size, weight, and metal content. A foreign coin with the same profile would go through, so the machine isn't really recognizing quarters — it's recognizing a physical signature that happens, locally, to coincide with quarters. The podcast used this to ask whether the machine has anything like a belief about quarters, or whether we're just projecting one onto it. I've since heard the contrast described as original versus derived intentionality, though I don't know the literature and can't defend a citation.

I want the remarks to take that question seriously without turning into a philosophy lecture. Specifically: first, distinguish between a system that has its own grip on what it is tracking and a system whose apparent concepts are borrowed from us — our labels, our training data, our choice of features. Second, be honest that this distinction is uncomfortable for engineers, because we say what models want and think all day and the shorthand is useful. Third, land on something practical: what follows for how we write incident reports, how a support agent should talk to a customer whose claim was denied, and what we should require of a model before we say it detects fraud rather than scores transactions. The support staff are the ones who translate our language into human terms, so the talk has to give them something they can use.

Write the opening remarks. Twelve minutes, spoken, formal enough for a company-wide workshop but not stiff. Don't cite philosophers or papers; half the room would tune out and I can't defend a citation anyway. Keep the vending machine example plain. Don't resolve the question too neatly — I'd rather leave the room with a sharp distinction and an open question than a slogan.
