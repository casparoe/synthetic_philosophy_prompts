For the past three years, since retiring from a career in neuroscience, I have been reading my way into the philosophy of well-being, and I keep striking the same wall. I am drawn to hedonism — the thesis that well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain — because it seems the most tractable position to defend, but I cannot get past its central difficulty: the pleasures seem to have nothing in common. The pleasure of cold water when parched, of a proof finally falling into place, of a warm bath, of a friend's good news, of a cruel joke — they share, so far as I can tell, neither a felt quality nor an object. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in hedonic psychology and the philosophy of well-being and explain this problem to me carefully. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you set out the candidate unifiers that have been or could be proposed. Then do two things. First, state the strongest version of the heterogeneity objection to hedonism and distinguish at least two importantly different hedonist replies — in particular, make the difference clear between locating the commonality in the sensory or feeling character of an experience and locating it in the subject's attitude toward a content. Second, tell me how each reply fares against a case I find especially troubling: an experience I would sincerely call unpleasant at the moment — the ache of a certain piece of music, or of a remembered loss — and yet that I seek out and do not wish to be rid of. Must hedonism deny that I take pleasure in it, redescribe my wanting, or is there a third option? Be concrete, and do not rest your explanation on names, dates, or titles, since I have no way to check them.
