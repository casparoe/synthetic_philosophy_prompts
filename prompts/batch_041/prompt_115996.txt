My two flatmates, Nia and Dev, got into a long argument after a semantics reading group, and I ended up as the referee even though I definately don't know enough to call it. The background: Dev says the Lewis triviality results are basically a proof that indicative conditionals can't be propositions, because any theory that says the probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent will collapse into triviality. Nia says that's an overreaction: the proof only shows something about a very strong, unrestricted equation, and she thinks you can keep the equation for the conditionals people actually assert while denying it for conditionals embedded under negation, disjunction, or in the antecedents of other conditionals. Dev replies that this is just hiding the problem, since the triviality proof can be rerun on the restricted class if the restriction is itself expressible in the language, and anyway people do embed conditionals in all those ways. Nia then brings in her own repair: she says the probability of a conditional is not a single unconditional probability but is always relative to a partition or a question under discussion, so the equation should be partition-sensitive, and the triviality proof equivocates by fixing one partition while the conditional shifts it. Dev says that partition talk is doing no real work unless she can say when a partition is the right one, and that if the partition can shift freely then her view predicts almost anything and is unfalsifiable. Nia says the same is true of any context-sensitive expression and nobody calls all of semantics unfalsifiable. Dev says fine, but then the view isn't a version of the original thesis anymore; it's a different theory that has to give up the neat equation, and she shouldn't get credit for saving it.

I couldn't tell who was winning. Can you adjudicate this move by move? In particular: (1) is Dev right that triviality shows indicative conditionals aren't propositions, or does it show something narrower? (2) Is Nia's restricted-equation response a real option or a dodge? (3) What exactly would a partition-sensitive or question-sensitive version have to say to avoid the triviality proof, and does it actually avoid it? (4) Who, if anyone, is conflating a proof about a formal principle with a claim about natural language?
