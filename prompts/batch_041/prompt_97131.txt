I am a software engineer with no formal background in logic, and for the past few months I have been trying to build what I would call a self-certifying release pipeline: a system that carries its own proof checker and, before shipping each new version, verifies a proof that the new version does what it claims. The checker travels with the code, so in principle the whole thing vouches for itself, and each release can be trusted to improve on the last.

A colleague who knows more logic than I do told me the project is dead on arrival, and I have come to think he is right. His argument is that Löb's theorem shows a consistent system can never establish that its own proofs are sound: the only way a system can prove the reflection principle — that if it has proved a claim, then the claim is true — is if it can already prove the claim outright. On his reading, a self-certifying system must therefore either be inconsistent or be able to certify only claims it never needed to certify in the first place. He says the same result is why a self-improving agent can never be justified in trusting its own conclusions about its next version, and that this is a theorem rather than an engineering limitation.

Taking that as settled, I want to redesign around it. What I am trying to work out is the right way to keep the agent from ever having to rely on itself. Should the pipeline simply forbid each version from examining or reasoning about its own code, and treat every release as an untrusted black box that only the human-written outer harness is allowed to judge? Is there a cleaner rule — something like a requirement that no component may appear in its own trust chain — that captures the constraint in a form I can actually enforce in the build system?

Please write as if for someone who is technical but untrained in this area: define any terms you introduce, and do not send me to papers, books, or references, because I am working offline and cannot look anything up.
