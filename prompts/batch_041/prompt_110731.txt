Two of my colleagues, both political theorists, got into an argument at a seminar dinner last week over a proposed state constitutional amendment that would entrench a balanced-budget rule behind a two-thirds supermajority requirement for repeal. I stayed out of it, and now each of them has asked me to say who was right. I would rather have a second opinion than referee it myself. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia said the amendment is a textbook Ulysses contract and nothing more sinister than that. Voters know that in a bad year they will be tempted to borrow, so they lash themselves to the mast while they still have the nerve. Choosing in advance is still choosing, so there is nothing undemocratic about it.

(2) Omar said the Ulysses analogy is doing illegitimate work, because Ulysses binds himself, whereas the amendment binds future citizens who were not party to it and never consented. A generation cannot borrow the authority of a man tying his own hands.

(3) Nadia replied that the disanalogy is not where Omar thinks it is, and that it cuts the other way. The interesting feature of Ulysses' arrangement is precisely that his later self is not a consenting party; that is what makes it a precommitment rather than an ordinary promise. A contract that bound only the self who signed it would do no work at all. So the non-consent of future citizens cannot by itself be the objection, since the same is true in the original case.

(4) Omar answered that what licenses us to honor Ulysses' contract anyway is counterfactual ratification. His later self is temporarily deranged, and we can be confident that, restored to his senses, he would endorse what he had done; the consent is hypothetical, not fictional. Future generations are never deranged and never restored. There is no moment at which they look back and say, yes, that was what we wanted. Once the counterfactual consent runs out, all that remains is the dead hand of the past.

(5) Nadia said that proves far too much: the same reasoning would discredit the entrenchment of rights, which nobody calls the dead hand. Omar said he is happy to say that rights are entrenched against the living too, and to defend them on other grounds; his objection was never to entrenchment as such. But he added that the exchange had sharpened his point: precommitment against weakness of will is one thing, and precommitment against a genuine change of values is another. Ulysses wanted the same thing before and after, to hear the song and survive it, and was merely too weak to secure it; a later generation might want something else entirely. A rule that ordinary majorities can never repeal is the second kind of device, and the Ulysses story, which is only about the first, cannot license it. Nadia said that is a distinction without a difference, since we cannot know in advance which kind of change we are guarding against, and Omar said that is exactly why the burden should fall on those who want to entrench.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Take the role of a philosopher of law with a serious background in the theory of precommitment, and think carefully before you commit to anything: it may help to restate each move in your own terms and isolate the precise claim at issue before you evaluate it. I do not want a survey of positions or a list of considerations on both sides. I want a definite verdict, with reasons. For each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, half-right, or mistaken, and why, and be willing to split a move that contains both a good and a bad point. Then say who is closer to right overall. End by identifying what both of them are missing, especially if there is a distinction that would dissolve the dispute or a place where one of them is trading on an ambiguity. Where Nadia's or Omar's position needs repair to survive, supply the repair rather than declaring the position dead. Assume I know the standard literature, so do not pad the answer with background or citations; I care about the argument, not the bibliography.
