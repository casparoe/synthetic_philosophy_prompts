I write the Ideas column for a general-readership science and philosophy magazine, and I'm at work on a short feature about a family of puzzles that decision theorists call money pumps. The feature is mostly explanatory, but I'd like to close it with a poem that does something prose cannot: make the reader feel the trap rather than merely follow the argument. The trap I have in mind is the diachronic one. An agent has preferences that are perfectly consistent at any single moment, and each choice she makes is the one she prefers at the moment she makes it, yet over an afternoon she trades away a small sum of money and ends up exactly where she started, holding the same good she began with. The puzzle is not that she is weak-willed or confused. It is that her preferences are arranged so that a patient counterparty can offer her a sequence of pairwise trades, each of which she prefers to the status quo, and the sequence returns her to the status quo minus the fees. A synchronic intransitivity can do this in a single sitting, but the diachronic version is stranger: her preferences shift predictably over time, and even if she sees the pump coming, she may be unable to stop it without some external commitment device, a way of binding her future self that her present self would not otherwise choose. I want the poem to live in that gap between foreseeing and preventing.

I'd like a pantoum, because the form is already a kind of pump: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, so the poem keeps handing itself forward and, if you close the loop, ends where it began. Please write a pantoum of six quatrains in the voice of an agent who is being pumped, not the pumper. Use the repeated lines to carry the trades: the same phrases should recur as the goods change hands, so that by the final stanza the reader hears the original line again and understands that the agent has paid for the privilege of arriving where she started. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who teaches decision theory would recognize the distinction between synchronic intransitivity and diachronic preference reversal, and between an agent who is merely inconsistent and one who is a money pump; it should not name-drop or explain the theory. The last line should close the circuit, but the closing should feel like a loss rather than a solution. Begin directly with the poem; no title, no preface, no commentary.
