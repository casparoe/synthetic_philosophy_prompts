For the last few years I would have said my views about rational choice were settled — the sort of thing one holds without noticing, and argues about only in seminars. Two decisions I made this spring have made the topic feel personal, and I would like an audit of my position before I write it up for a reading group. I bought a long-term care policy that costs me a real fraction of my savings, and in the same month I turned down a one-off bet that would have paid several times my stake on a coin flip. Both choices felt like the same disposition, and I now suspect that the commitments below do not jointly license it.

(1) Rational choice maximizes expected utility. I take this as a substantive claim about practical rationality rather than a modelling convenience: a rational agent's preferences are represented by a probability function and a utility function, and she chooses the act with the greatest expected utility.

(2) The utility of money diminishes. An additional thousand dollars is worth more to me when I have little than when I have much, and that, I think, is the whole explanation of why I insure against small catastrophes and decline fair bets.

(3) The certainty effect is not a mistake. Offered a guaranteed moderate sum, I take it. Offered instead a small chance of a large sum against a slightly larger chance of the moderate sum, I take the large sum. I believe I am within my rational rights in both, and I do not think the two answers can be explained by the diminishing utility in (2).

(4) I have no attitude toward chance as such. Whatever caution I display is a matter of what the outcomes are worth to me; I have no separate taste for or against riskiness, and two gambles inducing the same probability distribution over utility levels are, for me, the same gamble.

(5) Rational preferences should not depend on framing or on the order in which decisions arrive. In particular I want to be dynamically consistent: I want to be able to commit now to a plan for later without being able to predict that I will defect once the later choice comes.

I have also been reading about a proposal on which a gamble's value is assembled from the utility differences between its ranked outcomes, each difference weighted by a risk function applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least that well — so that an attitude toward risk enters as a separate ingredient alongside beliefs and utilities, and certainty effects come out permissible.

Which of (1) through (5) genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and how badly? Order the real tensions by severity; for each, say which commitment has to give and what is lost by giving it. Then tell me whether the proposal I mention is a way of keeping (3) at an acceptable price, and what else it would cost me — including anything it would quietly force me to abandon. If a single revision resolves the most while conceding the least, identify it and say what I would be giving up.
