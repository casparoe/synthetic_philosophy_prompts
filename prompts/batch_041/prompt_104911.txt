I've been reading rationalist blogs and alignment forums for about a decade now — long enough to have watched the elicitation-of-latent-knowledge problem get posed, re-posed, renamed, and quietly folded into adjacent research programs, and long enough to have developed a specific allergy. It isn't to people being dishonest. It's to a genre of announcement: the confident, well-formatted claim that a hard conceptual problem has been solved, where the solution turns out to consist of a proxy metric that has been allowed to stand in for the thing itself. I'd like you to write a parody of that genre.

The form: a launch post with an appended system-card section, from a fictional alignment lab, around 700 words, deadpan throughout, in the register of a company that sincerely believes it is being admirably cautious while it is in fact doing the thing. Voice: a safety-and-transparency team writing to a technical-but-adjacent audience; competent, self-congratulatory, allergic to the word "just." The headline claim is that the lab has solved elicitation of latent knowledge. They have a model whose internal states carry information about the world, and a "reporter" module that answers natural-language questions about that information.

Make the apparatus concrete enough that someone who knows the problem recognizes every moving part, and invent enough of the lab's own vocabulary that the piece stands on its own:

- a reporter and a predictor, where the predictor is trained to forecast the reporter's answers — and the headline success metric is reporter-predictor agreement;
- a ground-truth channel that is available only at evaluation time, with the piece not noticing what this implies about training;
- an explicit scenario in the lab's own idiom — a hidden object in a room, where the camera cannot see it — that is a cousin of the standard case, plus the lab's treatment of the two ways such a scenario can go (the sensor is being fooled versus the object genuinely isn't there), with the lab confidently collapsing the distinction;
- a stability metric defined over paraphrases or re-descriptions, presented as evidence of ontology identification;
- a deference or counterfactual check ("what would the reporter say if its outputs had been different?"), and a sandbagging check;
- a limitations section that confesses the central problem in the act of not noticing it, and an invented safety reviewer whose endorsement is the punchline.

The humor has to come from the content, not from winking. Exaggerate a real feature of the practice — the substitution of "reproduces what a human would say" for "reports what the model knows," and the laundering of that substitution through a metric — rather than inventing a flaw. A reader who has followed the technical discussion should recognize the setup and find the satire fair; a reader who hasn't should still be able to tell what is being mocked by the end.

Do not name real labs, real researchers, or real papers, and do not quote anyone; the company, its personnel, and every line of dialogue are invented. No citations or reading list, please — I can supply those myself if I ever show this to anyone.

Finally, outside the parody, add a short note — a few sentences, not part of the piece — telling me which of the jokes rest on points that are actually settled among people who work on this, and which rest on contested readings that a reasonable researcher might reject. I want to know where the parody is trading on a live disagreement rather than a consensus, and I'd rather you flag a contested claim than quietly present it as the received view.
