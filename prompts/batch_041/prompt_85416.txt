I'm in a third-year seminar on well-being and we've just finished the hedonism unit, and the heterogeneity problem is the bit I can't stop chewing on. My prof set up three rival answers to what makes something a pleasure: (a) the common-feel view, on which every pleasure shares a distinctive positive phenomenal character, a hedonic tone, some way it feels to be pleased; (b) the attitude view, on which a state is a pleasure just in case the person likes it — liking, not merely wanting, and not liking-it-at-a-higher-order either, just the occurrent thing; and (c) what our TA calls the reward-tracking view, on which a state is a pleasure iff it's the state the person's learning system treats as a reward, the thing that reinforces and gets them working to bring it back, whether or not there's any felt tone or any occurrent liking. For my essay I need a case built to order, and I'd rather not use masochism or sad music or chili peppers, everyone reaches for those.

Here's the spec. One ordinary person, one ordinary week, ordinary contemporary life — no brain surgery, no invented drugs, no coma or locked-in patients, nothing that definately needs a thought experiment about the future of neuroscience. Four episodes in that week, arranged so that two things vary independently: whether the episode has a positive felt character, and whether the person likes it (in the not-liked cells I mean indifferent rather than averse — he wouldn't lift a finger to have it again, but he isn't repelled by it either). So: one episode felt-positive and liked, one felt-positive and not liked, one felt-flat and liked, one felt-flat and not liked. Describe all four from the inside in concrete phenomenal terms — a warmth spreading, a loosening in the chest, whatever it is — but you can't use the disputed vocabulary: no saying he enjoyed it, no saying it felt good or that it was pleasant, since that's exactly what's under dispute. Also no pain in any of the four, no addictions, and nothing where the answer turns on mixed feelings about a single object. Then the demo, episode by episode: what the common-feel view has to say about each, what the attitude view has to say about each, what the reward-tracking view has to say about each, and why each says it. Tell me which single episode is doing the work — which one, if I deleted it, would let each theory off the hook — and what a defender of that theory would say about it. And if the reward-tracking view never comes apart from the other two anywhere in the four cells, say so rather than fudging it, and tell me what a fifth episode would have to look like.

I'm writing on whether the heterogeneity problem is really a problem for hedonism as a theory of well-being or only for theories of the nature of pleasure, so the case has to be usable by both sides — if it only works because I've smuggled in an assumption about what liking is, tell me which assumption. And please keep what's actually settled among people who work on pleasure separate from what's just one camp's line: if something in my setup is contested (whether the attitude view should really be about liking rather than desire, whether reward-tracking is a distinct position at all, whether a felt-flat-but-liked episode is even possible) say so instead of writing it up as the consensus, and flag the places where you're least sure.
