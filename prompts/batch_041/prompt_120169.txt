I'm a science journalist who writes about philosophy and the mind, and I'm sketching an essay on how much of our own mental lives we can really be sure of. Before I start writing I want to check my own position for cracks, because I have a suspicion it doesn't all hang togehter. Please take the role of a philosopher of mind and epistemology who knows this literature inside out, and think it through carefully before you commit to anything. Here's where I've landed:

1. The KK principle: if you know something, you know that you know it. I've never understood why people treat this as obviously false; it seems like the default to me.
2. I'm sold on the anti-luminosity argument, the one where the room warms up a degree at a time and at some point you're no longer in a position to know whether you're cold. I think it generalizes: nothing in the mind is such that being in that state puts you in a position to know you're in it, and I take that to include knowing itself.
3. I think knowing requires a margin: you only know something if you couldn't easily have been wrong about it.
4. The one place I'd call myself flat-out infallible is my own present experience. I can't be wrong about how things look to me right now, or that a face seems familiar, or that something seems to hurt.
5. I'm not a skeptic. I think I know there's a laptop in front of me and that I had coffee this morning.
