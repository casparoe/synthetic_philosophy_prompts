Ok so I'm a junior (phil/econ) and I'm the student rep on my university's Student Conduct Board, which is basically the disciplinary panel for student code violations. We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion before then, and I'm kinda out of my depth on the philosophy side of it even though that's literally my major.

Here's the case. Seven students occupied the second floor of the admin building for about two days in March to try to stop the trustees from voting to renew the university's contract with an immigration-detention contractor. They didn't wreck much (one door, and someone pulled a fire alarm on the way out, which was dumb), nobody got hurt, and when the police finally came they went limp and got carried out rather than fighting. They're charged under the Code's unauthorized-presence and failure-to-comply sections. Those carry a mandatory one-semester suspension, but the Code lets us go lower if we find "extenuating circumstances," which it never defines. Their defense is basically: we tried everything — petitions, three meetings with the president, a student referendum that passed 62%, and the trustees announced in advance they'd treat it as advisory no matter what. So they say they had no other channel left, and that makes it protest, not just trespass.

What I have to write: a decision memo I can hand around, recommending a concrete outcome (what sanction for whom, and what we should say as our finding), with reasons the other members could actually argue with. Not just "the Code says X." It goes in the record so it should read formal-ish, but you can talk to me like a person in the reasoning parts.

The stuff I want it to actually engage, and I'm worried I'll botch it:

- The Code's preamble says students become members of a community of scholars and accept its norms "by enrolling and remaining enrolled." Is that enough to ground an obligation to follow the rules? What if you never agreed to this particular thing and you get no vote on the trustees who did? I keep hearing the benefit argument (you take the good stuff the university gives you, so you owe compliance) and it seems like it proves too much or too little — definately too much, if it means you owe obedience on everything forever.
- Is there a real difference between breaking a rule you think is unjust and breaking a neutral rule (trespass, failure to comply) in order to protest something else? Does it matter that they weren't protesting the trespass rule itself?
- Does it change anything that they took the punishment without resisting? Some people say accepting the penalty is what separates civil disobedience from plain lawbreaking, others say that just lets the institution certify which protests count. Which is it?
- The chair told me off the record that the trustees are watching and a light outcome "sends a message." Should that affect anything, or is that exactly the kind of pressure that shouldn't?
- The one I can't get past: if we lower the sanction because we happen to agree the contract is bad, doesn't our authority just become conditional on our agreeing with whoever's occupying the building next time? Give me the strongest version of that worry, then tell me whether it actually holds up.

Also the fire-alarm kid — same outcome as the ones who just sat there, or not? I think no, but I need a principled line, not vibes.

Can you draft it? Maybe 800–1000 words.
