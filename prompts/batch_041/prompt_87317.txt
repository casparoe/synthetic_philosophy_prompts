I am convening the next meeting of a departmental reading group — seven or eight PhD students in philosophy, none of whom has taken a course in game theory, though two have picked up a little from economics — and the subject is common knowledge of rationality: why epistemic game theory treats it as the condition that licenses the standard solution concepts, and why it is a considerably stronger requirement than it first appears. Do not explain common knowledge to me. I want a plan for teaching it.

The meeting runs ninety minutes and I have a whiteboard. Tell me the order in which the components must be introduced, and why that order rather than another — what has to be in place before the phrase "common knowledge" can do any explanatory work, and what each later piece depends on. Tell me which single example should carry the session. I am tempted by the familiar puzzle in which everyone can see everyone else's mark but not their own, a public announcement appears to add nothing to what each of them already knew, and yet on the appointed night everyone who bears the mark acts at once; but I suspect at least two of the students have met it and will nod along without seeing the point. Advise me whether it should carry the session regardless, and if not, what should replace it, specifying what the replacement must have and what it must lack.

Then the part I care most about. I want the session to end by dislodging the comfortable thought that common knowledge is simply mutual knowledge taken far enough — that if everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on for sufficiently many rounds, one has effectively arrived at the real thing. Here is the construction I intend to use, and I need you to tell me whether it works as stated or whether I must specify the payoffs and the knowledge structure more carefully first. Two agents would each gain if each took a particular risky action, but the action pays off only when you are certain the other will take it as well. Each is rational, each knows the other is rational, each knows that the other knows, and so on, but the chain of higher-order knowledge is only finitely deep — and the claim is that coordination can nonetheless fail, not because anyone is irrational, but because the last link in the chain is never itself known to be in place, so each can reason that had the chain stopped one step earlier the other would have played safe. Plan how to build this so that it lands as genuinely surprising rather than as a conjuring trick, and say plainly if the construction needs repair.

Which misconceptions the students will bring with them and which they will form on the spot — I am expecting "common knowledge just means everyone knows," "more rounds of mutual knowledge approach it," and "common knowledge is psychologically impossible, so the whole framework is a fiction" — and at exactly which point in the sequence I should head off each one. What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and say that I am leaving it out. And a single closing question I can put to them that would reveal whether the distinction has landed, rather than whether they can recite the definition.
