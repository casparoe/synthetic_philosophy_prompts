Start with your verdict — whether the episode confirms Maimonides' account of sacrifice, refutes it, or lands in territory the account never anticipated — and then give the reasoning behind it.

Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed, treats the sacrificial laws as an accommodation to historical circumstance rather than as an expression of what God needs or of what worship essentially is. Animal sacrifice was the universal mode of approaching the divine in the ancient world, the Israelites were steeped in it, and to have abolished it abruptly would have been to demand a psychological impossibility; the Law therefore permitted the practice but redirected it — to one God, one altar, one place, a fixed order, and a rationale that detaches the act from what it superficially appears to be. I am not interested in whether the thesis is pious. I want it tested against an episode.

The episode is the rabbinic response to the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. With the cult impossible, the tradition substituted prayer and the study of the sacrificial order for the offerings themselves: the daily liturgy was mapped onto the daily burnt offering, the recitation of the relevant laws was held to count as their performance, and the words of the lips were read as the replacement for the animals. On the accommodationist reading, this is precisely what should have happened — an institution whose rationale lay in a vanished condition gave way to the practice suited to the new one. Yet the tradition did not treat the cult as outgrown. It preserved the laws as binding, codified them in full, continued to pray for their restoration, and mourned their loss; the one modern movement that acted on something like the accommodationist logic, by dropping the petitions for restoration from its liturgy, was experienced as breaking with the tradition rather than completing it. And Maimonides himself codifies the sacrificial law as eternally binding and holds that the commandments will never be abrogated.

I want the following, in order.

1. The mapping, kept disciplined. Who accommodates whom, and to what — is the agent God, the Law, or a historical tradition reading itself back into the text? What exactly does the thesis predict about the practice once the condition it accommodated disappears? State that prediction sharply enough to be falsifiable, then say whether the episode falsifies it.

2. The episode against three readings I want kept apart: concession (the cult is a concession to weakness, superseded in principle once the weakness is cured), pedagogy (the cult is a vehicle that teaches something about God, atonement, or the redirection of intention, and is disposable once the lesson is learned), and constitution (the cult is intrinsically efficacious and permanently ordained, so its persistence needs no accommodationist defense at all). Say which reading the episode supports and which it embarrasses.

3. Where the theory misdescribes rather than illuminates. The tradition's self-understanding at 70 CE was one of privation and exile, not graduation, and the survival of the laws in study and liturgy looks like the opposite of what a concession to a vanished condition should produce. Note the pull that the thesis's author would resist: an accommodationist account of sacrifice slides naturally toward the view that the cult was fulfilled and abrogated — a reading Maimonides cannot accept. Say what that tension costs him.

4. The counterfactual. Pagan sacrifice largely disappeared with the spread of Christianity and Islam, so the historical condition the thesis cites no longer obtains. Does the persistence of the law refute the thesis, or show that it was only ever an account of why the law was given and never a claim about how long it should last? If the latter, say what work the thesis is still doing.

5. Where the theory is silent — do not force it to speak. If the episode is a case the thesis never anticipated, a commanded practice outliving its stated reason while remaining binding, say so, and say whether Maimonides has resources to absorb it (for instance, that the permanence of a commandment is itself part of what was commanded, or that the reasons for a commandment do not condition its bindingness) and what those resources cost him elsewhere.

Keep the analysis on the episode and the thesis. No bibliography, and no need to survey the secondary literature. Lead with the verdict, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
