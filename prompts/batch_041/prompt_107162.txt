Some years ago I watched a comment thread on aggregation turn into a small disaster, and I have been carrying the wreckage around ever since. Here is the argument I found myself defending. I no longer believe its conclusion, and I want a diagnosis.

(1) An association that holds within each of several subgroups can vanish or reverse when the subgroups are pooled, and an association present in the pooled data can vanish or reverse within every subgroup.

(2) So for any body of data there are at least two partitions of it — pooled, and stratified by some third variable — and the two can disagree about whether X raises the probability of Y.

(3) The data themselves do not tell you which partition to use; the same numbers are equally compatible with both.

(4) If the data leave two partitions equally open, then neither partition is the correct one. The choice between them is a matter of convention or convenience.

(5) A claim that is true only relative to a convention is not a claim about the world but about our bookkeeping.

(6) Therefore, X raises the probability of Y is never a claim about the world. Every such claim is an artifact of how we happened to cut the data.

(7) But (6) proves far too much. It would make the smoking–cancer association an artifact of bookkeeping and a well-run randomized trial no better than a correlation fished out of a convenience sample. Whatever is wrong here, that cannot be right.

So one of (1) through (6) has to go, and I cannot see which. (1) and (2) look to me like arithmetic, and I would like to keep them. My suspicion is that the damage is in the step from (3) to (4) — that the data do not pick is being slid into nothing picks — but I cannot say what does the picking without appealing to something I already believe about causes. If that is the fix, tell me what it costs: what has to be assumed before any of this arithmetic gets off the ground, and whether that assumption is doing legitimate work or is smuggled-in metaphysics. If instead you think the real culprit is (5), tell me why relativizing a probability to a partition is not the same as rendering it arbitrary. And I have a nagging feeling that I am running together the case where the third variable is a common cause with the case where it is a common effect, and that these two cases pull the aggregation question in opposite directions; if so, point to where that distinction should enter the numbered steps, because I cannot find it. Name the step you reject, explain why it looked fine, and price what rejecting it commits me to.
