I am writing a novel whose final chapter turns on an apology, and I have been trying to work out from my own reactions, rather than from any theory, what makes one the real thing. What follows is a list of verdicts I actually hold. I would like the principle, or the small set of principles, that captures the most of them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of; and I would like an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

The postwar German state's apologies and payments for the Holocaust are, for me, the paradigm of a real apology, even though the state that made them presents itself as a break from the state that did the killing. A government apologizing for atrocities committed by the empire it seceded from strikes me as making no apology at all — the wrong is someone else's and the words carry no weight — though I would rather have them than not. A church or a university apologizing for what its own past officers did feels to me more fully an apology than either, and I cannot say why, since the continuity problem looks identical in all three.

Then the small cases. A town council, most of whose members descend from both the perpetrators and the victims of a massacre in that town, apologizing to the town: I feel this is not an apology, and also that it is precisely the right thing to do. An apology for a wrong from which no living person benefits and whose victims left no descendants — addressed, in effect, to the record — I count as a complete apology, more complete than the German one. An apology that arrives as one clause of a legal settlement, with the words dictated by the other side, I count as no apology at all, and I notice that this verdict turns on the motive rather than the wording. An apology whose account of the wrong is a euphemism or a passive construction — that mistakes were made — I count as worse than silence, because it installs a false record where there had been none. A state that has apologized four times for the same wrong, each time a little more fully, makes me feel that the fourth debases the first three. And a state apologizing for a wrong it is still committing: I want to say the apology is impossible rather than insincere.

Two more, which I suspect are the ones that will break whatever principle you give me. A descendant of slaveholders apologizing to descendants of the enslaved moves me and seems to me a real apology; a citizen apologizing to a foreign people for a war their government waged and they themselves opposed seems to me faintly presumptuous. That is backwards, and I know it.

My own working rule is that an apology is real when the one speaking takes the wrong on as theirs to answer for, and hollow when they merely deplore it. That rule dispatches most of the list, but it tells me the town apologizing to itself is a contradiction, which I do not believe, and that the apology addressed to no one is the emptiest rather than the fullest, which I also do not believe.

Tell me what principle, or pair of principles, does best by these verdicts, which case motivates each clause, what it predicts for two further cases I have not listed — please invent them, one involving an institution and one involving a family — and which of my verdicts I should give up. If it comes to a choice between a complicated principle that saves every verdict and a simple one that overturns one or two, tell me which to take, and which scene I should end the book with.
