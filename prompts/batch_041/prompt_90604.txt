For years I have read rationalist blogs and forums, and one habit I picked up there is reverse-engineering the single rule behind a person's individual judgments instead of treating each judgment as free-standing — the same game people play when they try to guess, from a handful of calls, whether someone is running causal, evidential, or timeless decision theory. I want to run that game on a friend's verdicts about laws of nature.

My friend Ravi spent two years in a philosophy of science reading group that kept circling the question of what makes a generalization a law of nature. He has now pronounced on enough cases that I suspect a single underlying view is doing the work. For each item below, the question put to him was simply whether the generalization is a law of nature.

- Every electron has a charge equal in magnitude to the elementary charge: a law.
- Other things being equal, a body in motion continues in uniform motion in a straight line: a law, even though it has exceptions in practice and is at best an idealization.
- Every radium-226 nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying within any given year, independent of its chemical environment: a law.
- The speed of light in vacuum is the same in every inertial frame: a law.
- All gold spheres are less than a mile in diameter: not a law, though he grants it may well be true; the simplest system that covers the facts does not need it as an axiom, and nothing about what gold is rules out a larger sphere.
- All the planets in the solar system orbit in nearly the same plane: not a law; an accident of how the system happened to form.
- About the radium case he adds that the decay rate is fixed by what a radium nucleus is, not by how many of them we happen to have observed.

Now the new case. A physicist friend proposes what she calls a new fundamental law: every particle of a certain hypothetical kind — call it a dion — decays, after a fixed time, into exactly two photons. On our best current physics dions cannot be produced at all, so there are no dions anywhere, and the generalization has no instances. The proposal is not derived from any law we already accept; it is put forward as basic. Ravi is asked whether the generalization "every dion decays into exactly two photons" is a law of nature.

I would like you to tell me:

1. What Ravi will say about the dion case, and why.
2. Which rule he is implicitly running — for instance, a regularity view on which laws are just true universal generalizations; a best-system view on which the laws are the axioms of the simplest and strongest true systematization of the world; an anti-Humean view on which laws flow from the natures of the properties involved and hold of necessity; or something else.
3. Which of those rules fit his earlier verdicts equally well, and whether any of the earlier verdicts already rules one of them out.
4. Your best guess at his view, with your reasons, and whether it treats the laws as contingent or as necessary.
5. If the dion case does not by itself settle which rule he is running, the further case that would, together with what each candidate rule would predict about that case.

Please answer in American English.
