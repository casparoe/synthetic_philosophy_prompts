I spent my working life in an experimental fluids laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science — largely, I now admit, to settle an argument I used to have with myself at the bench. The argument concerns a distinction that philosophers appear to treat as elementary, between a system's being deterministic and its being predictable, and I find I cannot state it in a way that survives contact with my own judgments. I have kept a list of cases and my reactions to them. I would like to know what principle, if any, those reactions express; and if no single principle fits, which of my reactions I ought to abandon.

My verdicts, as they stand:

1. The double pendulum above my desk. Its equations are deterministic: specify the state and the future is fixed. I nonetheless say I cannot predict which way it will swing after ten seconds, and I do not believe a finer instrument would help. I call it unpredictable, and I mean that as a claim about the system, not about my ignorance.

2. A marble rolling in a bowl lined with felt. Deterministic, and I say predictable: it comes to rest at the bottom.

3. The weather a month out. I am told the governing equations are deterministic. I call the month-ahead state unpredictable, though I will cheerfully predict that July will be warmer than January.

4. A fair die. I call the outcome unpredictable, but I say the distribution is predictable, and I feel no tension in saying both.

5. The decay of a single radioactive nucleus. Unpredictable — and here I mean something stronger than in cases 1 and 3. I do not think a perfect predictor with unlimited resources is merely impractical; I think there is no fact of the matter in advance.

6. A system whose initial condition is fixed by the outcome of a genuinely indeterministic quantum measurement, after which it evolves under deterministic, non-chaotic equations. I call the outcome unpredictable, and I do not think the downstream determinism rescues it, even though, once the measurement has occurred, everything follows necessarily.

7. A deterministic process — imagine a cellular automaton — whose future state I can obtain only by running it step by step, with no shortcut. Its outcome is fixed, and its evolution is not sensitive to small errors in the initial state, the rule being discrete and exact. I am reluctant to call it predictable even in principle, and this reluctance embarrasses me, because I can name nothing uncertain about it.

8. A chaotic system in which I care only about a coarse-grained feature — whether the pendulum eventually hangs straight down, say. I call that feature predictable even though the trajectory is not.

The principle I would offer, tentatively, is this: a system is predictable in principle just in case a being who knew its present state exactly, and the laws, could derive its future; and it is unpredictable just in case no such being could. But that seems to make cases 1, 3, and 7 all predictable in principle, contradicting my verdicts, and it leaves case 5 unpredictable for a reason the principle never mentions.

A second principle I have tried: a system is unpredictable just in case arbitrarily small differences in its present state grow into large differences in its future. That seems to save cases 1 and 3. But it seems to make cases 5 and 6 predictable — there is no amplification anywhere in them — and it makes case 7 predictable, which I resist. It says nothing about case 8.

I have also caught myself using "unpredictable" as though it were relative to a question — relative to "which way will it swing" but not to "will it end up at the bottom" — and relative to a tolerance or a resolution. I cannot tell whether that is a repair of the concept or a change of subject.

What I want from you. First, the best principle, or small set of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which case each clause is answering to. Second, its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and in particular on two: (a) a deterministic chaotic system whose present state is known to me only as a finite specification — a finite list of digits, or a finite description in words — so that any finite description I could ever hold underdetermines the outcome, and two predictors armed with descriptions one digit apart may diverge; and (b) a system that is indeterministic at the micro-level but whose coarse-grained statistics are exactly stable and reproducible. Third, an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which of them I should surrender. My suspicion is that cases 1 and 5, and again cases 3 and 6, are doing different work under one word, and that the tidy distinction the philosophers offer is concealing two distinctions. If that is right, say so, and say what the two are.
