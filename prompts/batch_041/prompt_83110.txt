Read E. M. Forster's *The Machine Stops* through the lens of Marx's discussion of fixed capital, general social knowledge, and alienation, particularly the section of the *Grundrisse* known as the fragment on machines. Marx argues there that the development of fixed capital reveals the degree to which general social knowledge has become a direct force of production; that the worker is reduced to a supervisor or regulator beside the production process rather than its chief agent; and that objectified labour comes to confront living labour as an alien power with an apparent life of its own. He also suggests that this same development, by making labour time cease to be the measure of wealth, creates the material condition for liberation.

Forster's story imagines a world in which the general intellect has become the Machine: a global apparatus of communication, provision, and regulation that its inhabitants worship and cannot imagine living without. Vashti and Kuno are not exploited in the classic Marxian sense—there is no visible capitalist class extracting surplus value—yet they are utterly dependent, their bodies and attention disciplined, their direct contact with the world mediated and degraded. Kuno's journey to the surface, his memory of the homeless woman outside the Machine, and the eventual collapse of the system all invite a Marxian reading, but they also strain it.

I want a reading that takes both Marx and Forster seriously and does not reduce either to the other. Where does the story dramatize Marx's account of the general intellect as objectified social knowledge that dominates its producers? Where does it complicate or resist that account—for instance, by depicting a system of domination without an exploiting class, or by giving the Machine a quasi-agency that Marx's categories may not capture? Does the story have anything to say about exploitation, or does it collapse exploitation into alienation? And does Forster's ending vindicate Marx's optimism about the liberatory potential latent in the general intellect, or does it suggest that Marx underestimated how thoroughly social knowledge can be turned against the society that produces it?

Which scenes and characters should I point to in a seminar, and how should I frame them so that students see both what the story gets right about Marx and where it goes beyond or against him? End by saying what *The Machine Stops* adds to the philosophical debate that Marx's own argument does not.
