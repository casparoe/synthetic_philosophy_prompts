So a guy on my team who runs our little Thursday lunch reading group circulated a one-page primer on Goodhart's law last week. Context: we're putting together an eval suite for an internal model and he wants to convince everyone that leaning on numbers is a trap. I've read it three times and I can't find a single sentence I can point at and say thats wrong, which is exactly why I'm nervous — its confident and tidy and it slides from one claim to the next very smoothly, and I have a bad feeling that the smoothness is doing the work. One person in the group muttered that the overfitting sentence was carrying more weight than it looked like it was, but she couldn't say why either. Here's the gist, close to his wording:

"Goodhart's law is named after the economist Charles Goodhart. His most quoted formulation is: 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.' He noticed it at the Bank of England, where statistical relationships that economists had relied on for years fell apart the moment the bank started using those same relationships to steer the economy. Underneath, the law is just overfitting: your metric gets fit to the particular noise of the situation you measured it in, and its correlation with the thing you actually care about decays the harder you push on it. In machine learning the same phenomenon goes by the name specification gaming — you write a reward function, the model finds the loophole, like the racing-game agent that works out it can drive in a loop hitting the same respawning bonus over and over instead of finishing the course. The upshot is that any number you use to evaluate a system will eventually be gamed, so quantitative evaluation is a dead end, and a serious safety team should stop leaning on benchmarks and just... watch the model. (The fix, if you want one, is to pick better metrics.)"

I'm inclined to agree with him, honestly — every metric I've seen at work gets gamed inside a quarter — but "every metric gets gamed" and "quantitative evaluation is a dead end" are not the same claim, and I don't know enough about this stuff to say which of his sentences someone who actually works on specification gaming or on designing evals would sign off on.
