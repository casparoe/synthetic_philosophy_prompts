I'm a retired soil scientist — 35 years on crop yields and drought, mostly in the Sahel — and since I packed it in ive been reading philosophy badly and enjoying it. My reading group is doing a term on famine and I keep ending up in arguments about whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, so I'd like a one-page decision tree I can hand round that sorts people into a position by what they actually say about cases rather than by the label they'd pick for themselves.

Roughly what I want: a top node almost anyone can answer, then the branches being the real fault lines — was there genuinely not enough food to go round, or was there food but people couldn't get at it (Sen's entitlement thing); if it's the latter, does the failure sit in markets and prices or in law and policy; does calling something a crime require that somebody meant the deaths, or is callous negligence enough; does the natural trigger (blight, drought, El Niño) count as an excuse, or become irrelevant once the shortfall was visible and preventable; and does the state's actual capacity to have saved people matter — i.e. could a competent response have prevented the deaths. Each node should be a question you can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

For the leaves, use named positions where a name exists and 'unnamed' where it doesn't: the Malthusian/food-availability view, Sen's entitlement view, de Waal's famine-crimes line, the Davis argument in Late Victorian Holocausts (the El Niño famines book), the genocide/deliberate-atrocity reading people apply to the Great Leap Forward, and whatever the international-law crowd says now that starvation counts as a weapon. Use Bengal 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward as the test cases every leaf has to say something about. And flag any two positions the tree can't really separate, plus any combinations of answers nobody occupies, with your guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just untried. Keep it to a page if you can.
