Question 3. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem states that any social choice function that is onto, is defined over at least three alternatives, and is strategy-proof must be dictatorial. (a) State the theorem precisely and explain what work each of its conditions does. (b) Explain how the theorem stands in relation to Arrow's impossibility theorem. (c) A colleague circulates a memo urging the department to adopt plurality rule for its hiring decisions, on the grounds that plurality is strategy-proof and therefore escapes the theorem's reach. Evaluate the memo's argument and describe at least one genuine route of escape from the theorem.

Student's answer. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that if a social choice function ranges over at least three alternatives, is onto, and is strategy-proof, then it is dictatorial: there is some single voter whose most-preferred alternative is the outcome, no matter what the other voters report. Each condition earns its keep. With only two alternatives, majority rule is strategy-proof and no one voter dictates, so three alternatives is the threshold; and if the rule is not onto, a rule that simply ignores everyone and always names a fixed alternative is strategy-proof without being dictatorial. Strategy-proofness itself means truth-telling is a dominant strategy — reporting one's true preferences is optimal whatever the others do — which is stronger than merely requiring that truth-telling be a Nash equilibrium. The result is really Arrow's impossibility theorem in game-theoretic dress: Arrow showed that no social welfare function over three or more alternatives satisfies IIA, Pareto, and non-dictatorship, and Gibbard–Satterthwaite shows that the impossibility resurfaces once voters report strategically, so it inherits Arrow's IIA requirement. The standard escape is to restrict the preference domain; on the single-peaked domain the median-voter rule is strategy-proof and plainly non-dictatorial, so it is the unrestricted domain, not aggregation as such, that drives the impossibility. My colleague's proposal is therefore sensible: plurality rule is strategy-proof — with three or more candidates a voter can never do better by misreporting than by naming her favourite — so switching to it would put the department beyond the theorem's reach. The lesson is not that democratic aggregation is impossible, but that we must choose rules with the right structural properties.
