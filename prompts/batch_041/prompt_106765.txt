I am preparing a session on uncertain evidence for an intermediate epistemology seminar, and I would like a model dialogue that my students can dissect line by line. The two interlocutors are a strict Bayesian, who holds that all genuine learning proceeds by conditionalizing on propositions that are learned with certainty, and a defender of Jeffrey conditionalization, who holds that when an experience shifts the probabilities across a partition without rendering any single cell certain, the correct response is to reweight the cells of that partition while holding fixed the conditional probabilities within each cell. Please have them work through one concrete case rather than trading abstractions: an art authenticator inspects a canvas under poor light and comes away judging that it is more likely than not a forgery, though she is far from sure, and she then reads a second, only partially reliable report bearing on the same question. The strict Bayesian should press the familiar line that her visual experience is itself something she is certain about — she can simply conditionalize on the proposition that the canvas looked thus-and-so — while the Jeffrey conditionalizer should explain why that move either relocates the uncertainty without dissolving it, or else smuggles in a likelihood for the experience that the agent never actually possessed.

I would also like the dialogue to reach the harder, less frequently rehearsed issues, so that it does not merely restate the textbook contrast. In particular, let them explore whether the two rules agree when the evidence is absorbed in different orders: a strict update applied in stages is order-insensitive, whereas a sequence of Jeffrey shifts can yield different posteriors depending on which partition is revised first, and I want them to press whether that order-sensitivity is a genuine defect of the rule or a symptom that the partitions being revised were not rigid — that is, that the conditional probabilities internal to the cells were themselves disturbed by the earlier update. Have the Jeffrey conditionalizer also confront the question of how one is supposed to identify the correct partition in the first place, and the strict Bayesian acknowledge the awkward cases (testimony from a partially reliable source, vague perceptual judgments, memory) in which no proposition appears to be learned with certainty. Both characters should be fully competent and should answer each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another; neither should be set up to lose. The dialogue should be reasonably compact but substantive, and should end in a genuine standoff or a precisely stated residual disagreement rather than a tidy resolution.
