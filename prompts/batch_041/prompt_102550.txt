I have been asked to give the wedding toast for two close friends, and I want to build it around a problem that has haunted me since I took a seminar on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism—the objection that a person who saves his wife only after asking whether impartial morality permits it has had one thought too many. The couple met in that seminar; one of them is now a sophisticated consequentialist who believes the objection can be answered by a suitably indirect theory, while the other is a particularist who thinks it shows that impartial morality simply has no jurisdiction over intimate relations. They have asked me, only half seriously, not to turn the toast into a defense of their relationship in terms of overall value. I want to honor that request without falling into the opposite error: a toast that offers no reasons at all, that treats their love as a brute fact beyond justification, can feel evasive and even a little cold to the non-philosophers in the room, who will want to hear something true about why these two people belong together.

Please write the five-minute toast I should give. It needs to be warm and specific enough for a mixed audience—roughly half the guests are philosophers who will catch every allusion, and half are family members who have never read a word of moral philosophy—and it needs to let Williams's point do real structural work, shaping what I can and cannot say rather than being mentioned and then abandoned. I would like the toast to distinguish between two ways a speech about a couple can go wrong: the failure of having one thought too many, where I justify their love by its impartial value, and the opposite failure, where I refuse to articulate anything about why they are good for each other. Consider building in a moment where I begin to justify their love by saying that they make each other better people, and then pull back—not because the claim is false, but because that kind of justification is exactly what Williams warned against. The toast should find a way between the two failures, perhaps by speaking from inside the relationship rather than from the standpoint of an impartial spectator, and it should end in a way that feels like a blessing rather than a conclusion. You may name Williams once, if it helps, but the speech itself should be comprehensible and moving to the relatives who have no idea what a utilitarian is.
