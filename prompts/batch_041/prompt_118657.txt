I write a column on philosophy and the sciences of mind for a general-interest ideas magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about the ethics of criticism — about the old intuition that someone can be right about what you did and still not be the one who gets to say it, and about the newer dispute over what hypocrisy does to blame: whether it strips the blamer of the standing to blame at all, or leaves the standing intact and merely spoils the blame's force, sincerity, or uptake. I want to open the piece with a joke, because the structure of the problem is easier to feel than to state, and I would rather readers laugh at it than nod at a summary of it.

So: write me one joke. Make it a short concrete scene with two or three people in it — a dinner, a meeting, a group chat, whatever suits — in which the humor comes from the logic of standing rather than from name-calling or from the well-worn look-who's-talking gag, which I don't want. The punchline should turn on a point readers will not have met in the usual treatment: that an accusation of hypocrisy is itself a piece of blame, so whoever levels it is exposed to the same question about their own standing, and the scene should exploit that regress rather than merely mention it. If the joke can also turn on the difference between the two main ways of understanding what hypocrisy does to blame — that it removes the authority to blame versus that it leaves the blame standing but ruins its force or its welcome — so much the better, since that contrast is the spine of the piece. It should be accurate enough that a moral philosopher would recognize the positions rather than wince, and self-contained enough that a reader who has never heard the phrase "standing to blame" can still laugh and then, a beat later, feel the problem. Don't name any philosophers and don't allude to any particular article or book; I can't verify attributions and I don't want the joke to lean on them.

Keep it under about 250 words, and write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no labels like "setup" and "punchline," and no explanation afterward. If the distinction needs explaining, it needs to be inside the scene. Just tell it the way you would tell it out loud to a friend who reads.
