I write about probability and decision theory for a general readership, and I am preparing a long-form interview for my podcast with a philosopher who has spent much of his career defending a modern version of the principle of indifference. His position, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: a credence is rationally assessable only relative to a fully specified chance setup; when the description of an experiment fixes a symmetry group, indifference is not merely permitted but required, and those symmetries single out a unique assignment of probabilities; and the classic problem of choosing a chord of a circle at random — by picking two points on the circumference, or by picking a point on a radius and drawing the perpendicular chord, or by picking a point in the disk to serve as the chord's midpoint — embarrasses the principle not at all, because the original question simply failed to specify the chance setup. He is, I should add, impatient with the suggestion that this defence is vacuously true, that "the problem was ill-posed" can be said of any paradox whatever.

I would like eight to ten questions that get past a restatement of the view. At least one question must press on those three ways of choosing a chord at random and on what exactly is supposed to be wrong with each; at least one must press the reparametrisation difficulty — a quantity that is uniform on its own range is not uniform in a monotone function of it — and ask what, on his account, fixes the right parametrisation; at least one must press the charge that calling a problem ill-posed is a way of never being wrong; at least one must confront him with a puzzle I have devised myself, described below; at least one must raise the concession, which he grants, that when the relevant symmetries form a group under which no probability measure is invariant — translations of the whole real line, for instance — his recipe delivers nothing at all, and ask how large a concession that is; and at least one must ask whether his symmetry principle is anything more than a special case of exchangeability, the idea that a sequence of observations has a probability unaffected by their order and that this alone, by a representation theorem, licenses treating them symmetrically without any separate appeal to indifference. For every question I also want the reply he is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply. The final question should ask what evidence or argument could count against his position.

The puzzle I have devised, to be put to him within one of the questions: a machine produces squares, and all I know about any given square is that its side length falls somewhere between 0 and 1. If indifference over the side length is licensed, the side is uniform on that interval, and then, conditional on the side being less than one half, the probability that it is less than one quarter is one half. If instead indifference over the area is licensed, the area is uniform on its own range, and the same conditional probability is one quarter. Both parametrisations look equally natural, and the answers disagree. I want the question to make him say which he would license and why, and the follow-up to press whatever answer he gives.

Please return the result as a single JSON object of the following shape. The top level has a key "guest_position", whose value is a two- or three-sentence summary, in your own words, of the view being interrogated, and a key "questions", whose value is an array of objects, one per question, each with the keys: "id", an integer giving the order in which the questions should be asked; "question", the question itself, phrased as I would put it out loud; "presses", one sentence naming the specific commitment of his that the question is meant to strain; "anticipated_reply", one or two sentences giving the answer he is most likely to give; and "follow_up", the question to ask next if he gives that reply. After the "questions" array, include a key "counterexample_test", whose value is the single question that would most clearly draw out what he would count as a refutation, phrased so that it fits at the end of the interview.
