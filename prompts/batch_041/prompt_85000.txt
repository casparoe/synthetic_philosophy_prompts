I am convening a seminar on history and narrative and want to close it with a poem that makes the argument the prose has only circled: whether the past has a shape of its own or receives one from the historian. The form, not the opinions, should carry the argument. Take a single small episode — a strike that failed, a flood, a shop closing after three generations, anything whose bare record historians could agree on — and invent it rather than borrowing a famous one, since a reader who already knows how the story ends will be doing part of the emplotting for you. Write five quatrains. In the first four, the same events, the same actors, the same order, recur unchanged; what changes is the plot they are cast in — first a romance, then a tragedy, then a comedy, then a satire. Keep the metre and the rhyme scheme identical from quatrain to quatrain, so that nothing distinguishes them but the shape of the story, and make the fourth line of each the same refrain, word for word. That refrain is the poem's one fixed point: flat and factual enough to survive all four settings, and made to mean four different things without a syllable of it being altered. The fifth quatrain belongs to the writer of the first four, who turns on the refrain and asks what it was ever picking out — whether it is the residue of fact that outlasted the four emplotments or only the thinnest sentence the four accounts could be brought to agree on — and who does not answer. Break the pattern there: the refrain may return, but not in its usual place. A reader who has followed the dispute from Ranke's ambition to show the past as it actually was, through the objection that facts become historical only once a historian selects and ranks them, to the claim that the historian has already cast the record in one of a few master plots before the evidence is sorted, should be able to identify each quatrain without being told, and should find the satire distinguishable from the comedy. Let the seminar's disagreement survive the last line intact.
