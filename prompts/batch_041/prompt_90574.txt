I write about science and ideas, and I'm pressure-testing a thesis for a piece on contingency in history. The thesis is this: the large outcomes we care about — which empires dominate, which languages and religions spread, which societies industrialize first — are radically contingent, so that small, morally arbitrary perturbations (a rider delayed by a day, a shift in the wind, one translation chosen over another) would have sent the whole trajectory elsewhere, and this is a claim about the world rather than merely about our ignorance of it. I want the strongest case that it is false. Which considerations should genuinely move me — for instance, evidence that such outcomes are overdetermined, reachable by many independent paths, so that the triggering event is arbitrary but the destination is not; or the idea, borrowed from dynamical systems, that a process can be chaotic at the level of events while still being drawn toward a few robust macro-attractors, which would make the missing-nail intuition systematically misleading. Rank these from decisive to merely awkward, say which of them a committed contingency theorist would concede as counting against the view, and tell me whether the thesis can be stated so that it risks anything at all — or whether it quietly collapses into the truism that something had to happen.
