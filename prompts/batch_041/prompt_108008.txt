I'm drafting a position paper for my seminar on the ethics of suffering, and before I commit to a line I want a consistency audit of my current view set. Here is roughly where I've landed; treat each item as something I would actually defend in the paper.

(1) Welfare hedonism: the only things that are intrinsically good or bad for a person are episodes of enjoyment and episodes of suffering.

(2) Commensurability: enjoyment and suffering lie on a single scale of felt goodness, and for any episode of suffering there is some finite quantity of enjoyment that exactly offsets it. They are the same stuff with opposite signs.

(3) Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: there is a level of suffering — call it the threshold — such that no amount of enjoyment, for anyone, can justify inflicting suffering below it. Above the threshold, ordinary trade-offs between suffering and enjoyment are allowed.

(4) No sharp joints: the moral weight of suffering varies continuously with its intensity. In particular, there is no intensity at which the exchange rate between avoiding suffering and producing enjoyment jumps from finite to infinite.

(5) Uniform aggregation: the same arithmetic governs trade-offs within a single life and trade-offs across different lives. The separateness of persons determines who gets what, not how the numbers add up.

(6) On the case that started me thinking about this: I would trade one hour of intense headache for one year of mild delight, and I would make the same trade on behalf of a child in my care.

Which of these can survive together? Please tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look that way, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say which commitment has to go and what the cheapest repair is — the revision that preserves the most of what I have said, and what it costs me elsewhere. If a single revision would clear up more than one tension, say so and say what that revision gives up in exchange. I don't want a survey of options; take a position on what I should drop. Keep the whole reply to 500 words or fewer, in American English.
