I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy training, but I've somehow ended up in a reading group that's doing Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day next month, and the theme we picked is self-knowledge — which is a bit funny, since the whole book is about a man who doesn't seem to have any. I saw the film years ago so I roughly know how it goes, but I'm reading the novel properly for the first time. While googling around I keep running into two ideas I'd like to use as lenses rather than just decoration. The first is the KK principle — if you know something, you can know that you know it. The second is luminosity, the stronger claim that any mental state you're in is one you're in a position to know you're in. I read somewhere that there's an argument that nothing interesting is luminous, roughly because our judgments about our own minds get unreliable right at the edges of a state — you can be in some discomfort without being able to tell whether it's discomfort or pain — and that this isn't just a gap in information but a structural limit on self-monitoring. I'm probably mangling it, so correct me where I'm wrong, and while you're at it explain whether 'being in a position to know' is doing any real work here versus just plain knowing, and whether luminosity is meant to be different from infallibility, because I can't tell if that distinction matters for the novel or is just terminology.

What I actually want is a reading of the novel through these ideas, not a summary of them. Stevens narrates his own life to us for hundreds of pages while evidently not knowing his own feelings about Miss Kenton, or why he treated her the way he did, and the last pages suggest he half-recongnises something without ever quite landing on it. So which is it: does the novel dramatise anti-luminosity, a mind that was never transparent to itself, or does it dramatise self-deception, where the knowledge was available the whole time and he simply wouldn't look at it? Those strike me as very different diagnoses and I want you to commit to one, with the scenes that actually settle it. Show me where the lens lights something up — the long stretches where he's reporting and plainly not seeing, the part near the end where he resolves to learn to banter with his new American employer — and also where the reading strains. My suspicion is that a first-person retrospective novel is an awkward place to test a thesis about knowledge of one's own mind, since the whole thing is filtered through the very consciousness whose transparency is in question, but you tell me. And if the novel shows something the argument on its own can't — what this looks like lived out across forty years rather than at a single moment — say that too. No quotes needed, I have the book.
