I'm in a political theory seminar that's been grinding through On Liberty for three weeks, and I keep bumping into a slogan I've realized I don't actually understand. It's the fist-and-nose one: you're free to swing your fist around as much as you like until it lands on my nose, and that's supposed to be the harm principle in a single sentence. Two recent uses of it have stuck with me because they pull in opposite directions. In section, a guy said a campus speech code banning "harassment" is obviously fine by that logic, since words can be a fist and the nose is the target's dignity or safety or whatever, so there's no free speech issue at all. Then my roommate used the exact same saying to argue that our city's noise ordinance is illegitimate, beacuse his speakers only reach other people's ears, not their noses, and ears don't count. Both felt like they were cheating somehow, and I couldn't say why. I suspect they're smuggling in different claims under one sentence.

Could you lay out the different precise things that one-liner could be claiming, from the trivial to the radical? I want the whole menu, not a verdict: what the "swing" is (conduct, expression, or both), what counts as a nose (bodily harm, setback to interests, offense, dignitary injury, mere discomfort), who gets to decide where the nose is, and what the saying implies about the non-harm grounds for restriction — paternalism, moralism, offense — since that's where I think the real disagreement is hiding. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false, and then tell me which reading each of my two friends was actually leaning on and whether it gets them their conclusion. I'd especially like you to flag any reading under which the saying is a tautology nobody could deny, since I'm fairly sure that's what's happening in at least one of the cases, and a short take on what Mill himself would do with the speech-code argument would be a nice bonus. No citations needed, just the arguments.
