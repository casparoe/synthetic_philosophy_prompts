So my buddy and I got into a long fight last night about Aristotle's bit on money in the Ethics — the stretch where he says money exists by convention and not by nature, and yet also that money is the measure that makes goods commensurable, and that what really holds exchange together is need. He thinks the whole thing collapses. Here's the argument he built, and I couldn't get out of it:

(1) A just exchange has to swap equal values.
(2) Things can only be equal in value if they're commensurable, i.e. share one common measure.
(3) The only real common measure is need — that's what actually makes a house and a pair of shoes comparable at all.
(4) But need isn't a fixed quantity; it shifts with the person, the moment, the situation, so it can't serve as a unit.
(5) Money supplies the missing unit, but only by convention — it isn't by nature, and the law can revalue it or void it.
(6) So the thing that makes goods commensurable (money) isn't the thing that makes them comparable (need).
(7) So the equality a just exchange lands on is an equality set by convention, not by the goods themselves.
(8) And if the equality is set by convention, then 'just price' just means 'whatever price the convention settles on', and there's nothing left for exchange justice to get right or wrong.
(9) But Aristotle plainly thinks there's a fact of the matter — the just in exchange is a real mean, a determinate proportion, not a legal artifact.
(10) So either his account is incoherent, or there's no natural justice in exchange at all and any price custom picks is just by definition.

I dont buy (10), and I'd rather keep (9) if I have to choose. But I can't find the slip — it looks airtight and yet it proves way more than he wants. Which step goes, why did it look fine, and what do I pay to drop it? And tell me whether the fix is really just a patch about money or whether it's a deeper problem for his whole idea of the mean.
