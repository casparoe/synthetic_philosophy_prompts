Two positions are frequently run together as a single claim about consciousness, and I suspect they are not the same claim. The first is Dennett's: the picture of an inner subject watching a show, and the accompanying notion of intrinsic qualia out of which the show is made, are both mistakes. What we call consciousness is a user illusion in the sense of a well-designed interface, one that hides the machinery beneath it and presents a simplified model of what the system is doing, with no further subject over and above the processes that generate the reports. The second is the position Keith Frankish calls illusionism: phenomenality is an introspective illusion, because introspection systematically misrepresents our inner states as possessing phenomenal properties, and once that is recognized we should abandon phenomenal realism while retaining the states themselves.

Compare these two. Where precisely do they come apart, and are the differences substantive or terminological? In particular, does the second position require a subject to whom the illusion is presented, in a way the first forbids, and does the first forfeit the right to call anything an illusion at all? Which of the two is the more stable position, and which of the usual objections to illusionism — that an illusion presupposes something it is like to be fooled, that the view merely redescribes the explanandum, that it cannot explain how an introspective judgment manages to be about anything — lands on one of them but not the other?

Give me a verdict rather than a survey, and tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I have no interest in citations; work with the positions as I have stated them.
