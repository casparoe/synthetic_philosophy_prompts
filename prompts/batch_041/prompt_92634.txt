Before I take up any more accounts of free-floating rationales, I want the specification. The notion I am working with is this: a trait, signal, or mechanism can have a rationale — a why-it-is-there and what-it-is-for that supports evaluation as good or bad reasoning — even though no agent anywhere entertains it, and even though the system that carries it may be incapable of representing anything at all. What I want from you is not a survey of positions but the desiderata: what any adequate account of free-floating rationales must deliver.

Fix adequacy relative to this purpose. The account must be usable (a) by biologists who want to describe organic design in teleological terms and to say when a design is better or worse, and (b) by philosophers who want that talk to be more than a useful fiction while refusing to posit a hidden reasoner. It need not, and should not, vindicate the claim that reasons exist in the world independent of any mind.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, grouped as follows.

Cases the account must get right. At minimum: an adaptation whose rationale is executed by a lineage rather than by any individual (no organism computes it, yet it is there and can go right or wrong); a signal whose point is to deceive or exploit its receiver (a plant recruiting the enemies of its attacker, a parasite steering its host); an artifact whose rationale is fixed by its designer but not by the artifact itself (a lock, a thermostat); a by-product that invites a teleological description it has not earned; and a physical or formal system that optimizes without having been selected to do so (a surface that minimizes area, a path that minimizes distance), which forces the question whether rationale requires a selection history or only an optimization story. Add any case you think the list is missing, and say why it is not already covered.

Distinctions the account cannot collapse. For each, say what breaks if it collapses: between a rationale being present and its being grasped; between a rationale that is an adaptation and one that is merely a by-product or a side effect; between a bad rationale and the absence of any rationale; between the rationale for a token behavior and the rationale for the mechanism that produces it; and between a reason-for and a reason-of — the whole point being that these are reasons without a subject, and an account that either assimilates them to ordinary reasons or reduces them to bare causation has failed at the task.

Results it must be consistent with. Name the findings from biology and cognitive science that the account may not contradict, and say why each constrains it.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp — the etiological (selection-history) camp against the stance-based or as-if camp, say — and flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; that flag is the part I most want. End by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate accounts, and say briefly which of the standard families falls to it. Keep the scoring to a sentence or two per family; the list is the deliverable.
