I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, working on constitutional monarchy and the fictions that keep it upright, and I've got an episode I want to run through a theory -- partly to see if the theory earns its keep, partly because I can't tell whether I'm fooling myself.

The episode: Norway, 1905. The Storting passes a law giving Norway its own consular service; Oscar II refuses to sanction it; the Michelsen cabinet resigns; and on 7 June the Storting declares the union with Sweden dissolved and Oscar no longer king of Norway. A plebiscite in August ratifies it, Sweden recognises it in October, and the Norwegians import a Danish prince as Haakon VII. It's always told as the quiet, legal ending -- no revolution, no heads on pikes -- but what hooked me is that the royal refusal just sort of evaporated, and nobody had to do anything about it.

The theory I want to use is Kantorowicz's old legal fiction of the king's two bodies: the body natural (a mortal man with his own will and conscience, subject to infirmity) and the body politic (invisible, never dies, void of the defects of the natural body, and what the king does in it can't be undone by any disability of the other). It's the machinery under the idea that the king can do no wrong and under blame sliding down onto the ministers; the 1814 Norwegian constitution has the same shape -- the King's person sacred and unblameable, responsibility on his council.

So map it: whose body natural refused, whose body politic was preserved when the Storting said he'd stopped being king? Is the fiction actually explaining why the crisis took this shape, or am I just redressing the standard story (personal union plus parliamentary sovereignty) in medieval clothes? Then push back on the theory: it was built for one immortal realm with the two bodies glued together in one man forever, and here you've got a body politic surviving a surgical seperation -- and the same body natural was carrying a second body politic, Sweden's, at the same time. Confirmation, anomaly, or something the theory never imagined? And does it matter that the fiction was never part of Norwegian legal culture -- can it be exported at all, or does using it commit me to a story about the Crown that the Norwegians didn't share?

Be disciplined about the mapping and say where the theory goes quiet instead of making it speak. End with a single line starting 'Verdict:' followed by one sentence saying whether 1905 confirms the two-bodies picture, is an anomaly it can't absorb, or falls outside its scope.
