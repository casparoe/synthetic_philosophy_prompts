Something's been bugging me about how we treat translated poetry and I want to figure out what view of poems, and of meaning, the practice assumes.

Here's the practice. A literary journal runs a translation of a short, tightly rhymed 16th century French poem. The translator says openly in a note that they changed some of the literal sense, dropped an image, and added a word that isn't in the original at all -- all so the English would rhyme and scan like the French does. The reviewer calls it the best English version we have, and noone accuses them of mistranslating. In the same issue, a translator of a novel inserts one sentence the author never wrote, just to smooth a transition, and gets roasted for unfaithfulness. Same journal, same month.

Then there's a third pattern: when the same poem gets translated to be *sung*, everyone agrees the words should be bent to fit the tune even if the sense drifts a fair way. But the translation printed in the liner notes is expected to track the French line by line. So the same text is bendable in one medium and not in another, and we don't seem to think we're contradicting ourselves.

What does this cluster of practices presuppose? Does it presuppose that in a poem the form *is* part of the meaning, so you can't keep the meaning while dropping the form -- and that prose has no such property? Does it presuppose that the unit of faithfulness is the whole work rather than the sentence or the line, so a loss in one place can be paid for by a gain somewhere else? Or could you explain the whole thing with something much thinner, e.g. that we just judge a translation by what the reader is going to do with it -- read it silently, sing it, or set it beside the original and compare?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which parts the practice actually needs and which rival theories it would sit just as happily with, and say where it's genuinely underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change in reviewing and publishing if we swapped in the view that form is decoration and only the sense is the poem.
