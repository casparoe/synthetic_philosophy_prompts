I am a high school student finishing a poetry portfolio for a seminar on kinds and boundaries, and for the last piece I would like to write about the species problem. What interests me is not the familiar complaint that biologists disagree, but the particular shape of the disagreement: the interbreeding criterion cannot get a grip on organisms that reproduce without sex; a criterion built on shared ancestry tells you about relationships but not about where one lineage stops and the next begins; and where a lineage changes gradually, any line drawn across it looks like a decision rather than a discovery. And yet the word is indispensable in field guides, in conservation law, and in ordinary conversation.

I would like the poem to be a pantoum: sixteen lines in four quatrains, in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain gathers lines from the opening one. The form's rule should hold exactly through the first three quatrains and then break in exactly one place in the last — a line coming back altered, or not coming back at all. The break should be the argument, not a slip: the form keeps its promise that everything returns and closes, and the biology does not.

A few constraints, because my biology teacher has agreed to read this and will notice if the science is only decorative:

- Build the poem around a ring of populations separated by some barrier, in which each population interbreeds with its immediate neighbours, but the two ends of the ring meet without interbreeding. Keep it generic — an unnamed valley or chain of islands, unnamed organisms — rather than naming a real taxon, since I do not trust myself to get the details of a real case right.
- Keep apart two questions I keep collapsing: what the word means in general, and where this particular boundary falls. I would like at least one recurring line to slide between them.
- Keep in play the thought that a kind may be less like a class of things sharing a definition and more like a particular thing with a history, so that calling two populations the same kind is closer to calling two stretches of water the same river than to calling two samples the same element.
- Do not resolve it. Neither "so the kinds are real after all" nor "so the kinds are merely conventional" should be available at the end; I want the poem to leave the specific discomfort that the word is at once indispensable and undefined.
- The word species should appear in the title but never in the body of the poem — I want the poem to have to do the work without the label.

Please add a short prose note afterwards, no more than six sentences, identifying which recurring line carries which of these jobs and where the form breaks, so that I can annotate the piece for my teacher. And if the constraints begin to make it a diagram rather than a poem, tell me which one to drop and write the poem without it.
