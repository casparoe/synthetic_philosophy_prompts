I've been circling the heterophenomenology thing for years now -- ever since I first ran into Dennett's version of it, and then got into a running argument about it with a friend of mine who's a died-in-the-wool hard-problem person. We've had this fight maybe twenty times and neither of us has ever moved an inch, and I'm starting to think we're not even disagreeing about the thing we think we're disagreeing about.

Here's the shape of it as best I can state both sides fairly. Dennett's move, as I understand it: when you want a science of consciousness, you treat the subject's reports as data the way you'd treat a text -- you record what they say, you don't take it as a transparent window onto a special inner realm with its own proprietary evidence. You build up the intentional stance, you explain the report-producing machinery, you correlate and predict, and whatever isn't explained yet is just unexplained, not a permanent mystery. My friend's objection: that's either circular or it's a relabeling. If you rule out in advance that "this is what the red looks like to me" is evidence about anything except my dispositions to say stuff, then you've baked the conclusion in, because the very thing we wanted to explain was how it seems to me. And she adds that no pile of third-person facts about discrimination, verbal report, and behavior ever adds up to there being something it's like in the first place -- you could have all of it and still be a zombie, so the method has smuggled the hard part out of the explanandum and called that progress.

So: is this a real, substantive disagreement, or a verbal one about the word "data" / "explanation"? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading -- one doing a science of how people come to say things about their experiences, the other asking a question that no third-person method could ever touch? Is there a hidden empirical question buried in there that neither side has made explicit (something about whether first-person reports carry information that third-person measures can't recover)? Or is it bedrock: one side just doesn't have the intuition that there's a further fact left over, and the other just does, and there's no argument that gets either of them across?

I have a working hypothesis, but I want you to treat it as a hypothesis and not a promt to agree with me: I suspect the fight isn't really about method at all, it's about what a scientific explanation is allowed to leave out, and about whether the explanandum gets its authority from the subject or from the theorist. If that's right, tell me what follows.

Give me a diagnosis, not a survey -- engage what each side actually says, name the kind of disagreement you think this is, and be honest about whether anything would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question, or just the admission that we should stop expecting either side to convince the other.
