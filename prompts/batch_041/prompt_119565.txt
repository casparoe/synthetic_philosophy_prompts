Our committee — two historians, a philosopher of science, a corpus linguist, and me — has two years and a modest budget to build a tool that assigns period labels (medieval, early modern, and the like) to a large multilingual corpus of European and non-European documents. The recurring discovery is that our real disagreements are never about the classifier; they are about what we take the labels to be doing. The familiar critique — that such periods are Eurocentric, teleological, and retrospective — we already accept, and I would rather not see it rehearsed again.

What I want is a defended research agenda: which open conceptual questions about periodization we should actually spend those two years resolving, in what order, and why. Treat this as a prioritization problem and be explicit about the criteria you use — how upstream a question is, whether philosophers can make progress on it without new data, how neglected it is, and how much would change downstream if we got an answer. Candidates I have been turning over, though you should not feel bound by them:

- whether a period is better read as a descriptive label for a cluster of features or as an explanatory hypothesis that posits a unifying mechanism, and what each reading implies for how a label could be validated;
- the grain question: whether there is a fact of the matter about how coarse a period should be, or whether this is like asking how many species there are — settled by purpose rather than by nature;
- the performative worry: that archives, curricula, and funding follow the labels, so that the periods become more real over time precisely because we kept using them, leaving it unclear whether we are documenting a distinction or manufacturing one;
- the cross-cutting problem: that art, climate, technology, and economy may each carry their own periodization cutting across the others, so that a single Middle Ages for the whole corpus might be a category error rather than a rough approximation;
- the residue problem: what to do with documents that do not fit — call them transitional, call them anomalies, or take them as evidence that the period, or the corpus, is misdescribed.

For each question you put on the agenda, say what makes it worth the years, which of the others it is upstream of, and which of my candidates are red herrings we should drop. Then rank the agenda, name the trade-offs the ranking makes, and tell me what we could learn in the first six months that would move a question up or down the list. I care about the reasoning, not the inventory.
