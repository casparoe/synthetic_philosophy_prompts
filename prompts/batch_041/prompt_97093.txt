I am the registrar of a small regional museum, and I have been trying to settle something that keeps arising in our insurance schedules, condition reports, and accession records. When a bronze sculpture in our collection is damaged, we are expected to record two things: the artwork, which is carried at a very high value, and the bronze itself, which has a scrap value and a composition and a weight. Conservators speak of repairing the metal; curators speak of restoring the work. I have begun to suspect that this is institutional superstition rather than a real distinction. The sculpture and the bronze occupy the same region of space, are the same atoms in the same arrangement, have the same mass, the same profile, the same color, and every measurement I could take of one I could take of the other. If two things share all of their properties then they are one thing — the identity of indiscernibles, which I take Leibniz's law to require — and so 'the sculpture' and 'the bronze' must be two descriptions of a single object, not two objects. On that view, our records are double-counting, and a damaged bronze sculpture is just a lump of bronze that we happen to describe as a sculpture.

Our one awkward case, which I keep turning over, is a piece of driftwood that we mounted and catalogued as a sculpture in 2019. The wood, obviously, is far older than the artwork, and it survived for decades on a beach being nothing in particular. I had thought this supported my view — the wood simply acquired a new description — but I confess I cannot make it sit comfortably with the other cases, and I suspect it is the key to whatever I am missing.

Before I argue at our next policy meeting that we should treat each bronze sculpture as one object under two descriptions, I want to know whether there is any principled reason to think there really are two objects here, or whether the entire debate about material constitution is just the elementary mistake of treating a change of description as a change of object. And if I am wrong, I would like to see precisely where the inference from 'same properties' to 'same object' fails — because as far as I can tell, the sculpture and the bronze are indiscernible, and I cannot find the property that one has and the other lacks.
