A philosopher I have been interviewing for a piece on the mind handed me the following argument and invited me to find its fault. I have been staring at it for a week and keep losing my place, so I would like a proper diagnosis.

He frames it as a dilemma about intentionality. Call a device a coin-checker: it is built to pass a metal disc only when the disc is a genuine quarter, and it sits inside a vending machine. Consider two such devices with physically identical internal states and identical manufacturing histories. One is installed in an American city and reliably responds to quarters; the other is installed across the border and reliably responds to a slug of the same size and conductivity. Intuitively we want to say that the first device's internal state is about quarters and the second's is about slugs. Now take a third device, physically identical to the other two at the moment of its first use, but shipped from the American plant to the border town after manufacture. Its history straddles both settings, and its future use is as yet undetermined.

He then argues as follows.

(1) For any state to count as being about something — for it to have content at all — there must be a fact of the matter as to what it is about.

(2) That fact cannot be fixed by the state's intrinsic physical properties, since physically identical states can occur in environments that differ, and we want to allow that their content can differ too.

(3) So content must be fixed by something relational: either by the environment the device currently occupies, or by its design and training history, or by the interests and interpretive stance of whoever attributes content to it.

(4) The current environment cannot do it, because the third device has not yet been used in either setting and we still want a determinate answer as to what it is about.

(5) Design history cannot do it either, because the third device's history is a mixture and, at the moment of first use, is no more a history of quarter-detecting than of slug-detecting.

(6) So the only remaining candidate is the interpreter's stance: what the state is about is projected by us rather than discovered in the device.

(7) But nothing in this reasoning turns on the device being made of silicon rather than neurons. Substitute a brain in the relevant respect and the same argument runs.

(8) So the content of any state, including our beliefs and desires, is projected by interpreters rather than fixed by the state or its history.

(9) If all content is projected, there is no original content anywhere for the projections to latch onto; and a projection that answers to nothing is not a projection of anything.

(10) That is incoherent, so at least one of the steps must be rejected.

He says the argument is a reductio: the conclusion cannot be true, so one of the premises about how content gets fixed must be false, and he wants to know which. I would rather not surrender (1), because without it I am not sure what we are even talking about. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what would each available repair cost?
