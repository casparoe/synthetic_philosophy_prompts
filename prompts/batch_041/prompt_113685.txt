I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a philosopher of language. She holds an expressivist view, though not about moral wrongness: she thinks that to call a belief justified is not to describe some property the belief has but to express one’s commitment to a certain inferential role for it. A rival in the novel keeps saying that her view cannot survive the Frege-Geach problem, and I have read enough to know the phrase but not enough to see the precise shape of the objection. I want to be able to write the dispute without having the characters trade slogans, so I need the underlying issue made clear.

Please explain the problem in a way that gets beneath the standard moral example. What exactly goes wrong for an expressivist when a term like justified appears in an unasserted context—inside a conditional, under negation, or in the antecedent of a modus ponens argument—rather than in a flat assertion? I want the difference between the negation problem and the embedding problem made clear, and I want to understand why the expressivist cannot simply say that in unasserted contexts the attitude is not expressed but only mentioned, or that the sentence is still truth-apt in a minimalist sense. If different expressivists have tried different repairs, say which repair buys what and at what cost, and tell me whether any of them saves the view without turning it into a form of descriptivism by the back door. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a single continuous explanation I could use to make the dispute vivid for readers who are not philosophers.
