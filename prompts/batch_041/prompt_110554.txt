I am finalizing the take-home final for an upper-level seminar on Nietzsche and the body, and I want to include one essay question that forces students to think about the late autobiographical remarks on nutrition, climate, recreation, and health as philosophy rather than as eccentric biography. The seminar has already covered the critique of ascetic ideals, the will to power, and the naturalistic turn in Nietzsche's later work, so the question should not be answerable by summarizing those topics. What I want is a question that makes them confront a specific crux: Nietzsche seems to treat questions of diet and regimen as having philosophical weight—as if the value of a philosophy could be measured by the life it sustains—yet he also insists that health is not a universal medical norm but something singular, tied to a person's task. That creates a tension between naturalism and perfectionism: if health is normative, how can it be read off from physiology without becoming either a crude healthism or a disguised moralism? And if it is singular, how can Nietzsche's own regimen function as anything more than autobiography?

Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks. The question should be designed for a 90-minute take-home essay and should include a case that students have not seen in the seminar: a contemporary Nietzschean who adopts a severe regimen of fasting, cold exposure, and elimination diet, reports heightened vitality, and treats his regimen as evidence of rank, while a chronically ill scholar who cannot adopt any such regimen produces difficult, life-affirming work. Students should have to say which life, if either, Nietzsche's late dietetic reflections would count as healthy, and whether the framework can distinguish enhancement from mere management of decline. The model answer should not merely rehearse Nietzsche's biography; it should take a position on whether the dietetic remarks are integral to his critique of morality or a dispensable appendix, and it should explain how the notion of physiological evaluation can avoid collapsing into the very asceticism Nietzsche criticizes. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain—for instance, distinguishing health as a normative ideal from health as a medical state, addressing the problem of transferring a singular regimen to others, and using the novel case to test whether the framework is coherent—and should list common errors that should cost marks, such as treating Nietzsche's remarks as straightforward lifestyle advice, reducing his naturalism to biological determinism, or assuming that health must mean the absence of illness. Students will not have internet access, so the question must not depend on identifying specific editions, passages, or secondary literature.
