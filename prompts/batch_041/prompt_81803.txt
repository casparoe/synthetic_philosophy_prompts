I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep running into a move in discussions of psychiatry that goes roughly like this: because diagnostic categories can alter the people who are diagnosed, they are not natural kinds, and the whole classificatory enterprise is suspect. The Hacking-style idea of looping effects is doing a lot of work in that move, but the more I think about it, the less I think the existence of a loop by itself settles anything. A category can loop through self-identification, through clinician expectation, through institutional incentives, through insurance coding, through peer communities, or through the simple fact that people learn the vocabulary and then report symptoms differently; some of those loops might make the category more tracking of an underlying condition, others might make it less, and some might be normatively desirable even if they do not track anything. I want to turn this into a two-page proposal for a small interdisciplinary grant that funds six months of part-time work, so that I can give a precise question, a taxonomy of looping mechanisms, and a method for saying which loops, if any, should undermine confidence in a psychiatric classification.

Please draft that proposal. State the question so it has a determinate answer: under what conditions does a feedback loop between a psychiatric category and the people classified by it constitute evidence that the category is not tracking a natural kind, and when is it instead compatible with, or even supportive of, such tracking? Explain why the existing positions leave this open: standard natural-kind realism and the standard social-constructionist or Hacking-inspired critique both tend to treat looping as a single phenomenon, so they cannot say which loop is epistemically damaging. Then lay out the approach: I would distinguish loops by their mechanism, including self-concept, clinician judgment, institutional recording, and community norms; by their temporal scale; and by whether they change the underlying trait distribution or only its measurement. I would then work through a small set of candidate categories, chosen for contrast rather than coverage, and ask what each loop does to the inference from diagnosis to underlying condition. Include a month-by-month plan, say why this taxonomy-first approach is better than just doing another single-disorder case study, say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and how to keep it doable in six months part-time. Do not bother with citations, reading lists, or a literature review by author; refer to positions and arguments and I will add the references myself.

As you write, be self-critical: tell me which part of this framing is most likely to collapse under scrutiny, and what argument or piece of evidence would make you change your mind about the central distinction. I would rather have a proposal that identifies its own weakest joint than one that sounds confident all the way through.
