So I'm a backend engineer (no philosophy degree, and honestly barely survived the one ethics elective I took in college) and I somehow got voluntold to give the keynote at my company's big annual offsite this year. Picture a hotel ballroom: roughly 60/40 split between engineers who all listen to the same two Stoicism podcasts on their commutes, and sales/ops/finance folks who've never read a word of Marcus Aurelius and will start checking their phones the second I sound like a TED talk. Our CEO asked for "something about resilience" because it's been a rough year — we did a layoff round back in the spring and missed our number two quarters straight.

Here's the angle I want to take, and I want your help turning it into an actual speech. Everybody keeps handing me the Stoic dichotomy of control — some stuff is up to us, some isn't — and it always rubbed me the wrong way, because in engineering almost nothing that matters is cleanly one or the other. A launch either ships or it doesn't, and you can do everything right and still get smoked by a flaky dependency or a competitor's surprise pricing move. Then I read about the reserve clause, this older Stoic move where you commit all the way to a goal but attach a little rider to it — I'm going to do this, assuming nothing gets in the way — so that caring about the outcome gets decoupled from needing the outcome to actually be okay. That's the thing I really want to teach people, because it's the whole difference between the engineers on my team who survive a bad quarter and the ones who burn out and quit.

Write me the keynote — around 8 minutes spoken, so maybe 1100-ish words. It has to land for the engineers without turning into a lecture for everyone else, and it absolutely cannot come off as management telling people to be grateful they still have jobs. Be honest that the layoffs were a call made by people way above us, not weather. Please write it in American English.
