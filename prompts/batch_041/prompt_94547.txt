I am not a philosopher by training—I manage a small payroll office—but I have been reading about a puzzle in my spare time and I would like to turn it into a joke for a dinner party I am hosting next month. The puzzle is the peculiarity of a sentence such as: it is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining. What strikes me is that the sentence need not be false; it can be true that it is raining and true that I fail to believe it. The trouble seems to be with asserting the two halves together in the first person present tense, while the same content in the third person, or in the past tense, is perfectly tolerable. I have been trying to invent a joke that turns on this exact difference rather than on a generic gag about philosophers being out of touch. Some of my guests are logicians and some are not, so the joke should work on both levels, but the punchline should especially reward someone who already understands why the first-person present-tense version is odd.

Please write that joke. Set it in the compliance office of a fictional company that requires employees to file a daily two-part weather report: the first line states a fact about the weather, and the second line states the employee’s belief about that same fact. A new software system is supposed to reject any report that is logically contradictory, but it begins flagging a report that is not contradictory in the ordinary sense—only odd in the way the puzzle is odd. The joke should be short enough to tell aloud, should make sense to someone who already understands the puzzle, and should not explain the puzzle after the punchline. It should not rely on any quotation, citation, or named author, and it should be self-contained.
