I'm a science journalist who covers neuroscience and bioethics, and I sit on the stem-cell research oversight committee at a university medical center -- I'm the community member, not the scientist. A lab here has applied to grow human brain organoids and run a consciousness battery on them: they'll show the organoids patterned light, measure whether the signal stays local or ignites across a distributed network in the way global workspace theory predicts, and use that to sort each organoid into either responsive or non-responsive. The PI's line is that if the workspace ignites, there's a subject there and the experiment stops; if it doesn't, there's no one home and they can keep going. My co-chair, a neurologist, thinks that's just good neuroscience -- no global access, no experience. But our philosopher member keeps saying the protocol is smuggling in a theory of access consciousness and treating it as if it settles phenomenal consciousness, which is exactly the thing we're supposed to be protecting. I've been on the committee for two years and mostly we argue about consent forms, so this one is definately above my pay grade.

We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a short written opinion: approve the protocol, reject it, or approve with conditions. I need a clear recommendation with a concrete time frame (e.g., approve a pilot for six months, or require the lab to pause the sorting rule until they add a second measure), and reasons the PI could actually respond to. Please also deal honestly with the strongest point on the other side -- if the neurologist is right that a silent workspace is the best evidence we're going to get, say so and explain why it still does or doesn't justify the lab's classification rule. I don't need a literature review or citations; just the memo I can read aloud.
