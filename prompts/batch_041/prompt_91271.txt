I host a small podcast on philosophy of mind and the history of AI, and next week's guest is a historian/philosopher of computing whose whole thing is that we've all been reading Turing wrong about machine learning. Her line is that the 1950 Mind paper gets all the attention while the 1948 report on intelligent machinery is where the actual theory of learning lives, and that what Turing is doing there isnt a reward-maximization story at all -- its an educational one. On her reading the teacher's job is to organize an initially unorganized machine, the nearest analogue is upbringing rather than reinforcement, and Turing's real preoccupation was a tension between getting a machine to do what it's told and leaving it enough slack to do something you didnt ask for. She also makes alot of his scepticism about purely evolutionary search (too slow) versus the shortcut of transmitting accumulated knowledge from one generation to the next, with education as the vehicle for that. And she thinks there's an unresolved tension between the behaviourism of the imitation game and the developmental picture in the later discussion of simulating a child's mind rather than an adult's.

Could you act as a philosopher of cognitive science and historian of AI and help me draft 9 or 10 interview questions? Start by brainstorming what the strongest objections to her reading would be, then write the questions. I want them to press on the substance rather than let her restate the thesis. For each question, add a one-line note on the follow-up I should have ready if she gives the answer I'd expect. Some things I'd like covered:

- whether education is a metaphor for Turing or a substantive claim about how learning has to work, and what actually turns on which it is
- the teacher problem: Turing's teacher looks like a single person shaping one machine's development, and I want to press on whether that picture can survive contact with anything like training at scale
- whether the rewards-and-punishments talk really is reinforcement learning avant la lettre or something importantly different, given that Turing seems to think the machine wouldn't actually mind being punished
- the discipline/initiative tension, and whether modern instruction-tuned models are, by her own lights, over-disciplined
- whether the cultural-transmission idea makes intelligence essentially a social achievement, and if so what that does to the imitation game's two-agent setup
- one question that puts real pressure on her historical claim: if the 1948 report is the real theory, why did Turing himself keep the 1950 framing so behavioural and so individual?

Keep the questions concrete and non-generic, and dont ask her anything that just invites a biography of Turing. Write them so I could read them out loud without sounding like I'm reading a seminar paper.
