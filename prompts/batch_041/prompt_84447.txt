I'm a cog sci grad student who's been slowly talked into Dennett's multiple drafts picture of consciousness — no Cartesian theater, no single finish line or privileged instant where the contents of experience get displayed to somebody, just a lot of parallel content-fixations competing and getting revised, with the self as a kind of user-illusion the brain spins up for itself. I want you to act as the sharpest philosopher of mind / cognitive neuroscientist you can be and genuinely try to break this, not defend it. Think it through carefully before you answer, and start by brainstorming the candidate defeaters so I can see the whole space, then rank them from fatal to merely awkward.

There are two things I'm especially hung up on, and I'd like them handled as parts of one question rather than seperate essays.

First, the drafts picture is famous for denying that there's a fact of the matter about *when* an experience happens, except relative to some probe or report. Picture the classic apparent-motion demo where subjects report seeing the dot change color halfway along its path, even though the only colored things on the screen were the two flashed dots — a change that only makes sense if the brain is revising the record after the fact. Fine, that seems to help the view. But now push the same move one step further. If there's no fact of the matter about *when* a content became conscious, isn't there equally no fact of the matter about *whether* it did, at least in borderline cases? Is the view committed to consciousness coming in degrees, and is that a cost or a feature? And what does the drafts theorist say to someone who insists that in some cases it's just plain obvious that a given experience either occured or didn't?

Second: suppose a lab reports the following (hypothetical, but I'm told it's not far off). In a masking task there's a window of a few hundred milliseconds during which the subject's later report is still undetermined — you can flip it with a post-stimulus cue — but once that window closes, activity across a broadly distributed network locks in, the report is essentially fixed, and you can decode it from that activity well above chance. The lab calls it 'ignition', and half my department reads it as the Cartesian theater sneaking back in through the back door. Does that actually count against multiple drafts, or can the drafts theorist absorb it? What's the difference between a 'broadcast' and a 'theater' that isn't just relabeling?

Beyond those two: what else would count against the view — which results, cases, intuitions, or arguments — and which of them would a drafts theorist *themselves* concede as counting against them, as opposed to the stuff critics like to throw? What does the picture predict that its rivals (a global workspace story, a higher-order thought story, a straightforward all-or-none view) don't? And then be honest with me at the end: does this view risk becoming unfalsifiable — if every awkward result just gets redescribed as 'another draft', is that a bug, or is it exactly what the view predicts, and how would I even tell the difference?

For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where the timing or the unity of an experience looks like a real, determinate fact that no amount of redrafting could dissolve.
