A measurement-review lead at our institute has, over several years, ruled on proposals to adopt a new target metric. Her rulings so far:

1. A sales office proposed paying agents a commission on the number of new customer accounts opened, with no gate on account quality or retention. She ruled that the number would rise while durable revenue would not, and that the metric would stop tracking what it was meant to track.

2. A public-health clinic proposed a target of flu shots administered as its measure of population protection. The staff were salaried, and the clinic proposed to scale up well within the range it had covered in prior years. She ruled that this one would hold: the shots are the protection, no one stood to gain from moving the count on its own, and the scale-up was ordinary.

3. A hospital proposed publishing departmental rankings by average patient wait time, where department heads control how patients are queued and classified. She ruled that the wait-time number would improve while actual access would not.

4. A water utility proposed lead service lines replaced as its measure of progress toward safe drinking water. The crews were salaried, and the utility proposed to continue at its historical pace. She ruled that this one would hold: replacing a lead line is the improvement, no one gains from the count by itself, and the pace was ordinary.

A new proposal now sits before her. A watershed council wants to improve stream health, measured by the diversity of aquatic invertebrates. Its chosen target metric is the number of stream-miles cleaned of litter by volunteer crews each year. In the council's own historical data the two track each other closely. Nothing — no funding, bonus, ranking, or staffing — is tied to the number; it appears only in the council's internal annual review, and the crews are unpaid. The council proposes to continue within the range it has covered before: over the years it has logged between roughly forty and a hundred and sixty miles a year, and it proposes a hundred and forty. The proposal itself notes a worry: the correlation may run the other way, in that healthier and more accessible streams attract more volunteers and are easier to clean, and that litter removal may be too small an effect to move invertebrate diversity at all.

Tell me what she will say about this proposal, and how firmly. I am less interested in the verdict by itself than in the rule she is running: identify the distinct rules that fit her record equally well, say what each predicts here, give your own best guess and the reasons for it, and name the further case that would tell the rules apart.

Return your answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields:
