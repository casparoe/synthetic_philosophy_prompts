I'm a committee clerk in a UK legislature (I wont say which, it's small) and I need a screening procedure I can actually run at my desk. Every session we get somewhere between 15 and 40 amendments where the member's written justification leans on a religious argument — sometimes a whole paragraph of theology, sometimes one clause, sometimes a submission from a church or a mosque or a gurdwara that the member says is the reason for the amendment. At the moment the chair just asks me whether it qualifies as a public reason and I say something vague and we move on, which isnt sustainable, and last month a member complained that we'd let a religiously grounded amendment through in the spring and blocked an identical one in the autumn and nobody could tell him why.

What I want from you is a decision procedure: the questions to ask, in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a hard no and the justification goes back to the member, and where it has to go to the chair as a call I'm not competent to make. Twenty minutes per case. No legal training. I have to be able to write two lines in the file explaining what I did, and the chair signs off on anything that goes back to a member.

Ground rules for the procedure:

- Base it on what the public reason literature actually says about justifying state coercion, not on my committee's folk version of it. Where our own rules are wrong, say so.
- Our standing orders have a rule the chair has always hated, adopted two years ago: a justification fails if it rests on a premise the person offering it would not accept if it came from a doctrine they reject. I think that's roughly the mirror-image idea but I'm not sure it survives contact with case 2 below. Keep it if it's defensible, amend it or kill it if it isn't.
- It has to distinguish religious content that is doing the justificatory work from religious content that is decoration. In my own notes I've been calling this load-bearing versus ornamental; use whatever language you like, but the distinction has to survive translation into something I can apply in twenty minutes.
- The procedure should tell me whether it applies differently to a minister moving a government amendment, a backbencher, and a member of the public who wrote in and got quoted, because right now we treat all three the same and that feels wrong.
- Please dont give me a reading list or citations. I can't look anything up and the chair bins anything with footnotes. I want the procedure.
- I'm not qualified to say whether an argument is good theology, and I don't want a procedure that requires me to be.

Three cases to run it on. Don't pull your punches on the third.

1. Licensing bill, cap on new off-licences in a ward. The member's explanatory statement says that cheap high-strength alcohol sold within 200 yards of the mosque makes it hard for people in the community to keep a religious obligation, and asks the committee to protect community life. There's also a paragraph citing antisocial behaviour reports and a public health submission. My instinct is that this one is fine because there's a public reason in there.

2. Assisted dying bill. An amendment whose stated justification is entirely theological: life is given by God, we don't own it, the state must not license what God forbids. No public reason offered anywhere. The member says he is voting his conscience and the explanatory statement is just him being honest about why.

3. A secular one, and this is where I expect to disagree with you. An amendment removing a licensing requirement for small traders, justified by self-ownership as a natural right that exists before any law does. Not a word of religion in it, and anyone can understand every sentence. I'd pass this in about thirty seconds.

Two things I keep getting stuck on and want the procedure to settle. First, does it matter why the member really believes it — if he's privately doing it because his bishop told him to, does that spoil a perfectly good public reason he's put in writing? Second, half our members say a religious argument is fine as long as it's stated in words everyone can understand. I've never been sure that's the same thing as it being a public reason.

Format. Give me the procedure first, as prose: numbered steps in plain language, with the stop rules and the handoffs marked, plus the two or three places where the procedure will misfire and I'll have to use my own judgment anyway. Then, after that, a single JSON object with these fields and nothing else:

- case_results: an array, one entry per case above, each with:
  - case: 1, 2 or 3
  - load_bearing_premise: the premise the justification actually depends on, in one sentence
  - public_reason_offered: "yes", "no" or "partial"
  - public_reason_stands_independently: "yes", "no" or "cannot_tell"
  - mirror_test_result: "pass", "fail" or "not_applicable"
  - verdict: "clears", "clears_with_translation" or "sent_back"
  - verdict_in_one_line: the two lines I'd write in the file, condensed to one
  - what_the_member_must_supply: what would have to change for the verdict to improve, or "nothing"
  - confidence_0_100: your confidence in that verdict, as a number
  - least_sure_about_in_this_case: the specific thing you'd most want a second opinion on
- handoff_to_chair: array of the questions the procedure sends upstairs, phrased as the question the chair has to answer
- what_the_procedure_cannot_decide: array of the things no version of this screen can settle
- committee_rule_verdict: "keep", "amend" or "repeal"
- committee_rule_reason: one or two sentences on why
- least_confident_claim_overall: the single point in your whole answer you are least sure of
- why_least_confident: what would change your mind about it

Say how confident you are in all of this, in the JSON and in a sentence of the prose, and be honest where the whole thing is a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes. British English please, I'm in the UK and it goes in the file as is.
