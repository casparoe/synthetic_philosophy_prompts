I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and this term I let myself get talked into co-teaching an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, which means I'm on the hook for the final. I want one essay question on computationalism -- whether the brain is a computer -- and I don't want it answerable by reciting the Church-Turing stuff plus a paragraph on multiple realizability. The crux I want them to hit is this: if you're allowed to map physical states onto computational states however you like, then just about anything implements just about any computation (a bucket of water running the same algorithm as a laptop), and a thesis that can't come out false isn't much of an empirical thesis. The version of that I like best: two neuroscientists who agree on every measurement and still disagree about which computation the brain is running. So the question should force them to say what has to be added for a claim like "the brain implements this computation and not that one" to be real and checkable -- causal individuation of the states? banning disjunctive or gerrymandered mappings? robustness under perturbation? something else? -- and then judge honestly whether those fixes are principled or just glued on to keep the thesis alive. I'd also like a part where they say what empirical result would actually count against computationalism once it's been tightened up that way. Give me the question, a model answer of 600-700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points that names the specific moves that seperate an A from a B, with a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs marks at each point. American spelling, please.
