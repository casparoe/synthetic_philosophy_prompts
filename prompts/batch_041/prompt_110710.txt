Hey — I need a hand with something for my concepts & categories seminar. We've spent the last two weeks on letterforms, of all things, and two of my students have been circling each other all term on what makes every 'a' an 'a'. I want to force them into the document an adverserial collaboration would actually produce, so they find out whether they disagree at all.

Take on the role of a philosopher of mind with a genuine side interest in type design and visual categorization — someone comfortable moving between 'what is a concept' and 'why does that ampersand work'. Think carefully before you write, and please start with a short brainstorm: list the candidate answers to what fixes a letter's identity (topology, stroke count, position in a contrast space, function, community use, genealogy, whatever else you can come up with), and test each against the wierd cases below. Then write the document.

The two camps, as my students have drawn them:

(1) The Essentialists. A letterform's identity is fixed by an abstract, style-independent structure. Every 'a' shares a skeleton; style is flesh draped over it. Whether any reader ever encounters a given glyph is irrelevant to whether it is an 'a'.

(2) The Practitioners. Letterhood is constituted by participation in a socially maintained practice of reading and writing. An 'a' is whatever competent readers of the relevant script treat as an 'a' — graded, contrastive, revisable. There's no skeleton in the glyph, only an idealization the reader imposes on it.

Cases they keep fighting over, and that I want handled: a photocopied 'a' smeared halfway toward a 'd'; a mark sitting in a font file that has never been rendered or seen by anyone; a squiggle that a whole community has agreed to read as 'a' even though it breaks every structural generalization they can state; the single-story and double-story 'a', and whether those share a skeleton at all; Cyrillic 'а' versus Latin 'a'; letters drawn on a coarse grid where neighbouring letters differ by a single pixel; a script with only two letters, where identity is carried by one dimension; and the modal question of whether 'a' could have been shaped like our 'e'. One of them keeps bringing up an old AI font-design project that tried to generate whole fonts with a consistent 'spirit' — every letter recognizably the same letter, every letter recognizably the same font. That tension is basically the whole seminar, so make sure the document speaks to it.

Deliverable — keep it tight, maybe 1000–1200 words:

- A numbered section of agreed claims both camps can sign without wincing. Not platitudes: real constraints on any theory of letterform identity that both sides actually accept.
- A disagreements table. Each row: the question, the Essentialist's answer in wording the Essentialist would accept, the Practitioner's answer in wording the Practitioner would accept. Neutral, un-slanted, no strawmen.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction about how it comes out.
- Finally, say which parts of the dispute you think are merely verbal — where they're using 'essence' or 'convention' for different jobs — and which are genuine.

Don't hedge the whole thing into mush. The point of the exercise is that the residual disagreement comes out exact.
