I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the better part of a year a colleague and I have been arguing about whether misleading someone is morally on a par with lying to them. It began as an argument about how we word release notes for customers and has since spread to everything else. We have agreed to stop re-litigating and to produce the document our argument should have generated, and I would like you to draft it. Treat the two positions as parties to an adversarial collaboration.

Position one, the distinctness view: lying and misleading differ in kind. A lie asserts something the speaker believes to be false; a misleading statement asserts something the speaker believes to be true and leaves the hearer to draw a false conclusion from it. On this view, at least part of what is wrong with lying is that it breaks a norm governing assertion, since the hearer is entitled to rely on a speaker's assertions being true, and misleading does not break that norm, even when it produces precisely the same false belief.

Position two, the parity view: the difference is a technicality that moral assessment should look through. What matters is the intention to produce or sustain a false belief in the hearer, or to exploit the hearer's trust, and lying and misleading both do this. On this view, misleading is not a lesser wrong in kind, and can be a greater one, because the victim is left holding a false picture with no false statement to correct, and the speaker keeps a deniability the liar forfeits.

Please produce the document in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign: what a lie is, what a misleading statement is, what the two share, what each side concedes to the other, and what both would count as evidence bearing on the dispute. The formulations must be neither empty nor slanted; each side should be able to say that its own view is consistent with every line.

2. A precise statement of each remaining disagreement, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own position.

3. The cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, identify a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about how that case should come out, written down in advance.

4. An honest account of which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, and why.

Work with the following distinctions and cases, and add others if they help.

Three ways a statement can be deceptive: (a) asserting something the speaker believes false; (b) asserting something the speaker believes true while intending the hearer to infer something false from it; (c) asserting something the speaker believes true while intending the hearer to fail to consider some possibility, through selection, omission, or a presupposition embedded in the question.

Cases to compare. In case (a) an engineer tells a customer, "there is no bug in that release," believing there is one. In case (b) the same engineer says, "that release passed all of our tests," believing it did, knowing the tests do not cover the path that fails, and expecting the customer to conclude there is no bug. In case (c) the engineer says, "I have not seen any bug reports about that release," which is true, though the engineer has seen telemetry showing failures.

Questions I want the document to face: does a misleading statement assert anything false, or only invite a false inference? If the hearer would have formed the false belief anyway and the speaker merely declines to correct it, is that a lie, a misleading statement, or neither? Does the wrong lie in what the speaker intended or in what the act was? If the hearer is sophisticated enough to parse the statement correctly, is anything wrong? And is the misleader's ability to say truthfully "I never said that" a further wrong, or only a fact about the odds of detection?

Format the agreed claims as a numbered section, the disagreements as a table (the question at issue, the wording each side accepts, the crux it turns on), and the cruxes after that, each with its case and the two predictions. I do not need a reading list or citations; I would not be able to check them.
