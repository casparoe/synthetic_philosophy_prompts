I don't want a reading list or citations, just the argument. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Whether something is a disease is a factual, value-free matter — it's a deviation from the species-typical functional organization of the organism, full stop; values come in later, when we decide whether to treat it. I know "species-typical" is carrying a lot of weight, and I've talked myself into thinking it survives the annoying cases: myopia is super common in some populations but still a dysfunction, adult lactose intolerance is common in some populations and rare in others, and you handle both by fixing the reference class to the design plan the organism was selected for (roughly the ancestral one), not to whatever happens to be most common in your zip code. A friend says this forces me to call male-pattern baldness a disease, and I'm fine biting that bullet. (2) Homosexuality was never a disease, and when the APA took it out of the DSM in the early 70s that was the correction of an error, not a renegotiation of values. (3) I think the DSM got it wrong when it decided that ordinary grief, if it runs long enough and ticks enough boxes, counts as major depression — grief isn't depression even when it's severe, and whether something is a disorder here seems to me to turn on context and harm, not just on function. (4) On psychiatric disorder specifically I think you can't draw the line without bringing in harm and social norms — there's no value-free way to say a mind is disordered. (I can hear this fighting with (1); my move has been to say "disease" is the value-free cousin and "disorder" is the value-laden one, but I'm not sure that move actually works, tell me.) (5) The treatment/enhancement distinction is real and does real moral work: I'm fine with public money restoring normal function and not fine with it buying people upgrades, which is why a pill that let you skip sleep entirely would be enhancement, not treatment. And (6) methodologically I think "disease" just means whatever medicine's practice commits us to — there's no hidden essence we're discovering, and we should use the word the way clinicians and patients actually use it instead of revising it for philosophical tidiness.
