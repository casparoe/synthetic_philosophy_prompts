I'm trying to decide where to point a small research programme, and I'd like your help. Assume you're a philosopher of language and mind who's spent the last several years embedded in an AI lab — you know the philosophy of lying and misleading cold, and you also know the safety literature well enough to be cynical about it. Take your time with this, and start by brainstorming out loud for a bit: list the candidate conceptual questions you see in this space before you commit to anything. I want to see the space you're working with, not just the conclusion.

The setting: I control a grant that can fund roughly three philosophers for two years, and the remit is purely conceptual — definitions, arguments, taxonomies, counterexamples. No benchmarks, no eval engineering, no training runs. So tractability for people who read and argue for a living matters a lot here.

What I want from you is an ordering of the open questions about defining honesty and deception for AI systems, with the reasoning defended. For each one: how far upstream is it (how much other work depends on settling it), how tractable is it in two years without lab access, and what argument or development over the next couple of years would move it up or down my list.

Things I keep chewing on, in no particular order:

- Where the line between lying and merely misleading falls, and whether that line even survives translation to a system that may not have beliefs in the first place. A lot of the practical worry — sycophancy, hedging, the 'some researchers think' style of attribution — sits on the misleading side rather than the lying side, and I'm not sure our definitions reach it.
- Whether we need a doxastic requirement at all, or whether there's a workable notion of functional deception that drops the belief and intent conditions and just talks about the causal role of an output in producing false impressions.
- The difference between honesty-as-truthfulness (output matches the world), honesty-as-sincerity (output matches the system's own beliefs, whatever those turn out to be), and honesty-as-faithfulness (output matches the internal process that actually produced it — the thing people gesture at when they worry a chain of thought is post-hoc rationalisation). I have a hunch these three get conflated constantly and that most of the confusion in the literature is downstream of that, but I'd like you to push back if you think that's wrong.
- Whether the unit of assessment should be the output, the policy, or a stable disposition of the system — and whether that choice is substantive or just bookkeeping.
- Deceptive alignment and sandbagging: same phenomenon, different definition, or something that needs its own treatment entirely?
- Calibration. A perfectly calibrated model can still be systematically misleading, and I want to know what that shows about whether calibration is a decent proxy for honesty.
- Whether honesty is even the right virtue to be aiming at, versus trustworthiness, non-manipulation, or some norm on assertion (truth, knowledge, belief — pick your poison, they give different verdicts).

Rank these, tell me which ones you'd actually fund and which you'd cut because the answer wouldn't change anything downstream, and flag anything I'm missing that beats what's already on the list. Also tell me which of these are really empirical questions wearing a philosophy costume — I'd rather not pay philosophers to guess at them.
