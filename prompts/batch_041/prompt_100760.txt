I am a novelist, and I have agreed to give a single seventy-five-minute session at a residential seminar for working novelists — a room of people who have staked their professional lives on events and on individuals, and who will hear any talk of geography, climate, and demography as an argument that their kind of writing is beside the point. My subject is the contrast Braudel drew between the longue durée — the slow, nearly immobile structures of geography, climate, demography, and material life — and the short time in which events occur and individuals appear to make them; I mean also to touch on the intermediate register he placed between the two, the medium-term rhythms of economies and social cycles. I want you to plan how I should teach this, not to explain it to me.

What I need first is the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and why that order and no other — which idea is load-bearing for which, and what collapses if I reverse two of them. Then the single case I should build the whole session around: give me one example, not a menu, and tell me what it must have and what it must lack to do this work; I have been considering building it from a river that silts up and quietly relocates a town's livelihood across three centuries, or from a real episode such as a run of harvest failures, but I want your committed choice and your reasons. Then the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will actually surface, and the precise moment in my sequence where each must be headed off: I expect them to hear the longue durée as geographical determinism, to conclude that individuals and events therefore do not matter, to collapse the middle register into the long one, and — this is the one I fear most — to turn a structure into scenery with a grudge, an atmosphere or a mood rather than a standing constraint. I also want a sharper distinction than I currently have: between a structure that is merely slow, a change that takes a long time to complete, and a structure that is a standing condition which does not change at all within the frame but shapes what can happen. Decide which of these the session must make the audience feel, and how.

Then the check at the end that would tell me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been admired — something they must be able to do or say that a person who has absorbed only the vocabulary could not. And tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and say so out loud.

Take positions throughout. Where I have offered you a choice, make it and defend it; where you think I have framed the problem wrongly, say so and give me the frame you would use instead. I would rather have a plan with edges than a survey of options.
