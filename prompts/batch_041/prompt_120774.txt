I'm giving a paper at our comparative philosophy workshop in a couple weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet — not comments on the paper, I've got those. Here's the line I'm defending. Everyone treats karma as a theory of moral desert: you suffer now because of what you did before, and the suffering is supposed to fit the deed. I want to argue that's a mistake, and also that it's a trap, because it's the desert framing that generates every classic objection — proportionality, the no-self problem, why innocent and collective suffering get swept in — while the doctrine is better read as making a *different* claim. Not "this suffering is deserved" but "this suffering is intelligible": it can be placed in a story that makes the world morally coherent. Read that way, karma is a constraint on narrative, not a causal mechanism. The twist I actually want to land is the dilemma. Once it's a constraint on narrative rather than a causal law, it can't do any of the work people want from it — justify who's on top, motivate restraint, explain why *this* person and not that one — because a good enough story can absorb any distribution of outcomes. So karma-as-desert comes out false and karma-as-hermeneutics comes out defensible but inert. I think that's a real dilemma and not just me being clever at the doctrine's expense.
