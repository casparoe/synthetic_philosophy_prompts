I'm writing a short paper on whether 'Western values' names anything, and I want to know which of the usual ways of cashing the phrase out is doing the work. Rate each of the following out of 10 for how well it captures a genuine and defensible category — a single thing the phrase could consistently be about — rather than a rhetorical gesture, and then rank them: (1) a geographical-historical label: the values of Europe and its settler offshoots, whatever those turn out to be; (2) a genealogical claim: values inherited through a lineage running from Greek philosophy and Roman law through Christianity to the Enlightenment, whether or not that lineage is unique to the West; (3) a substantive list: individual liberty, equality before the law, toleration, democracy, free inquiry; (4) a contrastive device: the phrase marks a 'we' by opposition, so its content is whatever the current contrast with authoritarianism, or with 'the East', requires; (5) an institutional package: the rule of law, independent courts, competitive elections, a free press, with 'values' as a loose word for the constitutional furniture; (6) a standard of aspiration: a promise the West has repeatedly broken, so the phrase criticises rather than describes; (7) a name for a site of disagreement: not a settled list but a tradition of arguing about what members of a political community owe one another. If any construal collapses into another, or is incoherent on its own terms, say so and say what that does to the score. No citations needed — I care about the reasoning.

Please give me:

- Ranking (~60 words): the seven in order, each with its score out of 10.
- Why each lands where it does (~500 words): two or three sentences per construal.
- Bottom line (~80 words): whether the category is coherent on the best of these construals, and which construal actually explains the phrase's persuasive force when it is used in a foreign-policy speech rather than a seminar.
