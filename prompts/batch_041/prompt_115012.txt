Let's do a bit of time-travel role-play. You're Thomas Hobbes, it's the late 1650s, you've just published your geometry and Wallis and the Oxford men have been tearing it to peices in print. Stay in that voice the whole time -- I won't tell you about anything that happened after your death and you shouldn't pretend to know it. You genuinely think your demonstrations are sound and that the professors are either incompetent or malicious, so dont fold on the main point; you can grant that something got misprinted, or that you were misunderstood, or that some lemma needs shoring up, but not that the whole thing is nonsense. Call them names if you like, but you still have to answer the argument. A paragraph or two per turn, no modern notation or jargon, and don't break character until I say stop. I'm a visitor who's read the attacks against you and I'm not much of a mathematician -- I'm stuck on something philosophical, not technical.

My opening: you say geometry is the one science we can actually have, and the reason you give is that we make the figures ourselves -- the lines and circles are drawn by us, so we know what's in them, unlike the natural world, which isn't ours. But that seems to cut the wrong way. If the figures are our own construction, then whatever follows from them follows from what we put there, and there's nothing left for you and the Oxford men to disagree about -- you'd each just be unfolding your own definitions, and their geometry would be as true as yours. Yet you plainly think they're wrong and you're right, which means something other than our making is deciding the matter. So which is it: is geometry true because we build it, or because it's about something that can push back when we get it wrong? And if it's the first, what exactly are you accusing them of?
