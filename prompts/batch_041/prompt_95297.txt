I'm a PhD student in classics, and I've been staring at a sentence in Cicero's *Cato Maior* that I can't get to sit still. Scipio has just said that most old men call old age a burden heavier than Etna, and Cato begins his reply as follows (text as I have it, *Cato Maior* 5):

"Quocirca si sapientiam meam admirari soletis (quae utinam digna esset opinione vestra nostroque cognomine!), in hoc sumus sapientes, quod naturam optimam ducem tamquam deum sequimur eique paremus; a qua non veri simile est, cum ceterae partes aetatis bene descriptae sint, extremum actum tamquam ab inerti poeta esse neglectum. Sed tamen necesse fuit esse aliquid extremum et, tamquam in arborum bacis terraeque fructibus maturitate tempestiva quasi vietum et caducum, quod ferundum est molliter sapienti. Quid est enim aliud Gigantum modo bellare cum dis nisi naturae repugnare?"

What I can't work out is whether the passage runs two pictures of the end of life that quietly cancel each other, or whether the doubling is doing deliberate argumentative work. The first picture is dramaturgical: nature is a playwright who has composed the earlier acts of life well, so it is not plausible (non veri simile est) that the final act was left unwritten, and we are to follow nature as a guide and a god. There the end of life is a structured part of a well-made whole. The second picture is botanical: the end is like berries on trees and fruits of the earth that, at their timely ripeness, are withered and ready to drop, something that befalls us and that the wise man must bear gently (ferundum ... molliter). If the last act is a withering and a fall, what work is the playwright doing? And if the playwright's ending is well made, why does it need to be borne at all rather than simply lived? I also can't tell whether maturitas names the peak of the fruit's goodness or the onset of its rot, or whether quasi vietum et caducum (I take vietum to mean something like shriveled) concedes precisely the charge Cato means to answer, namely that old age is a decay.

Two further things I'd like sorted out. First, how should I hear the closing question about warring with the gods in the manner of the Giants? The Giants lost, so is the warning that resistance to nature is impious, futile, or both, and does it sit oddly in a speech whose whole purpose is to argue against the complaint that old age is an evil, since arguing with that complaint looks like a form of resistance? Second, how much Stoic machinery is really in naturam optimam ducem tamquam deum sequimur? Is that a technical premise doing the argumentative work, with the fruit and Giant images as rhetorical dressing, or is the real load carried by the weaker, probabilistic inference about the playwright, with the appeal to nature as ordinary piety?

What I want is a reading that says how the two images divide the labor, or a clear verdict that they don't and why, plus some indication of which of my worries are artifacts of reading the sentence in English. I'm not after a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature; I want to understand the sentence. Please write your answer in American English.
