I'm a novelist, and the word "knew" does a lot of work in my scenes, so I've been trying to work out what I actually think it takes to know something. Here are my verdicts on five cases, some from life and some from drafts, and I'd like to know what single principle, if any, they add up to.

1. I've checked the timetable twice, and I say I know the 9:12 goes to Hull; if I'm wrong I miss lunch. I know it.
2. Same timetable, same two checks, but this time I'm catching that train to sign the contract that decides whether I can keep writing. I say "I'm fairly sure" rather than "I know," and I don't think that's mere hedging: I've stopped knowing.
3. In a draft, a character in that same position says "I know the 9:12 goes to Hull." I let her say it, and I think the sentence is true of her, even though her stakes are exactly as high as mine in (2). She checked twice, the timetable is what it is in the world of the book, and there is nothing left for her to be wrong about except what no one could see.
4. A reader writes to say that in that scene the character didn't know, she only believed, because so much rode on it. I think the reader is wrong about my book and right about herself: the same sentence, about the same woman, is true in my mouth and false in hers.
5. Looking back on (2) after the contract was signed, I say I knew the train was at 9:12 all along. That feels true too, and it is the same person with the same evidence as the version who had stopped knowing.

My tentative principle is that you know P when your evidence leaves no room for the kind of error that would matter to you. It explains (1) and (2) and is uneasy about the rest: in (3) the error matters enormously and I still want to say she knows, and in (5) nothing about my evidence changed when the stakes fell away. Is there one principle that gets all five, or must I give one up, and if so, which? State the principle precisely, say which verdict it overturns and why that verdict is the one to sacrifice, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a surgeon who has read the scan correctly, is certain of what she sees, will lose the patient if she is wrong, and who says, and I think truly says, that she knows.
