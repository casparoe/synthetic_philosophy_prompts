I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on me. I'm about three years into a book about a woman who falls out of love very slowly — no dramatic breakup, just a long slide over maybe eight months — and the whole thing lives or dies on what she can and can't know about her own state at any given moment. A philosopher friend read the draft and said I'd basically wandered into the fight between the anti-luminosity crowd (the margin-for-error people, the slowly-warming-bath and slowly-dimming-light stuff, the ones who say no mental state is luminous and knowledge doesn't iterate) and the other camp who think at least some inner states are things you can just know you're in, and that the knowing can itself be known — the KK principle side.

They've been at each other for decades and, from where I sit, mostly talking past each other. What I want is the document they should have written together. Draft me the adversarial collaboration joint statement, the thing both camps could actually put their names to.

So:

1. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign — what luminous is supposed to mean, what the KK principle is actually claiming, what the margin-for-error argument does and doesn't establish, and what's genuinely at stake for a creature trying to know its own mind.
2. Then the real disagreements, each one phrased the way that side would want it phrased, so nobody feels strawmanned.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one side. I have a hunch one is whether knowledge needs a margin for error at all, and another is whether it matters that the states in question are fine-grained or coarse-grained, but you tell me.
4. For each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about it, written down in advance.

And please use my love-fading case as one of the test cases. Somewhere in that eight-month slide, can she know she no longer loves him? Can she know that she knows? Does it change anything if we ask whether she can know it now versus whether she can know it looking back a year later? Both camps should say what they predict. And if you think part of their fight is really just about words, say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table. No need for reading lists, just the argument.
