I am a graduate student in theology, and I have recently found myself defending a position I never expected to hold. I was raised in a tradition with a robust doctrine of divine predestination, and I had always assumed that God's knowledge of what creatures would do was simply an aspect of his decree. This term, however, a colleague in my seminar introduced me to the account of middle knowledge associated with Luis de Molina — the claim that, prior even to his decision to create, God knows what any possible free creature would freely choose in any circumstances in which he might place it — and I found that it resolved a difficulty that had troubled me for years, namely how petitionary prayer for a particular person's conversion, or evangelism undertaken with real hope, can be intelligible if the outcome is already fixed. The more precisely I try to state the view, though, the more I suspect it may be incoherent, and I cannot tell whether my worry is a genuine objection or merely a failure of imagination. I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors: a Molinist, who affirms middle knowledge, and a divine determinist of a broadly Thomist or Calvinist sort, who holds that God's causal concurrence is what brings about the creature's act and who therefore regards the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom as false, vacuous, or idle.

I want the determinist to press the grounding objection in its strongest form — that a true counterfactual of creaturely freedom would have to be true in virtue of something, and nothing is available for it to be true in virtue of, since it cannot be grounded in God's will (which would reduce it to natural knowledge of what God has decided) nor in the creature's own nature (which would make the freedom illusory) — and I want the Molinist to offer a real, non-evasive reply, drawing perhaps on the divine ideas or on an analogy with God's knowledge of pure possibilities. Neither party should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual formulations rather than a convenient caricature. Toward the end, I would like them to test their positions against a case I have devised and cannot get either side to handle cleanly on my own: suppose God considers a possible person, call her Anna, and considers the counterfactual that if Anna were confronted with a shattering personal loss, she would freely turn toward God rather than away. Suppose further that whether Anna is ever confronted with such a loss depends on the free choices of other creatures — say, on whether her brother freely chooses to abandon the family. Is the truth of the counterfactual about Anna settled independently of the counterfactuals about her brother, or do these depend on one another in a way that makes God's middle knowledge a single indivisible package that he either receives whole or not at all? And if the antecedent of a counterfactual is something God himself does — if God were to send Anna a dream, she would freely repent — does the truth of that counterfactual wait on God's own will, and if so, has middle knowledge quietly collapsed back into the natural knowledge of what God has decided?

Please let the exchange run long enough to reach a genuine impasse or a genuine resolution rather than a polite draw, and keep the two speakers' voices distinct, with the determinist arguing as a serious theologian rather than a bully and the Molinist conceding whatever honestly needs conceding. After the dialogue, on a final line by itself, state in a single sentence the one point on which the whole exchange turns and which, as the dialogue stands, remains unanswered, in the form "Verdict: ...".
