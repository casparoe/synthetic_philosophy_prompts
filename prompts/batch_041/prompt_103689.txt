I'm 67, spent thirty-odd years doing probabilistic risk assessment for nuclear and aerospace systems, and then a few years ago I got the philosophy bug and just finished a part-time MA. There's a small fellowship at my old university for people who come to philosophy late from another field, and I want to apply with something that actually uses what I know. Two pages, thats the limit.

Here's the half-formed idea. The AI alignment world seems stuck in a shouting match between the worst-case people and the average-case people. The worst-case people want guarantees that hold no matter what the system does or what world it's dropped into; the average-case people point out that this is either impossible or vacuous, and that in practice we just measure how models do on average over a pile of tasks. But in my old job we never did either of those things. We built systems against a "design basis" — an agreed set of hazards and severities — and the real work, the work that took years and lawyers, was arguing about what belongs in that set and who has to justify putting something in or leaving something out. Nobody thought a structure was safe in the absolute worst case, and nobody thought it was enough that it does fine on average.

So the question I want to nail down: is there a coherent notion of a worst-case guarantee for an AI system that is neither trivial (guaranteeing against an adversary who can do anything) nor just an average-case claim wearing a disguise? And if so, what has to be true of the set of possibilities you're guaranteeing against, for the guarantee to be both non-trivial and non-circular? I suspect the productive move is to disaggregate "average case" — average over what? deployment tasks, the training distribution, the model's own randomness? — and to treat the choice of the uncertainty set as the thing actually under dispute rather than as a technical given. The engineering analogue isn't perfect but it's suggestive: safety margins, and that third option of minimising regret against the best you could have done in hindsight, which is neither worst-case nor expected value.

Lay out what I'd actually do for a year: I'm imagining mostly conceptual work plus a few toy formal examples showing where a proposed guarantee collapses into triviality, or where it quietly turns out to be an average-case claim in disguise. Say what a real result would look like versus what would count as the project failing. And be honest about scope — is a year enough, or am I about to bite off something that needs three?
