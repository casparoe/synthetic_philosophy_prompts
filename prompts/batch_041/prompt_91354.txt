For something like fifteen years my main intellectual diet has been the rationalist blogosphere — the older rationality archives, the recurring attempts to squeeze usable advice out of toy games, the decision-theory arguments that never quite resolve — and the posts I keep returning to are the ones that refuse to stop at the standard moral of a model. The dollar auction is the one I have never been able to convert into behaviour. The standard moral is easy enough: don't enter, and if you are already in, don't escalate. The more sophisticated version adds that a sequence of individually defensible steps can leave both parties worse off than never playing, that escalation is often rational given the position you are already in, and that only a binding precommitment helps. I believed all of that, in a general way, until I ended up inside a real one.

Here is the situation. I chair the board of a small housing co-operative. Two years ago we began a dispute with the company that owns the building next door over a shared structural wall and the strip of land along it; it started as a letter about drainage. We are now, by our treasurer's count, roughly even with what the disputed strip would fetch on the open market, and the strip has become close to worthless to both sides because the fight itself has made it unusable. The neighbour is spending their firm's money; we are spending a reserve fund belonging to forty households, none of whom will personally bear the next increment of cost. Twice we have reached the edge of a settlement and backed away. The option of simply agreeing to split the value and stop paying the lawyers is on the table at every meeting and is never taken, and I cannot give a clean account of why. A colleague describes the whole thing as a war of attrition with a fixed prize, which I suspect is not quite the same animal; another friend insists we are merely exhibiting the sunk-cost fallacy, which implies the remedy is a private one about how I think about money already spent.

What I want from you is the most important implications of the dollar-auction model, together with the adjacent psychological work on escalation of commitment, for what we should actually do — not a summary of the model. I am especially interested in what changes when the prize erodes rather than staying fixed, and when both sides are spending other people's money; in why the obvious bargaining move, agreeing to split the thing and stop, keeps failing and what that failure reveals about the structure; in whether the usual remedies — a hard cap set in advance, a disinterested decider brought in from outside, simply resolving to ignore sunk costs — do what people think they do, or mostly relocate the escalation while saving face; and in whether a proposal a colleague has floated, that each side post a deposit forfeited if it takes any escalatory step within seventy-two hours of the other's, follows from the analysis or merely builds a second auction underneath the first. My own suspicion is that the sunk-cost framing is doing real damage here, because it locates the trap in my psychology rather than in the other side's response function. So please tell me which implications genuinely bear on a case like ours, which are overstated or folk wisdom, and what I would be doing differently if I took the three or four that matter most seriously.
