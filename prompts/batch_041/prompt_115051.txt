I need help with a recurring thing at work. I'm the compliance officer at a mid-size investment platform — not the lawyer, the one who has to sign off on new customer-facing features before they ship, which happens two or three times a month, and I usually get about a week per feature, sometimes less when marketing has already anounced it. My team keeps handing me designs that are all about removing friction: switch between funds whenever you like, undo any switch for free, cash out instantly, top up any time. My gut says some of these are traps — not scams exactly, but designs that let a customer be walked round a circle and bled a little on each lap — and I can't articulate why in a way the product team can't wave away with 'we're giving people choice'. A colleague who did a philosophy degree says there's a whole literature on this under the name money pumps: the classic case is preferring A to B, B to C and C to A, so someone can trade you round the loop and charge you for every trade, and the more interesting case is where your preferences don't cycle at all but change over time, so every step is one you'd endorse at the moment you take it and you still end up worse off. I don't want a reading list and I definitely don't want citations; I won't read them and the analyst who actually runs this won't either.

What I want is a procedure we can run: a reusable exploitability audit for a proposed feature plus a described customer behaviour pattern. It should be a sequence of questions in order — the ones a person with no decision theory at all can ask — with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence stops with a no (feature ships, or feature is blocked), and where it stops and hands the decision to me because the question is about values or about our appetite for risk rather than about facts. It needs to separate three things I keep mudling together: (a) the customer's own preferences are the problem, they genuinely cycle or reverse; (b) our fee schedule or environment is the problem — the loop only costs money because of how we've set fees, spreads or settlement; (c) nothing is wrong and the complaint is really a request to be insulated from the customer's own inconsistency. And it has to take seriously the obvious escape hatch, which is that a customer who sees the loop coming can simply refuse to start, or can ask us to lock them in — so say when foreseeability, or the availability of a commitment device, means there's no case to answer, and when it doesn't. Also flag which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on three things. First, free switching between three funds, where a customer has gone A to B to C to A twice now, each switch costing a small platform fee, and each time they said they preferred the new fund to the one they were in. Second, a retirement-style product with a free top-up button and a cash-out costing 2%: a customer has topped up every January and cashed out every June for three years, says they endorse it in June and regret it in December, and there's an optional lock buried in the settings. Third, and this is the one I'm dreading, our proudest feature — any switch can be undone free within 24 hours — where I suspect the answer will be that the reversibility is exactly what makes the loop cheap enough to be worth walking, and that the fix is to add friction. I'd like the procedure to say so if that's what it says, and to be honest about the cases it can't settle at all, for instance a customer who says they enjoy the switching and treat the fees as the price of a hobby.

Write the procedure out in plain prose first so my analyst can follow it, then give me the same thing as a JSON object with these fields: preconditions (array of strings, what must be known before you start), steps (array of objects, each with n, name, question, sufficient_answer, if_yes, if_no, outcome — outcome being one of continue, stop_ship, stop_block, hand_to_human — and judgment_required, true or false), cannot_decide (array of strings, what the procedure is not entitled to rule on), misfire_notes (array of strings, where it will produce false positives or false negatives), glossary (object mapping each technical term the analyst has to understand to a one-sentence definition), and cases (array of objects, each with case_name, assumed_facts, steps_that_fire, verdict, judgment_calls, and unwelcome_to_us, true or false).
