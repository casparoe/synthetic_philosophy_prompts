Hey, so I'm in a seminar on the Scottish Enlightenment and we've been reading Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I keep getting stuck on something the secondary stuff we're reading seems to skate right past. I get the basic machinery — sympathy as the mechanism by which we enter into other people's feelings, and the impartial spectator as the ideal judge we imagine when we ask whether our own conduct is fitting. Fine. But here's what bugs me: the impartial spectator is supposed to be the standard by which we can criticize not just ourselves but our actual society's norms — Smith really does seem to think there's a difference between being praised and being praiseworthy, and that the impartial spectator tracks the latter. But where does the impartial spectator get its standards from, if not from the sentiments of the actual spectators we've encountered? It looks like it's built out of the very social material it's supposed to sit in judgment over, which would make the whole thing circular and just a fancy name for internalized social convention. And if that's right, the praiseworthy/praised distinction collapses into whatever our neighbors happen to approve of, which can't be what Smith wanted.

Can you walk me through how Smith gets out of this — or, if you think he doesn't, whether the theory can be patched, and what the most plausible patch is? I want the conceptual anatomy of the problem, not a summary of the book: what exactly the impartial spectator is supposed to be (an actual spectator idealized? a hypothetical fully-informed judge? a separate faculty?), why the praiseworthy/praised distinction is meant to do the load-bearing work, and precisely where the remaining gap is. Please don't just tell me "he says we imagine a better spectator" — I want the actual arguementative moves and where they're weakest. Also, if it's relevant, say something about whether the corrective role of the impartial spectator depends on Smith's claim that we naturally over-value our own case, or whether that's doing seperate work that we could drop without losing the rest. I'd also be curious whether you think the same circularity worry shows up in a different form for Smith's account of how we judge others versus how we judge ourselves, or if it's really only a problem for the self-assessment case.
