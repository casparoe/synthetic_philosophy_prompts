I'm slowly working my way through Bertrand's old probability book (the 1889 one, Calcul des probabilités) and I've gotten stuck on the chord-of-a-circle example that everybody cites. I don't have the French in front of me so I'll paraphrase the passage: he asks you to draw a chord at random in a circle, and wants the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Then he runs through three different ways of doing the random draw. First, pick two points independently on the circumference and join them; that gives 1/3. Second, pick a random direction, so a random radius, then pick a point uniformly along that radius and draw the chord perpendicular through it; that gives 1/2. Third, pick a random point inside the disk and take the chord for which that point is the midpoint; that gives 1/4. His punchline seems to be that the principle of indifference can't break the tie, because in each case we're equally indifferent among the things being randomized over, and yet the answers come out different.

What I don't get is why this is supposed to be devistating. Isn't the lesson just that "at random" was underspecified — you have to say which physical procedure you're running, and once you say it the answer is determinate and there's no paradox left? If that's all it is, why do people talk about this example as if it killed the principle of indifference for good? And when people say the fix is to use the invariant measure (Haar measure or whatever), I get lost — invariant under what group of transformations, and why is that group the privileged one instead of just being one more arbitrary choice? I'd like help seeing what the actual philosophical sting is supposed to be. Are the three answers really three answers to the same question, or three answers to three different questions that we've confusingly labeled with the same words? And does Keynes's treatment of the principle in his Treatise on Probability handle any of this better, or does he basically concede the same point?
