I have never studied politics or philosophy at a university, but I have spent several years thinking and arguing about one question on my own, and I have arrived at a set of positions that I now suspect cannot all be true together. What I want is an audit rather than a survey, so please work from the five commitments below rather than from what other people have written about them.

(1) The right way to decide who has a say in a decision is to ask who is seriously affected by it. If a decision will change your life in a substantial way, you have a claim to be at the table, whether or not you hold the passport of the place that is deciding.

(2) A vote means something only if the people casting it belong to one another in a stronger sense than merely being in the same place. Law made by people who feel no tie to one another, and who would not be willing to accept a loss for the sake of the rest, is not self-government; it is a poll. A body that decides things therefore needs a shared life and some sense of a common fate, or its decisions are not legitimate.

(3) Within that body, votes must be equal. I am against any arrangement in which some people's votes count for more, whether the reason offered is property, education, or the fact that the decision affects them more than it affects others.

(4) On two particular cases: someone who has lived and worked in a town for twelve years, paid its taxes and sent children to its schools should be able to vote in its local elections even if they have never taken out citizenship; and someone who holds that country's passport but moved abroad twenty years ago, owns nothing there and has no intention of returning should not be voting in its national elections, even though they remain formally a citizen.

(5) I think the way to reconcile the first and second commitments is to have two bodies rather than one: an ordinary parliament elected by the people who live in the territory, and a second body made up of outsiders who can show that a particular law would seriously affect them, such as the town downstream of a new sewage outfall, fishermen whose waters a quota decision would empty, or relatives abroad whose remittances a tax change would cut. This second body would not be able to propose laws or spend money; it could force a delay and a fresh vote, and its objections would have to be answered in writing.

Please tell me which of these can be held together and which cannot. Where two of them look as though they clash but turn out not to, say why the clash is only apparent. Where a clash is real, say what would have to be given up, and rank the real clashes from the cheapest to the most expensive. I have no particular attachment to any one of the five, so do not assume that any of them is fixed. Please write in British English.
