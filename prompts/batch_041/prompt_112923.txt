A couple weeks ago I made the mistake of bringing up On Certainty at dinner. I'm doing an MA and I'm in a seminar on skepticism that just did Wittgenstein's hinge stuff — the idea that some of what we hold isn't a belief we've justified but more like the frame that makes justifying anything possible in the first place. I got weirdly into it because a colleague of mine, Des, is going through something that fits the shape of it almost too well: he's convinced his old employer faked his performance reviews and then covered it up, and by now the HR person, his union rep, an employment tribunal and two different lawyers are either in on it or too naive to see it. Every document that says otherwise he reads as part of the cover-up. He's not stupid, and it started from one thing that definately did happen, which is what makes it unsettling.

So I explained the hinge idea, and now my friends Nadia and Ravi have been going at it for two weeks over text and I keep getting forwarded the threads. Nadia's line: giving reasons has to stop somewhere, and where it stops isn't another belief you've justified, it's just how you go on, so you can't call Des irrational for failing to justify his starting point, because none of us can justify ours — the demand for justification is the confused thing, not him. Ravi: if "he can't justify it" is enough to get someone off the hook, then hinge-talk is just a machine for making any conviction respectable, and Des's belief is plainly false, so calling it a frame rather than a false belief is a dodge. Nadia: hinges aren't private, what makes something a hinge is that it's how a whole community goes on rather than how one person happens to feel, and she added something about a river-bed — propositions harden and act as channels and the bed itself can shift, so being a hinge doesn't mean permanent immunity, only that it isn't currently up for testing. Ravi: then a hinge is just a belief nobody happens to be questioning, and your theory collapses into ordinary talk about consensus and evidence — and anyway Des doesn't experience his conviction as a frame, he thinks he's making an ordinary evidential claim that everyone else is too scared or compromised to accept, so your description doesn't fit his case at all. Nadia: that's a good objection but it cuts the other way, because hinges aren't a special class of propositions you can identify from the outside, so you can't use his self-description to rule her reading out. Ravi: and if nothing he does can count against the diagnosis, it isn't a diagnosis.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I especially want to know whether Ravi's "makes any conviction respectable" charge actually lands, and whether Nadia can use the community point without sliding into "whatever the majority takes for granted is beyond criticism." Also, does it matter that Des's conviction is about what particular people are secretly up to, rather than about hands or the age of the earth? Whether that kind of thing can be a hinge at all seems to be doing a lot of work here and neither of them has touched it. And what are they both missing about Des's case specifically? Please write it in British English.
