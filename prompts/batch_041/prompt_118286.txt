I am a software engineer on a small team that builds anomaly-detection tooling for language models. My background is in distributed systems and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I would like you to take the role of a philosopher of science with expertise in mechanistic explanation and causal modelling, and to think carefully and explicitly — brainstorming the possible readings of the question before you commit to an answer. Here is the problem. When our tooling flags something unexpected in a model's internal computation, we want to establish whether it is the same mechanism as an anomaly we have already diagnosed, so that we can reuse the diagnosis rather than redo the work from scratch. Two colleagues have proposed rival criteria and neither will budge. The first, which I will call the interventionist criterion, holds that two episodes count as the same mechanism exactly when there is a mapping between the two systems' causal variables such that the same interventions — clamping a variable to a fixed value, ablating a component, patching an activation from one forward pass into another — produce corresponding changes in downstream behaviour. On this view, sameness of mechanism is sameness of causal structure up to a chosen level of granularity, and it is entirely acceptable for the two systems to share no parameters and no substrate. The second, which I will call the role-based criterion, holds that two episodes count as the same mechanism exactly when the same organised activity is realised — the same kinds of components performing the same kinds of operations in the same arrangement — so that a mechanism can recur in models that share no variables and no weights at all, and is identified by its place in a larger organisation rather than by any particular causal graph.

Please compare these two criteria in detail, and then tell me which I should adopt for triage in practice. I would particularly like you to: (a) say what the question of sameness is really supposed to answer here — individuation, explanation, prediction, or something else — and how that shapes the comparison; (b) explain how each criterion behaves as we shift the level of description, for instance from individual attention heads to whole circuits to behaviour at the output; (c) assess whether either criterion collapses into plain behavioural equivalence once it is made precise, and whether that would be a defect; (d) identify where each criterion is likely to over-generate or under-generate sameness, with a small worked example for each (you may invent the examples, but please make them concrete); and (e) give a clear verdict, first on which is the more defensible account of what counts as the same mechanism, and second on which is more useful for a working engineer who mainly needs to predict what will happen when the flagged computation is perturbed. Please write in British English, be concrete rather than gestural, and state your verdict plainly rather than hedging.
