I am writing a chapter in which my narrator, a cartoonist, discovers that the two hands she draws each drawing the other can form a closed loop only if something outside the loop draws them — an undrawn level, which I have been calling the inviolate level — and that anything she names as that level can itself be drawn in turn. What I want from you is not a discussion of the puzzle but a single worked example of the conceptual issue, drawn from some ordinary rule-governed practice rather than from mathematics or from the familiar print of the two hands: a legal code whose amendment clause is itself amendable, a will that names the person empowered to rewrite it, a game whose rulebook contains a rule for changing the rules. In that example, say precisely what the inviolate level would have to be for the practice to be coherent, what breaks in the practice itself — not merely in our description of it — when someone tries to absorb that level into the loop, and, ideally, place a person or an office rather than a bare rule at the undrawn level. Then tell me whether the example supports the thought that the difference between a loop that holds and one that destroys its own structure lies in the pattern itself or only in the standpoint from which we look at it. Write in American English, in plain prose, and assume I have no library at hand: no citations, no reading list, and no appeals to what some author is supposed to have said.
