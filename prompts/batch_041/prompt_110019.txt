I have just finished a semester as a visiting student in a city whose conventions of street-crossing, queueing, and tipping all differ from those I grew up with, and I have now had the proverb "when in Rome, do as the Romans do" deployed against me twice in ways that seem to pull in opposite directions.

The first time, my flatmate — a local — watched me stand at an empty crossing at two in the morning, waiting for a signal that would not change for another ninety seconds, and told me to get on with it: nobody here waits, this is simply what is done. What is done here is also, on the books, illegal. The second time, a friend visiting from home refused, on principle, to leave a tip at a café, arguing that a system in which a server's wage depends on the customer's whim is degrading and that he would not lend it his support; a local friend of mine at the table replied that while in Rome one does as the Romans do, and that a guest has no business lecturing his hosts. Both remarks were meant to settle the matter, and neither did.

What I want from you is not a verdict on the two incidents but an inventory of what the proverb could actually be claiming, ordered from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: which readings are platitudes no one would deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which the people who repeat it probably have in mind. I am especially interested in these fault lines:

- whether the proverb is advice about prudence and friction-avoidance or a claim about obligation, and if the latter, whether local practice is the source of the obligation or merely fixes its content while something else — respect, reciprocity, fair play, tacit consent, the authority of the local legal order — supplies its force;
- the gap between what the Romans do and what the Romans are required to do. The proverb tells me to follow the practice, not the statute; does that commit it to the view that an unenforced law no one observes has lost its claim, and does a visitor's obligation track the same thing as a resident's?
- who the Romans are when they disagree. Tipping expectations here run from nothing to twenty per cent, and the locals argue about it among themselves; can there be a fact of the matter about a local convention that is contested or changing, and what does the proverb say to a visitor standing in the middle of the disagreement?
- whether the obligations in question are moral at all, or only matters of etiquette, and what work the proverb is doing when it is used not to guide a visitor but to silence criticism of a local practice. Which reading would have to be true for my host's rebuke to land, and which for my flatmate's?
- the position of the guest as such: whether a visitor owes more deference than a resident because he is a beneficiary of institutions he did not build, or less because he never accepted them and can leave; and whether ignorance of a local norm excuses a breach or merely explains it.

Lay the readings out as a numbered menu rather than choosing one and defending it, with a sentence of verdict on each; then say in a short closing paragraph which reading does the work in each of the two incidents, and whether the proverb on its best reading supports the use it was put to. I do not need citations or a reading list — I would rather have the distinctions than the names.
