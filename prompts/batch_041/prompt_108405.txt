I am writing a short position piece for a graduate seminar on the definition of life, and before I commit to a line I want to know whether my instincts even hang together. I have been keeping a list of verdicts on borderline cases, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they encode — and if there is no single principle, which of my verdicts is doing the damage. The list, roughly in the order I wrote it down: (1) a bacteriophage is not alive; (2) a cell infected by that phage is alive, and its aliveness is not the phage's doing; (3) a prion is not alive; (4) a plasmid is not alive, and neither is a free RNA molecule in a test tube, even one that catalyses reactions; (5) a mitochondrion is not alive, even though it has its own genome, its own membrane, and divides, whereas the free-living bacterium it descends from is alive; (6) a mule is alive despite being sterile, and so is a sterile worker ant; (7) a seed, a spore, and a desiccated tardigrade are alive — dormant, not dead; (8) a corpse is not alive, and an extracellular virus particle has never been alive; (9) the HeLa cell line is alive although the woman it came from has been dead for decades; (10) a bacterium whose genome was chemically synthesized and pared down to a minimal set of genes is alive; (11) a test-tube lineage of replicating RNA molecules that has been evolving under selection for months, with no membrane and no protein enzymes, is not alive; (12) a self-replicating lineage of digital agents in a simulated environment — mutating, competing, persisting for years — is alive, since I cannot see why the substrate should matter; (13) a forest, or an ant colony, is not alive, though I am tempted.

I have tried to fit these to the two candidate principles I keep meeting. The first is the gloss from astrobiology: life is a chemical system that sustains itself and is capable of evolving by natural selection. The second is the older autopoietic idea: a living thing continuously regenerates its own components and maintains its own boundary. Each seems to force me to give up something, and I cannot tell whether the principle is too crude or the verdict is mine. So please state precisely the principle — or the small set of competing principles — that captures as much of my list as possible, and say which case motivates each clause. Separate the tensions that are genuine from the ones that only look like they are, and rank the genuine ones by severity. For each, tell me which verdict has to go and what the cheapest repair is, and then tell me which single revision would buy the most consistency at the least cost. I am not after a survey of the literature; I want the principle itself and its verdicts. Finally, apply the winning principle to two or three cases I have not listed, including one that is not an organism in any ordinary sense, and say where it lands relative to my intuitions.

Throughout, be explicit about how confident you are in each claim, and single out the point you are least sure of — including any place where my list is too thin to force a choice between principles.
