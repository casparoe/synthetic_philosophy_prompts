I'm in this reading group that's slowly grinding through Williams — we did the Smart and Williams For and Against volume and then a couple of essays from Moral Luck — and last week two of the regulars, Marta and Owen, had an arguement I completely failed to referee, and it's been nagging at me since.

It kicked off with Williams's case where a man can rescue only one of two people and one of them is his wife. Marta said Williams is plainly right: if the man saves her and the thought sitting in the background is that saving her is what an impartial calculation licenses here, then that's the extra thought Williams is objecting to — the implication that her being his wife isn't by itself a sufficient reason and needs an impartial credential to cash out. Owen said that only touches a strawman, because utilitarianism is a claim about what makes an act right, not a rule about what has to pass through your head; the utilitarian can cheerfully say both that the rescue was right and that the man's way of deciding — "she's my wife" — was exactly what it should be, with no impartial thought anywhere in sight. Marta said fine, but then the theory has quietly excused itself from the man's life, and that's a worse problem, not a solution; and anyway the objection she means isn't about deliberation. Her point was about grounds: on the theory the thing the man cares about, her, is a means to an outcome, and his love is a good only insofar as it happens to produce good states of affairs. Owen said that's a different objection — that's the integrity thing, not the one-thought-too-many thing, and you're running the two together — and that it also has a standard reply: utilitarianism counts people's projects and attachments as valuable in themselves, as components of their well-being, so it doesn't reduce them to instruments. Marta said, as a quantity of well-being though, and pressed the fork: either the theory has to show up in your practical reasoning, in which case it alienates you, or it needn't, in which case a utilitarian and a non-utilitarian can live identical lives and the theory has no grip on anything — and if it also tells you it's better for you to stop believing it, that isn't a feature, it's a reductio. Owen said self-effacing theories are perfectly respectable — a correct theory of humor would tell you not to try to be funny — and the fork is false, because the theory can supply a criterion of rightness and separately tell you which dispositions to cultivate, dispositions that never mention utility. Marta said that just relocates the problem, since her ground projects are now line items in an impartial optimization over dispositions; and the humor analogy misfires, because the humor theory doesn't claim authority over your laughing while the moral theory does claim authority over your life. Owen said authority over what you do isn't authority over what you think, and that Williams is conflating the reason I acted with the reasons there were; he added that if the argument works it sinks Kant too, and any view on which partiality stands in need of a justification. Marta said Williams might well accept that, since the target is any theory that makes the agent step outside her attachments to validate them — and she offered an analogy of her own: a person who declares "I love you because you're objectively the best available partner by the metrics I care about" may be stating something true, but as a declaration of love it's a category error, and that's the flavor of what the theory says about the man's love. Owen said the analogy misfires because the man is never required to say or think anything of the kind — the theory is third-personal, like a biologist's account of love as a pair-bonding mechanism, and the biologist's description doesn't make anyone's love alienated. Neither would budge, and I couldn't tell who was landing blows and who was just re-describing.

(I also have a half-formed feeling that I only got confused because I've read too much of the rationalist decision-theory stuff where a theory of rightness and a decision procedure are supposed to come apart — maybe that's the crux, maybe it's a red herring.)

Can you go through the exchange move by move — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one-line justification — and then say who's more right overall? Two things I'd especially like settled: (a) are the one-thought-too-many complaint and the integrity objection genuinely distinct, is Marta conflating them, and does it matter which she's really pressing? and (b) does Owen's two-level reply (criterion of rightness vs. what's going through your head) actually answer Williams or just change the subject? And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
