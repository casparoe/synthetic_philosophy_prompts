I teach a second-year phil of language course, about 18 students, one 90-minute session, and I want to run something on speech acts — Austin's basic move that some utterances aren't descriptions of anything, they're actions: promising, betting, naming, appointing, firing. The part students always nod along to and never actually feel is that the force of these isn't sitting in the words or in the speaker's intentions — it's in the constitutive rules and in the other people taking it up. Change the rules or lose the uptake and the exact same words do nothing; they don't become false, they just misfire. And I want them to feel the difference between doing something in saying it (the illocutionary act) and doing something by saying it (the perlocutionary one — persuading, alarming, getting someone to move), because they always muddle those two.

Design me one runnable activity for this, fully worked out — don't hand me three options, pick one and commit to it. I'm imagining a made-up institution with a short written constitution of constitutive rules (X counts as Y when Z), roles with specific powers, and a point scheme (no real money) for pulling off institutional acts. Then partway through you change one constitutive rule and the same utterances stop working, and they have to work out why. Ideally there's also a round where a player tries to get an effect by causing it rather than by performing it, so the in/by contrast lands on its own.

Give me: the setting and how you brief them, the constitution and roles, the materials, the scoring, the mid-game rule change (and maybe a second one), how you stage the in/by contrast, and a debrief that maps what happened onto misfires vs. abuses, uptake, constitutive rules, and the illocutionary/perlocutionary line. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I suspect its the fact that asserting and warning don't need a written constitution at all, so the game makes every speech act look more conventional than it really is, but tell me if I'm wrong.
