I'm writing an essay on Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels for a collection on the meaning of life, and I want to pair it with a text that almost nobody in this literature reaches for: the Epilogue to Frank Ramsey's collected logical essays. What Ramsey says there is neither consolation nor despair. He does not feel humble before the vastness of the heavens, because "the stars may be large, but they cannot think or love; and these are qualities which impress me far more than size does"; his picture of the world, he adds, "is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with human beings in the foreground and the stars as small as threepenny bits. Importance, on this view, has nothing to do with magnitude — and the eventual cooling of the universe does not make the present less valuable.

Gulliver's Travels is a sustained experiment on precisely the variable Ramsey dismisses. Swift makes his narrator a giant among pygmies and a pygmy among giants, and lets our moral judgments slide with the scale: the Lilliputian war over which end of the egg to break reads as petty partly because the combatants are six inches tall, while the King of Brobdingnag's verdict on the history of Europe lands with authority partly because he is enormous — yet in both cases the content of the judgment is ordinary human politics and ordinary human pride, and Gulliver himself is flattened and then inflated without becoming wiser. Later, the Laputans contemplate the heavens with total absorption and cannot attend to their wives, their children, or their neighbours; the Struldbrugs get the endless time Gulliver naively wishes for and are wretched; the Houyhnhnms are larger, better, and rational, tempting us to think worth tracks nature and rank rather than thought and love; and Gulliver comes home preferring the company of horses to that of his family.

What I want is a reading that pushes in both directions at once. Where does the novel dramatize Ramsey's point — where does it show that our sense of what matters is a trick of the foreground, and that size, duration, and cosmic address are morally inert? Where does it resist him, and where does the satire's own machinery depend on the scale effects he denies — could the egg war or the King's contempt do their work if magnitude really carried no weight with us? Does the Houyhnhnm ideal, which is a hierarchy of natures rather than of sizes, escape the charge, or does it reinstate exactly what Ramsey denies? And is Ramsey's cheerful foregrounding of human beings a refusal of something the novel sees — in Gulliver's misanthropy, in the Struldbrugs, in the fact that its most clear-sighted creatures are the most wretched?

Name the three or four scenes I should build the essay around and say what each one shows. Don't quote the novel at length, since I have the text, and don't survey the criticism — I want the argument about which of the two is right about scale, and what the novel understands that the Epilogue does not.
