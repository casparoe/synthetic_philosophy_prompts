Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether the poverty of the stimulus is really an argument for innateness, and we eventually realized we were defending two different theses under a single name. I would like you to reconstruct the comparison we kept failing to make, and to take sides.

Compare two ideas. The first — call it constraint nativism — treats the poverty of the stimulus as a claim about the shape of the learner: the data available to a child are too sparse, and too lacking in information about which sentences are ungrammatical, for any unrestricted hypothesis space to converge reliably on the adult grammar; therefore the learning function itself must be restricted in advance. The second — call it content nativism — treats the same argument as a claim about what is antecedently present in the mind: certain grammatical principles are there before experience and are merely triggered or selected by it, rather than constructed from it. My friend ran the two together; I suspect they come apart.

Please work through the following, and end with a verdict rather than a survey.

First, are the two theses independent? In particular, could constraint nativism be true while content nativism is false? Consider the substitution move: for any proposed language-specific constraint on the hypothesis space, one can posit a general-purpose prior — say, a bias toward simpler or more compressible generalizations — whose effects coincide with it on the data a child actually receives. If that move always succeeds in principle, does it make constraint nativism untestable, or does it leave it intact as a claim about which priors are psychologically real? And does the same move threaten content nativism at all, or is content nativism a different kind of claim that the substitution move cannot touch?

Second, consider three pieces of evidence that are usually bundled together: the absence of negative evidence in child-directed speech; children's striking failure to make certain overgeneralizations that a pattern-matching learner would be expected to make; and the structure-dependence of the child's question-formation, where a generalization that counts linear positions rather than structural ones is never entertained. Which of these, if any, actually supports constraint nativism as opposed to content nativism, and which supports neither? Be specific about what each one would have to show.

Third, suppose someone concedes that the child must bring some prior to the task but insists that calling that prior innate is empty, because every learning mechanism embodies some prior and nothing is gained by the label. Is that objection fatal to either thesis, or does it trade on a confusion between a prior's existence and its domain-specificity?

Finally, state which reading — constraint nativism, content nativism, both, or neither — you think the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument can actually sustain, and give your reasons for preferring that verdict over the alternatives.
