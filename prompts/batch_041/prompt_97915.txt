A philosopher friend sent me a short blurb she wrote for a general-audience piece, and I want to check it before I run something like it, because I'm not an ethicist and I'd rather not get filleted in the comments. Here's the gist of her version: hedonism is the theory that pleasure is the only thing that's good in itself, and it's basically been the default view of human well-being for most of history. The trouble is the heterogeneity problem — when you actually look at the things we call pleasures, they have nothing in common. The pleasure of a cold beer on a hot day, the pleasure of a chord resolving the way you wanted it to, and the pleasure of watching a rival flop share no single feeling; there's no common ingredient you could point to. So "pleasure" is a word for a grab bag, not a natural kind. That's why Mill had to bring in higher and lower pleasures — he was patching a hole that was already there. And if hedonists can't even say what pleasure is, the whole theory collapses under its own weight.

I'm pretty sure some of that is fair and some of it is doing something sneaky, but I cant tell which. Can you go claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on well-being would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And if it's true that pleasures are wildly heterogeneous, does the last move — so hedonism is dead — actually follow from that, or are there live replies the blurb is quietly ignoring? I'd like to know how you'd describe the real state of play, not just a verdict. Answer in American English, please.
