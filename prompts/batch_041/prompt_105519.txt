Last weekend I was at a puzzle night and my friends Dana and Marcus spent basically the whole evening arguing about a single card from a homemade analogy set someone had brought. The card showed one pair, "1, 2, 3 → 1, 2, 4", and then asked you to finish a second pair, "7, 8, 9 → ?". Dana wrote down 7, 8, 10. Marcus wrote down 7, 8, 1. And then they went at it for like forty minutes, and honestly I couldn't referee it, both of them kept making points that sounded right until the other one answered. I'm not on either side here, I just want to know who's actually right about what.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Dana said the example just shows the rule "bump the last number up by one", and 9 bumped up by one is 10, full stop; going to 1 means quietly deciding that 9 is "the last one" and that the sequence loops, which is a whole different idea that's nowhere on the card. (2) Marcus said the example doesn't show "bump by one" at all, it shows "replace the last item with whatever comes next" -- and the card by itself never tells you what comes after 9. If you say 10 you've imported the integers; if you say 1 you've imported a cycle; neither is printed on the card. He kept going on about an old AI program from the 90s that solves letter-string analogies (stuff like "abc → abd, so ijk → ?") and said the interesting thing about it was that it doesn't just apply a fixed rule, it lets the concepts slide depending on what's salient -- he called it slippage, or maybe he said "fluid concepts", I forget. (3) Dana said fine, but 10 is the smallest possible import, since it just keeps using the counting you already needed for 1→2 and 2→3, whereas looping needs a brand-new notion that nothing hints at; and anyway if 1 were allowed then so is 0, so is 100, so is a banana, because you can always cook up some rule that fits one example and spits out whatever you wanted. (4) Marcus said the banana point was unfair, because 7, 8, 1 isn't arbitrary -- it's exactly what you get if you take "successor" seriously as "next thing in the ordering" and then notice the ordering shown has a last element; and he said the arbitrariness worry cuts both ways, because with a single example "bump by one" and "bump by one except at 9" are equally consistent with the card, so Dana can't use fit-to-the-example to pick between them. (5) Dana said that just proves Marcus is doing guesswork dressed up as principle, and that if a program gives different answers depending on the day it isn't modeling anything, it's just being flaky. Marcus said that's the whole point -- people do give different answers, and a model of analogy should reproduce that, not paper over it.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about each specific point, not just who wins overall? And I guess the big thing I want to know is whether there's actually a fact of the matter about what the right answer is, or whether that's the wrong way to frame the question entirely -- and what both of them are missing.
