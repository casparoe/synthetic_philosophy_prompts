I have been trying to settle my reading of Adam Smith on sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I would like my commitments audited before I commit them to a seminar paper. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Approval is a sympathetic sentiment. We approve of another's conduct when we find that we can enter into the feelings that produced it — when the agent's sentiments, as we imagine them, strike the same chord in us. To judge an action meritorious is, over and above this, to feel a sympathetic gratitude or resentment on behalf of those it touches.

(2) The measure by which our approvals are corrected is not the sentiment of any actual spectator but that of an imagined impartial spectator, whose responses have been purged of self-love and of the partiality that attaches each of us to his own case.

(3) This impartial spectator is not innate. It is formed, in the species and in each of us, out of the remembered and anticipated responses of actual spectators: we first learn to see ourselves through the eyes of others, and the inner judge is assembled out of what we have seen there.

(4) For all that, the impartial spectator's verdict is not reducible to the verdict of any actual community. A reformer condemned by every voice around him may still be in the right; and if the standard collapsed into prevailing opinion, Smith's account of how whole societies corrupt their moral sentiments would be unintelligible.

(5) What governs a good person is the love of praiseworthiness rather than the love of praise — the desire to be worthy of approval in the eyes of the inner judge, rather than merely to receive it from those around one.

(6) I take it that a person who is just but cold — who does the right thing from a dry sense of duty and no warmth of benevolence — is genuinely approved of, but is not the object of our warmest approbation.

I suspect that not all of this hangs together, but I cannot tell which commitment is doing the damage and which is merely doing its job. Please audit the set. Identify the tensions among these commitments, order them by how much they cost, and for each say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost. Where a pair only looks like a conflict, say so and explain why it is not; where the conflict is real, be specific about which commitment has to yield. I would rather have two or three tensions ranked honestly than a list in which everything is flagged as equally problematic.
