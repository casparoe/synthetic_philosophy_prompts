I sit on the approval panel for a small independent adoption agency -- we're the ones who make the final call on whether prospective adopters get approved, and Thursday we're deciding on a couple, Priya and Tom, who've been in assessment since the spring. Here's my problem. Our assessment framework (borrowed from the regional consortium, so I can't just rewrite it) has a criterion about applicants having realistic expectations of what adoptive parenting will demand of them, and the standard way we test it is to ask them to describe how they think their lives will change. Priya and Tom are otherwise strong candidates -- together nine years, both mid-thirties, went through two rounds of IVF before turning to adoption -- and Tom had a bout of depression a couple of years back, but our medical adviser and his GP both signed off, so that isn't what's worrying me. What's worrying me is that at the second interview they basically refused to play along. They said they've stopped believing anyone can know from the outside what parenting is like, that they're aware the people who come out the other end aren't quite the people who go in, and that they'd rather say that honestly than perform the script the assessor was clearly fishing for. Our chair read this as a failure of preparation and wants to defer them again. One member says it's the most self-aware thing she's heard in years and wants to approve them outright. And now our head of service has proposed a new form -- a sort of informed commitment declaration, where applicants sign that they accept they can't foresee the change, won't hold the agency responsible for it, and agree the commitment stands even if they later feel like different people. My cousin did a philosophy MA and says there's a whole literature on this, but she could only half remember it, and I think that form is either exactly the right answer or a piece of magical thinking and I genuinely can't tell which.

Can you draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting? I want a clear recommendation on Priya and Tom -- approve, defer, or approve with conditions, and if it's conditions, spell out exactly what they'd be and over what timescale -- plus a view on whether we should adopt the commitment declaration at all, and if so how we should explain it to applicants so it doesn't read as a liability waiver dressed up as counselling. It needs to take seriously the worry that we're being asked to approve a decision nobody is in a position to make, since the whole point is that they can't know what they're choosing and the choosing will change what they want; and it also needs to take seriously the counter-worry that if we took that seriously we'd never approve anyone, because every first-time parent is in the same boat. What I really need is a principled line between the two, not just a gesture at "it's a leap of faith" -- I have to defend this to the chair, who's a former social worker and starts twitching the moment anyone sounds philosophical.
