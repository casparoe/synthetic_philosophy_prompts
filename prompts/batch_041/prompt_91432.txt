Bit of an odd request, I'll warn you. I retired three years ago after about 35 years as a soil scientist (nitrogen cycling, lots of field work, not much maths beyond what you need for a t-test), and since then I've been reading philosophy of science and a couple of the popular causal-inference books. Never touched any of it as a student. Now I sit on the quality-and-safety committee of the small regional health network where I live — unpaid, meets monthly, and they keep handing us dashboards and asking us to flag outliers.

Here's my problem. Every few months the numbers flip on us and the committee dissolves into an argument about which number is the real one. Last meeting: Hospital A had a higher mortality rate than Hospital B overall, but once you broke it down by how sick people were when they arrived, A looked better in every single severity band. Two members wanted the overall figure in the minutes, two wanted the bands, and one said we should not report anything. Nobody could say why. I gather this is Simpson's paradox, and that the textbook answer is that it depends on the causal question you're asking, which is true and completely useless to a committee with forty-five minutes and a chief medical officer waiting to sign off.

What I'd like from you is a decision procedure I can actually run at the table. Ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the rest is values or politics rather than evidence. Keep it usable by people who don't know the words confounder and collider — I do, barely, but the retired schoolteacher next to me doesn't, so any term like that needs to be unpacked in the procedure itself. I can do arithmetic and cross-tabs in a spreadsheet; I cannot fit models, and we will not be hiring anyone mid-meeting, so if a step genuinely needs an epidemiologist, have the procedure say so and say what to hand them.

Two things it has to handle, because they've both bitten us. One: sometimes the reversal runs the other way, and it's the stratified comparison that's the artefact, because we've broken people down by something that is itself a consequence of the thing we're studying — we only have records for patients who were admitted, or who made it to discharge. Two: we keep getting comparisons of improvement between two units where one analysis adjusts for the patients' starting scores and the other just looks at how much each patient changed, and the two analyses tell opposite stories about the same data. I'm told that one is a known puzzle too. I don't need the history, I need the rule.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) the hospital A versus B thing above; (b) a case where the overall figure favours the unit I personally suspect is worse, so the procedure tells me something I won't enjoy; and (c) a case where the honest output is that the data we have cannot settle it and we should go and collect something else — say clearly what we'd need to collect.

Also tell me where this procedure will misfire or get gamed, and don't pretend the judgement calls away.

Finish each worked case with a line in exactly this form, nothing after it: \verdict{aggregate} or \verdict{stratified} or \verdict{neither}. And make the very last line of your whole answer \answer{...} with the braces containing the single question in your procedure that does the most work when everything else comes out even, in one sentence.
