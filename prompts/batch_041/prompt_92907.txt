I am a high school student working on an independent study in aesthetics, and I need your help with a puzzle about sad art. Please act as an expert philosopher of art and emotion, and think carefully and systematically before you answer. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references; I want a clear, original analysis that I can understand and defend.

The puzzle is the so-called paradox of tragedy: we often seem to enjoy works that make us sad, afraid, or grief-stricken. I have collected my own verdicts on a range of cases, and I want to know whether a single principle, or a small set of principles, can capture them. Here are the cases and my gut reactions.

1. A well-made film about a doomed romance: I cry at the ending, feel a heavy sadness, and yet I say I loved the film and would happily watch it again. Verdict: I enjoy it.

2. A friend tells me, in ordinary conversation, that her father has died: I feel sadness, but I do not enjoy the experience at all. Verdict: no enjoyment.

3. A sad song on repeat: I feel melancholy, almost pleasantly so, and seek it out when I am already sad. Verdict: I enjoy it.

4. A horror movie: I feel fear and tension, but I enjoy the thrill. Verdict: I enjoy it.

5. A documentary about a real famine: I feel grief and moral horror, and I do not enjoy it, even though I think it is important and well made. Verdict: no enjoyment.

6. A fictional sad story that is clearly manipulative and poorly written: I feel sad, but I do not enjoy it. Verdict: no enjoyment.

7. A fictional sad story that is beautifully written but has no redemption or insight: I feel sad and I am unsure whether I enjoy it. Verdict: mixed, leaning no.

8. A virtual-reality experience where I live through a character’s grief and cannot pause it: I feel sad and trapped, and I do not enjoy it. Verdict: no enjoyment.

9. A tragedy where the sadness is distant in time and place, such as an ancient play: I feel a moved sadness and enjoy it. Verdict: enjoy.

10. A memoir about the author’s real grief: I feel sadness, but I also feel that the book is beautiful and I value the experience; I might say I loved it. Verdict: enjoy, or something close to it.

My tentative principle is this: I enjoy sad art when the sadness is part of a larger experience that I judge to be valuable, and I do not enjoy it when the sadness is the whole experience or when it is about real suffering that seems to demand action from me. But this principle struggles with the memoir case and with the difference between the documentary and the ancient play, so I suspect it is either too simple or wrong.

Please start by brainstorming at least four possible principles that might explain these verdicts. Then test each one against the cases, including the pair that seems hardest to reconcile, and repair the most promising principle so that it handles as many verdicts as possible without becoming ad hoc. State your final principle or small set of principles precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for cases I have not considered: a sad video game in which I am responsible for the tragedy, a sad story about a character who shares my name and situation, a real news story written in the style of a novel, an artwork that makes me sad but also bored, and a case where I enjoy hearing about a real person’s sadness because it is narrated as a story. Finally, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict or complicate the principle to keep it. I want reflective equilibrium, not a survey of what other people have said.
