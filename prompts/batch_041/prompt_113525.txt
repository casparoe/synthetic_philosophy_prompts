I am preparing a seminar session on the ethics of work and leisure, and I want to spend most of it on a single paragraph rather than on the usual survey. The paragraph is from Chapter 5 of Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, in Talcott Parsons's translation:

"Not leisure and enjoyment, but only activity serves to increase the glory of God, according to the definite manifestations of His will. Waste of time is thus the first and in principle the deadliest of sins. The span of human life is infinitely short and precious to make sure of one's own election. Loss of time through sociability, idle talk, luxury, even more sleep than is necessary for health, six to at most eight hours, is worthy of absolute moral condemnation. It does not yet hold, with Franklin, that time is money, but the proposition is true in a certain spiritual sense. It is infinitely valuable because every hour lost is lost to labour for the glory of God. Thus inactive contemplation is also valueless, or even directly reprehensible if it is at the expense of one's daily work. For it is less pleasing to God than the active performance of His will in a calling. Besides, Sunday is provided for that, and, according to Baxter, it is always those who are not diligent in their callings who have no time for God when the occasion demands it."

I have read this many times and I can paraphrase its conclusion, but I do not think I understand the argument, and I want to be able to explain it slowly to students who will otherwise take it as a piece of colourful rhetoric. Please help me with the following, in order, and be explicit when you are reconstructing a premise the passage leaves unstated rather than reporting something the passage says.

First, the superlative. What has to be true for wasting time to be not merely a sin but the deadliest one, and deadliest in principle rather than merely in frequency? Is the claim that it is the condition of the other sins, that it is the one sin that cannot be repented of because it consumes the time repentance would need, or something else? Give me the strongest reconstruction you can, and then tell me where it is weakest. I am especially interested in whether this ranking can survive once the doctrine of election is dropped, since that doctrine is doing visible work in the third sentence.

Second, the instrumental structure. Wealth is condemned elsewhere in the chapter only because possession invites rest, and here sleep is condemned only beyond what health requires. That looks like an ethic whose real target is inactivity rather than pleasure. If so, does the passage need the premise that every hour belongs to God, and is that premise asserted anywhere in the passage or only presupposed? I want to know whether the argument is a piece of theology or a piece of bookkeeping with a theological cover.

Third, the qualifier. What is the force of saying that the proposition that time is money is true in a certain spiritual sense? Does the qualifier mark a difference in kind between the Puritan's valuation and Franklin's, or only a difference in the reason given for the same valuation? And note, if you can, where Weber's own voice is audible here as opposed to his exposition of Baxter: the Franklin sentence sounds to me like Weber commenting, while the sentence about the deadliest sin sounds like doctrine being reported. Tell me whether that division can be made with any confidence, and why it matters for how much of the paragraph Weber is endorsing.

Fourth, the technique. Labour is described earlier in the chapter as an approved ascetic technique, which suggests it is valued as a means of defence against something. What is the temptation it defends against, and why is labour a better defence than prayer, fasting, or contemplation — the very things one would expect an ascetic to reach for? If labour is valuable only as technique, what remains of the language of the calling, which sounds like it makes work valuable in itself?

Fifth, the concession about Sunday. Sunday is said to be provided for contemplation, which appears to undercut the attack on contemplation two sentences earlier. Is this a genuine boundary within the ethic, a pressure valve, or an inconsistency Weber has not noticed? I would like your best reading, and I would like you to say what would count as evidence against it.

Finally, the reason I am asking. I want to use this passage in a discussion of the thirty-five-hour week and of karoshi, and I want to avoid the lazy move of saying that the Protestant work ethic causes overwork. What I actually need is a clear statement of which premises a contemporary argument against overwork would have to retain if it wanted to keep the shape of this argument, which it would have to replace, and what the argument would look like with the theological premises stripped out — specifically, whether there is any non-theological way to make wasted or extracted time the gravest wrong, or whether the passage's force is inseparable from the claim that the time is not ours. If any part of the passage's apparent force rests on choices made in translation, say so, but do not speculate about wording you cannot check; I would rather have a careful reading of what is in front of us than a guess about the German.
