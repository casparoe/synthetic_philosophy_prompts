Question 3 (10 marks, about 20 minutes). Take the predicate 'is a crowd', applied to groups of people standing in a square, running from 1 person up to 10,000 people, everything else held equal. Suppose we grant that 1 person is not a crowd, that 10,000 people are a crowd, and that adding or removing a single person never makes the difference between a crowd and not a crowd.

(a) [3] Set out the supervaluationist semantics for this predicate. Say what it takes for a sentence containing 'crowd' to be true, for it to be false, and for it to be neither.

(b) [3] Using that apparatus, say exactly where the sorites argument goes wrong, and which premise the supervaluationist rejects.

(c) [2] Does supervaluationism avoid the consequence that there is a sharp cutoff somewhere in the series? Does epistemicism? Answer both.

(d) [2] Is supervaluationism a three-valued logic? Explain.

Student's answer:

'A precisification of 'crowd' is a way of making the predicate completely precise, i.e. a way of fixing a definite cutoff in the series 1 ... 10,000. A precisification counts as admissible if it respects the clear cases (1 person is not a crowd, 10,000 people are a crowd) and doesn't put the line somewhere absurd. Supervaluationism then says: a sentence is true if it is true under all the admissible precisifications, false if it is false under all of them, and neither true nor false otherwise. So '27 people are a crowd' is neither true nor false, because some admissible precisifications make it true and others make it false. That is the truth-value gap.

The sorites runs from '1 person is not a crowd', through 'for any n, if n people are a crowd then n−1 people are a crowd', to the conclusion that 1 person is a crowd. The supervaluationist says the argument is valid but unsound, because the second premise is false. Every admissible precisification has to break the series somewhere, so under each precisification some instance of the conditional fails, which means the universally quantified premise comes out false. Vagueness is a feature of our language rather than of the world, and the sorites premise is simply not true.

That gives supervaluationism a genuine advantage over epistemicism when it comes to sharp boundaries. Epistemicism has to say that there is some n such that n people are a crowd and n−1 people are not, and that we can never know which n it is; supervaluationism doesn't have to say this. No particular n is such that 'n people are a crowd and n−1 people are not' is true — each such claim is indeterminate — so the view accounts for the vagueness without positing a boundary anywhere in the series.

On (d): yes. Supervaluationism is a three-valued semantics: next to true and false there is a third value, indeterminate, which a sentence gets when the precisifications disagree. The connectives are handled truth-functionally, so a compound is indeterminate whenever a part of it is. '27 people are a crowd or 27 people are not a crowd', for instance, is neither true nor false, just like its two disjuncts.'
