I've been drafted to cover a session in a second-year interdisciplinary seminar -- fifteen students, eighty minutes, whiteboard, a mix of history, econ and biology majors, none of whom have taken any anthropology. The topic is the agricultural revolution and I want to run it as a single question: was it a mistake? I have the two obvious anchors in mind, Sahlins' argument that hunter-gatherers were the original affluent society because affluence is really a ratio of wants to means -- you can get there either by producing more or by wanting less, and he called one route Zen and the other, after Galbraith, Galbraithean -- and Diamond's counterblast calling farming the worst mistake in the history of the human race, backed by the health evidence from early farmers' skeletons. My worry is that the whole thing collapses into mush. The econ students will hear "affluent" and think high GDP per head; the history students will talk as if a committee sat down and chose agriculture; everybody will answer "was it a mistake" as though that were one question rather than at least three (a mistake for the people who did it, for individual welfare, for the species -- and judged from then or from now?); and then there's the counterfactual problem, that the comparison is always against a forager life those particular people didn't actually have available.

So what I want from you isnt the content, it's the plan. What has to come first for the question to even be askable? I have a hunch the definitional move about wants and means has to land before any of the empirical material, otherwise "affluent" is just doing free advertising for one side -- but tell me if I've got the dependency order wrong, and what else has to be in place before the mistake question is even well-formed. Then: what is the one case that should carry the whole eighty minutes, and what does it have to have and not have? I was going to hang everything on the transition in the Fertile Crescent and I'm not sure that's right. A single region might let them conclude "well, in that valley it was a mistake," and any one place drags in local conditions -- climate, which species happened to be domesticable -- that quietly do the explanatory work. If there's a better carrying case, or if the honest answer is that no single case bears it and I should run two side by side, say so. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off: the GDP reading of affluence; the "they chose it" reading, where I want them to see agriculture as a slow unplanned ratchet rather than a decision, and I'm not sure how to get that across without a whole lecture on domestication; the individual-versus-population slide, where farming looks dreadful for the people doing it and wonderful for the number of people doing it and nobody notices they've swapped the unit of evaluation mid-sentence; the work-hours number that everyone loves, which I gather depends entirely on whether you count processing and cooking or only the time spent getting the food; the idea that farming caused inequality, where I'd like them to at least see that affluence and inequality come apart, and that some foragers were neither mobile nor equal; and the moralizing ending where someone asks whether we should all go back to foraging.

Last three things. Give me one question to put to them in the final ten minutes that will tell me whether they've understood the question rather than just picked a side. Tell me what I should openly refuse to settle and say out loud that I'm refusing, because I'd rather they leave with a clean sense of whats genuinely unresolved than a tidy verdict. And please, no reading list -- they wont do it and it isn't the point.
