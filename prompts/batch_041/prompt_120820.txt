I'm in a final-year seminar on free will and responsibility and we've just done Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment". My tutor has this analogy he keeps coming back to and I want to know how much it can actually carry. He says the reactive attitudes -- resentment, guilt, indignation, gratitude, all of it -- are like the grammar of the language you grew up speaking. You can't ask whether a grammar is *true*, you can only describe it from the inside; and someone who says resentment is unjustified because determinism is true is like someone announcing that English is false. He uses this to get us to the conclusion that the hard determinist's scepticism isn't so much false as idle -- the question gets opted out of rather than answered.

Can you audit it for me? I'd like the correspondance table first (what plays the role of the grammar, the native speaker, the linguist describing it from outside, learning a second language, the objective attitude of the therapist), then where the mapping breaks, then sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for his conclusion and the ones that are just incidental. Does the conclusion survive once they're on the table? My suspicion is that the "you can't step outside it" bit is doing all the work, and that the analogy quietly imports the idea that responsibility-claims are conventional in the way grammar is. Tell me if I'm wrong about that. And if the analogy is bad, what would be a better one for the same point?
