I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in metaphysics and action theory, and I am writing the take-home paper for the causation-by-omission unit. The paper is closed-book and the students have no web access, so the question must be self-contained. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar contrast between doing and allowing, or by recycling the standard lifeguard and drowning-child examples. The question should make students say what is actually doing the causal work when an outcome is attributed to someone's failure to act, and it should force them to confront the suspicion that the answer is covertly moral rather than metaphysical. I also want it to test whether they can hold two ideas apart: that an omission may be a cause in the counterfactual sense, and that it may be a cause in the sense relevant to blame, compensation, or punishment.

The case I have been using in lectures is this. A reactor has an automatic cooling system designed to open a release valve when pressure crosses a threshold. An operator, Priya, sees the pressure rising but does nothing, because she believes the automatic system will act. In fact a technician, Tom, disabled the automatic valve during maintenance and failed to re-enable it. The valve does not open, and the core melts down. I have been teaching a device I call the Two-Baseline Test for such cases: an omission counts as a cause only if (i) the outcome counterfactually depends on the omitted action and (ii) the omission is a deviation from the baseline that the agent's role or the relevant norm defines as the default. I want the exam question to require students to apply that test separately to Priya, to Tom, and to the automatic system's failure, and then to turn the test on itself. It should include a variant in which Priya is the only safeguard and no automatic system was ever installed, and a variant in which the automatic system is an AI trained to defer to human operators, so that students must say whether the test gives the right verdict in each and what that reveals about the test's dependence on a contested baseline.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, each worth a stated number of marks, totalling 20, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. The model answer should be pitched at a strong final-year student, not a research specialist. It must be self-contained: no citations, no reading lists, no appeals to named authors or works, and no reliance on theories the student would have to have read. The question itself should be answerable in 90 minutes and should have two parts, so that students who only apply the test and students who also criticise it can both be differentiated. The model answer should end with a short section saying what the case shows about the Two-Baseline Test: whether it succeeds, whether it collapses causation into responsibility, and what a better test would have to do.
