Last night a friend and I spent the better part of three hours arguing about the careenium — Hofstadter's image of a vast billiard table crowded with magnetized balls, the simmballs, careening about and clumping into higher-level structures, the symmballs — and about whether it makes any sense to say that the symmballs, rather than the simmballs, are what shove things around. My friend's line was that the question is confused at the root: only the simmballs ever touch one another, so talk of symmballs pushing anything is a manner of speaking, good for prediction and nothing more. My line was that this concedes too much — that if the higher-level pattern is what does the work in our explanations, and if it supports counterfactuals the micro-level story cannot, then calling it mere is a mistake. We got nowhere, and I have now agreed to run a session on it, which means I need to work out how to teach it rather than how to win the argument.

Do not explain the careenium to me; I understand it well enough. What I want is a plan for teaching it — and specifically for getting a group from zero to the point where the question of whether symbols push the substrate is one they can pose sharply for themselves rather than merely repeat back to me.

The audience: a mixed reading group — graduate students in cognitive science, two engineers, one or two philosophers who work on other things. None of them will have met the careenium before. All of them are comfortable, in the abstract, with the idea that a system can be described at different levels; none of them has ever had to defend that idea under pressure. I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no assigned reading (I do not want a reading list; the session has to stand on its own).

Here is what I need from you.

The order. Which pieces have to be in place before the question even makes sense, and in what sequence, with the dependency spelled out — what breaks if I introduce X before Y. In particular, tell me where the exclusion worry belongs in the sequence: before the students feel its pull for themselves, or after, and how to make them feel it rather than simply hand it to them as a puzzle.

The carrying example. I want one example to do the heavy lifting across the whole session. I was going to use the glider in Conway's Game of Life: a pattern that persists and travels across the lattice even though nothing at the level of individual cells moves anywhere, and whose trajectory you can predict without simulating every cell. Tell me whether the glider can carry the session or whether it is too clean — because in that case the micro-story is deterministic and the macro-pattern is exactly recoverable, which may let the students conclude that the higher level is just a compressed summary and never feel the pull of the question at all. If it is too clean, what should replace it, and what exactly must the carrying example have and lack? I have considered and am unsure about a flock of starlings, the price of a commodity, and the temperature of a gas. Also tell me where in the sequence the example should appear — before or after the abstract distinction between levels of description.

The misconceptions. I want a map: which misconceptions the group will arrive with, which they will form under pressure, the order in which these surface, the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and what the heading-off should consist of. My own guesses, which I would like you to correct and extend: (a) that if the higher level is real it must be a further cause, exerting some downward force on the substrate; (b) that being reducible to the simmballs means not being real, so the symmballs are froth; (c) that because the microphysics is complete, any higher-level cause would be redundant, and therefore the higher level does nothing; (d) that the question is settled by observing that the higher level is just a description, as though that were an argument rather than a restatement; (e) that the two levels are two competing causes rather than one system described twice — or, conversely, that they are trivially the same thing and there is no question at all. My suspicion is that (c) and (d) do the real damage and arrive at different moments; tell me whether that is right and where each lands.

The check. One question I can put to the group near the end that would show whether they can now pose the problem properly — not whether they agree with me, but whether they see what is at stake — together with a description of what a wrong answer would look like and what it would indicate.

What to leave out, said aloud. The parts I should explicitly park rather than half-do at this level — I am thinking of the self as a symmball and the question of what the word I refers to, the free-will connections, and the surrounding literature on mental causation — and how to park each without making it look like I am dodging.

One further thing. My friend's deflationary position deserves a fair hearing inside the session rather than serving as a strawman, both because it may be right and because the students will learn more from a live disagreement than from a rehearsed refutation. Tell me where in the sequence the strongest version of that line should be voiced, and by whom — me playing devil's advocate, or a student I prime beforehand — so that any resolution, if there is one, does not feel like a foregone conclusion.
