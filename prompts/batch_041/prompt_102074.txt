Been going down a futarchy rabbit hole lately — the idea (Hanson's, I think) that we should vote on what we want, some measurable national welfare number, and then let prediction markets decide how to get it: you bet on whether the metric will be higher in ten years if we do X than if we don't, and whichever policy the conditional market prices as best is the one that gets implemented. The slogan is basically vote on the goals, bet on the means.

Anyway it got me wondering what John Stuart Mill would have made of it. He's my go-to Victorian for this sort of thing — utilitarian, obsessed with representative goverment, wrote about liberty and the marketplace of ideas, and he famously thought some people's votes should count for more than others (the educated getting extra votes), which is exactly the kind of move that makes him interesting here. He died long before anyone could bet on anything like this, so there's no answer sitting in his collected works.

So put yourself in Mill's shoes and tell me what he'd actually say about futarchy. Would he be delighted — markets as the ultimate version of his let-the-competent-run-things compromise, with everyone still getting a say in the goal — or appaled, because it hands the running of society over to gamblers and prices instead of deliberation and character? Be specific about which of his actual commitments pull which way; don't just give me vibes.

And here's the wrinkle I want you to take seriously. Futarchy needs one number to bet on. Whoever picks that number is quietly deciding what matters, and the betting public never voted on it. Is that fatal for someone with Mill's views on liberty and on the tyranny of the majority, or does his plural-voting scheme show he'd have been quite comfortable with a technocratic thumb on the scales?

Then a specific variant I saw floated somewhere that I want him to react to: every winning policy gets a sunset date, and it only survives if the market keeps pricing it as better than the status quo — a kind of rolling futarchy where nothing is ever locked in for good. Would Mill like that better, or does it just reproduce the instability he worried about in democracies?

Finally, suppose Mill got to redesign futarchy from scratch to fit his own politics. What would he keep, what would he rip out, and what would he add? Concrete design please, not he'd want it more deliberative. I also keep hearing the objection that futarchy is near-unfalsifiable because the metric is never really fixed — would Mill have spotted that, and what would he do about it?

British English please, ta.
