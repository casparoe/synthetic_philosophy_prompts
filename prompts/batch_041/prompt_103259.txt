I write a science-and-ideas column — general audience, but I have a phil degree so I can go a bit deep — and I'm doing a piece on mental causation, the whole "is your belief that it's about to rain actually doing anything, or is it just the neurons" mess. Before I get to who I think is right, I want to hand readers a spec: what would any theory of mental causation actually have to deliver to count as a serious contender? So write me that desiderata list. Not the ranking of the usual suspects, I'll do that part myself — the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it. Things like: the everyday phenomena it has to keep (a belief that it's raining causing you to grab an umbrella, a decision causing your arm to move, the way a chess player's plan seems to shape which piece she moves and not just run alongside it); the distinctions it can't flatten, like between an event causing something and a property doing causal work, or between being a cause and merely being causally relevant, or the proportionality thing where the "right" cause is the one that's neither too specific nor too general; the background results it can't just wave away (physical closure, the supervenience of the mental, no rampant overdetermination); and the traps it has to avoid, as opposed to the positions it has to take.

Two more things I keep bumping into and want the list to speak to. First, I notice people run together two different complaints — one that there are somehow too many causes competing for the same effect, another that the mental candidate is the wrong kind of thing to be a cause at all — and I suspect a lot of the muddle is that these get merged, so I'd like the requirements to keep them seperate and say which complaint each requirement is really aimed at. Second, a friend who does econometrics jokes that the exclusion problem is just a collinearity problem in disguise — you're asking which of two perfectly correlated variables has a nonzero coefficient — and I'd love the spec to say whether that analogy survives, because if it does it changes what a theory even has to do. Flag any pair of requirements you think can't be satisfied together, that's the part I'll build the column around, and for each one tell me whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or just something one camp insists on.
