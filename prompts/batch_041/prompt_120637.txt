I am a member of a translation workshop that has been stuck for months on a single exercise: how to render a short fixed-form poem—say, Clément Marot’s A une Damoyselle malade, the Renaissance French poem that Douglas Hofstadter uses as the spine of Le Ton beau de Marot—into English. The poem is tightly constrained: short lines, a dense rhyme scheme, a fixed syllable count, and a tone poised between playful and tender. One faction in the workshop argues that a version which abandons the rhyme and meter has failed as a translation of that poem, because the poem just is its formal body; a prose crib may convey the sense but not the thing. Another faction argues that preserving the form at the cost of changing the images, the tone, or the plain sense is the real betrayal, and that a free-verse or prose version can be more faithful to what the poem says and does. We keep recycling the same examples—Hofstadter’s many versions, the usual literal/free dichotomy, the claim that something is “lost”—and nobody moves. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not another round of examples.

Specifically: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it a substantive disagreement about what translation is or ought to be, a verbal dispute over the word “translation” (or “fidelity”), or two or more groups answering different questions under one heading—one about semantic equivalence, one about artistic re-creation, one about the reader’s experience, one about the author’s intention? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work (e.g., whether form can be separated from content, whether a poem has a determinate “meaning” apart from its form, whether there is a fact of the matter about which version is better)? Are the sides differing about starting intuitions that neither can argue for? My working hypothesis—offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion—is that this is a mixed case: a metaphysical disagreement about whether form is part of meaning, an evaluative disagreement about what translations are for, and a verbal disagreement about “fidelity,” all compressed into one debate. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a distinction, a change of question, a criterion of adequacy, a formal result—or whether no progress should be expected.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know translation theory and literary translation, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are themselves contested.
