I teach an upper-level seminar on social epistemology, about 20 students, one 60-minute session. I want an in-class game where they run into an information cascade without me naming it first -- the moment where, after two or three public guesses, it becomes rational for each next person to ignore their own private evidence and just follow the crowd, and they can feel that this isn't stupidity but the correct Bayesian move. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so the cascade forms on its own and nobody is told what to look for until the debrief.

One wrinkle I want built in, because the usual urn version is too symmetric and half of them have seen it: give a few players private signals of higher reliability than everyone else's, and don't announce who they are. I want the cascade to form and then be visibly fragile -- a single high-precision dissenter should be able to flip the whole run, which is the part that surprises people. If the timing works, add a second round where players can pay a small cost (points, not real money) to peek at their own card before guessing, so they feel how a running cascade kills any incentive to acquire information in the first place.

Give me the rules, the materials (cards, a payoff table), the scoring scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal apparatus: private vs public information, Bayesian updating, why the public pool can end up worse than any single private signal, cascade fragility, and the difference between a cascade and plain herding. Also tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the phenomenon the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
