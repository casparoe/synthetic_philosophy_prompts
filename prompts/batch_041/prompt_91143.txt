I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social choice and political philosophy — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, in a room with movable chairs — and I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the force of Amartya Sen's liberal paradox from the inside rather than hearing me describe it.

Two features of the paradox should shape the design, and I am spelling them out here so the activity can be built on them without anyone consulting a source. The first is that the collision is driven by preferences about what other people do inside their own designated spheres — call them meddlesome preferences. Where no one has such preferences, a unanimity requirement and a rule of personal sovereignty sit together comfortably; where people do, a group's ranking can be forced into a circle, and one of the two conditions has to give. The second is a live disagreement about where the trouble lies: if a right is modeled as one person's preference being decisive over a pair of alternatives, the conflict appears; if rights are modeled instead as constraints that rule certain outcomes out, some argue the conflict dissolves, and others argue that move only relocates it. I want students to feel the first point in their hands and then argue about the second.

Here is my sketch; improve on it freely. Five or six participants sit as a residential council that must write one charter covering a handful of matters, each designated as some single member's own — music late at night, keeping a pet, what one eats, smoking on the balcony, a front garden. Before drafting, the council must adopt two rules by consensus: unanimous preference prevails (if every member prefers one arrangement to another, the charter may not rank the other higher), and on the matter designated as yours, your preference between the two options settles it for the group whatever anyone else thinks. Then the sealed preference cards are dealt, one per member, each giving a full ranking over the arrangements, including rankings of what others do in their own spheres, engineered so that no charter can satisfy both rules and a three-way cycle is unavoidable. One member serves as clerk, whose only job is to test a proposed charter pair by pair and announce the first violation.

Then the second round: swap in cards rewritten so that each member ranks arrangements solely by what happens in their own sphere, and have them draft again. It goes through immediately, and the contrast is the lesson. The debrief should map the first round's cycle onto the general result, price each escape route the council actually considered (drop unanimity, narrow the sovereignty rule, leave matters undecided), and make clear how weak the sovereignty rule they adopted was — a few matters for a few people, far short of liberty in general — so that the paradox is not mistaken for an argument against liberalism as such. It should also say plainly that the result is conditional: meddlesome preferences are what put the two conditions at odds.

Expect the council to try declaring a matter not personal, to quietly drop someone's preference from the unanimity count, and to mistake a complete ranking for one that honors sovereignty — the rule concerns pairs, and a linear order can still override someone on a pair. Expect someone to call the deck rigged, which it is, and to treat that as the end of the matter; expect at least one group to find the leave-it-undecided escape and declare victory, which is a real result worth pressing on. Be honest about the ceiling: the activity stages one engineered case under one model of rights, so it cannot show that the impossibility holds for any number of people and any three or more options, and it cannot supply the proof or the sense that the result is a theorem rather than a trick of the cards. I will have to say that part myself.

Give me the design as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no closing remarks — each item a short paragraph of two or three sentences. The five should cover, in order: the setup, roles, and materials; the two rules and a minute-by-minute timetable for the ninety minutes; the preference cards and the moment the cycle appears; the debrief, including the second round; and what will predictably go wrong and what the activity cannot reproduce. American English, please.
