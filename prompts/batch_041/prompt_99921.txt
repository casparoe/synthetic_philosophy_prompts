I've been going around in circles with a friend about illusionism in the philosophy of mind, and I want a real diagnosis of why neither of us can move rather than another round. She's an illusionist in the Frankish/Dennett line: there are no phenomenal properties, no qualia, nothing with the intrinsic what-it's-like character we pre-theoretically assume experience has; there are only introspective judgments, reports, and a brain that represents itself as having such states, and the genuine explanatory target is why we're so convinced there's more. I'm on the other side with Chalmers: if you explain every judgment and report and leave out the fact that there's something it's like, you've changed the subject, not solved it, and the meta-problem is a fine research program but not a substitute for the hard one.

Every exchange runs the same track. She gives a deflationary story of how the illusion gets generated, I say that story explains the belief and not the experience, she says I'm re-asserting the thing to be explained, I say she's denying the datum. Nobody budges, and alot of the time we seem to be agreeing about the neuroscience and still talking past each other. My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than just endorse, is that this is not a disagreement about any fact about brains and not even really about whether qualia exist; it's a disagreement about what the explanandum is — whether the first-person seeming counts as data or as one more item to be explained — plus a disagreement about what would count as explaining it, so that a functional/representational account can't in principle satisfy one side and a non-functional residue can't be pointed to by the other. I also suspect a verbal layer: 'phenomenal' doing double duty for a property of experiences and for the character of our representations of experiences, the phenomenal versus epistemic 'seems', with heat generated by sliding between them. And I wonder whether this is the same shape as the old eliminativism fight about propositional attitudes — where the question was whether the eliminativist was changing the subject or revealing that there was no subject — and whether whatever diagnosis worked there carries over.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, two people answering different questions under one word, or a clash of unarguable starting intuitions about what needs explaining? Diagnose it from what each side actually commits to, tell me which parts of my hypothesis are wrong, and say what would count as progress — a distinction, a result about explanation, a case where the two sides' predictions diverge, or honestly nothing at all. Don't survey the literature; I want the structure of the stalemate and a straight answer about whether it can be broken.
