I've been circling a puzzle in the philosophy of moral language and would like to be handed a single worked example rather than a survey. The puzzle is the one usually filed under Frege–Geach. The expressivist holds that a moral sentence — the claim that lying is wrong, say — does not state a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude, a stance of being against the thing in question. The difficulty is that the same words turn up embedded: in the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, in a disjunction, in a question, in a because-clause, under a probability operator, and inside sentences whose remaining parts are plainly descriptive. In those positions the moral sentence behaves like a genuine sub-sentence that contributes a content the larger sentence operates on, and the larger sentence enters into inferences — from a conditional and its antecedent to its consequent, from a disjunction and a negated disjunct to the other disjunct — whose validity does not look like a fact about what attitude the speaker is expressing. If the moral term's meaning shifts when it is embedded, the inferences equivocate; if it does not shift, the attitude story looks false for the embedded occurrences. That is the squeeze.

What I want is one example that puts real pressure on it — not the stock conditional about lying that everyone reaches for. Construct a sentence, or a two- or three-line exchange, that embeds a moral predicate in a position the usual treatments handle least comfortably: a question, an imperative, an explanatory because-clause, a counterfactual whose consequent is purely descriptive, or an anaphoric dependency in which a pronoun in one clause picks up a moral predicate introduced in another. Then do four things with it. State the sentence or exchange. Display one inference that ought to be valid on its face, and say exactly where the equivocation would have to fall if the expressivist cannot keep the meaning fixed across the embedded and unembedded occurrences. Name the two things the expressivist must hold apart and cannot — I take the candidates to be the attitude expressed and the content the embedding operates on, but you may have a cleaner cut. Finally, sketch the smallest addition to an expressivist semantics that would cover your case, and say honestly whether it succeeds or merely relocates the problem.

Treat this as a request for one sharp worked example with its failure mode marked, not a literature review; I would rather have a case you have actually constructed and pushed on than a paraphrase of the standard moves. If the example I would naturally reach for turns out to be easy, give me the harder one instead.
