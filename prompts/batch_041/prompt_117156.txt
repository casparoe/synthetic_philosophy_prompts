I recently read a popular essay on Thomas Kuhn — a chapter in a trade book about how science works, written by someone who is clearly well read but is summarizing rather than quoting. The passage I want to check goes roughly like this:

"Kuhn's great lesson is that science does not accumulate truths; it lurches from one paradigm to the next, and each revolution replaces the old picture wholesale. Rival paradigms are incommensurable — they have no common measure — so they cannot be compared at all, and there is no neutral standard of evidence or rationality that partisans of both accept. Theory choice therefore comes down to conversion rather than argument: as Kuhn liked to say, scientists never change their minds, they simply die off and a new generation grows up with the new view. Kuhn's conclusion is that science is not a rational enterprise in the traditional sense, and that progress is an illusion — later theories are not closer to the truth, only different. The clearest proof is the replacement of Ptolemaic astronomy by Copernican and of Newtonian mechanics by Einsteinian: each new theory redefines what counts as an explanation, so that the old theory's successes cannot even be stated in the new theory's language. Only nostalgia for a rational science makes critics insist that the evidence decided these cases."

I read Structure once, years ago, and I know it is a book people argue about, but I cannot tell from memory which parts of this are fair. I am inclined to think the essay is being unfair, largely because I have a vague sense that Kuhn spent his later career insisting he had been misunderstood — though for all I know he was simply retreating under criticism, and the essayist may be reporting the view the book actually defends rather than the one its author later wished he had held.

Please audit the passage for me sentence by sentence. For each claim, tell me what a careful reader of Kuhn should accept, what should make them wince, and what is simply wrong; where the writer has collapsed two things Kuhn kept apart, say what the two things are and why the distinction matters. Then tell me what a defender of Kuhn — Kuhn himself, in his later essays — would say in reply to the passage as a whole, and whether the essay's concluding verdict, that science is not rational and that progress is an illusion, survives once the passage has been corrected, or whether it was doing something sneakier all along. Finally, distinguish the errors that are the essayist's fault from the ones Kuhn's own rhetoric invites, and tell me which single sentence of the passage I should trust least.
