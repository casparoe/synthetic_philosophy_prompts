I have been reading Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of history rather than the summary versions, and I have hit a stretch I cannot get a stable grip on. The summaries I grew up on say he denied history to Africa and to "peoples without states," which I can repeat, but I want to know what the sentences actually claim and how the claims hang together. The following is from J. Sibree's nineteenth-century translation:

> The diffusion of Language and the formation of races lie beyond the limits of History. History is prose, and myths fall short of History. The consciousness of external definite existence only arises in connection with the power to form abstract distinctions and assign abstract predicates; and in proportion as a capacity for expressing Laws (of natural or social life) is acquired, in the same proportion does the ability manifest itself to comprehend objects in an unpoetical form. While the ante-historical is that which precedes political life, it also lies beyond self-cognizant life; though surmises and suppositions may be entertained respecting that period, these do not amount to facts. [...] Of the several parts of Asia we have already eliminated as unhistorical, Upper Asia (so far and so long as its Nomad population do not appear on the scene of history), and Siberia. With China and the Mongols – the realm of theocratic despotism – History begins.

I do not want a general lecture on Eurocentrism, and I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to secondary literature. What I want is the internal argument of these sentences, and my working reading is that there are at least three separable claims being run together, which I will state so you can tell me where I am wrong. First, a claim about the object: some human groups lack the features that make a stretch of human activity historical at all. Second, a claim about the knower or the record: history is a certain kind of discourse (prose, laws, abstract predicates) and where that discourse is absent there is nothing for the historian to write. Third, a claim about Spirit's self-consciousness, on which "unhistorical" means not "unrecorded" but "contributing nothing to the development of freedom." My suspicion is that the moral force of the passage comes entirely from the third claim, while the persuasive work is done by the second, and that the first is nearly empty.

Please work through the following, in prose, with the reasoning shown rather than asserted:

1. What is the direction of the inference from "the power to form abstract distinctions" to the possession of a state, or from the state to that power? Does the argument require a direction, or can it run both ways? If it runs both ways, does the passage still do any work?
2. "History is prose, and myths fall short of History." Is this a claim about the subject matter or about the discourse? If myth can be a genuine form of collective memory, on what grounds is it excluded — falsity, form, or something else? Note that the sentence seems to exclude myth even where it is written down.
3. "Eliminated as unhistorical... so far and so long as its Nomad population do not appear on the scene of history." The qualifier looks conditional and revisable. On what scene, set by whom, and is the criterion circular — a people is historical once it enters history?
4. Is "unhistorical" descriptive or evaluative here? If it is evaluative, the sentence about Siberia is not an empirical claim and cannot be refuted by pointing to Siberian records or polities. If it is descriptive, say what evidence would count against it.
5. Steelman it: what is the strongest version of this passage that is not merely nineteenth-century European self-satisfaction? Then say where the steelman breaks — in particular, whether the criterion (state, written law, subjective inwardness) can be separated from the specific European path that supplies it.
6. If I wanted a global history that treats these peoples as agents without adopting his criterion of historicity, which single move here would I have to reject, and could I reject it while keeping the rest of the argument — say, the thought that freedom requires institutional embodiment — intact?

If some of these questions have no answer because the passage is simply confused or because the answer requires material Hegel supplies elsewhere, say that plainly instead of constructing a reading.
