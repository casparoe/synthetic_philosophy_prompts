I'm a PhD student working on the wrong kind of reasons, and I've come to think the debate has stalled not over any particular case but over what the cases are supposed to establish. Both camps accept the familiar datum — the demon who will torment me unless I admire him gives me some reason to admire him, yet not the kind of reason that makes the admiration fitting, and it would be perverse to admire him for that — and then redescribe it rather than explain it: one side says the demon gives me a reason to want the admiration, or to bring it about; the other says he gives me a reason to admire that is simply the wrong kind of reason to bear on fittingness. Each redescription receives the other's objection back, and the exchange repeats.

I have a hypothesis about why. The parties are working with two different conceptions of what it is to have a reason at all — one on which a reason is whatever counts in favor of an attitude, another on which a reason must be able to operate in the agent's deliberation about that very attitude — and the demon case is exactly the case where the two conceptions come apart. I've been testing this against a case I invented: a reliable and benevolent predictor will make my life go well only if I come to believe, sincerely and without evidence, that a certain colleague is trustworthy. My colleague says the predictor's benevolence is a reason to believe; I say it is only a reason to want to believe; and we cannot get further because each of us treats the other's "reason" as a different kind of thing.

Diagnose this for me. Is the impasse between the two camps, and between me and my colleague, a genuine disagreement about reasons, a verbal one over the word "reason," or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Is my deliberative-constraint hypothesis the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work — a suppressed disagreement about whether belief can be reasons-responsive at all, or about what "fitting" adds to "counting in favor"? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a case one side must accept and the other must reject, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed?
