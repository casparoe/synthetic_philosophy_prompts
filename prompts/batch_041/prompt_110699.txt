I have been trying to find a name for a pattern I keep noticing in myself and in others, and I would like to know whether it corresponds to anything established. Someone is asked to report on their own mind — why they dislike a colleague, why they chose one offer over another, why a certain consideration matters to them. The explanation is not fabricated: it names something they genuinely think. But the asking itself appears to do work. Once the reason has been stated, it begins to guide the person's subsequent behavior more strongly than it guided the behavior being explained, and the person becomes, in retrospect, more accurately described by what they said than they were when they said it. The report was true of the attitude but not of what had been driving it, and the request for the report is what made it true of the latter as well.

This is not quite confabulation, because nothing is invented; not quite a self-fulfilling prophecy, because the person is not predicting anything; and not quite the familiar laboratory problem of subjects picking up on what the experimenter wants, because there is no experimenter and no cue. Is there an established term for a report that becomes true in a new respect because it was elicited — and if there is not, how should the phenomenon be defined so that it is clearly separated from those three neighbors? The label matters less to me than the boundary: what exactly must happen for a report to count as an instance, and what would be a case that resembles this one but is not an instance at all?
