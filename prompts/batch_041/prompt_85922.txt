Ok so my econ teacher showed us the centipede game last week and I've been thinking about it non-stop and it's kind of making me crazy. Quick version in case you need it: two players take turns, there's a pot of money that grows every time someone passes, and on your turn you can either take the pot or pass. If you take it you get the bigger share and the other person gets less; if you both keep passing to the very end, the last player takes a huge pot. The math answer is that you should take on the very first turn, because you reason backwards: on the last turn the player will obviously take, so the person before them has no reason to pass, so... all the way back to turn one. But every time I've seen it actually played, including with grown-ups and real money, alot of people pass for ages and both end up way better off than the math says. So there's this weird standoff where the logic seems airtight and also seems obviously wrong about what people do, and nobody can settle it, and apparently people have been arguing about it for decades without moving. Thats the part I want help with — not really "which side is right", but what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Here's my hunch, tell me if it's dumb: I think both sides agree on all the math and probably the experiments, and the real fight is about what the word "rational" is supposed to be doing. Like, is game theory supposed to describe what an ideal perfectly logical agent would do, an ideal that real humans can just fail to live up to? Or is it supposed to predict what actual people will do, in which case the math is just wrong here? If that's the split, then it's a fight about the point of the theory, not about the game itself. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually an empirical thing that the right experiment could settle, or a fight about whether the backward-induction reasoning is even valid as logic in the first place (someone told me it quietly assumes each player knows the other is logical, and that they know that they know, forever, and that this might be doing all the work).

So: diagnose it. Substantive disagreement, a verbal one about "rational", two groups answering different questions under one name, or a hidden empirical or formal question in disguise? And the thing I really care about: if there was a small chance the other player was just the type who keeps passing (like 1 in a million, or they might be a "cooperator" bot), does that actually dissolve the paradox, or does it just swap in a different game that has a boring answer? I have a feeling it dissolves it but I can't tell if that's a real resolution or a cheat. And last thing — what would actually count as progress here? A new experiment, a theorem, some distinction nobody's drawn yet, or should I just accept this one can't be settled and stop losing sleep? I want the diagnosis with reasons, not a history lesson.
