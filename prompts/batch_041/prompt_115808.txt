Two years ago a close friend and I fell into a disagreement that has since become a permanent feature of the relationship. The subject is mundane and I won't burden you with it; what matters is its shape. We are equally informed, we have exchanged every argument and every piece of evidence either of us has, no further inquiry is available to us, and we are still on opposite sides. Each of us privately suspects the other of a failure of reasoning rather than a gap in information.

I recently encountered the common prior assumption and Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want to know whether they condemn us. Please give me your credence in the following claim:

P: Whenever two agents are fully rational, share all their evidence, and have common knowledge of each other's credences and reasoning, they cannot persistently disagree; persistent peer disagreement of this kind entails that at least one party is irrational or in error.

I don't want a survey. I want a number and its decomposition. Break P into the sub-questions it actually rests on, give each a rough probability and one line of justification, and say how they combine. I would include at least: whether the common prior assumption is a requirement of epistemic rationality or a modeling convention (Harsanyi's purification argument cuts one way, the uniqueness-versus-permissivism debate about rational credence the other); whether the shared-evidence and common-knowledge premises are ever approximated by real deliberators, or whether the theorem's antecedent is an unattainable idealization that makes P vacuously safe; whether divergence in rationally permissible priors or inductive biases is a genuine possibility; and whether the machinery survives agents whose awareness of the space of possibilities can grow. Add any term you think belongs. Then name the sub-question the number is most sensitive to, and the consideration that would move it most.

My own credence in P is about 0.35; my friend's is about 0.8. Tell me which sub-question we most plausibly disagree about.

Throughout, keep clearly separate what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested; do not present a contested position as the consensus. End with the number on its own final line, in the form \answer{0.XX}.
