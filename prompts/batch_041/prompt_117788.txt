I am a professor of philosophy, and next month I have to run a single seventy-five-minute session in our department's interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar — non-majors, mixed years — on whether everyday conventions such as tipping, queueing and jaywalking impose obligations rather than mere expectations, and on whether a visitor is bound by the conventions of the place she is visiting. I do not want the explanation itself. I want the plan for delivering it, and I want it to be exact about what depends on what.

The session has to install apparatus the students will not bring with them. I want them to separate three grounds on which a breach of a convention can be criticised: the costs it imposes on identifiable others; the reliance it defeats, where others have arranged their conduct around the expectation; and the slight it conveys, understood as a refusal of membership or of the respect the practice is taken to express. The three are independent — a breach can impose real costs and carry no slight, and can carry a slight while costing no one anything — and I want that independence to be visible to them rather than merely asserted. Keep separate from all three the further question of standing: who is entitled to object to a breach, and in what register. The visitor question should arrive late, as the discovery that a visitor's obligation and a local's standing to object can come apart in both directions, and not as an occasion for observing that customs differ.

So: give me the sequence of components, with the dependency that fixes each placement. Then the example that should carry the session. I am inclined toward queueing, and I want to be told whether that is right, what the example must have — a breach that is visible, cheap to avoid, and not already answered by a statute — and what it must lack; I take jaywalking to be disqualified because the law does the students' thinking for them, and tipping because the absent server and the wage structure will swallow the session in distributive questions. Then the misconceptions, in the order the room will produce them — the students will arrive regarding all of this as etiquette, beneath philosophical notice — and I expect the conflation of arbitrariness of content with optionality of compliance, the assumption that conventional and moral are exclusive categories, the belief that the law settles the matter, the thought that the visitor is simply exempt, and the inference that a convention binds because of what would happen if everyone broke it; for each, the precise point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. Then the single question I should ask at the end, chosen so that the answer separates a student who has the three grounds and the standing distinction from one who has merely acquired the opinion that conventions matter. Then what I should refuse to explain at this level, and say that I am refusing it. Several students in the room are international, so the visitor question is not hypothetical for them; the plan needs to keep it philosophical without turning anyone into a specimen and without letting the discussion collapse into the observation that customs differ. No readings, no citations.
