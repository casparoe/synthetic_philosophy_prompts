I am a novelist, and for three years I have been writing a book about a man whom everyone, himself included, calls decent — decent in every circumstance but one. Whether that phrase names a person or only a set of occasions has become the whole question of the book, so I have been reading the psychology of character, and a friend who teaches philosophy and I have assembled the following argument, which I have not been able to break.

(1) To say that someone has a character trait — honesty, courage, compassion — is to say that the characteristic behavior shows up across the whole range of situations in which the trait is relevant, so that what he does is explained by the trait rather than by the occasion.
(2) The experimental record says otherwise. Whether a man stops to help a stranger depends heavily on whether he has just found a dime in the coin return of a payphone; seminary students sent across a campus to speak on the Good Samaritan walked past a man slumped in a doorway when they had been told they were late and stopped when they had not; and when the same people are watched across many occasions, the correlation between their conduct in one setting and their conduct in another is slight.
(3) So features of the situation, not traits, are what predict what anyone will do.
(4) Therefore no one has the sort of trait that holds across situations. My friend grants that people have local habits — cordial to strangers and curt at home — but insists that a virtue confined to a single setting is not what anyone means by a virtue.
(5) Virtue ethics is a theory of precisely those traits, and of the praise and blame they license; if there are none, the theory has no subject matter.
(6) So virtue ethics is false, and the moral credit and discredit we assign to character is unearned.

She calls this decisive. My objection is only a feeling, and the feeling is that the argument proves too much: the experimenters ran the same procedure on hundreds of subjects and got the same result, and the researchers who report that situations dominate keep finding that situations dominate. If (2) and (3) are true, then the people who produced the evidence are as situation-bound as their subjects, and a regularity that rests on nothing stable in anyone is not the kind of thing that could survive the trip from one laboratory to the next. If I must choose, I would rather keep (2) and lose (6), but I want to know what that costs me.

Tell me which step I should give up, why it looked harmless, and what giving it up costs. Answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion; let the first name the step to abandon and the last name what I lose.
