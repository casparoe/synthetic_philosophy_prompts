Question: In class we used two labels. A lump of clay is *shape-indifferent*: squash it, roll it, rearrange its parts however you like, and the lump is still there. A statue is *shape-committed*: squash it and it's gone. (a) Is the statue identical to the lump of clay it's made of? Give the argument. (b) What does your answer to (a) tell us about the constitution relation? (c) The artist now squashes the statue and then remoulds the same clay into a shape you can't tell apart from the original — same clay, same shape. Is that the original statue back, or a new one? (d) Suppose the lump had already been exhibited as a found-object artwork by someone else before the sculptor ever touched it, so that for a while there were two artworks made of exactly the same clay. Are those two artworks identical?

Student's answer: No — the statue and the lump aren't identical. They're made of the same clay and have all the same parts, but they differ in their properties: the lump is shape-indifferent and the statue isn't, and by Leibniz's Law two things that differ in any property can't be one thing. So we have two objects in the same place at the same time, which is what the constitution view says: the lump constitutes the statue. Constitution is a kind of relative identity — the clay and the statue are the same clay but not the same statue. On the constitution relation itself: since the statue is composed of the lump, and the lump is what the statue is made out of, the lump is a proper part of the statue, with the statue being the whole. For (c): once you squash it, both go — the statue is destroyed because it's shape-committed, and the lump goes with it, because a pancake of clay isn't a lump anymore, it's just a flat piece of clay. When the artist remoulds it, what you get is the original statue back: the statue just is that shape, so the same shape plus the same clay gives you the same statue. For (d): those two artworks have to be the same object — they're made of the same clay and there's no room for two different things sitting in exactly the same place with exactly the same parts, so if one's an artwork and the other's an artwork, they're the same artwork.
