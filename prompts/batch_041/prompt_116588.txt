I'm the TA for a third-year course on evolution and the philosophy of biology, and I'm grading midterms. There is no official key for part (a) — I'll take your view on it, so please say what you take the paper's primary target to be and why.

The question was worth 10 points, split 3 / 4 / 3 across (a), (b) and (c):

Peter Godfrey-Smith distinguishes three kinds of adaptationism. Empirical adaptationism: most traits are in fact adaptations, and selection is the dominant cause of evolutionary change. Methodological adaptationism: the research strategy of looking first for the adaptive function of a trait. Explanatory adaptationism: the view that the ubiquity of adaptive design is the central phenomenon that evolutionary theory has to explain. (a) Which of the three does Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme" primarily criticise? (b) Explain how the architectural metaphor is meant to work as an objection, and why the authors think byproducts matter. (c) If the critique is sound, does it show that the other two kinds of adaptationism are false?

The student wrote:

In "The Spandrels of San Marco" Gould and Lewontin are attacking empirical adaptationism. The empirical adaptationist holds that essentially every trait is an adaptation shaped by selection, and the authors' objection is that this claim cannot be tested: for any trait, the adaptationist can invent a story about how it once raised fitness, and no observation could refute the story. The spandrel metaphor makes the point. In the Basilica of San Marco the spandrels are the triangular panels between the arches and the base of the dome, painted with the evangelists; nobody designed them as frames for the evangelists. Their number and shape follow necessarily from the geometry of arches meeting a dome, so they are byproducts of the building rather than products of design. Gould and Lewontin's claim is that many biological traits are like this: not adaptations but random products of genetic drift, noise in the system rather than engineering. Their name for the opposing view, "Panglossian", comes from Voltaire's Candide, whose Dr Pangloss insists that everything happens for the best. They also introduce the term "exaptation" for such byproducts when they are later put to use. Since the adaptationist programme cannot be falsified, it is bad science, and it follows that empirical adaptationism is false — selection is at best a minor force in evolution. The burden of proof therefore falls on anyone who wants to treat a trait as an adaptation.
