I should say at the outset that I have no academic training — I have spent my working life handling insurance claims, and what little philosophy I know I have picked up on my own in the evenings — so please bear with a layperson's way of putting things. For some time I have been trying to get straight on a disagreement about how a rational person ought to choose under uncertainty. The familiar view is that you take each possible outcome of a gamble, multiply the value it has for you by the chance of its occurring, add up the results, and pick whatever scores highest; on that view the spread of the risk across the outcomes does not matter in itself, only the average does. Against this, the philosopher Lara Buchak, in a book called Risk and Rationality, has argued for what she calls risk-weighted expected utility, on which a rational chooser has not two ingredients but three — beliefs, desires, and a risk attitude — where the risk attitude is a single function that re-weights the outcomes, bending the cumulative probabilities so that a given chance of landing in a bad state may count for more or for less than that chance would under the familiar rule; someone averse to risk gives added weight to what happens in the worse states, while someone venturesome does the opposite. What I find most striking is that the two pictures are not really rivals in one special case: when the risk attitude is the straight line that leaves probabilities untouched, the risk-weighted rule simply collapses back into ordinary expected utility. The whole quarrel, then, seems to live in the shape of that one function, and I would like to see that shape made visible.

Next month I am starting a small reading group — a few friends, some of whom have never seen a utility function and some of whom will have read the book — and I want to open the first meeting by reading a short poem aloud that makes the contrast vivid before we begin to talk. I would like you, acting as a poet who also understands this decision theory well enough not to fudge it, to write that poem. Here is the constraint I care about, because I think the form ought to carry the argument rather than merely decorate it. Picture a gamble with a fixed set of outcomes, all equally likely, ranked from worst to best. Give the poem one stanza for each outcome, in that order, worst first — but let the stanzas dwindle as the outcomes improve, the stanza for the worst outcome the longest and the stanza for the best the shortest, so that the shape of the poem on the page is the shape of a risk-averse weighting, with the worse states literally receiving more of the poem's weight than their bare probabilities would grant them. Then close with a brief coda that flattens the whole picture, saying in effect that if every one of those stanza lengths were made equal you would have the orthodox expected-utility chooser back again.

Please work this through before you write: first set out, in a sentence or two each, what the gamble is, how many outcomes it has, how sharply you intend the stanzas to shrink, and how you will make the coda land; then give me the finished poem, with a title, in whatever meter and rhyme you judge best serves it. Keep it honest to the theory — the outcomes should be plainly ranked, the shrinking should read as weighting rather than as a mere diminishing list, and the coda should fall out of the structure rather than be announced. It should be short enough to read aloud in under a minute, and I hope that someone who knows the theory will find it right while someone who does not will still find it a good poem.
