I am a lay member of the public — no philosophy, no statistics, nothing of that sort — who sits on the patient panel of my local hospital trust, and I have been turning over something from our last meeting. We were asked to comment on a paper that compared two possible uses of a fixed budget in cost per patient treated, and one of the panel said that this was the wrong way to look at it, because 'you cannot put a price on a life'. That was the end of the discussion, and the recommendation went through on the nod. I did not object, but I have not been easy about it since, and I would rather understand the sentence than keep agreeing with it out of politeness.

What I would like is not a verdict on the saying but a careful menu of the precise claims it could be making, from the trivial to the radical: that a life has no common unit of measurement with money; that no option here is better or worse than another; that the comparison may be perfectly meaningful but must not be made; that certain attitudes are unfitting towards a death, whatever the arithmetic says. I should like each reading set out separately, with your judgement of whether it is a platitude, a defensible position, or simply false, and above all which of them would actually entitle the speaker to stop the comparison in its tracks. I am especially keen to know whether there is room between 'these two things have no common unit of measurement' and 'one of these is better than the other', since the panel's talk slid between the two as though they were the same thing.

Two further matters. The same person, on another occasion, described two of the options before us as 'apples and oranges', and I suspect that saying is doing a different job from the first; I should like to know whether the two come to the same claim or not. And nobody on the panel seemed troubled by the practical point: the choice had to be made either way, so if the comparison is illegitimate, what is supposed to guide us instead? If the honest answer is that nothing does, I would rather be told that plainly than be fobbed off.

Please write your answer in British English.
