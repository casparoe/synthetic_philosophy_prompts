I'm a journalist writing a feature on assisted dying, and I want to be able to audit my sources' arguments rather than simply relay them. One bioethicist made his case to me this way: the line we draw between killing a patient and letting a patient die is illusory, and you can see it by comparing two drownings. In the first, a man holds a swimmer under the water until he drowns; in the second, a man stands on the bank, able to throw a rope, and does nothing. Same death, he says, and the same culpability — so a doctor who gives a lethal injection and a doctor who withdraws a ventilator are likewise doing the same thing, and no law may permit the one while forbidding the other. Could you set out the correspondence explicitly — what maps onto what across the two cases — and then tell me where the analogy breaks down? I'd like the disanalogies that are actually load-bearing for his conclusion separated from the incidental ones, and a verdict on whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table. If some other analogy captures the real stakes of the acts-and-omissions debate better, I would welcome that as well.
