I want the specification before I settle on a theory. By a theory of dispositions I mean an account of what it is for something to be disposed to produce a manifestation of type M when circumstances of type C obtain — solubility, fragility, flammability, infectivity, and the rest. The account is meant to underwrite the explanatory and predictive work such ascriptions do in the special sciences (materials testing, pharmacology, ecology) and the counterfactuals they are supposed to support. What I want from you is the list of demands any adequate account has to meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, rather than a verdict on the contenders.

Build the list around at least the following, and add whatever they force you to add:

- A stimulus that destroys the disposition at the moment it arrives, so that the thing would not in fact manifest if the circumstances obtained, though it is plainly disposed to.
- Conditions that block the manifestation while leaving the disposition untouched, where the blocking factor is no part of the thing itself.
- The manifestation delivered under the stimulus conditions, but by a mechanism independent of any disposition to deliver it.
- Dispositions whose manifestation is not an event but the absence of one — resistance, immunity, stability, a beam disposed not to fail under a given load.
- Manifestations that are probabilistic, or that come in several tracks, so that no strict conditional of the simple form holds.
- Dispositions that are never manifested, and whose stimulus conditions are physically impossible or otherwise unavailable, so that nothing settles what would have happened.
- Dispositions to acquire further dispositions, and dispositions whose stimulus conditions themselves presuppose the disposition.
- Bearers that are not individual material objects: a design, an algorithm, a population, an institution.
- Dispositions that exist in virtue of conventions or norms rather than physical structure.

For each requirement, state the case behind it and say what it rules out. Then sort the list: which of these are genuine desiderata, and which are demands that only one camp in the dispute has reason to press — for instance those who require that dispositions be analyzed into non-dispositional, categorical facts, as against those who treat dispositions as primitive features of the world. Flag any pair or subset of requirements that cannot be satisfied together, say which member has to give way and why; that is the part I most want. Then say which single requirement eliminates the most candidate accounts. A brief assessment of how the familiar conditional analysis fares, and of the two camps just mentioned, is welcome at the end, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves — I will do the scoring on my own. No citations or reading lists; I want the requirements.
