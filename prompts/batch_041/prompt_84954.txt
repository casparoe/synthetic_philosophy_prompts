I'm writing an essay on Shakespeare's Julius Caesar and want to read the play through Aquinas's account of tyrannicide rather than treat it as a mere illustration. Aquinas's position is narrower than the usual "tyrannicide is sometimes permitted" summary: a tyrant who has seized power without legitimate title may be resisted or killed, but against a ruler whose title is lawful and whose rule is merely oppressive, private individuals may not take the killing into their own hands, since that belongs to public authority, and he warns that removing a tyrant may deliver the polity to something worse. Rome in the play offers no such authority to appeal to, the conspirators are private men, Caesar holds lawful office and has not actually taken the crown, and what follows is the triumvirate and the proscriptions. Read the play under that framework. Does Brutus's conclusion that Caesar must die come out as justified resistance to a usurper or as precisely the private presumption Aquinas forbids, and do the last two acts vindicate or complicate his warning about the aftermath? I am especially interested in the anticipatory case, killing someone who is not yet a tyrant but is feared to be becoming one, which the Thomistic framework does not seem to cover cleanly, and in whether the play's Rome, where the republic is already an oligarchy and the crowd is turned by Antony, shows that the distinction between legitimate and illegitimate rule presupposes a healthy political order the play denies. Tell me where the play resists the reading, since I don't want to force it, and whether it dramatizes anything about the tyrant's ambition or the killer's psychology that Aquinas's account leaves out.
