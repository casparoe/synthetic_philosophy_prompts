A friend of mine and I have been circling the same argument for about two years now, ever since our book club read Samuel Scheffler's short book Death and the Afterlife, and I would like your help working out what kind of argument it actually is, because it has become clear to me that we will never settle it by trading examples over the wine. Here is the dispute. Scheffler asks us to imagine that we learn with certainty that the human race will be wiped out by an asteroid a few decades after everyone now living has died — not next week, but soon enough that there will be no generations after the ones we know. He suggests that if we truly absorbed this prospect, most of us would find our work, our hobbies, and even our attachments drained of their point, because the things we care about seem to borrow their importance from the assumption that human life, and the practices we take part in, will go on without us; he calls this the afterlife conjecture. My friend and I disagree about what follows. My own reading is that if he is even roughly right, then the extinction of humanity would be bad in a way that is not captured by counting up the deaths and the lives that would otherwise have been lived — there is an additional loss, something like the loss of the whole human enterprise and of the very conditions under which anything can matter to us. My friend thinks this is a confusion. She says the deaths and the un-lived lives are the whole of the harm; the asteroid is a catastrophe, but not a special kind of one, and the extra loss of meaning I keep invoking is either a fact about our psychology that we ought to get over (the apathy would be a failure of nerve, not a revelation about value) or simply the ordinary badness of all those future lives being cut off, dressed up in grander language. I find myself on Scheffler's side but I cannot defend it without repeating him, and she finds my reasons question-begging, and neither of us has moved an inch.

What I want from you is not a verdict on who is right but a diagnosis of what is going on. Is this a substantive disagreement about what makes extinction bad, or a disguised disagreement about a factual question — namely whether the conjecture about our psychology is even true, which neither of us can settle from the armchair? Is it two people answering different questions under one heading, one of us asking what actually moves us to care about things and the other asking what makes a thing worth caring about, so that we keep talking past each other while sounding like we disagree? Or is the word bad itself doing double duty, sliding between a loss that is bad for someone and a loss that is bad in some impersonal sense, so that the exchange is closer to verbal than either of us would like to admit? My own hunch — offered as a hunch — is that mattering is being used in two incompatible ways: for Scheffler it seems to require an ongoing audience, a community of valuers who will keep the practice alive, whereas my friend treats mattering as a property a state of affairs either has or lacks regardless of who is left to notice it. If that is right, is there any common measure that could let the two senses be weighed against each other, or is this the kind of disagreement that no argument could close? And whatever your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a thought experiment that actually separates the two claims, some finding about how people really respond, or a reason to think the question was badly posed from the start and ought to be replaced by a better one.
