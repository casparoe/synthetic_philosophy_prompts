I am a philosopher of language, and next month I am presenting a paper at a small workshop. Before I face the room I would like to rehearse the discussion in writing.

The paper defends a claim that will be familiar in outline and, I hope, sharper in detail than the version usually heard: that the fluency of contemporary neural machine translation gives us no reason to credit the translating system with understanding, and that the standard functionalist reply — that a system which reliably produces what an understander would produce simply is one — mistakes a correlation for an explanation. To state the claim precisely I distinguish two grades of translational competence. Mapping is the capacity to produce, for a given source string, a target string that fits the distribution of human translations of comparable strings. Interpretation is the capacity to recover what the speaker was doing in uttering the sentence — which of its presuppositions are live, which reading the context selects, what the utterance is meant to accomplish — and to let that recovery govern the output. My claim is that current systems have become very good at mapping while remaining close to zero at interpretation, and that the gap surfaces in a diagnosable class of cases: sentences whose correct rendering depends on inferring communicative intent rather than on any regularity in the bilingual data — bridging reference, for instance, where the target language forces a choice that the source leaves to inference, and the right choice is fixed by what the speaker is trying to do rather than by any lexical correspondence. I take this to be a sharpened restatement of the old charge, associated with Hofstadter, that statistical translation is fluent without being understanding, and I take the stakes to be a question about what benchmark success entitles us to infer.

The audience will be about twenty people. Most are computational linguists who work on evaluation and are functionalists by temperament, for whom understanding is either operationalised behaviourally or not a real question at all. Two are philosophers of language, externalists about meaning, who are likely to press whether understanding is a natural kind in the first place. One is a deflationist in the Dennett mould who holds that the distinction I am drawing is a distinction without a difference.

I have twenty minutes to speak and roughly fifteen for questions. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a room full of Searleans — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do; each attributed to the segment of the room that would raise it, and phrased the way that person would phrase it; each with a reply that fits in two minutes; and, for each, a note on where that reply is weakest. Close with the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, and say why I am hoping it.
