I am presenting at a departmental colloquium next month a position I call procedural infinitism. The thesis: the regress-of-reasons debate — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — has been conducted as though the regress were a chain of beliefs an agent must actually possess, but the regress that matters is a regress of challenges. What is potentially infinite is the space of legitimate questions, not anyone's stock of reasons. Justification is therefore a standing ability to meet challenges, modeled as an open-ended dialogue rather than a completed structure; I hold that the premise-to-premise principle is the wrong regress-generator, and I replace it with a closure principle on challenges.

The audience is mixed and I can predict the factions. Two formal epistemologists will ask whether challenge-meeting can be represented probabilistically at all, and whether my dialogue is supposed to be diachronic. One colleague works on epistemic injustice and will argue that any account tying justification to the capacity to keep answering challenges will further penalize speakers whose credibility is already discounted, and that my dialogue rules silently assume a patient, symmetric interlocutor. A Wittgensteinian will say I have redescribed the regress rather than dissolved it, and that reason does not work the way my model needs it to. The rest are graduate students who will raise the standard objections from the literature.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede at the outset, which objection deserves a long answer if it comes, what I should refuse to defend rather than concede, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
