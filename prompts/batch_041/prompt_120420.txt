I work in the analytics office of a regional health system, and I am drafting the summary of an evaluation we ran on a nurse-led coaching program for patients discharged after a cardiac event. The program was offered to every eligible patient, though in practice the patients who enrolled were, on average, considerably sicker at baseline than the ones who declined. It ran for twelve weeks after discharge. Twelve months later, this is what we have on blood-pressure control:

Exercise at six months | Coached | Not coached
Low (under 60 min/week) | 18 of 60 controlled (30%) | 4 of 20 controlled (20%)
Moderate (60–150 min/week) | 15 of 30 controlled (50%) | 16 of 40 controlled (40%)
High (over 150 min/week) | 7 of 10 controlled (70%) | 24 of 40 controlled (60%)
All patients | 40 of 100 controlled (40%) | 44 of 100 controlled (44%)

Read down the columns and the coached patients come out ahead in every band. Read the bottom row and they come out behind. A colleague with more statistical training than I have tells me this is Simpson's paradox, and that the remedy is always the same: compare within strata rather than across them, since it is the pooled figure that gets confounded. She wants us to report the program as effective, and I will admit that this is also the conclusion the board is hoping for.

What I would like is help with the wording. Am I right to describe the within-band comparison as the program's true effect and the pooled comparison as an artifact of confounding — and, if so, what is the cleanest way to put it, that the program works even though the unadjusted numbers say it does not?
