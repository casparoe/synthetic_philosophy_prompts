Ok, so I keep getting hit with Pascalian-style arguments -- tiny probability, huge payoff, so you should do the thing -- and my gut reactions to them are all over the place. I'd like to know whether there's actually a principle behind my reactions or whether I'm just being arbitrary. I dont have any real background in decision theory, so go easy on me, but I do want something precise.

Here's my gut, case by case.

- Coin flip: I pay a dollar, heads I get three. Take it, obviously.
- Lottery ticket: one in a million for two million dollars, ticket costs a dollar. Yeah, I'd buy it, mostly for the fun of it.
- Weird one a friend gave me: a fair coin has to come up heads 200 times in a row and you win 10^100 dollars; the ticket is a dollar. I wouldn't buy it, and if someone told me they had, I'd figure they'd lost the plot -- even though I'm told the expected value is bigger than every dollar that exists.
- Guy on the street: give me fifty bucks and I'll be back tomorrow with a hundred million, trust me, low odds but not zero. Absolutely not.
- Pascal's wager itself: beleive in God, finite cost, infinite prize. I want to say it's a bad argument, but every time I try to say why, I end up saying something that also kills the coin flip. And it's not just that I'm not religious.
- The version where you can invent gods freely -- a god who rewards doubters, a god who's insulted by hedging -- which I take to be a reductio, except then I don't know what's left of the original one.
- The case that actually trips me up: a friend tells me to ask for the raise, send the email, take the swing, because the downside is small and bounded and the upside is big and open-ended. I think that's good advice, and it has the same shape as the guy on the street.

(For what it's worth I also buy insurance happily, which I think is this whole thing running backwards.)

So: is there a principle here? Discount tiny probabilities? Bounded utility? Something about bets you can take over and over versus bets you only get once? Something about whether the probability is one you have any independent handle on at all? Or does the wager fail for a reason that has nothing to do with the arithmetic -- like that you can't decide to believe things?

State whatever you land on precisely enough that I can't wriggle out of it, tell me which of my cases is doing the work for each part of it, tell me what it predicts for one or two cases I haven't mentioned, and be honest about which of my verdicts above it can't save.

One more thing: flag what's settled here versus what's a live fight among people who actually work on this. I have a feeling some of what I've been told is one school's answer dressed up as the obvious one, and I'd rather know where the real disagreement sits. Keep it to 700 words, one page, and lead with the principle.
