Last spring I served on a jury in a homicide case — nothing exotic; the dispute was over who had started a fight and how it had ended — and the rules the judge gave us have bothered me ever since. Each witness swore to tell the truth and to leave nothing out, and we were told that a witness who lied under oath could be prosecuted for it. Yet when one witness began to describe what a neighbour had told him, the judge stopped him and told us that we must not treat the neighbour's statement as evidence of what had actually happened, even if we found the witness entirely credible about having heard it. The lawyers were equally insistent that a witness tell us what she had seen and heard, not what she believed or supposed. But the pathologist who had performed the autopsy was permitted to tell us what, in her opinion, had caused the fatal wound, and a second expert was permitted to say whether the physical evidence was consistent with the defendant's account of the fight. Neither had been present; both conceded under cross-examination that they could not be certain. So within the space of an afternoon we were told to set aside a sworn, credible report of what someone had said, and invited to lean on an expert's judgment about matters she had not witnessed and could not have known.

I have been taking an evening course in epistemology, so I know there is a literature on when a speaker is entitled to assert something — that some philosophers say you may assert only what you know, others only what is true, others only what you have adequate reason to believe. My question is what our courtroom practice presupposes about that. Does the law of evidence rest on a view of when someone may assert something, or is it really a body of rules about what a jury may rely on — a matter concerning hearers rather than speakers — so that it presupposes an epistemology of evidence rather than an ethics of assertion? If it does presuppose a view of assertion, which one fits? How can a single practice forbid assertions the witness plainly knows and permit assertions the expert cannot possibly know? And which rival views does it quietly rule out — in particular, what must it assume about whether knowledge can be passed from one person to another by testimony, given that the witness with the neighbour's story may well have known precisely what he was forbidden to tell us?

What I would like is the implicit theory, stated as plainly as you can, followed by an honest accounting of how much of it the practice genuinely commits to and how much is left open, so that a knowledge account, a truth account, and a purely evidential account might each license what we saw. I have noticed the same shape of rule in another place — the contributor guidelines of a large online encyclopedia, which tell contributors that what they know first-hand does not belong in an article unless a published source says it — and I would like to know whether that is the same puzzle or a different one. Finally, suppose we replaced whatever view is operating with a single flat rule: a witness may assert exactly what he knows, and nothing else. What would actually have to change in the courtroom?
