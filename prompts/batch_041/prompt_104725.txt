Last night my friend and I burned about two hours arguing about the absent-minded driver problem, and I've decided there's an actual paper in there, so I want to turn it into a proposal. I'm applying to my department's small summer research fund — like four grand, one summer, and the proposal has to be two pages max, so the scope has to be believable for a summer and a half-time me.

Where we got stuck. As I understand it, the standard resolution is that you can't ask what to do at the intersection; you have to evaluate whole strategies, and the reason the local calculation says exit is that it double-counts the possibility of being at the second junction (if you were going to exit you'd have exited at the first). Correct me if I've mangled that, and if I have, write the proposal around the corrected version, because I don't want to build on a misreading.

Here's the half-formed idea. The puzzle is always set up as a fact about the driver's memory — he's absent-minded, he forgot he already passed the first intersection. But the version that interests me doesn't need any forgetting. Suppose the road loops and the two junctions are genuinely identical, or suppose I'm working through a written procedure and I've lost my place: nothing has gone wrong inside my head, the world is just symmetric with respect to what I can tell. Does the paradox survive that translation? I think it does, and I think that's the interesting result, because it means the puzzle was never really about imperfect recall — it's about the same act being either the first or the second move in a plan and you can't tell which. Second, in every real case I care about, the agent can usually break the symmetry cheaply: tie a string on your finger, leave the box on the counter, make the nurse tick the whiteboard. So does cheap endogenous marking dissolve the problem, or just push it up a level, since now you can be unsure whether you already left the marker? Third — and this is the bit I think is a real contribution — most practical versions are design problems rather than agent problems. Not what should I do, but what protocol do we hand someone who can't tell which stage they're in: the surgical instrument count, the pre-flight checklist, the did-I-already-take-my-pill case where taking it twice is worse than missing it. There the fix isn't better reasoning about strategies, it's changing the payoffs so that the first-stage exit isn't catastrophic.

So: write the two-page proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, an honest account of why the existing treatments leave it open (positions and arguments, no citations — I'll fill those in myself), the method — what I'd actually do, month by month, over one summer, and keep the formal apparatus small enough that I can actually solve it (I'm thinking a two-stage model with a marker action, then three or four informal protocol cases) — what a result would look like versus what would count as failure, and a scope that a skeptical reviewer won't laugh at.

One more thing. My friend's parting shot was that this is a pseudo-problem: any real agent would just leave a marker, and the puzzle only bites for an artificially stupid agent who is conveniently forbidden from changing the world. Take that seriously — I'd rather the proposal say up front that this is the thing that could kill the project, and say what would have to be true for it not to.
