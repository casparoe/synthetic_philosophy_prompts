Two colleagues of mine — call them the Maximizer and the Restrictionist — have spent a year arguing about expected value fanaticism, and both have agreed to co-sign a joint statement of what they agree on and where they still differ. I am chairing the session at which they present it, and I need you to draft the document they put their names to.

The Maximizer's position: among the acts available to an agent, the one with the greatest expected value is the one it is rational to perform; expected value is computed from a real-valued, possibly unbounded value function together with the agent's credences; no probability is so small that a sufficiently large payoff becomes decision-irrelevant; and no outcome is so valuable that a single act cannot be rationally required in pursuit of it. He treats every proposed restriction on the rule as either a change of subject — a different decision rule rather than a repair of expected value — or an unprincipled fudge, and he is willing to accept the counterintuitive verdicts.

The Restrictionist's position: she accepts the arithmetic and denies that it binds. Expected-value maximization, on her view, is the right rule only where the magnitudes and probabilities it takes as inputs are ones our evidence and our theories license us to treat as determinate; where a recommendation turns on a credence of 10^-20, or on a single outcome worth 10^24 units, no act is rationally required, because the inputs are artifacts of an idealization rather than genuine quantities. She denies that this amounts to refusing to follow an argument where it leads.

The statement must be built around these three cases. Read units of value as whatever the parties take value to be measured in, and treat the numbers as exact.

Case 1, the Toggle. Act S yields a certain 10^7 units. Act T yields 1.1 × 10^7 units with probability 1 − 10^-20 and a loss of 10^24 units with probability 10^-20. The expected value of T exceeds that of S by roughly 10^6.

Case 2, the Ladder. At rung n the choice is between a certain cost of 10^6 units and a lottery that averts, with probability 10^-n, a harm of 10^(n+9) units. The lottery's expected benefit is 10^9 at every rung, so the arithmetic recommends it at every n; what is in dispute is how far up the ladder the recommendation binds.

Case 3, the Ordinary Case. A certain cost of 10^6 units against a lottery that averts, with probability 10^-2, a harm of 10^9 units. I expect both parties to take the lottery; if one does not, that is itself worth recording.

The document should contain:

A numbered list of agreed claims: what both can sign, about the cases, about what expected value is, about what would count as a reason, and about the status of their disagreement. Each claim must be one a third party could sensibly deny — no truisms, and nothing about the value of continued dialogue. No more than ten.

A disagreements table: for each sub-question on which they differ, give the sub-question, the Maximizer's position in wording he would accept, the Restrictionist's position in wording she would accept, and whether the difference is genuine or verbal. No more than six rows.

The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each, state the case or argument both parties agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. For Case 2 I want the disagreement located as a region and not a point: as a function of probability and magnitude, where does it begin, and what does each side predict at a rung in the middle of that region?

A verdict on the following candidate principle, which I put forward so that they can accept, amend, or destroy it. The Grain Principle: credences come in a finest grain — for any kind of event there is a smallest difference in credence that a person can coherently hold or that evidence can support — and no comparison turning on a finer difference can impose a rational requirement. Does either side accept it? If the Restrictionist accepts it, does it do the work she needs, or does it leave fanaticism intact whenever the probabilities are coarse and the stakes are large? If the Maximizer rejects it, on what ground?

A short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, if any, and what turns on disambiguating them.

A closing section on what each party would count as evidence or argument against his or her own position, and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate.

Constraints: the statement must be self-contained, since it will be read by graduate students who have not met this debate before, and it must not cite, name, or report the views of anyone — no authors, no papers, no appeals to what is generally thought. Argue the positions; do not summarize a literature. Keep the whole document under about 1,800 words.

Finally, I want a committed document, not a survey. Where the drafting forces a choice, make it and defend it. If you judge that one of the two positions cannot be stated without incoherence, say which and why. If you cannot find a formulation of some disagreement that both parties would sign, say so, give the formulation you used, and state what it costs the party whose wording you did not use. If you think a crux cannot be settled by any evidence either party would accept, say that plainly rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
