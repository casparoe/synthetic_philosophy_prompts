My aesthetics reading group keeps re-running the same argument about the paradox of fiction. Everyone grants that we are moved by characters we know to be invented; everyone grants that we know they are invented; all the fighting is over the further claim that an emotion requires a belief in the existence of what it is about. I want a decision tree that turns this into a diagnostic instrument: a flowchart that takes a reader from answers to concrete cases to a named position, so that we can see whether we genuinely disagree or are describing different parts of one experience.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of labels. The first node should ask whether any genuine emotion is present at all, as opposed to a pretend or make-believe state, an as-if state adopted without belief, or bare arousal with no object. Later nodes should split on (a) whether the reader accepts that emotion requires a belief in its object's existence; (b) where the reader locates that object — the character as a nonexistent particular, the thought or proposition the text supplies, the real-world situation or human type the fiction makes salient, or nothing determinate at all; (c) whether the state is a full occurrent emotion or an offline, decoupled, muted version of one; and (d) whether the opening triad is a contradiction to be solved or a reductio of one of its members. Add a node testing whether the reader's account of fiction-directed emotion is meant to fall out of a general account of emotion — one that also covers fear of merely possible accidents, dread of losses that have not happened, and anger at the dead — or is a fiction-specific patch; a position that fails that test should be flagged at its leaf rather than quietly dropped.

The cases have to be ones on which the positions actually come apart, so include at least: recoiling from a monster on screen; weeping at a character's death in a novel; grieving after a long series ends; a reality-television contestant whose presentation you suspect is partly scripted; a documentary that uses actors to voice real people; a game character whose fate is under your control; a fiction whose villain you find yourself rooting for against your own judgment; a story about a real historical atrocity from which you cannot separate the real victims; and an abstract painting or instrumental passage that moves you with no character anywhere in it. Every node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node may restate a position ("do you think the emotion is real?") or ask for a label.

Deliver roughly six to nine branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. Each leaf should carry the position's name where a standard one exists, or a descriptive label where it does not, plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, and a mark for whether the account generalizes beyond fiction or is fiction-specific. Follow the tree with a table of the answer combinations it cannot reach, with a short diagnosis of each (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored); a list of the pairs of positions the tree cannot separate, saying what further question would separate them and why the obvious question fails; and a note on what a reader who reports no emotional response to fiction at all gets sorted as, and whether that leaf is occupied by any view you think anyone actually holds. Handle two sibling paradoxes — why anyone seeks out sadness in tragedy and fear in horror — either as sub-branches or with an explicit argument for why they need their own tree; the self-imposed-dose hypothesis, on which we enjoy horror the way we enjoy chili and rollercoasters, belongs somewhere in that part, and I want to know whether you treat it as a rival to the philosophical resolutions or as a supplement to them. Do not supply titles, dates, or reading lists, and do not name an author unless you are confident of the attribution; where you are not, describe the view instead. Assume a mixed group, so gloss any technical term on first use.
