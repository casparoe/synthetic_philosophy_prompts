Two friends of mine, Mira and Dan, spent most of a long car journey arguing about a puzzle, and I sat in the back without contributing anything, partly because I couldn't decide which of them was making sense. I have no training in philosophy or economics; I've just been chewing on this sort of thing on my own for a while, and I'd like you to tell me what is right and what is confused in the exchange below.

The puzzle they were arguing about is a one-shot game between two strangers. Neither player knows who the other is, they will never meet again, and each privately presses either the green button or the red button. If both press green, each gets £3. If both press red, each gets £1. If one presses green and the other red, the one who pressed red gets £5 and the one who pressed green gets £0. Each player is told that the other is a stranger who has been given the same puzzle and has spent a good deal of time thinking about games of this kind.

Dan: Press red. Red is better for me whatever they do — if they press green I get 5 instead of 3, and if they press red I get 1 instead of 0. Nothing about my situation changes that.

Mira: That is precisely the argument the other player will make. If red is better for you whatever they do, then it is better for them whatever you do, and you both press red and come away with 1 each, when both pressing green would have given you 3 each. Your argument has the two of you landing somewhere you would both rather not be, and where each of you could see it coming. That isn't a proof of what to do; it's a reductio of the reasoning.

Dan: It's a proof about me. What they do is a fact about them; what I do is a choice. If they press green, red is still better for me, and I would take the 5 and be pleased with myself. The fact that they may run the same argument tells me something about what they will probably do. It doesn't change what is best for me to do.

Mira: You keep treating their button as though it were already fixed while you decide yours. But it isn't fixed independently of how a person in exactly this situation reasons, and they are in exactly this situation. If the reasoning that gets you to red is correct, it gets them to red as well. So "red is what a rational person presses here" and "they will press red" come to the same thing — and once you say that out loud, you have said that your correct reasoning guarantees you £1. A piece of reasoning cannot be correct if it can see its own conclusion and still walk into it. The only reasoning that survives that test is: press the button you would want pressed if everyone in your position pressed it on the same reasoning. That gives green, and it is stable — you both get 3, and neither of you can point to the reasoning that robbed you.

Dan: "Everyone in your position" is doing all the work there, and it is sleight of hand. They are not in my position; they are in theirs. They might be sentimental, or a Kantian, or bad at arithmetic. And even if they were exactly like me, "same situation" doesn't give "same conclusion" unless we are the same machine, running the same code, on the same inputs. All I can really have is a probability. And the moment you let the probability in, your special principle evaporates into ordinary expected-value reasoning with a guess about the other person's psychology — at which point the answer depends entirely on the guess, and there is nothing left over that deserves the name of rationality. There is something fishy about the shape of your argument, too: you tell me to press green because you predict I will press green.

Mira: It isn't a circle, it's a fixed point. You are looking for a rule that is stable when both people use it, not for a premise you already believe. And the probability doesn't dissolve my case, it makes it. Look at what your argument quietly assumes: that their button is causally independent of my reasoning. But my reasoning is a process happening in a person, and theirs is a process happening in a person, and if the two processes are alike then the two buttons are correlated. "Red is better whatever they do" only holds when the correlation is zero. If there is any real chance the other person is running this same argument, pressing red is a bet against myself.

Dan: Then here is a case that shows how little your principle is worth. Suppose we are told the other player is a perfect duplicate of me — same history, same code, guaranteed to reach the same conclusion I do. Your principle says green, we both press green, we both get 3, and you are satisfied. Now change one thing: the duplicate gets an extra pound for pressing red. He is no longer in my position — our payoffs differ — and your fixed point is gone. Your principle falls silent, and mine answers instantly. A principle that speaks only when the other person is my twin, and cannot manage a word the moment anyone's payoffs differ, is not a principle of rationality. It is a description of a coincidence that happens to hold when two people are identical.

Mira: Then answer me this. In the twin case, your rule tells me to press red and tells him to press red, and we each end up with 1, each of us knowing that green would have got us 3, and each of us knowing the other knew it too. You would call that the rational outcome?

Dan: I would call it the correct one. The tragedy is that it is correct. If you want to call it irrational, you have to stop asking what a person should do and start asking what two people should do — and then you owe me an account of why the pair of us is a unit whose payoff is the thing to be maximised.

Mira: I never said I was maximising the pair's payoff. I am still maximising mine. It is just that mine isn't separable from theirs.

Dan: Then you are maximising yours by a rule that says: never mind what your button does, press whatever would be best if everyone pressed it. In a room where nobody else follows that rule, it just makes you the one who goes home with nothing.

Mira: And in a room where everyone follows it, you are the only one who goes home with 1. Which room are we in? You can't answer that by staring at the game. You have to look at the person across the table, which you told me was irrelevant.

Dan: It isn't irrelevant. It is just not part of the choice. I look at the person across the table to work out what they will do, and then I press red. You look at them, decide they will press green, and call your guess a principle.

I honestly could not tell who had the better of it, and they have both been insufferable about it since. Please adjudicate. Specifically:

1. Go through the exchange move by move and rate each contribution, from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. I would rather have a point-by-point verdict than a general verdict.
2. Dan's "red is better whatever they do" argument is supposed to settle the matter on its own. Mira says it only works if the two buttons are causally independent, and that they are not, because both are outputs of similar reasoning processes. Who has the better of that exchange, and why?
3. Is Dan's twin case — the duplicate who gets an extra pound for pressing red — a genuine counterexample to Mira, or does it only mark the outer boundary of where her principle is meant to apply?
4. Mira says her rule is a fixed point rather than a circle. Does that answer Dan's circularity charge or merely restate it?
5. I came across the word "superrationality" afterwards and couldn't tell whether what Mira was describing is the same idea, a version of it, or something she made up on the spot. I don't want a reading list, just an assessment of how her position relates to whatever that term names.
6. What are both of them missing? Is there a third way of thinking about the puzzle that neither of them considered?
7. If you had to say who is more right overall, who would it be, and on what grounds?
