I teach a 300-level seminar on social epistemology — 18 students, one 75-minute session next Thursday — and I want to do the discursive dilemma as something they run into rather than a lecture I deliver (I don't want to even name it until the debrief). Here's the skeleton I want you to turn into a runnable activity: I hand out a short civil-case packet (a delivery driver says a customer's dog bit him; there's disputed evidence on causation, and disputed evidence on whether the driver provoked the animal or assumed the risk), and the class sits as a liability committee. Three propositions get voted on by everyone: P, the dog's bite caused the injury; Q, the driver provoked it or assumed the risk (i.e. there's a valid excuse); and R, the customer is liable — which by the stated rule of the case is true exactly when P and not Q. Each student votes on all three, and I want the case packet rigged so that every individual's three answers are internally consistent, but the group's majority comes out yes on P, no on Q, and no on R, so the group's own winning premises imply liable while the group has just voted not liable. The whole point is that nobody made a mistake and there's still no coherent group answer. Then I want to split the room: one half redecides the case by voting only on the verdict R (conclusion-based), the other half votes on P and Q and lets R follow (premise-based), and the two halves — same people, same evidence — walk away with opposite verdicts. Please make the case packet concrete enough that the rigging is invisible, give me roles or a voting procedure and a timeline, and keep it moving so we actually hit the split with about 25 minutes left.

Then design the debrief. I want it to walk them back through the exact moments — where they assumed their own consistency was enough, where the tally surprised them, the moment the two halves disagreed — and map each onto the actual structure: the inconsistency is a property of the agenda (the logical connections among P, Q, R), not of anybody's preferences being weird, which is what seperates this from the Condorcet voting-cycle thing they'll want to compare it to. I'd also like a beat where someone tries to fix it by telling people to just vote their true underlying values, and we see that doesn't help; and if there's room, a second round where we add a fourth proposition or change the agenda to see whether the trouble can be designed away (my impression is that some agendas escape it and some don't — is that right? get the details straight for me). Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: a chunk of the class insists the case was just ambiguous and they should've been given more evidence, and another chunk jumps straight to saying majority rule is broken), how to handle both, and which feature of the real result the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
