I manage a small data-science team at a mid-sized insurer, and I've spent the last month inside what I'm fairly sure is one argument wearing three hats. Here is the setting. Our churn model is an ordinary gradient-boosted tree ensemble — a few hundred features, nothing exotic — but my team and I have drifted into talking about it as though it had aims: "it's trying to hold on to the high-value accounts," "it thinks this one is already gone." Priya, the engineer who maintains it, says this is a category error and that we should stop, because there is nothing in there that wants anything, and the loose talk will cost us later when we start explaining its failures in terms of motives it doesn't have. My defense is that the talk earns its keep: when I think of the model as trying to keep customers, I can guess what it will do on an account I've never seen, and when I think of it as a large pile of arithmetic I mostly can't. Marcus, who handles deployment, says we are both making too much of it — he treats the model as a box with a contract (this input, that output, these failure modes) and doesn't need to say anything about what's inside, one way or the other. Meanwhile marketing has built a retention campaign around a customer segment the model surfaced, and our staff statistician says segments like that are cheap — with enough features and enough rows you can always carve a population into groups that look coherent — and that ours is an artifact of the particular feature set we happened to collect. Marketing counters that it has held up across two years of data and predicts response. Nobody is sure what would settle it.

The third thing, and the reason I'm writing: Priya has been reading around and now claims there is a fourth way of predicting what a system will do that none of us has been using, which she calls the audit reading. You don't ask what the thing is made of, or what it was built to do, or what it is aiming at; you ask what its outputs have to look like for it to keep being used — who reads them, what they get compared against, what happens to the system if the numbers move the wrong way. She says you cannot recover that from the other three, because it is a fact about us and not about the model, and that it is the reading that actually predicts deployed models, since they drift toward whatever the metrics reward. Marcus thinks she has just relabeled the deployment reading. I suspect she may be right, but I can't tell whether that is a real distinction or a verbal one.

So what I want is an answer that hangs together rather than three separate opinions. Is Priya's fourth reading a genuine addition, or a re-description of something we already had? Was she right that our "trying to" talk is a mistake, or is the shorthand defensible even though nothing is in there that wants anything? And what would actually settle the fight over the customer segment — what kind of evidence would tell us the segment is a real feature of our customers rather than a convenient artifact of the feature set we happened to collect? Please open with your verdict in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the situation back to me.
