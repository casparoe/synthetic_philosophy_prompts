I am convening what I intend to publish as a joint statement arising from an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft it. The two camps are the pattern realists, who hold that the design stance and the intentional stance succeed in describing patterns that are genuinely there in the systems so described, so that there is a fact of the matter, independent of our convenience, about which patterns a system has; and the pattern instrumentalists, who hold that talk of patterns, and of a pattern being real, names at most a pragmatic status conferred by our predictive and explanatory needs, with no further fact of the matter beyond usefulness. This is not adjudication and not a dramatised dialogue; the deliverable is the document that both camps would put their names to.

Both camps have agreed to work with the following proposal of mine as the collaboration's working definition, and not as a settled result: a pattern is present in a system to the degree that a description which mentions the pattern yields shorter or more reliable predictions of the system's behaviour than any description which does not, and this talk of degrees is not a manner of speaking but does irreducible work. Neither camp may set the definition aside as a mere reformulation; each must state what follows for its own position if it is accepted.

The document should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both camps accept: what the three stances (physical, design, intentional) are for, and what each does and does not claim; which predictive and explanatory successes are common ground; what the difference between a stance that is useful and a stance that is true could even amount to; and what both agree would settle the matter. Second, a disagreements table with one row per residual dispute, giving the disputed claim, the realist's formulation in wording the realist would sign, and the instrumentalist's formulation in wording the instrumentalist would sign. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and, for each crux, the case, thought experiment, or body of evidence that both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Where part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why, and what would dissolve the appearance of disagreement.

A further requirement: separate plainly the claims that are settled for anyone who knows this area from those that are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. Do not supply citations or a reading list; where a point turns on a particular published position, characterise the position rather than attributing it. Write in British English.
