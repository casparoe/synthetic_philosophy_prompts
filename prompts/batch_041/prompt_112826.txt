I'm 41, just came out the far side of a divorce I didn't want, and I spent most of last winter not sleeping and rereading whatever was on the shelf. Somehow I ended up on Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness — she wrote it in the 1740s, it's this odd half-diary essay about what actually makes a person happy, and I'm sure I dont understand half the context but the part I can't stop chewing on is her claim that you need your illusions. Like, someone who sees their life exactly as it is — no self-flattery, no rosy picture of how much the people around them love them, no exaggeration of their own importance — is going to be miserable, and the fix isn't to strip the illusions out, it's to keep them and *also* have one big passion that doesn't depend on anybody else. Her candidate is the love of study, because nobody can take it away from you. My therapist would have opinions about this, ha.

Anyway, I want a short story out of that tension. Setting: not quite now, some company runs a residential program people pay through the nose for — call it the Clear Room — six weeks where you systematically dismantle your own illusions. You write a pitiless inventory of your relationships, your friends and family submit anonymous ratings of you, a "reader" sits with you and describes exactly how you come across, and you're forbidden from saying anything flattering about yourself the whole time. There's some mechanism that makes cheating hard — I'll leave the details to you, but I want a scene where the protagonist almost breaks it. She's a woman in her forties, recently divorced, signs up believing that finally being honest about herself will make her better; she's also genuinely an intellectual, the kind of person who really does love studying things, and that's the one thing the program can't confiscate. I want the story to put the two du Châtelet ideas in a headlock: the illusion that's quietly load-bearing for her, and the passion that could survive without it. The turn should hinge on one specific belief she has to decide whether to keep — about her ex, or her late father, or whoever, your call — and please don't land it on either "she learns to live in the truth" or "ignorance is bliss." I want to feel why the illusion might be holding something up, and why the studying might be enough anyway even if it isn't.

1500–2500 words, close third person. And one picky request: nobody in the story should ever say the word "illusion" out loud — no reader explaining the philosophy in dialogue, let it live in the rules and the behavior. I want to be able to hand this to my reading group without them thinking it's a lecture.
