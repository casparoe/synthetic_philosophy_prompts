I chair the remembrance and collections committee of a small independent trust that holds some four hundred recorded testimonies from survivors of a mass atrocity committed a generation ago, together with photographs and case files. At our next meeting we must commit a single ring-fenced sum to one of two proposals: a permanent public exhibition with a schools programme, which the survivors' association has campaigned for, or the director's alternative — thirty years of restricted access to the most graphic recordings, with the money instead funding bursaries for descendants — which she defends on the ground that perpetual exposure conscripts a community into living the remainder of its life as victims. Complicating matters, about a fifth of the recordings were given on terms of anonymity, and many narrators have since died, so their families now claim a say in how the material is used.

Two members have circulated notes that I would like the memo to engage with rather than pass over. The first argues that an archive is never a neutral container: silences are produced at four separate points — when an event is first made into a fact, when facts are assembled into a collection, when a researcher retrieves them, and when retrospective significance is assigned — so that an embargo is an intervention at one of those points rather than a mere omission, and she asks who has standing to make it. The second argues that a duty to remember is owed within a community that already shares a memory, that what is owed to those outside such a community is only the avoidance of humiliation, and that we should therefore stop invoking a collective duty to remember on behalf of people who do not share one.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, give the strongest case for the option you reject its due, and write in British English.
