I'm a reinforcement learning engineer, and my working vocabulary is MDPs, Bellman backups, value functions, discount factors, episodic versus continuing tasks, and the standard engineering arguments about how to make a problem well-posed. In a design review last month we spent an hour arguing about γ for a long-horizon planning agent: one colleague wanted γ around 0.99 because we cannot trust our model of the distant future, another wanted γ = 1 with a proper absorbing terminal state because discounting is a bookkeeping convenience that should not be smuggled into the objective, and a third said the choice of γ is simply a preference and therefore nobody's business but the product owner's. It struck me afterwards that we were having a much older argument in a new notation, and I would like you to make the translation rigorous rather than suggestive. Take the main positions in the philosophical debate about pure time preference and the discounting of the future — roughly: (i) that ranking a good lower merely because it lies further in the future is a bias, on a par with ranking it lower because it is spatially distant or because it accrues to someone else, and that an agent's own future selves deserve no less weight than its present self; (ii) that discounting is a response to uncertainty, to the risk of non-arrival, or to the ill-definedness of infinite sums, and therefore carries no ethical content at all; and (iii) that the intergenerational case is not a fact about any single agent's preferences but a distributive question among parties who never coexist — and restate each of them, one by one, in my vocabulary: discount factors, episodic versus continuing formulations, average-reward objectives, value functions, naive versus sophisticated time-inconsistent agents, reward shaping, and the usual tricks for rendering a problem well-posed. Be as faithful as you can, and resist the temptation to let the translation flatter my framework: where a philosophical position can only be expressed by assuming something I don't normally notice I'm assuming, say so explicitly rather than quietly supplying the assumption.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of these claims survive the translation intact, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether my framework can even mark the distinctions the philosophers seem to care about — between discounting the time at which a good occurs and discounting the person who receives it, between a preference that is instrumentally justified and one that is merely held, and between a future populated by the same agent and a future populated by different people or by no one in particular. I would also like the reverse report: what does my vocabulary smuggle in that the philosophers would reject, for instance in treating γ as a free parameter belonging to a single decision-maker with a fixed identity across time. I know the standard results — that γ < 1 buys convergence under bounded rewards, that γ = 1 requires care, that average-reward is a different objective rather than a limiting case — and I am comfortable with the usual models of time-inconsistent preferences, but I have no philosophy training and my acquaintance with the ethics literature consists of a few half-remembered arguments I could not reconstruct on demand. Where the residue looks like a gap in my framework, tell me what the gap is; where it looks like an artefact of the original dispute being vaguer than it sounded, tell me that instead.
