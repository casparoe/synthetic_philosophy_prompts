After thirty-five years in a physiology laboratory — most of it on whole-animal respiratory control, with the last decade spent reading philosophy in a fairly systematic, if amateur, way — I have arrived at a view about causation that I would like you to try to break. The view is this: causation is a relation between occurrent, positive states of affairs, realized by physical processes, and an omission — a failure to act, an absence, a non-occurrence — is never itself a cause, however convenient it may be to speak as though it were. When we say that a guard's falling asleep caused the fire to spread, that the absence of a screening program caused a death, or that a knockout animal's missing protein caused its phenotype, I claim we are doing something pragmatic: picking out, against a background of normal conditions, the one deviation that a given practice of blaming or explaining finds salient. The causal work, I want to insist, is always done by what is present — the embers, the untreated lesion, the disinhibited pathway that has lost its brake. What I want from you is a map of how exposed this view is. Not the general complaint that it is counterintuitive, which I have heard, but specifics: cases where an absence appears to do causal work for which no positive state can be substituted without explanatory loss; results in causal modeling that handle omissions and events with identical machinery, such that the difference would be arbitrary if it were merely pragmatic; the failure-analysis and loss-of-function practices of working scientists and engineers, who seem to get real predictive traction out of assigning causal responsibility to things that did not happen; and the old suspicion that every cause is entangled with absences — a match lights only because nothing nearby is wet — which, if true, would make my criterion vacuous rather than restrictive. I should add that a laboratory memory of mine may be a test case: two cultures failing to grow, one because a growth factor was left out of the medium, one because a receptor was deleted from the cells, and I have never been able to see how either description survives translation into a list of positive states without remainder.

There is a further worry I would like you to take up directly, because it may be fatal to my own position rather than to its rivals. I find that I can never identify which omission caused a harm without appealing to what someone was supposed to do — to a norm, a duty, an expectation, a designed function — and yet I also want to hold that such norms are justified, where they are justified, by the causal facts themselves. That looks circular, and I would like to know whether it is viciously so. If the distinction between an omission that causes and a merely absent condition is drawn entirely by our expectations, does that vindicate my view, on the grounds that there is no natural difference to be drawn, or refute it, on the grounds that a difference evidently exists and I have mislocated it? Please sort whatever you give me by weight — what would be decisive against the view, what would be awkward but survivable, and what a defender would simply concede without giving ground — and tell me what my position predicts that its rivals, the counterfactual-dependence accounts and the accounts that admit omissions as causes of a second-class or derivative sort, do not predict. Finally, say plainly whether my insistence on positive processes has quietly become unfalsifiable, and whether that is a defect in a metaphysical view of this kind or simply the price of the sort of claim it is. I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking for the shape of the case against me.
