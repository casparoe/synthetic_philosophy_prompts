Here's the answer:

"Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth: a planet molecule-for-molecule identical to Earth except that the clear, drinkable, life-sustaining stuff in its lakes and rivers is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ. Oscar and Twin Oscar are exact duplicates. When Oscar says 'water' he means H2O; when Twin Oscar says 'water' he means XYZ. Since the two are physically identical but their words mean different things, meaning can't be in the head. Putnam stresses this was already so in 1750, before anyone knew the chemistry -- each one's 'water' picked out the local stuff all along, whether or not the speaker could tell the difference. So the experiment establishes externalism about meaning: what a term refers to, and therefore what it means, depends in part on facts about the environment rather than on the speaker's internal state. Burge's arthritis example makes the same point about the environment of the individual: the man who believes he has arthritis in his thigh has a belief whose content is fixed by the physical facts about his body and the world, which he may be completely unaware of. The upshot of both is anti-individualism: the content of a person's mental states isn't determined by what's inside the skin. This also shows we can't have privileged first-person access to our own thoughts -- knowing what you believe turns out to be an empirical matter, since it depends on facts you'd have to go and investigate. And it's a point about natural-kind terms specifically: only there is there a physical fact of the matter to fix the extension, so nothing like this carries over to a term such as 'marriage' or 'cruel'. The moral is that the mind extends beyond the head, so any psychology that treats mental states as internal states of the organism has been refuted."
