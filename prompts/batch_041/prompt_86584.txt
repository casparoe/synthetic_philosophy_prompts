So I moved to Leipzig about four months ago for a job (I'm American, grew up outside Columbus) and something small happened that I can't stop chewing on alot. I crossed a pedestrian light on red, like 11:15 at night, not a car anywhere. A guy walking the other way stopped and said something to me. Not aggressive. Just corrective, the way you'd tell a stranger his bag was open. Then he looked at the light, then at me, and kept walking. I felt like a kid and I couldn't tell whether that was his doing or mine.

I told this at a dinner at my flat last week and it turned into a two-hour thing between Jonas, who's German and works with me, and Dana, an American friend who was visiting. Neither of them was being stupid, which is the annoying part. Roughly how it went:

Dana: the light is a safety device, full stop. Its purpose is to keep you from getting hit. At 11pm on an empty street, obeying it isn't moral, it's just failing to think about what the rule is for. If you can't tell a rule from its purpose, she said, you're not being good, you're being obedient, and those aren't the same thing.

Jonas: the purpose isn't only safety, it's that nobody has to work out what everybody else is about to do. The whole value of a norm is that it applies without anyone re-deciding whether it applies today. And once each person gets to decide when the rule is silly, it stops being a norm and becomes a negotiation, and the people who pay for a bad negotiation are the slow ones — kids, old people, anyone who can't dart.

Dana: then you've just told me the norm is useful, not that it's binding. If its value is saving everyone the trouble of guessing, then where there's nobody to guess about, the value is zero, and obeying anyway is superstition.

Jonas: if you cross, you're saying the rule is for other people. He was sharp about this one. Dana said no, she's saying the rule has nothing to do there, and that's a different claim.

Then Jonas added something I keep coming back to: you're not a conformist if you'd of done the same thing anyway. Following a rule you happen to agree with isn't obedience, it's just agreeing. Dana said fine, but then from the outside nobody can ever tell a free person from a very well trained one, and that matters, because the well trained one doesn't know he's trained.

And then it flipped, and this is the part I actually want help with. Dana said Americans are the rule-bound ones, not the Germans. In the US nothing happens without a contract, a waiver, a sign, an HOA covenant, a form, a lawyer; we don't trust each other to just know how to behave, so everything has to be written down and enforced, which is why we sue each other over everything. Germany, she said, runs on the unwritten stuff — you all just know, and that's why you can be loose. Jonas said that's backwards and it's exactly the problem: a country that has to write everything down has already lost the thing that makes it a country. But then he said something that confused me, which is that the German way is better precisely because the norms are inside people, not because they're rigid — the rigidity is just the price of not needing a cop on every corner.

Dana: your norms are habits you were born into and never chose. Mine are at least things people agree to. Conscience is something you answer for yourself; your Sitte is just the neighbors watching. A rule I didn't pick and can't question isn't morality, it's furniture.

Jonas: that's the whole American disease, and it comes down to which philosopher you took seriously. He said the old Kantian line is that an action has moral worth when you do it because it's required, not because you feel like it or because it pays. If you only follow a rule after you've personally signed off on it, you've made your own judgment the standard, and then the standard is different for every person, which is the same as no standard at all.

Dana: Kant is the villain here. Doing a thing because it's required, when you can see it's pointless, isn't autonomy, it's obedience in a costume. Being an adult means using your own judgment, including about when a rule has stopped earning its keep.

I have to live here, and I'd honestly rather understand this then just pick whichever side makes me feel less like a jerk. So can you go through it move by move — the safety point, the coordination point, the "rule is for other people" point, the "you're not a conformist if you'd have done it anyway" thing, the inversion about who's actually more rule-bound, Sitte vs. conscience, and the Kant exchange — and say who's right about what? Rate each move if that helps. Then the big one: which of them has the better of it, if either? I have a hunch they're answering different questions, but I don't trust that hunch, so tell me what each of them is missing and whether there's a version of either position that survives. Don't just tell me it depends.
