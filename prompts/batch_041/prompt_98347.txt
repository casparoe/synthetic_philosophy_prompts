My old roommate has a habit of ending arguments with a line he picked up from some pop-philosophy podcast: 'Death is nothing to us.' The way he uses it, it's a trump card. Last month my grandad died about six weeks before my wedding and I said I was gutted he never got to be there, and my roommate told me that was incoherent -- grandad doesn't exist anymore, and you can't be harmed if you don't exist, so there was never anything for me to be sad about on his behalf. Since then he's pulled the same line out to say it doesn't matter whether he finishes the book he's writing before he dies, that someone who dies at 30 hasn't lost anything that someone who dies at 90 gets to keep, and -- this is the one that got me -- that it's silly to care about what happens to your reputation after you're gone. Though weirdly, when a dead author he likes got accused of something awful last year, he was definately furious on the guy's behalf. I pointed out the tension and he just said the two cases are different.

I want to go back at him with an actual list instead of a vibe. 'Death is nothing to us' seems like it could be saying several pretty different things and he keeps sliding between them. Some readings sound almost tautological, some sound like a real metaphysical claim about what can be bad for whom and when, some sound like nothing more than advice about what not to fear. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, say for each whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or just false, and then tell me which readings actually support which of his moves -- the grandad one, the unfinished-book one, the reputation one? And is there a reading under which his being angry on behalf of the dead author is consistent with the rest, or does he have to give something up somewhere? Please don't just hand me the standard 'the dead don't exist' gloss; I already know that one, it's the one he's hiding behind, and I want to see the whole menu including the readings that would make him right.
