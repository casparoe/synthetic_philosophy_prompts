I am a secondary-school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy and economics society, and I would like your help making it more rigorous than the standard treatment. The topic is the independence axiom of expected utility theory and the Allais paradox. Our society has already heard the textbook version, so I want to approach the subject through a mistake I suspect we have all been making. A friend summarised the axiom for our handout as follows: if you prefer one gamble to another, then adding the same extra chance of some third outcome to both of them cannot reverse your preference. Please do three things. First, explain in what sense this is a misleading statement of the independence axiom — what it leaves out about the structure of the lotteries, or about what is being held fixed — and give the formulation we should have used instead. Second, explain why the preferences commonly associated with the Allais paradox are said to violate the axiom, and identify the precise step at which the violation occurs rather than simply asserting that the two conflict. Third, consider a defence I have not seen addressed in our reading: suppose the chooser insists that his preferences are not over final outcomes at all but over the gambles taken as whole objects, so that the element the axiom calls common is never genuinely common for him. Does this rescue his preferences from the charge of irrationality, or does it merely relocate the difficulty? Please pitch the explanation at an intelligent peer who has not studied decision theory, make the reasoning explicit at every step, and flag any point at which the standard treatment is genuinely contested rather than presenting it as settled.
