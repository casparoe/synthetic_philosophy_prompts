I'm designing a five-year research program on precommitment, and I need to decide what it should work on first. The group will be three to five philosophers and legal theorists. There is no lab, no dataset, and no realistic prospect of empirical data, so the work has to be conceptual. What I want from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey: an ordering of the open questions, together with the reasoning that generates the ordering.

The program's remit spans both the individual case (Ulysses bound to the mast, the dieter who empties the house of sweets, the addict who signs a commitment contract) and the collective case (constitutional entrenchment, independent central banks, supermajority and entrenchment clauses, treaties that outlive the governments that ratified them). Let me impose a framing on that remit, and then ask you to prioritize within it.

The standard treatment runs the two cases together, on the thought that a constitution is an individual's self-binding writ large — a collective self binding its future self. I want you to work instead with a sharper distinction, which I will label as follows, though you should tell me whether it survives scrutiny:

- Diachronic self-binding: one and the same agent at two times, where the later agent is plausibly a continuation of the earlier one, and where the earlier agent can claim some authority over the later one's choices.
- Heteronomous entrenchment: the binding party and the bound party are numerically distinct people, and the supposed self that does the binding may be a fiction — there may be no single agent whose future preferences are being protected.

If that distinction is real, it threatens to break the analogy on which a great deal of the existing discussion leans. Assume for the sake of argument that it is at least worth taking seriously.

Given that framing, which open questions about precommitment are most worth the group's five years, and in what order? For each question you rank highly, tell me:

- why it is upstream of the others — what downstream work would be wasted or misconceived if the question went unanswered;
- how tractable it is for people working with conceptual tools alone;
- whether it is specific to one of the two cases above or sits at the joint between them;
- what, concretely, would move it up or down your ranking over the next couple of years.

Please also flag the questions you take to be overrated — the ones that look central but on which little downstream work actually depends — and say what makes them dispensable. Two candidate questions I want you to place explicitly somewhere in the ordering, even if you conclude that they are badly posed. First: whether a precommitment requires a meta-rule governing its own revocation, and whether that meta-rule generates a vicious regress or a benign one. Second: whether there is such a thing as a precommitment to a procedure for forming future preferences, as opposed to a precommitment to an action or a policy, and whether that is a genuinely distinct object or merely ordinary precommitment one level up.

Close by telling me what you would drop if the group had three years instead of five, and why.
