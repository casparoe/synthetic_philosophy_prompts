I'm giving a paper at a Nietzsche workshop in a few weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet, because this audience is going to come at me from four directions at once and I want to know the order.

Heres the thesis, roughly. The bits in Ecce Homo where he goes on about diet and climate and where he lives are not just the old man's self-report or late-style performance. They're the load-bearing test case for his own claim that a philosophy is the expression of a physiological condition. So: if the diet talk is just autobiography, then the whole "philosophy as symptom of the body" line collapses into autobiography with it, and the will to power stuff goes down too. But if we take it seriously as doctrine, something worse happens -- whether a philosophy is any good gets indexed to the health of the body that produced it, his included, which means only the healthy can assess it, which is a nice self-sealing circle. My paper bites that bullet. I argue he knows it and wants it: the diet talk is meant to be *run* by the reader as a test rather than believed as a claim, so Ecce Homo is an instrument, not a treatise. I also lean on the point that in the dietetics tradition the regimen is addressed to a particular body rather than to everyone, which is why he can say what's healthy for him isn't healthy for you without being a relativist about truth. I think that's both the best defense of the reading and the crack in it.

The room: mostly people who work on the texts. Several are alergic to treating Ecce Homo as philosophy at all -- persona, rhetoric, late style, the whole thing. Two or three are deep in the Nachlass and will smell biographical reduction on me immediately. There are a pair of naturalist readers who like Nietzsche as a philosopher of human nature and will say that drives and health already do all the work I'm asking the diet anecdotes to do, so why do I need the anecdotes. And there's one person who works on nineteenth-century medicine, who will point out that in the medical language of the period a "Diät" was the whole regimen -- sleep, air, exercise, company, the old dietetics inheritance -- not a menu, and I'm fairly sure they're going to use that against me.

What I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, not the ones I'd get at a general theory conference, phrased the way each of them would phrase it and attributed to whoever in the room is likely to say it. Order them by how likely they are times how much damage they do. For each one give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft or where I'm bluffing. Tell me what to concede early to buy credit with the philological wing, and what I must not concede even under pressure. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have a bad feeling about it.
