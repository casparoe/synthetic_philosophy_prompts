Two friends of mine have been arguing for a year and neither will budge. One is a legal moralist in the Devlin line: a society is partly constituted by a shared morality, so the law may ban conduct just because it's morally wrong, even when it harms nobody but the willing participants -- with the caveats that the immorality is only a prima facie reason, defeasible by factors of principle and of expediency, that the judgment of wrongness has to be the ordinary person's rather than the reformer's, and that the conduct has to be a real, established deviation rather than an isolated private act. The other is a liberal in the Hart/Mill line: harm to others is the only legitimate ground of coercion, offence and moral disgust don't count, and the costs of moralistic enforcement -- hypocrisy, blackmail, arbitrary policing, erosion of respect for the law -- are reasons of the same empirical kind that the moralist has to weigh.

Write the joint statement they should have produced instead of a year of shouting. Numbered list of agreed claims first (about what each side is actually committed to, about what the other side's view does and doesn't require, about which parts are empirical), then a dissagreements table: for each crux, the two positions in wording each side would accept as its own, plus the case, argument or evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Also say what each would count as evidence against its own view, and whether any part of the fight is verbal, given how far the word harm can be stretched to cover the erosion of a shared morality.

I'd especially like these pressure points worked out rather than restated: (1) does the moralist need the moral judgment to be an actual consensus in the community, or just the correct one, and what happens to the view in a town where 45% think the practice is fine and 55% think it's wicked; (2) the pair -- the same act done quietly by eleven people for thirty years, versus the same act franchised, advertised and sold, which moralists always treat as a different case and liberals usually don't; (3) whether it matters that the proposed ban is a criminal offence rather than a licensing or zoning rule, and whether each side's principle actually covers both; (4) my friend's proposed test -- a ban on conduct that harms no one but the willing participants is legitimate only if two-thirds of residents call the conduct morally wrong, they call it wrong without attributing any harm to it, and it gets enforced at the same rate in every neighbourhood -- is that a fair statement of either side's view or a straw man, and what does each side predict about how such a rule would operate in practice? And give me one case neither of them has thought about that the agreed claims don't settle.
