I work on distributed systems — protocols, consensus, safety and liveness, Byzantine faults, deadlock and livelock, resource contention, congestion collapse, backoff and retry, priority inversion, and the stability of configurations in systems with no central controller. I am not a philosopher and I have no formal training in game theory, but I have been reading the cluster of ideas that travels under the names Moloch, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom, and I would like to understand it by translating it into the vocabulary I actually think in, rather than by absorbing the moralizing register in which it is usually presented.

The claim I want translated is roughly this: a collection of agents, each pursuing its own interest under competitive pressure and with no coordinating authority above them, can be driven into a configuration that every one of them would prefer to leave, that none of them can leave unilaterally because the agent who defects from the local best response is the one who is punished, and that no participant intended, designed, or wants — and yet the configuration is stable and self-reinforcing. Restate that claim as faithfully as you can in the terms of my discipline: agents with local state and local objectives, protocols and the rules they encode, safety versus liveness, equilibrium and stability, protocol and incentive design, failure modes and their taxonomy, convergence and divergence. Use one or two concrete failure modes as the test case — congestion collapse under individually optimal retransmission, livelock in a mutual-retry protocol, priority inversion, the thundering herd — and say which is the closest structural analogue to the multipolar trap and where each analogue breaks.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the important part. Which components of the claim translate without loss — for instance, does the stability of a trap correspond exactly to the stability of a bad equilibrium or a bad fixed point, and does "no one is to blame" correspond to a global failure with no faulty component? Which components translate only under distortion, and what exactly is distorted — for example, when the source analysis personifies the outcome as a quasi-agent with its own appetites, is there anything in my vocabulary for that (a scheduler, an adversary, an environment, an emergent objective), or is the personification something my framework cannot cash out at all? And which components cannot be expressed in my vocabulary under any translation: in particular, my discipline normally presupposes an external designer with the authority to rewrite the protocol and a specification that says what correctness is, so do I have any way to state the claim that in the real cases there is no such designer, that the participants cannot reach the fix even when all of them want it, and that "better" is defined by the participants' preferences rather than by any spec? Where I have to smuggle in an assumption — a central planner, a well-defined objective, a bounded adversary, a correctness criterion — tell me plainly that I have done so, rather than letting "equilibrium" or "coordination failure" quietly stand in for the original point.

Close by telling me whether the residue is a defect of my framework — that it can only model systems with an external specifier and a prior notion of correctness — or a sign that the original claim is doing something less precise than it appears.
