I'm in a reading group on preference learning, and I've found myself defending a view I'd now like to stress-test. Roughly: the right primitive for human preference is a scalar reward over state–action trajectories — a utility-like quantity — and behavior is the evidence for it; given enough of a person's choices, a reasonable prior about how they go about choosing (say, that they're noisily optimal rather than exactly optimal), and a fixed state representation, you can recover that reward well enough to predict and shape their behavior in new situations. Call it reward recovery. As I understand it, something like this is the working assumption behind most practical work on learning from demonstrations and from human feedback, and it has the virtue of turning preference inference into a well-posed estimation problem, which is exactly what you want if you intend to build anything on top of it.

What would count against it? I don't want the textbook objections restated; I want the ones that would actually move someone who already knows that any finite stretch of behavior is consistent with many reward functions, and that a reward is only pinned down up to transformations that leave optimal behavior untouched — so shaping a reward by a potential function of the state, for instance, leaves the optimal policy exactly where it was. Two things in particular deserve a serious look. First, the evidence base is behavior in the contexts we happened to observe. Cases where two people behave identically in-distribution but would diverge off-distribution — the donor who would stop giving if nobody could see, the person who complies only because compliance is cheap — are cases where behavior is not merely noisy evidence about a reward but systematically silent about it; and the standard reply, that we should simply collect off-distribution behavior, runs into the fact that off-distribution is precisely where reward models extrapolate worst. Second, a colleague's suggestion that the reward-plus-noise picture mislocates the primitive: on her view, much of what we call preference is really constraint-following — things a person won't do and won't trade off at any price — and a scalar reward that can price everything is a bad description of an agent whose values have that structure. I'd like you to say what the reward-recovery view predicts that a constraint-based rival doesn't, and whether the constraint view is a genuine competitor or merely a redescription of the same data.

Then the part I actually care about: sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be. Which of them would a committed defender concede counts against the view, and which would they dismiss as a change of subject or as a limitation of data rather than of the framework? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — every anomaly absorbed either by the rationality prior ("that's just noise") or by the hypothesis class ("the representation was wrong"), so that no observation could ever count against it — and if so, is that a defect or the unavoidable price of any inferential framework? Finally, tell me which of these considerations should carry the most weight with someone who starts out sympathetic, and what evidence would actually change my mind.
