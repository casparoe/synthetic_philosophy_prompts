A friend of mine is defending a dissertation on Frege's puzzle about identity statements next month, and she's asked me to write a poem to read at the party afterward — something that actually gets at the puzzle instead of just being nice about her. So that's the job: you be the poet, and definately a poet who knows the philosphy cold, since the puzzle is the whole occasion. The puzzle, flatly, in case it helps: a=a and a=b look like they ought to say the same thing whenever a is b, and yet a=b can teach you something while a=a never can — which means the news an identity statement carries can't be a fact about the object, since the object was never in question to begin with.

Here's the shape I want, and honestly the shape is the entire point. Two halves, line-for-line parallel — same number of lines, same metre, same rhyme scheme — so the second half reads like the first half in a different coat. Both halves should be about one and the same thing, but named and described in two different ways, and those two descriptions have to be things the reader can check against the poem itself, so that the identity clicks in them rather than getting asserted at them. Exactly one line should appear word-for-word in both halves (that's the a=a, the line that carries no news), and exactly one line should change between them (that's the a=b, the line where the floor moves). By the end I want the reader to feel the dead weight of the repeated line and the small jolt of the changed one, even though both halves concern a single thing; and the closing lines should land the thought that nothing about the object ever changed, only the two ways of putting it — that uncomfortable suggestion Frege flirts with, that an identity statement is really a statement about the signs and not about the thing they stand for.

Please keep it well out of the textbook: no morning star and evening star, no Cicero and Tully, none of the examples that live in the first chapter of every introduction. A place, a person, a number, a tune, whatever you like — as long as it's an identity the reader can feel happen. And before you write a single line, brainstorm a handful of candidates (some thing under two descriptions), pick the one where the click is sharpest, and only then build the parallel structure around it. Mind the metre in both halves — if the parallelism slips even a little, the whole conceit falls over.
