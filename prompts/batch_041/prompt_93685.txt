I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I'm trying to write a seminar paper on the standoff between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness, but the more I read the more I suspect I'm watching two people talk past each other rather than disagree. As I understand the two views, the epistemicist says a vague predicate like 'heap' or 'tall' does have a precise extension — there is a fact of the matter about the last grain that makes a heap, the last millimetre that makes someone tall — but the boundary is unknowable, so vagueness is a matter of our ignorance rather than of the world or the language being indecisive. The supervaluationist says the predicate has no single extension but many admissible precisifications, and that a sentence is true when it comes out true on all of them, false when false on all of them, and neither when it splits — so 'borderline' cases are cases where the sentence lacks truth value, and the felt absence of a boundary is vindicated rather than explained away. Both, as far as I can tell, accommodate the same ordinary linguistic data: that we use the predicate confidently in clear cases, withhold it in clear cases, and are at a loss in the middle. Both can run the sorites reasoning and block it somewhere. And yet the disagreement never resolves; every objection I read gets an answer that the other side finds irrelevant rather than wrong.

My working hypothesis — and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis to test, not as the answer — is that this is not really a disagreement about heaps, grains, or the sorites at all, but a disagreement about what a theory of vagueness is for. One side seems to treat the manifest absence of a boundary as a datum the semantics must honour, so that any view on which there is a sharp cutoff is disqualified no matter how well it does elsewhere; the other treats that felt absence as a fact about our epistemic situation and thinks a semantic theory need not reproduce it. If that's right, the two sides have different adequacy conditions and their arguments can't reach each other. But it might instead be a hidden formal disagreement (say, about whether higher-order vagueness is coherent for one view and fatal for the other, or about whether unknowable truths are possible), a disagreement about whether classical logic and bivalence are non-negotiable constraints or negotiable costs, or simply two theories answering different questions under one heading — one about the semantics of vague terms, one about the metaphysics of boundaries. I want you to adjudicate between these diagnoses rather than survey the literature.

Please act as a philosopher of language and logic with a specialist's command of this debate, think carefully before answering, and begin by laying out in your own words what each side is committed to and which of their arguments actually engage the other side's commitments. Then give me a diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about facts or about adequacy conditions, a hidden formal or empirical question in disguise, or two questions wearing one name? Support it with the specific moves that do the work — tolerance principles and how each side blocks them, the treatment of the determinacy operator and the higher-order problem, the epistemicist's claim that a boundary must exist even if we cannot locate it, and the supervaluationist's claim that there is no boundary to locate. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all, with reasons. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I have the literature — and do not hedge your way out of a verdict. Where my framing of either position is inaccurate or unfair, say so explicitly.
