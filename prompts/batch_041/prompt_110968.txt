I am a novelist, and for the past several months I have been circling a puzzle that belongs to you rather than to me. My whole craft runs on sentences of the form if this, then that: the if-clause is how I raise a possibility without asserting it, and a reader's sense of what is likely to happen next is really a sense of how probable the consequent becomes once she has taken the antecedent on board. So I have been trying to describe what I do in the vocabulary I actually think in — the story so far, what is established as against what is merely presupposed, the nearest world in which the protagonist has already made the fatal phone call, the difference between what a sentence says and what it does, subtext, free indirect discourse, the chance that a later chapter retcons what an earlier one seemed to mean, unreliable narration — and I have run up against a result I only half understand. As I have it: if you hold that the probability a reader assigns to an indicative conditional equals her conditional probability for the consequent given the antecedent, and you also hold that the conditional is a proposition, a determinate thing true or false at each possible world with its truth value fixed by the truth values of its parts, then, provided the equation must hold not only for her present degrees of belief but for all the degrees of belief she would acquire by learning something new, the position collapses — the conditional turns out to be the material conditional after all, or else, once you take the antecedent for granted, nothing is correlated with anything and the consequent is probabilistically independent of every other proposition, or the probabilities are otherwise degenerate. I have absorbed the shape of the argument from a magazine-level summary and cannot follow the algebra; what I want from you is not the algebra.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of language and logic who has genuine sympathy for fiction, and to do two things. First, restate the triviality argument, and the pressure it puts on the propositional view of conditionals, in the vocabulary I have just laid out, as faithfully as that translation allows — the story so far standing in for the context, the reader's conditional confidence as the thing to be explained, the move to the nearest world where the antecedent holds as the supposition, and so on. Second, give me the residue report: which steps of the argument come across cleanly, which had to be distorted to fit my terms, and which have no counterpart at all in how a novelist thinks. Then the question I actually care about. My hunch is that the trouble never gets started in my vocabulary, because I never treat an if-sentence as one fixed proposition with a truth value in every world; I treat it as an instruction about how to revise the story — add the antecedent, see what the story then demands — and the story so far is not a proposition but a body of presuppositions that can be adjusted wholesale. Tell me whether that hunch is a recognized escape route from the triviality results or something else, whether it genuinely escapes or merely relocates the difficulty, and which single assumption of the argument my vocabulary refuses to make; then tell me honestly whether that refusal is a principled position or just the absence of the apparatus that generates the problem in the first place.

Please think this through carefully before you write, and work the argument out for yourself rather than reciting what is usually said about it; if the formal version is unavoidable, give me the intuition behind it and spare me the notation. Write to me in continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I would also like you to be candid about your confidence: say how sure you are of each substantive claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of and why.
