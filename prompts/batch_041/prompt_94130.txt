Two of my classmates have been going back and forth for a week now about the so-called Knobe effect, and they have pulled me into it as a referee. I know the basic finding — the chairman who says he doesn't care about the environment and only wants profit, whose program foreseeably harms the environment, is said to have harmed it intentionally, while the chairman whose program foreseeably helps the environment is said not to have helped it intentionally — but their argument has gotten well past the textbook version, and I honestly cannot tell who is winning. I am reporting it as neutrally as I can.

First round. Ravi says the asymmetry is not an error at all; it shows that the ordinary concept of intentionality is not the neutral, purely causal notion philosophers of action tend to assume, but a "thick" evaluative one, closer to an assessment of the agent's quality of will. On his reading, calling the chairman's harming "intentional" is a way of saying his will toward the environment was bad, and the reason we don't credit the benefactor is that mere foresight of a benefit, without any concern for it, doesn't express a good will the way indifference to a harm expresses a bad one. So the effect is the concept working as designed, not misfiring.

Dana says that is backwards. Foresight is foresight; whether a side effect was foreseen with certainty does not change depending on whether the side effect is good or bad, so there is a fact of the matter and the asymmetry is a distortion of it — a moral-evaluation bias, like the way hindsight bias corrupts judgments of foreseeability. She adds that "intentionally" cannot simply mean "blameworthily," because we say people intentionally do good things all the time, and we ask whether someone acted intentionally precisely in order to decide whether to blame them; a term cannot presuppose the very verdict it is used to reach.

Second round. Ravi says she has conflated two things: aiming at an outcome and merely foreseeing it. The concept, he says, requires a certain kind of concern, and that is why it comes apart for good and bad side effects. Indifference to a harm you foresee is itself a defect of will, so the harm counts as intentional; indifference to a benefit you foresee is not a virtue, so the benefit does not. His view, he insists, is not "intentional = blameworthy" — it is that intentionality ascription tracks the quality of will, which happens to be valence-asymmetric without collapsing into blame.

Dana says this just relabels the problem, and then proposes what she calls the two tests that should settle it. Test one: a case where the side effect is bad but the agent is justified and genuinely not blameworthy — say a journalist publishes a true and important story, knowing with certainty that a source will lose her job and be harassed as a result, having done everything possible to protect her and being, we stipulate, within her moral rights. If Ravi's quality-of-will reading is right, the good will should block the "intentional" ascription; if the bare badness of the side effect drives the effect, people should still say the exposure was intentional. Test two: a case where the side effect is good but the agent is blameworthy for producing it — someone whose wrongful act foreseeably benefits a stranger, done for a corrupt reason. Ravi's reading predicts "intentional" here because the will is bad, even though the outcome is good; the asymmetry reading predicts "not intentional." She says she knows which way she is betting.

Ravi's reply is that the justified cases are exactly where folk concepts are supposed to get fuzzy, and that his account can absorb them by treating intentionality ascription as a graded, all-things-considered affair that is sensitive to justification — not a simple switch. He then turns the tables and says Dana's "distortion" reading leaves the ordinary concept an incoherent mess with no independent standard for what the undistorted answer even is, so she cannot say the folk are wrong without quietly importing the philosopher's neutral concept as the correct one.

Please adjudicate this point by point. For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then tell me who is right about what, and what both of them are missing — in particular, what you think ordinary people would actually say about Dana's two test cases, and what that shows about which reading of the effect is better supported. Finish by telling me how confident you are in each of your main claims and singling out the one point you are least sure of.
