I am putting together the closing session of a short course on Keynes's conception of the good life, and I want to read H. G. Wells's The Time Machine through it. Keynes, in the 1930 essay in which he predicts that the economic problem will be solved within a century, treats the real difficulty as what his grandchildren will do with their leisure, and assumes they will have to learn to live wisely and agreeably and well; he also expects the love of money to come to be regarded as a semi-pathological trait. In his late memoir of his Cambridge youth, he describes a generation that took from Moore a religion of states of mind while dropping conventional moral rules, and afterwards judged that outlook partly foolish for treating goods as commensurable and ethics as a sort of calculation. Wells, writing a generation earlier, imagines the arrival of abundance and leisure without anyone having solved Keynes's problem: the Eloi are beautiful, idle, incurious, and finally livestock, and the Morlocks are the labour on which the plenty silently depends. Read the novella against Keynes, and tell me what Wells's future implies about the assumption that leisure is good in itself, and about the thought that a leisured class will be the bearer of civilisation. Which scenes should I put to the class — the Time Traveller's first meal among the Eloi, Weena, the Palace of Green Porcelain, the machinery below, the dying world at the end — and what does each one show? Where does the book resist the reading, given how restless and work-driven the Time Traveller himself is and how far the novella is invested in struggle rather than enjoyment? And does Wells put his finger on a gap in Keynes's picture that argument alone would not expose, or is his pessimism just a Victorian faith in toil dressed up as prophecy?
