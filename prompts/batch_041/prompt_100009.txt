So I'm a journalist — I write about science and ideas, mostly, which means I spend a lot of time interviewing people who are smarter than me and then trying to explain what they said. Lately I've been messing around with this personal experiment: I write down little money gambles and record which side I'd actually pick, gut-level, no calculation. I've got a list now and I'm starting to think theres a principle under it, but I can't get it stated right. Wondering if you can.

Here's the list. (All one-shot, I'm not rich, treat the amounts as if they matter to me but not ruinously.)

1. A: a million dollars, guaranteed. B: 89% chance of a million, 10% chance of five million, 1% chance of nothing. I take A.

2. C: 11% chance of a million, otherwise nothing. D: 10% chance of five million, otherwise nothing. Here I take D. (Yes, I know these two together are supposed to be inconsistent if I'm maximizing expected money. I don't care, both verdicts feel right.)

3. E: nine thousand dollars, guaranteed. F: 95% chance of ten thousand, 5% chance of nothing. I take E. I'd pay real money just to delete that last 5%.

4. G: 1% chance of ten thousand, otherwise nothing. H: a hundred dollars, guaranteed. I take G. Same hundred in expected money either way and I still want the ticket.

5. Everything's a loss here. I: lose five hundred for sure. J: 50/50 between losing twelve hundred and losing nothing. I take J — I'd rather gamble. Which is the opposite of how I am with gains, and that bugs me.

6. Three outcomes: K: 50% chance of two hundred, else nothing. L: 25% chance of two hundred, 50% chance of a hundred, else nothing. Same expected money. I take L, but only barely — I feel like the middle outcome is doing almost no work for me.

7. And the one I keep going back and forth on: M: 95% chance of five thousand, 4% chance of five hundred, 1% chance of losing twenty thousand. N: four thousand, guaranteed. Some days N, some days M. I honestly can't tell which way I really go.

My hunch, for what its worth: I think I compute something like an expected value but then I bend the probabilities — the two ends of the distribution (best outcome, worst outcome) get weighted more than they should, and the boring middle gets squashed. And when the whole thing is losses I think I bend it the other way, which is maybe why #5 looks different.

So: is there a single precise rule that gets all of 1–6? State it properly. Tell me what it predicts for 7. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to throw out, because I'd rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that's secretly just "whatever I said." If my hunch is close but wrong, fix it and tell me where it broke.
