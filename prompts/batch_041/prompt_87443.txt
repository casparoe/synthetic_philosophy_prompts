Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend of mine — a sharp person with an undergraduate philosophy minor and a habit of settling arguments by naming a famous one — about whether facts can ever tell us what we ought to do. I left convinced that he was wrong somewhere and unable to say where. His position, as he laid it out, was this. Hume proved that no set of purely factual premises can entail a moral conclusion, which is why the is-ought gap is the first thing anyone learns in ethics; Hume's own view, that reason is the slave of the passions, is the same point in psychological dress. Moore then explained why the gap is unbridgeable: moral terms cannot be defined in natural terms at all. Whatever natural property you nominate — being pleasant, being desired, promoting survival — it always remains an open question whether things with that property are good, and that shows that 'good' names a simple, non-natural property. This, he said, is the naturalistic fallacy, and it is the same fallacy people commit when they argue that something is wrong because it is unnatural, as when someone says we should not eat processed food because our ancestors did not, or that a practice is suspect because it does not occur in nature. From all this he drew three conclusions, each of which he treated as settled: that science can have no bearing whatever on morality; that moral realism is a dead option; and that anyone who argues we ought to act on climate change because of the empirical facts about what will happen is committing precisely the fallacy he had identified. As a parting shot he added that Moore's open question argument is the most overrated argument in philosophy, since one can ask the same open question about water and H2O.

I suspect the package is wrong in ways that matter, but I cannot separate the strands. Please audit it. Which of these claims would a philosopher working on metaethics accept as stated, which only with a qualification, and which are misrepresentations? Are Hume's point and Moore's point actually the same point, or has my friend run two or three different claims together? What would Moore himself have said about the conclusion that moral realism is dead? What does the everyday charge of appealing to nature have to do with the technical fallacy Moore named, if anything? And does the verdict on the climate argument follow from anything true in the summary, or does it depend on the parts that are wrong? Where his complaint about the open question argument is a real criticism, say so; where the water and H2O analogy breaks down, say why. Finally, tell me what I should have said back to him.
