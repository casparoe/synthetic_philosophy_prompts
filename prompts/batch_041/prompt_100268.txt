I'm in a reading group that's been slowly working through stuff on the ethics of war, and last week two members, Dana and Marcus, went at each other for basically the whole session over the claim that soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on a par -- that once you're actually in the fighting, it isn't morally worse to be the one serving the aggressor. I mostly stayed out of it because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, but now I want to understand it properly. Roughly how it went:

Dana opened: blame has to track what you could reasonably have known. A conscript who honestly believes his country was attacked, and fights to defend it, isn't blameworthy for fighting even if his leaders lied and the war is unjust -- and the guy on the other side is in exactly the same spot, so neither is worse than the other. Marcus: that's just an excuse, and an excuse isn't equality. If someone drugs me and uses my body to shove a person off a cliff, I'm not to blame, but I'm also not on a par with the man who deliberately pushed -- my act still wasn't permitted, it just isn't punished. Dana: follow that through and you can't run a war at all, because the whole law of armed conflict treats the two sides symmetrically -- enemy soldiers get prisoner-of-war status, and neither side's troops are prosecuted merely for killing the other side's troops. Marcus: that's confusing how we design institutions with moral truth; we give both sides' prisoners the same meals and hearings because it's the only workable rule, not because kidnapping and a lawful arrest are morally the same thing. Then Dana tried a reductio: if unjust soldiers are basically murderers, then the civilians who voted for the war and pay for it are complicit, so on that view they'd be fair game too -- which would wreck the rule against targeting civilians, which is the one part of the law of war everyone agrees on. Marcus said complicity comes in degrees and the threshold for being liable to attack is high, so you can call the soldier a wrongdoer without licensing attacks on his parents.

Then Dana changed tack: both soldiers are doing the same thing, staking their lives on the same lethal bargain, each only able to kill because the other has taken up the same risk -- there's something fair-play-ish about it, like two people agreeing to box. Marcus said a mugger also risks getting shot and that doesn't make mugging fair. And then Dana threw in something I honestly didn't follow: imagine the very same person, conscripted into the unjust side, across two versions of the same war -- in one he's been lied to and believes the cause is just, in the other he knows it's unjust but fights anyway out of terror. Dana said if the moral equality of combatants depends on what he knows, then it was never equality of combatants, it was equality of the ignorant. Marcus said yes, that's exactly the point, and Dana had just shown the whole thing collapses into "we excuse people who couldn't have known better."

Can you referee this move by move -- Dana's opening reasonable-belief point, Marcus's excuse/equality reply, the law-of-war symmetry argument, the institutions-versus-truth reply, the complicity reductio and Marcus's answer to it, the boxing/fair-play point, the mugger reply, and finally the two-versions thought experiment? For each one say whether it's a good point, a bad point, or a decent point that overreaches, and briefly why. Then tell me what both of them seem to be missing -- I keep feeling they're talking past each other somewhere but I can't put my finger on where, and I'd rather hear what the actual crux is than just who "won."
