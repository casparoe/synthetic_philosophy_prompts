A colleague of mine has proposed an analogy for the long dispute about objectivity in history, and I would like it audited rather than endorsed.

His proposal is this. The quarrel between Ranke's aspiration to show the past as it actually was and the scepticism of Carr and White — that the historian's selection, interpretation and narrative form are constitutive of the account rather than regrettable excrescences on it — has been conducted with the wrong picture. The historian is neither a mirror nor a painter but a cartographer. The past is the territory; a historical account is a map. Maps are plainly capable of being objectively right or wrong — a map either matches the coastline or it does not — and yet every map projects, selects, generalises and omits. Once the historian is seen this way, he says, Ranke's ambition is not naive but mis-stated: the historian cannot reproduce the past in full, but can be objectively faithful to its structure, as a map is faithful to the relations among places while remaining silent about the colour of the soil. As a specimen he offers two histories that agree on every date, agent and sequence of events yet disagree about whether what occurred was a revolution or a coup; they agree, he says, on the structure and differ only in the labels, so the structure, at least, is objective — and Carr's and White's scepticism trades on an impossible standard, a map that is the territory.

I want an audit of this, not a verdict on the historians. Please do the following, in order.

1. Give the correspondence table. Say what plays the role of the territory, the map, the projection, the scale, the legend or shared convention, the surveyor, the instruments, the map's user, and the purpose for which the map is drawn; and mark the places where the target supplies no counterpart, so that one has to be stipulated.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for his conclusion from those that do not. I am especially interested in whether the past can be a territory in the required sense: whether there is a description-independent coastline to match; whether rival accounts can be laid side by side and combined as maps of one territory can; whether the inhabitants of the past are themselves mapmakers who act on their own representations; and whether a structure can be specified without the very labels he wants to quarantine.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports — which features of cartography (a single determinate territory, settled conventions of projection, convergence on the coastline, the mapmaker's externality to what is mapped) are carried over without argument into the target.

4. Then answer the question the analogy was introduced to settle: does the conclusion — that objectivity survives as fidelity of structure — follow once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along? If it does not follow, say whether a better analogy exists and what it would and would not support.

Tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of. End with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by a short phrase stating whether the cartographic analogy can bear the conclusion he draws from it.
