Bit of a wierd one, but here goes. I run a small film-and-philosophy group and it's my turn to lead on Kurosawa's Rashomon. What I'm after is a reading of the film through the epistemology of testimony — the scrap between reductionists and anti-reductionists. Reductionists say you're only entitled to believe what someone tells you if you've got some non-testimonial backup: you know the speaker's track record, the story hangs together with what you can check for yourself, and so on. Anti-reductionists say testimony is a basic source of knowledge like perception — you're entitled to take it at face value unless something specific gives you a reason not to.

Rashomon looks almost rigged for this fight. Four accounts of the same killing in the grove, and they can't all be true, and we never get the event itself — no impartial witness, just testimony piled on testimony. So the reductionist seems to be asking for a floor of non-testimonial evidence that the film flatly refuses to hand him. Does that make the film an argument for anti-reductionism? Or does the woodcutter — who turns out to have seen more than he first let on — cut the other way, and show that the sensible thing is to monitor every teller, since the most reluctant witness is the one sitting right there at the gate?

What I want from you: take the film seriously as film, not as a fable with a moral stapled on. Point me to specific moments and say what the staging and the framing are doing to the epistemology — the light through the trees, the way the camera stands in for a witness we never get. And bring in one idea that isn't obviously already in the film: the thought that testimony might be irreducibly social, that you can't cash out the justification of one bit of testimony except by leaning on other bits, so the reductionist's "floor" is a fiction. Test that against the film rather than just asserting it. If you want to use the notion of testimonial injustice — credibility granted or withheld because of who's speaking, the wife, the bandit — go ahead, but tell me honestly whether it's earning its keep here or whether I'm forcing it.

The part I care most about is where the film resists your reading; I don't want a tidy alegory. Finish by telling me which scene or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is weakest.

British English please, and no reading lists — I can't look anything up.
