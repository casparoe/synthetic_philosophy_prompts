My friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether the way governments put a price on safety is philosophically coherent, and I would like your help working out what the practice actually commits us to.

Here is the practice, as best I can describe it. When a regulator weighs a new rule — say, a tighter limit on an air pollutant or a required safety feature in cars — it typically estimates the number of deaths and illnesses the rule would prevent, converts those health gains into dollars using a single value of a statistical life derived from surveys and wage studies of how much people will pay, or demand to be paid, for small changes in risk, discounts benefits that arrive in the future, and compares the total dollar benefit against the compliance cost. The same single figure is applied to everyone, rich or poor, young or old. Yet elsewhere in the same legal system we behave as if lives cannot be priced at all: wrongful-death awards vary wildly, are often defended as being incommensurable with money, and public rhetoric insists that no price can be put on a human life. And the willingness-to-pay studies underneath the uniform figure show that people with more income pay more to reduce the same risk — precisely the variation that the uniform number suppresses.

What does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments: about whether one person's welfare can be compared with another's at all; about what the common measure is; about whether a dollar is worth the same to everyone or whether the practice quietly corrects for the diminishing marginal utility of income; and about whether the value of a life is meant to be independent of whose life it is. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify which rival views of interpersonal comparison it rules out and which it leaves open. Then tell me whether its parts presuppose consistent things — in particular, whether the uniform value of a statistical life is compatible with the income-sensitive willingness-to-pay numbers it is built from, or whether one of them has to give. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a frankly egalitarian one, or with one that refused interpersonal comparison altogether, say what would have to change in how the analysis is actually carried out.

Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
