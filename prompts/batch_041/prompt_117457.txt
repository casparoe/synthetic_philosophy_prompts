Okay, I've got a legal-philosophy knot I keep chewing on and I want a careful take on it. Put on two hats at once: you're a philosopher of logic who works on knowledge and reasoning, and you also know your way around criminal law. Think it through before you write anything -- sketch the options out for yourself first -- but what you hand me at the end should be just the list I ask for below.

Here's the thing. The criminal law runs on the old maxim that not knowing the law is no excuse. You're presumed to know what the statutes say. Fine. But nobody knows all the statutes, and nobody knows all the *consequences* of the statutes -- which charges follow from which facts, which defenses cancel which elements, what happens when two rules interact. And then the very same system turns around and says a mistake of fact is a defense, and in tax cases the government has to prove you actually knew the law applied to you before it can convict you of evasion, which is a strange thing to insist on if we're all already presumed to know the law. So the law sometimes talks like we're all walking statute books and sometimes acts like it knows we aren't.

Some background you may not have at your fingertips, so I'll just hand it to you. Logicians who study knowledge have a name for what the maxim seems to assume. An idealized knower who knows a set of facts also knows everything those facts logically imply, knows every tautology, and can never hold two beliefs that contradict each other. That's logical omniscience, and it's usually treated as a defect in a theory of knowledge rather than a feature of real agents. Two standard patches: one is to split knowing from *awareness*, so the agent has an awareness set of propositions it's actually entertained, separate from what it knows, which lets it fail to draw an obvious consequence. The other is to let the agent entertain logically impossible worlds, so that p implying q can fail to be transparent to it. Use either or both. There's also an older strand of thought -- the philosophy of as-if, Hans Vaihinger's thing from around 1911 -- that says we can knowingly use false assumptions as instruments, and the question is whether the law is doing that here or actually believes its own story.

What I want: what theory of knowledge does the presumption actually presuppose -- full logical omniscience, or something weaker like everyone being *aware* that the statute exists, or everyone being able to find out, and which of those does the practice really need? Does the rest of the system presuppose the same thing, or do the mistake-of-fact rule and the tax willfulness rule presuppose something inconsistent with it -- and if so, is there one theory that reconciles all three or are these just three different fictions stacked on top of each other? Be honest about what's strictly presupposed versus what merely fits well: could an awareness-set theory or an impossible-worlds theory license the same practice without the omniscience? If you take the gap between knowing and being aware seriously, does the maxim survive at all, or does it collapse into something else -- and what exactly would have to change (jury instructions, defenses, sentencing) if we swapped the presupposed view out for a bounded one? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where does the law stay silent and several theories would do just fine?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion. American English.
