My two brothers and I are dividing our late mother's estate, and my older brother proposes what he calls the only fair procedure: each of us privately puts a number on how much better or worse off each possible division would leave him, expressed as what he would pay to avoid it, and we adopt the division with the highest total. When I object that this rewards whoever complains loudest, he says I have missed the point — the procedure favors no one in advance, since each of us might equally well have been born as any of the three, and a person choosing under those odds would maximize the total. Complicating matters, he has always had money and insists that a small share would ruin him, while our younger brother, who has lived on very little for a decade, says he would be content with almost nothing. I would like the argument assessed rather than the family politics: does the equal-odds move really establish this as the fair rule, and should the resulting arithmetic carry any weight with me? Give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
