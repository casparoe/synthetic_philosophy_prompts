I am a high school senior writing a long paper on why some nations are wealthy and others are poor. I have read enough to know that the debate divides into institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations, and that each camp tends to argue by pointing at the same historical episodes and reading them its own way. Before I commit to any of them, I want to know what an adequate answer would actually have to deliver. Please write the desiderata: the full list of requirements that any theory of comparative national wealth must meet to be taken seriously.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I expect the list to cover the episodes a theory has to fit, including the period when some regions pulled sharply ahead of others and the episodes that some historians describe as reversals, in which a region that had been relatively prosperous later became relatively poor. It should also cover the distinctions a theory cannot collapse — for instance, between explaining why a country is rich now and explaining why it grew; between a proximate cause and a fundamental one; and between a country's total output and the living standards of the people who live there. And it should cover what a theory must be consistent with, such as the fact that one people divided into two states can end up with two very different economies.

I want one requirement to be about evidence rather than about the world. Whatever cause the theory names has to be identifiable from something other than the wealth it is meant to explain, and the theory has to be able to say which historical findings would count against it. Be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp.

Then tell me whether any subset of these requirements cannot be satisfied at the same time. In particular, I want to know whether the causal claim the debate keeps reaching for — that this factor made that country rich — is the kind of claim the historical record can support at all, or whether the honest answer is that we can only ever rule some stories out. That possibility is what I most want you to address.

Finally, tell me which single requirement rules out the largest number of the popular answers, and why.

Here is a case I invented for the list to handle: an island whose two halves share the same language, religion, climate, and soil, where a colonial power drew a border and imposed different property rules on each side, and where forty years later one half is several times richer than the other. Does that settle the question? Say which requirement determines whether it does.
