I work on probabilistic forecasting and out-of-distribution detection, so my working vocabulary is priors and posteriors, likelihood ratios, model classes and misspecification, identifiability, no-free-lunch results, calibration, and distribution shift. I have no training in epistemology. My entire acquaintance with G. E. Moore's proof of an external world is the slogan version: he holds up one hand and then the other, observes that here is a hand and here is another, concludes that an external world exists, and insists that this counts as a proof in the strict sense even though he cannot prove his premises. To me that looks like a triviality dressed up as an argument.

I want two things.

First, a restatement of the proof and of the dispute it provoked in my vocabulary, as faithfully as the translation allows, and not pitched as an explanation for a general audience. Treat the anti-skeptical premise as a claim about evidence and hypotheses: say what the priors, likelihoods, model space, and identification assumptions would have to be for the argument to go through, and then say where the skeptic's denial lands in those terms.

Second, the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical dispute carry over cleanly, which carry over only with distortion, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all? Four candidates interest me. Does the skeptical scenario belong inside the model space as an alternative hypothesis, or is it better understood as a rival account of what the evidence is, and is that distinction even available to me? Is there any counterpart to the claim that Moore's premise cannot be known independently of the conclusion, given that probability is indifferent to the order of epistemic dependence? Does knowledge have any counterpart at all, given that a posterior near one neither entails truth nor separates reliability from factivity? And is the demand to justify one's priors the skeptic's demand, or a weaker one?

A colleague proposes that Moore's proof is just the assertion that the skeptical hypothesis carries zero prior mass, and that this is unobjectionable because every non-trivial inference needs some such commitment. Assess that rather than adopt it: is it a translation of what Moore is doing, a replacement for it, or an argument that he was doing nothing else?

Close with a verdict on the residue: is it a limitation of my vocabulary that I should not mistake for a limitation of the problem, or evidence that the philosophers' claims are less determinate than their idiom suggests? I do not need references or reading suggestions; I want the translation and the diagnosis of what it fails to carry.
