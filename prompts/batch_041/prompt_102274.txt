I'm an undergraduate double-majoring in history and statistics, and this term I'm taking a seminar on historical explanation alongside a course on causal inference, so I keep trying to run the two through each other. The seminar has spent three weeks on cases of overdetermination: the collapse of a regime whose fiscal crisis, elite defection, and military defeat are each cited by a different historian as the thing that did it; a revolution that, on most accounts, would have happened even if its most famous triggering incident had never occurred; a famine in which policy, drought, and war each look sufficient on their own. The philosophers we read treat this as a puzzle about causation — whether a cause has to make a difference, whether two causes can each be sufficient and yet neither be redundant, whether we should analyse causes as insufficient but necessary parts of unnecessary but sufficient conditions, and whether counterfactual dependence is the right test at all. Meanwhile the statistics course hands me a vocabulary that feels as though it ought to say all of this precisely: potential outcomes, treatment variables and the interventions that set them, directed acyclic graphs and the paths they block, identification under confounding, effect modification, and the sufficient-component cause models epidemiologists draw as pie charts, where each pie is a minimal sufficient cause and each slice a component cause that is itself insufficient and non-redundant.

What I'd like from you is a translation of the historians' and philosophers' debate about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation into that second vocabulary, done as faithfully as you can and taken one position at a time — the counterfactual-dependence account, the regularity or covering-law account, the insufficient-but-necessary-part account, and the historians' own looser talk of multiple causes and of causes operating on different time scales — followed by a residue report. Which claims map cleanly onto something I already have words for? Which translate only if I distort them, and what exactly gets distorted in the process? Which have no counterpart at all — and is that because causal inference is missing something historians legitimately care about (singular rather than population-level causation, the meaning the actors themselves attached to what they did, the difference between explaining why something happened and explaining why it happened rather than something else), or because the historians' formulation was less clear than it looked and the translation simply exposes that? I'm reasonably fluent on the statistics side — I can read a DAG and I know what an average treatment effect is — so please don't simplify there; but I'm only a semester into the philosophy, so flag any point where I'm likely to be running two distinct positions together. Treat the residue as the interesting part: I want to know whether the pie-chart picture of sufficient causes genuinely dissolves the puzzle of overdetermination or merely relocates it.
