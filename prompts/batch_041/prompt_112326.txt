Right, I'm a journalist on a science-and-ideas desk and I've been going round in circles on this one, so I want you to write the essay I can't seem to finish myself.

The question: when we say a large language model *believes* something — that Canberra is the capital of Australia, say, or that it's Tuesday — are we doing anything more than using a handy shorthand? Dennett's intentional stance treats belief-talk as a predictive strategy you adopt when it pays off, and fair enough. But two things make me think these systems are a genuinely awkward case, and I want them both handled.

First, the instability. The same model will cheerfully "believe" p under one framing and not-p under another, and it isn't remotely bothered by the clash — whereas for us, that's usually the mark of someone who hasn't got a belief at all, just a habit of saying things. Second, and this is the bit I keep tripping over: there's a view going round that these models aren't agents with beliefs so much as simulators of a whole space of possible characters — some of whom believe p, some of whom don't — which would make "what does the model believe?" a bit like asking what a novel believes. Is that a real alternative to the intentional stance, or just the stance with extra steps?

Weigh the main positions but don't just survey them — land somewhere and defend it. And tell me what you'd say to the reader who insists the whole question is a category error. British English please, and go easy on the jargon; my readers are smart but they're not philosphers.
