I have been going back and forth with two friends about American zoning — one of them a Burkean who reaches for Chesterton's fence in every argument, the other a YIMBY who thinks that heuristic is mostly rent-seeking wearing a philosophical costume — and the thing that keeps tripping me up, as someone who reads a lot of the rationalist-adjacent writing on institutions, is that the actual legal treatment of pre-existing buildings matches neither of their stories. Here is the pattern, as best I can reconstruct it. When a town adopts or tightens a zoning or building code, uses and structures that were already there are "grandfathered": they may continue indefinitely even though they now violate the code, and a buyer typically inherits that privilege along with the deed. But the reprieve is conditional in two different ways. First, many ordinances contain a discontinuance rule — if the nonconforming use stops for some specified period, the right is lost and the property must thereafter conform, so the protection attaches to the ongoing activity rather than to the owner or the structure. Second, many ordinances go further and amortize the nonconforming use outright: instead of waiting for abandonment, they give it a fixed term of years and require it to be wound down and the property brought into compliance when the term expires, with no compensation beyond the grace period; property-rights advocates call this expropriation, and a few states agree with them. Some jurisdictions also cut the reprieve short when a structure is destroyed past a threshold, so that fire rather than deliberation decides whether the old arrangement survives. Meanwhile an empty lot gets no such indulgence: build there and you meet the current code in full, on day one. So one legal order appears to hold that settled arrangements deserve to survive, that they forfeit that claim the moment they lapse, and that they may be terminated on a schedule regardless — three different stories about why the past gets deference, and I cannot tell whether they are consistent with one another.

What I would like is an account of what this package presupposes. Is there a single implicit view of the authority of existing arrangements — something recognizably Burkean, on which settled practice encodes knowledge we could not reconstruct from scratch — under which grandfathering, forfeiture on discontinuance, and scheduled amortization all look like natural expressions of one idea? Or is the package instead a stalemate among distinct commitments: an epistemic claim about dispersed tacit knowledge, a reliance-interest claim that people who invested under the old rules are owed a soft landing, and a plain political-economy claim that present owners are organized while future owners are not? Be precise about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely accommodates, because my suspicion is that amortization sits badly with the epistemic reading — a term of years does not restore whatever tacit knowledge the ordinance destroys, so someone who really took the Chesterton's fence heuristic to heart should be a holdout against phase-outs rather than a fan of them; tell me if that inference is wrong. Then say which rival theories the practice quietly rules out, and whether a welfarist, a Nozickian libertarian, and a legal positivist who thinks the only question is what the current rule says could each reconstruct it without strain. Finally, tell me what would have to change institutionally if we swapped the implicit theory for a purely epistemic one, or for a purely welfarist one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question.
