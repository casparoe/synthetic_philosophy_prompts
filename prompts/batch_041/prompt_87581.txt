The setting is a 90-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice and its critics — 18 students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, several of whom have already read a textbook description of the dollar auction. I want to run an activity that gets past the standard demonstration, and I need you to design it.

The standard version — auction off a dollar bill, highest bidder pays and takes it, second-highest pays and gets nothing — reliably produces escalation, and the standard debrief says: sunk-cost fallacy, escalation of commitment, don't throw good money after bad. My students can recite that before the auction starts. What the recitation obscures is the thing I want them to feel: that in this game the escalation is not obviously a fallacy. Once the bidding is at a dollar and change, the next bid is locally the better move, and stopping is what would need a defense. The trap is in the structure of the game, not in anyone's psychology. The sunk cost is not doing the work; the structure is.

Design the activity with these requirements.

First, the escalation has to happen before anyone names it. No framing, no vocabulary, no hint of what the game is about up front.

Second, the design must let participants feel the difference between two sources of escalation: escalation driven by sunk cost, where money already gone is treated as a reason to continue even though by itself it should be irrelevant, and escalation driven by structure, where each further bid is the best response to the last one, so that someone reasoning correctly at every node still ends up overpaying. Arrange the rules and the debrief so the group can tell which one is operating, and make sure the run produces a moment in which they see that the option to stop existed only at the beginning, and that by the time they wanted it, it was gone.

Third, include one variant run that isolates a single factor, and state what it isolates and what outcome would count as evidence for it. Choose among: (a) bidding against a mechanical outbidder rather than a person, which tests whether the escalation is about recovering money or about not letting the other player win; (b) a round in which the first bid is made on a participant's behalf by someone else, an inherited commitment, which tests whether escalation requires having chosen to enter; (c) a round with a ceiling written down in advance and a visible penalty for exceeding it, which tests whether pre-commitment is available at all once the auction is live. Say which you would pick for this room and why, and what to cut if you want a second variant.

Fourth, no real money. Payoffs in chips or points against a small non-monetary prize, with a settlement rule credible enough that the loss is actually felt.

Fifth, the debrief has to do real work rather than name the phenomenon. Map specific moments in the run onto specific parts of the analysis; distinguish the structural trap from the sunk-cost story and say which one the room's behavior actually supports; and confront whether any bid sequence in this game is rational at every node. My own belief is that the game has no equilibrium in pure strategies, so there is no single rational line of play to point to and the "fallacy" label is doing rhetorical work — if that is wrong, say so and explain the correct analysis, and do not let the debrief rest on it. Participants will almost certainly explain their own conduct in psychological terms ("I got carried away," "I couldn't let him have it"), so build in a way of testing that self-report against the structural explanation instead of accepting it.

Sixth, end with a transfer step. Write a short realistic escalation case — a project, a lawsuit, a war, a failing product line, one you invent so that nothing has to be looked up — and give a small ordered set of questions that let students determine whether the escalation there is structural, sunk-cost, or commitment-driven. That discrimination is the practically important skill, and it is where the activity should leave them.

Also give me the materials and setup, the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the exact payoff table, and the facilitator's script for the moments when the room has to be kept from dissolving into "this is rigged." Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including a table that refuses to escalate because someone has read about the auction, and a group that never feels the loss so the whole thing becomes a joke — with what to do in each case.

Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. My guess is that real escalation unfolds over months, that the decision-maker is a coalition rather than a person, that the prior commitments are public and identity-bearing, and that the prize is partly constituted by the escalation itself rather than fixed in advance. Tell me which of those the activity can gesture at, which it cannot, and what I therefore have to supply myself in the debrief.
