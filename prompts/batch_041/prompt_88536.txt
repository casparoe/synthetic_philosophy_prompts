I am a professor of philosophy, and I am assembling material for a graduate seminar on collective action and the division of responsibility; the following puzzle has been nagging at me since a lunch conversation with a colleague in economics, and I would like your help in settling whether it is a recognized phenomenon or merely one of its better-known cousins in disguise. Here is the pattern as I encounter it. A group of people share an end, and each of them is separately able to take a step toward it, with no one empowered to decide for the group and no coordination among them. Taking the step costs the individual something modest; declining to take it costs nothing. What happens next is not a random sample of the group's judgment. Those who act are, with striking regularity, the members least equipped to judge whether acting is wise — the least informed, the most inflamed, the readiest to be seen doing something — while the best-placed members hold back, each reasoning that if the step were genuinely worth taking, someone better positioned would already have taken it, and that her own contribution is unlikely to be the decisive one. The upshot is a systematic distortion rather than a simple excess or deficit of action: the outcome is settled by a self-selected minority whose very self-selection is evidence that its judgment is poor.

I have the uneasy sense that I am circling several things I already know — the thought that when many people can each act alone and the harm of a bad action is spread thin across all of them, bad actions get taken too readily; the volunteer's dilemma and the bystander effect; the problem of many hands; ordinary adverse selection; and, at some distance, Gresham's law — yet none of these seems to be the thing itself, and I cannot rule out that I am merely redescribing one of them in new clothes. What I want from you is not a survey of the neighbourhood but a verdict and a carving. Does the pattern have an established name and a body of literature? If it does, say which, and be explicit about what the established concept captures and where my description departs from it. If it does not, coin a term and define it so that the definition earns its keep: whether the correlation between who acts and who judges well is essential to the phenomenon or whether a case in which the actors are well chosen but simply too numerous still falls under it; whether it survives full mutual transparency among the agents or depends on their ignorance of one another's reasoning; and how, precisely, it is to be separated from a bare combination of the volunteer's dilemma and a selection effect. Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
