At a policy reading group, a colleague argues for a serious carbon tax by analogy with tobacco: the cigarette tax is the success story — consumption fell, the policy survived its opponents, and the resistance proved transitional — so a carbon tax can be expected to do the same, and today's backlash is a phase rather than a verdict. I am not asking for a general case for or against carbon pricing; I want an audit of the analogy she is using to carry that conclusion. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the cigarette, the smoker, the tax, the substitutes, the harm, the industry, and the political fight — then say where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that are incidental from those that bear on the conclusion. Identify the features of the tobacco case that the analogy quietly imports into the carbon case (about the nature of the taxed good, about the structure of the harm, about what counts as the policy succeeding, and about the political economy of the taxed population), and say which of them the carbon case lacks. Close by saying how much of her conclusion survives, and whether a different comparison would carry more of it.
