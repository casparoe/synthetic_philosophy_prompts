I've been part of a small online argument group — we all found each other through the same blogs, and we meet every few weeks to take one idea apart — and for the past couple of months we've been circling the moral trade thesis: the claim that two people whose values diverge can each end up better off by their own lights if they swap concessions in domains they care about less, the way countries gain from trade. The trouble is that we have all converged. We can now recite the standard objections and the standard replies, and I have a suspicion that our convergence is itself a symptom of our having stopped noticing something. So I'd like you to write the dialogue that would unsettle us, rather than the one we already know how to have.

The two speakers are old friends in their late thirties who have actually done this. One is a fairly thoroughgoing consequentialist who gives away a serious fraction of his income and regards most personal consumption as morally frivolous; the other is a particularist who thinks the substance of ethics lies in the obligations you have to your own people, place and craft, and that impartial maximising misses the point of morality entirely. Last year they struck a bargain: he diverted a slice of his giving budget to her village-hall restoration, and she spent two weekends a month canvassing for the charity he supports, and each of them believes he or she came out ahead. They are now arguing about whether that belief is correct — whether the bargain was a genuine mutual gain or a mutual corruption that the arithmetic of the ledger conceals. The consequentialist should have the better of the exchange at least as often as the particularist; neither may be a caricature, and neither may win by being wittier. They should answer each other's actual moves rather than restate their own openings, and they should not cite literature at each other — the point is for them to argue it out.

I'm especially interested in one strand, because it is the one we in the group cannot get past. He wants to say that the bargain needs no common currency: each party ranks his own options, the exchange is an improvement on each party's own ranking, and nothing further is required. She wants to say that the thing that gets traded is not the thing that was valued — that once you have named a price for a commitment you have made it fungible, and that the second trade comes more cheaply than the first, so that what looks like a Pareto improvement at the moment of exchange is a step along a path neither of them would have chosen at the outset. She calls this the fungibility ratchet; he should press her on whether it is a real mechanism or merely a vivid label, and she should press him on whether his accounting can even represent the loss she is alleging. I'd like the dialogue to end with the two of them stating precisely where their disagreement now sits — whether it has turned out to be empirical, conceptual, or about what counts as a value in the first place — and with at least one of them conceding something real. Please write it in British English.
