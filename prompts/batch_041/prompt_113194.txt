I'm a third-year phil student and I'm in a course on Indian philosophy that's mostly been Buddhist material, but the last three weeks we did the Jain stuff about reality having many sides and every statement being true only from a standpoint. Annoyingly, it's the only part of the course that's landed on something real in my life, and I can't get it to do any work.

Here's the situation. My grandmother started a bakery decades ago. About twenty years back her son, my uncle Ravi, took it over, moved it across town, changed the name, changed most of the recipes, and kept the old oven only because grandma threw a fit about it. Ravi's daughter Priya runs it now. Grandma is in a home with pretty advanced dementia. My aunt Nisha thinks the family should keep the place forever and says selling it would be a betrayal of grandma. Priya wants to sell to a chain and says it isn't grandma's bakery anymore and hasn't been for years, it's just a business she owns. At dinner I said you're both kind of right, it depends what you mean by the same bakery, and they both turned on me — Nisha said I was letting Priya off the hook, Priya said if I think both of us are right then I must think nothing is ever true, which is obviously garbage.

What I want is to know whether my move was principled or just cowardice. I half-remember from lecture that the tradition doesn't say all views are equal — theres a difference between a standpoint that's a genuine partial grip on a real thing and one that's gone wrong by insisting it's the only one; and there was another bit where the professor drew a four-box grid about the different ways you can mean a thing when you use its name, like pointing at a picture of it, or meaning the stuff it's made of and what it's been, or meaning what it is right now, and he said a lot of disputes are just people using one word in different boxes without noticing. He also had a list of seven standpoints, one of which was roughly just the present moment with the history chopped off, and another about words and their etymologies. I can't look anything up and I don't have the textbook.

So: is there a way to say both of you are right that isn't relativism and isn't me dodging? Specifically (a) what actually distinguishes one of those standpoints as a real partial truth from a mistake, (b) does the four-box thing about meaning genuinely resolve a same-bakery dispute or just rename it, and (c) what do I say to Priya's "then nothing is true" argument — is there a reply, or is the position self-undermining? And practically, what do I say at the next dinner. Tell me if I'm wrong that this is just relativism with extra steps.
