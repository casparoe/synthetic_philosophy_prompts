I am working through Book I of Aristotle's Politics and have gotten stuck on the closing argument about usury in the chapter on wealth-getting. The fairly literal translation I am using renders the key lines this way:

"usury is most reasonably hated, because its gain comes from money itself and not from that for the sake of which money was invented. For money was brought into existence for the purpose of exchange, but interest increases the amount of the money itself (and this is the actual origin of the Greek word: offspring resembles parent, and interest is money born of money); consequently this form of the business of getting wealth is of all forms the most contrary to nature."

A little earlier in the same book, the text floats the opposing thought that "money is nonsense, and nothing by nature but entirely a convention, because when those who use it have changed the currency it is worth nothing."

What I cannot see is how these two claims are supposed to sit together, and I would like help reconstructing the argument rather than a summary of the usual reading. My specific difficulties are these. First, in what sense can usury be "contrary to nature" if money is itself "nothing by nature" and exists only by convention? Does "contrary to nature" here turn on a notion of nature as function or end — money's nature being to serve as an instrument of exchange, which usury defeats — or does the unnaturalness attach not to money's essence but to a broader teleology of the household and the city, so that what is really being condemned is the unlimited, self-augmenting form of acquisition for which usury is only the limiting case? Is there a third reading I am missing? Second, does the argument depend on the idea that the usurer trades not in goods but in the instrument of exchange itself, a transaction at one remove from any real exchange of needs — and is that the same point as the complaint that the gain "comes from money itself," or a distinct one? Third, what argumentative work, if any, is the etymology doing? Is "offspring resembles parent" a mere remark about the Greek word for interest, or is the idea that money "breeds" load-bearing — and if it is, is the claim that interest literally produces something of the same kind as its source, and is that supposed to be absurd, impious, or simply unnatural? Fourth, how should I take the normative force of the whole passage: is "contrary to nature" a descriptive claim about a failure of function from which a prohibition is then inferred, or is the appeal to nature already doing independent normative work?

Please lay the candidate readings out as distinct options, indicating for each what in the passage or its immediate surroundings supports it and what would decide between them, and add an honest note on which steps a modern reader would be right to reject and why. I would rather have the options reconstructed from the text itself than a survey of what the commentators say, since I am trying to form my own view before I consult them.
