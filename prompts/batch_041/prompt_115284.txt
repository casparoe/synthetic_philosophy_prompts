For an essay I'm writing on what it means to say that a person could have been otherwise, I have been keeping a ledger of my own verdicts rather than anyone else's, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please state that principle — or the small set of competing principles — precisely, tie each clause to the case that forces it, say how it rules on cases I have not listed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here is the ledger.

I could have been a dentist: true. There is a way things could have gone, from my adolescence onward, in which I went to dental school, and the man in that story is me with a different job.

I could have been a poached egg: false — and false for the deeper reason that no egg, however I describe it, would be me.

I could have been born in 1850: false. A man born then, however closely he resembled me, would not be me. Whatever else shifts, I take it that these parents and this moment are not negotiable.

I could have been cheerful: true. A version of me whose temperament made him much easier to live with would still be me.

I could have failed to exist at all: true; my parents might never have met.

My parents might have had twins instead of me — a single embryo that split. Call them A and B. Each grows up with my genetic endowment, in my family, in my year; neither is any nearer to being me than the other. Here my verdicts are these: "I could have been A" is false. "I could have been B" is false. But "I could have been one of the twins" is true. This is the entry that has me stuck, since I cannot see what makes the disjunction true if both disjuncts are false.

The pocket watch my grandfather left me could have been cast from a different batch of brass at the outset: true. Here, unlike with my parents, a change in origins seems not to matter. I do not know whether to treat that as a fact about artifacts rather than persons, or as evidence that I am wrong about the 1850 case.

What I have been working with, tentatively, is this: an individual could have been F just in case some way the world could have been contains that very individual and it is F there. That handles the first five entries and the watch, but it cannot handle the twins, since no one can be two people in one world; it appears to force "I could have been one of the twins" to be false. The alternative I keep encountering — that such a claim is true just when some world contains a counterpart of mine who is F — buys me the twins verdict but seems to sell off the 1850 verdict, since I do not see why a sufficiently similar man born a century earlier would not count as a counterpart, and I want him excluded.

Is there a principle that gets all of this, or nearly all? If something has to go, which verdict should it be, and what does the surviving principle say about cases I have not put to it — for instance, whether I could have been an only child, or whether I could have been a person who duplicates me atom for atom but arrives in a different century? Please state the principle precisely, name the case that motivates each clause, give its verdicts on at least one case I have not raised, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
