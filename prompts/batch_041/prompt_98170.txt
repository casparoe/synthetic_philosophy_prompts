Animal welfare legislation and institutional ethics review protect vertebrates as a matter of course, and a few frameworks have been extended to cephalopods and to decapods, yet insects are left almost entirely outside their scope — even though insect rearing for feed and food and the use of insects in research now involve numbers that dwarf all the vertebrates we do protect, and even though the question of insect sentience is taken seriously but remains unresolved. Note the apparent inconsistency: a single protected animal counts as morally considerable on the strength of a merely probable claim to sentience, whereas a trillion farmed insects attract no comparable concern, and the scale of the numbers is as often cited as a reason for dismissal as for alarm. What conception of moral status does this pattern presuppose? Does it commit us to a threshold view, on which protection turns on whether the probability of sentience clears some line and sheer numbers cannot make up for a probability below it; to an expected-value view, on which the number of insects multiplied by even a small probability of sentience should outweigh all vertebrate welfare combined; or to a pragmatic view about what is administratively tractable, which may reflect no considered theory of value at all? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates comfortably, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and indicate what would have to change in welfare law and ethics review if the practice were rebuilt on an explicitly probability-weighted version of the scale argument. Please answer in British English.
