I have spent enough time in the rationalist blogosphere to have absorbed two habits at once: a deep suspicion of anyone who says the realism of assumptions does not matter, and a genuine admiration for the models that seem to get away with exactly that. Friedman's old methodological claim — that the truth of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to the quality of its predictions, so a model can be better precisely because its premises are false — strikes me alternately as profound and as a licence for motivated reasoning, and I have never managed to decide which. I want to make the disagreement tractable by scoring it, so consider the following idealizations, every one of them standard in applied modelling:

(a) treating a continuous quantity as discrete, or passing to a continuum or thermodynamic limit;
(b) assuming agents are perfectly rational and purely self-interested;
(c) assuming the relevant events or errors are statistically independent;
(d) assuming the system sits at equilibrium;
(e) assuming linearity, that is, dropping higher-order terms;
(f) assuming away friction, transaction costs, and other dissipative detail;
(g) replacing a heterogeneous population with a single representative agent;
(h) assuming stationarity — that the process which generated the data will keep generating it;
(i) deliberately omitting a factor you know to be operative, the spherical-cow move.

For each, assign a score from 0 to 100 on what I will call its exit cost: how much of the model's machinery and conclusions has to be rebuilt rather than merely re-tuned once the idealization is false for the case in hand — 0 meaning the assumption is presentational and the results survive untouched, 100 meaning the model is a different model the moment you drop it. Then sort the nine into four tiers, highest exit cost first, and give one sentence per item justifying its placement. Finally, tell me which single idealization is the strongest counterexample to Friedman's claim and which is the strongest evidence for it, and give me a single number in [0,1] for the fraction of the list that is load-bearing in the sense that its falsity would sink the model's headline conclusions rather than merely dent them.
