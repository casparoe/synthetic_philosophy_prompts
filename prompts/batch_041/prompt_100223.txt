I have come to accept a thesis I will call causal parity, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. The thesis is this: omissions — failures to act, absences, non-occurrences, "negative" events — are causes in exactly the same sense in which positive events are causes. Where an action would count as having caused an outcome by making a difference to whether and how the outcome occurred, an omission that makes the same difference counts in the same way. The stubborn intuition that omissions are not really causes, that they are at best causes in a secondary or honorific sense, is on this view not a metaphysical datum about the causal relation but a fact about us: about which difference-makers we select for mention, where that selection is governed by norms, expectations, salience, and attributions of responsibility rather than by the causal structure of the world.

I want an honest map of how exposed this view is. Please address the following, in order.

First, which results, cases, and arguments would genuinely count against parity, sorted by how decisive they would be? I am interested in more than the familiar appeal to intuitions about killing and letting die. Tell me which formal or structural results bear on the thesis — for instance, whether the interventionist machinery for defining causation can even admit an omission as a relatum, given that one cannot "wiggle" an absence the way one wiggles a switch, and what it would take for that to be a genuine defect rather than a technical inconvenience. I would also like you to consider the awkward cases directly: double prevention, where an omission disconnects a would-be cause; joint causation by two omissions, where neither alone makes the difference; and cases where an omission is itself the preempting rather than the preempted factor.

Second, which of those defeaters would a committed parity theorist themselves concede as counting against them, and which would they be entitled to dismiss as intuition-mongering or as a confusion between causal relevance and causal selection? Fill in the concession side, not only the critics' side.

Third, whether the view risks anything at all. In particular, does parity, once qualified enough to survive the obvious objections, collapse into the trivial claim that anything making a difference is a cause — and if so, is that a problem for a view about omissions specifically, or a problem for the difference-making account of causation more generally?

Fourth, what parity predicts that its rivals do not — the rival that omissions are not causes, and the rival that they are causes but of a distinct, deontic kind — and whether any of those predictions have been or could be tested.

I would be most moved by an argument showing that the asymmetry is not merely pragmatic: a case in which an omission makes the same counterfactual difference as an action, yet we have an independent, non-normative reason to treat it as causally different. If you can construct such a case, do so; if you think none exists, say so and say what that shows.
