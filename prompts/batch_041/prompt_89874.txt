so I'm in the philosophy club at my high school, we meet wednesdays after school for about 50 minutes, usually 13-15 of us show up, and next week I'm the one running the session. I picked the topic myself: Moloch, multipolar traps, races to the bottom.

Here's my problem with it. Every time someone explains this to me they say something like 'it's the prisoner's dilemma but with more players' and everyone nods and we move on and I honestly dont feel anything. I don't think you can feel it that way. So I want to run an actual game instead.

What I want out of it:

- Something we physically play in the room. Paper, tokens, whiteboard, whatever — no phones, no laptops, and no real money. Some kind of score (points on the board, or candy as chips, doesn't matter) so there's something at stake but nobody's losing anything real.
- I want us to end up in the bad outcome. And I want it to be genuinely not because anyone was dumb or mean. If at the end someone could point at a kid and say 'that's the guy who ruined it,' the game failed.
- I don't want it to be obvious from round one. I'd rather we play for a while, then notice near the end that we're all worse off than if we'd just done nothing from the start. So the scoring has to be set up so that the bad outcome creeps in rather than announcing itself.
- Once we notice, I want us to try to fix it — make a pact, agree to stop, whatever — and I want the fix to fail. But it should fail for a boring structural reason, not because one kid decides to be a villain. If someone breaks the pact it has to be for a reason that would make sense for literally anyone sitting in that seat. That's the part I care most about and it's the part I've never seen an activity actually do.
- I want the game to have a ratchet: whatever you spend in a round is gone for good, you can't un-spend it. I think that's what makes a trap a trap rather than just a coordination problem you solve by talking for five minutes, and I want to see it happen.
- Related: I want the payoff to depend on where you are relative to everyone else, not just on your own total. So falling one step behind is much worse than being tied, which should make everyone keep climbing even when climbing is stupid. Please make the numbers actually do this and show me the numbers.

What I need from you: the full rules, materials list, the payoff table, how many rounds, how we divide into whatever the units are (individuals? small teams? does it matter?), how the rounds are run time-wise, and a debrief that maps specific moments of what just happened onto the actual idea — including why 'it's just a prisoner's dilemma' is the wrong description, since that's the thing I'm trying to kill.

Also:
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with 14 teenagers and how I should handle it in the moment. I'd rather know now.
- Be honest about which part of the concept the game can't reproduce, so I know what I have to just say out loud at the end instead of pretending the game showed it.
- Nobody should hear the name Moloch or any of the labels until the debrief. It should show up in what happens, not in what I say at the start.
- It has to fit in 50 minutes including the debrief, and I'm also playing, so the rules need to work when the facilitator is one of the players.
