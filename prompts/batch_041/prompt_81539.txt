I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether lying is really any worse than merely misleading, and I'm now marking the first exam in the undergraduate course I teach on truth-telling and deception. Before I hand the scripts back, I'd like an independent read on one of them.

The exam is marked out of 10, on this scale: 10 = no substantive errors; 8–9 = strong, with one small gap; 6–7 = main claims right but a significant point mishandled; 5 or below = a central claim or inference fails.

The question was:

"In the course we distinguished three views of the lying/misleading distinction.

(1) The falsity view: a lie is an assertion the speaker believes to be false; misleading is knowingly producing a false belief by some other route. Lying carries an extra wrong because it breaks the norm against asserting what one believes to be false.

(2) The deception view: there is no intrinsic difference between the two; both are wrong, when they are, for the same reason — the aim of producing a false belief.

(3) The warrant-to-rely view: what matters is not truth value but whether the speaker has knowingly put themselves forward as a guarantor of the content the hearer is meant to rely on. A lie is a knowing breach of that guarantee. That can happen even when every sentence uttered is true, if the speaker knowingly invites reliance on what the sentences imply; conversely, a sincere false assertion is not a lie, because there is no knowing breach.

Consider two cases.

(A) A landlord, asked by a prospective tenant whether previous tenants ever complained about noise, says: 'The building has never had a formal complaint filed against it.' The sentence is true; he knows of several loud disputes that were settled informally. He intends the tenant to conclude that noise has never been a problem here.

(B) A bookkeeper tells her employer that the firm's insurance covers a recent flood. She believes this; the policy in fact excludes floods.

For each case, say whether the speaker lied, misled, or neither, on each of the three views, and then say which view handles the pair better, and why."

The script reads:

"On view (1), a lie is an assertion the speaker believes to be false. The landlord's sentence is true, so he has not lied on view (1); he has misled the tenant, since he knowingly brought about a false belief by letting the tenant draw the wrong inference. The bookkeeper, by contrast, asserts something false, so hers is a lie: she has broken the norm against asserting falsehoods. It is not misleading, though, because she is not trying to bring anyone to a false belief — she has simply got the policy wrong. So the two cases fall on opposite sides of the distinction.

View (3) says the distinction is not about truth value, but I think it collapses into view (1). The landlord did put himself forward as a guarantor of what he said, so on view (3) his reply is a lie; the bookkeeper has betrayed no one's reliance, so hers is not. Yet the cases are sorted exactly as they were before, so the guarantee talk is doing no independent work: what is really doing the sorting is still the truth value of what was asserted.

That is why I favour view (2). The landlord case shows that what we resent is the intention to produce a false belief, not the false assertion; the deception is doing all the work, and the bookkeeper case confirms it — no deception, nothing to resent. So there is no moral difference between lying and misleading as such. Lying is simply the more conspicuous way of deceiving: easier to catch, harder to explain away. The difference in how badly we react is therefore about detectability, not about anything the liar has done that the misleader has not."
