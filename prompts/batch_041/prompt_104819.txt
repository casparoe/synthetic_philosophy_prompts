Hey — hoping you can help me untangle a passage that's been bugging me for a while now. Put on your early-modern-philosophy hat for this one: I want the read of someone who actually knows Newton's theology and metaphysics, not just the physics.

The passage is from the General Scholium Newton tacked onto the Principia. Two chunks of it:

"God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God, not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants. The supreme God is a Being eternal, infinite, absolutely perfect; but a being, however perfect, without dominion, cannot be said to be Lord God [...] The word God usually signifies Lord; but every lord is not a God. It is the dominion of a spiritual being which constitutes a God."

Then a few sentences later:

"He is not Eternity or Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite; he is not Duration or Space, but he endures and is present. He endures for ever, and is every where present; and by existing always and every where, he constitutes Duration and Space. Since every particle of Space is always, and every indivisible moment of Duration is every where, certainly the Maker and Lord of all things cannot be never and no where. Every soul that has perception is, though in different times and in different organs of sense and motion, still the same indivisible person. There are given successive parts in duration, co-existant parts in space, but neither the one nor the other in the person of a man, or his thinking principle; and much less can they be found in the thinking substance of God."

What I can't get past:

1. What's the actual content of "he is not Duration or Space, but he endures and is present"? Is that just a grammatical scruple (don't name God after an attribute), or is he drawing a real metaphysical line — God not *being* space vs. God being *in* space somehow?

2. Two sentences later he says that by existing everywhere God "constitutes Duration and Space." That sounds like the exact opposite of what he just said. How do I square those? And what does "constitutes" mean there — makes them exist, fills them, is the ground of them?

3. The inference "since every particle of Space is always ... the Maker ... cannot be never and no where" feels like a non sequitur to me. Reconstruct the argument charitably and tell me whether it actually goes through.

4. Why drag in the soul analogy — the bit about a person staying "one and the same" across times and organs? What is that supposed to establish about God's relation to time?

5. And how does any of this hang together with the "God is a relative word / dominion" stuff? Is he saying God's very essence is being Lord, so that "eternal and infinite" are somehow secondary? That seems like a strange order of explanation.
