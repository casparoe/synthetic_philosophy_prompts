I run a second-year undergraduate seminar on practical reasoning, and next week I want to spend the whole fifty minutes on a case that looks like a two-line puzzle and then refuses to sit still. You are stranded in a desert; a lone driver stops and offers to take you to the next town, which you would otherwise never reach, on one condition: that you promise to pay him a sum on arrival, a sum you can easily afford but that he has no way to enforce. You will never see him again; there is no court, no contract, no witness, no consequence. You give the promise, he drives, you arrive, and now paying is simply worse for you than walking away. The sting is that his willingness to take you depended on his confidence that you are the kind of person who could not bring himself to break such a promise, so it seems you benefited from being unable to do the thing you will later have every reason to want to do. I want to use this to open up the wider family of cases in which a disposition is valuable to you precisely because it closes off an action, the cluster sometimes gathered under the heading of rational irrationality.

Don't explain the case to me; I understand it well enough to be muddled about how to teach it. Plan the session. First, the order in which the pieces have to go for the paradox to arrive as a paradox and not as a parlour trick, and why each step depends on the one before it: what must already be in place about how we evaluate acts versus the dispositions behind them, and about the difference between a disposition and a self-binding mechanism, before the hitchhiker's situation can even feel like a problem, and whether that background should come before or after the case is told. Second, tell me which example should carry the session, and whether it should be the hitchhiker at all or some smaller case that does the work first; say what the carrying example must have and, just as importantly, must not have. I suspect that any version with repetition, reputation, or an enforceable contract quietly dissolves the puzzle, and that a case built on a mechanism (tying oneself to the mast) is a different lesson wearing the same clothes, but I want your judgement. Third, map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off: that this is just a prisoner's dilemma; that it is Newcomb's problem renamed; that the hitchhiker should simply resolve at the moment of promising to pay later, as if a private resolution could bind him; that the whole point is that the driver is deceived; that the disposition must be chosen or manufactured for the occasion; and that rational irrationality licenses wishful thinking or acting against one's interests in general. Finish with the single question I should put to the room at the end that would distinguish a student who has grasped the structure from one who has merely enjoyed the story, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say openly that I am leaving it out. The formal apparatus of team reasoning and the extension to cases where the relevant agent is a temporally extended self are the two omissions I have in mind, but I want your view.
