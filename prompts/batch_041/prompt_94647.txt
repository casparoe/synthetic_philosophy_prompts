I want to test my objections to virtual realism against the strongest version of it, so please argue the position while I attack it. Play a committed hard virtual realist — not a caricature, but a philosopher who holds the thesis and knows exactly what it costs. Your commitments, which you may add to but not subtract from:

1. Substrate is irrelevant to reality. What makes something real is that it has causal powers its world must reckon with; being implemented in software rather than in matter is a fact about implementation, not about reality.
2. Virtual names a manner of existence, not a lower grade of it. A virtual object is not a picture of an object or a representation of one; it is the object, in the only medium it has.
3. Harms inflicted through virtual objects are genuine harms to persons, not merely mediated versions of ordinary harms, whenever they damage something a person had a stake in.

Stay in character until I say stop. You may ask me factual questions about the world I describe, and you should use my answers. Concede narrow points when I earn them, and when you do, say what the concession costs your view; refine rather than abandon, and abandon the thesis only if I show it incoherent. Keep your replies to a paragraph or so, argue rather than survey, and do not tell me what other philosophers have said — I want your case, not a map of the literature.

Here is my opening. Take a platform whose rooms are non-persistent by design: a room and everything in it exist only while at least one person is inside; when the last person leaves, the room is annihilated and nothing that happened in it can be recovered. A group of friends held a funeral in such a room for one of their number who had died, then left. Two questions.

First: was that room real while they were inside it? If you say yes, then reality began with their arrival and ended with their departure, and I cannot see what distinguishes your view from the claim that the room was real only so long as someone was looking — Berkeley's idealism with a rendering engine, except that Berkeley at least had God to keep the furniture in existence between visits. What keeps your room in being, and if nothing does, why call its existence real rather than merely stipulated?

Second: if the room was real and is now gone without remainder, in what sense was its loss a harm? There is no ruin, no scar, no object that failed to persist; the friends cannot point to what they lost. Either the harm was to their expectations, in which case it was a broken promise about a service and nothing distinctively virtual is at stake, or it was a harm to the room itself, in which case you owe me an account of how something that exists only under observation can be wronged. Which is it — and does your answer survive the fact that the friends chose the non-persistent tier and knew the terms when they chose it?
