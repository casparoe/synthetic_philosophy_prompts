ok so my reading group went down a rabbit hole last week and now none of us can sleep. it started from the thought that a single person can now do things that used to take a lab or a state — publish a dangerous result, mail out a self-replicating organism, that kind of thing — and my friend J built this argument for why you should basically never do the controversial thing on your own. i think someone named it the unilateralist's curse or something. anyway here it is:

(1) Suppose there's some act that any one of us could do unilaterally, and it gets done if any one of us decides to do it.
(2) Each of us will do it only if, on our own judgment, it's worth doing — net positive, expected value above zero.
(3) Each of us is fallible; call it a 5% chance that any given person judges wrong.
(4) So the act can happen because one person got it wrong, even if everyone else correctly judged it not worth doing.
(5) The more people who could do it, the more chances for at least one wrong judgment, so the more likely it happens even when it's a bad idea. With 60 people at 5% each you're already at roughly 95% that at least one of them pulls the trigger.
(6) A bad thing that one person does is bad no matter how many others wisely abstained, so the more people who could do it, the worse the expected outcome.
(7) So if you're one of those people and the matter is contested, you should hold back and act only once there's something like a consensus that it's worth doing. The takeaway is a principle of conformity.

heres my problem. i buy 2 and 5 — the math looks airtight — but the thing prooves too much, and in both directions. flip it:

(8) Suppose the act is actually a good idea that most people wrongly judge bad. By the same arithmetic, the more people who could do it, the more likely at least one of them correctly sees it's good and does it.
(9) By the logic of 6, more people able to act now gives a better expected outcome.
(10) So the same argument that says crowds make bad things happen also says crowds make good things happen. It doesn't actually tell us whether crowds are good or bad — only that with enough people, whatever some single person thinks is worth doing eventually gets done.
(11) And conformity can't be the fix, because if the consensus is wrong, conformity is exactly what guarantees the good thing never happens.
(12) Worse, "hold back and go with the consensus" is itself something any of us can unilaterally refuse to do — if someone thinks conformity is a bad idea they can just act anyway, so the curse seems to eat its own recommendation.

so (6) or (7) has to be where it breaks, but i can't tell which. is the flaw in (6) — some premise it's smuggling in, like that the abstainers would've done something else, or that we get to treat the no-one-acts world as the safe baseline, or that the act is more likely to be bad than good in the first place — or is (6) fine and the illegitimate bit is the leap from the group-level fact in (5) to the individual instruction in (7)? which step do you reject, and what do i pay for rejecting it? i'd rather keep 2 and 5 than 7 if i have to choose, but i want to know what i'm giving up.
