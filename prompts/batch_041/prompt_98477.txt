I have taught the debate over whether the numbers count for several years now, and I have come to suspect that the impasse is not one impasse but two, stacked on top of each other and traded under a single question. I would like you to diagnose the stalemate rather than adjudicate it.

State the two sides as I understand them, and correct me where I am wrong. On the aggregative side, the claim is that when I can save either one person or five and cannot save all, the fact that there are five is itself a reason—perhaps defeasible, perhaps to be weighed against the stringency of each individual claim—to save the five; the numbers enter the reasons. On the non-aggregative side, in the tradition of Taurek's argument that the numbers do not count, the claim is that each person's claim is equal and independent, that five such claims are not jointly greater than one, and that when not everyone can be saved the fitting response is a lottery among the persons rather than a headcount. Both sides, as far as I can tell, accept the same description of the case and the same underlying valuation of individual lives. Yet the argument has not moved in nearly fifty years, and each side takes the other's central move to be not merely mistaken but confused.

My hypothesis—I offer it to be tested and, if it fails, discarded—is that the parties are counting at different levels and never notice that they are. The aggregationist counts in the reasons: more claimants, more reason. The non-aggregationist declares counting in the reasons illicit, but then recommends a lottery, and a lottery among the persons is precisely a device in which the numbers re-enter through the outcomes: if there are six persons and five of them are on one side, the group of five holds five chances in six, and the expected number of survivors is five. If that is right, the non-aggregationist does not deny that the numbers count; he denies only that they count in the reasons, while permitting them to count in the distribution of outcomes—and the two sides are then answering different questions under one heading.

What I want from you is a diagnosis that takes this hypothesis seriously enough to break it if it is wrong.

First, is the disagreement substantive, or does it dissolve once we separate counting-in-reasons from counting-in-outcomes, or is that separation itself doing illegitimate work by assuming that reasons and outcomes are independent sites of moral assessment?

Second, if the disagreement survives the separation, what is the residue? A difference about what a reason for action is; about whether reasons that attach to distinct persons are additive at all; about which description of the case is canonical—a problem of maximizing a quantity versus a problem of honoring claims; or intuitions about the particular cases that neither side can argue for? Say which of these is doing the real work and why the others are not.

Third, consider a case I will stipulate, which I do not believe appears in the literature in this form. You can save A alone, or B and C together, and you agree with the non-aggregationist that no one's claim outweighs another's, so that some lottery is required. But there are two lotteries available. Under the first, you lottery among the three persons, so that the pair B and C together win with probability two-thirds. Under the second, you lottery between the two options, so that the pair wins with probability one-half. The non-aggregationist must choose one. If he chooses the first, he has let the numbers count in the outcomes, and my hypothesis is vindicated. If he chooses the second, he owes an account of why fairness at the level of options outranks fairness at the level of persons—and, more pressingly, of why that account does not simply relocate the numbers into a different place rather than expelling them. Tell me what he should say, and whether either answer vindicates or undermines the hypothesis.

Fourth, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal result, a new case, a change of question—and is there any reason to think that none is available and that the impasse is the honest resting point of the question?

Throughout, tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
