I am a final-year undergraduate enrolled in a course on the theory of rational belief, and I am trying to write down, as precisely as I can, what I actually think about a family of cases that keep arising in it: cases in which I seem to believe each of a great many things while refusing to believe them all together. I would like to know whether a single principle underlies my verdicts, or whether I am simply inconsistent. My verdicts, reported as honestly as I can manage:

1. A fair lottery with one thousand tickets, one winner, drawn tomorrow. Of each ticket I am willing to say flatly that it will lose, and I would say that I know this rather than merely that it is probable. I am not willing to say that no ticket will win, and I do not think anyone could know that. I would also assert of any given ticket, in ordinary conversation, that it will lose.

2. The same lottery, except that I hold 999 of the 1000 tickets. I am willing to say that I will win. I am equally willing to say, taken ticket by ticket, of each ticket in my hand that it will lose. I see that these cannot both be true, and I am reluctant to give up either attitude: asked ticket by ticket I give the second answer, asked whether I will win I give the first.

3. Two independent, well-supported claims, each about as likely as a single ticket's losing. Here I think it entirely rational to believe each and to believe them both; likewise for five, ten, or twenty such claims. I cannot locate the point at which I would stop, and I would resist the suggestion that there is one.

4. A careful researcher's book of five hundred substantive claims, each individually checked, with a track record suggesting roughly one error per book. I think she is rational to believe each of her claims, rational to believe that at least one of them is false, rational to publish, and under no obligation to have any view about which claim is the erroneous one. I do not think her position is incoherent.

5. The same book, but written by two authors, each of whom checked only their own half and took the other's half on trust. My verdicts are as in (4), for each author separately — including the belief that the book contains an error, which may well lie in the half they did not check.

6. A report with five hundred contributors, each individually 99.9% reliable, each asserting a single claim. I think the report may be issued, that every contributor is rational, and that no one — contributor or reader — is in a position to believe the whole conjunction of the report's claims.

7. Five hundred claims that all depend on a single shared assumption I would put at even odds, though each is close to certain if that assumption holds. Here I do not think it rational to believe any of them flatly.

8. One person who asserts both P and not-P. Incoherent, obviously.

What troubles me is that (8) is the one-claim version of the position I endorse in (4): there I believe each of five hundred claims and also believe that not all of them are true, and with a single claim that is exactly believing P and believing not-P, which I have just called incoherent. (1) and (2) likewise have me believing each of many things and believing something that rules one of them out. And I keep wanting to endorse, as a general rule, that if I am rational in believing each of two things then I am rational in believing both — which is precisely what my verdicts in (1) and (4) deny. I would rather not be told to preserve every verdict by adding epicycles; if a clean principle costs me one of these verdicts, I want to know which, and why that one.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of rival principles, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which clause of it is answerable to which case, and tell me what it implies about cases I have not listed — in particular one in which the number of claims is large but the conjunction still seems safe to believe, and one in which the number is small but the conjunction does not. If no single principle saves everything, say which verdict I should surrender. Finally, name the single further case that would most efficiently force a choice among the surviving candidates. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your answer, and identify the point you are least sure of.
