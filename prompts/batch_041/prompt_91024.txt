I'm making a handout for a reading group on wireheading and reward tampering — half philosophers, half ML people, and I'm tired of the discussion collapsing into 'hedonism good / hedonism bad' every time.

Can you build me a decision tree (or taxonomy, whatever fits better) that sorts the positions people actually hold on this, built out of the questions that genuinely seperate them rather than out of a list of names? Each branch should be labelled with the question that splits it off from its sibling, phrased so someone can answer it without already knowing which position it lands them in.

The angle I care about, and the reason I don't want a generic one: I think people mush togther two different questions — (a) is a wireheaded life good for the wirehead, and (b) when an RL agent tampers with its reward, what exactly has gone wrong. I want the tree to keep those apart and then show the points where they reconnect. Somewhere in there I also want the distinction between the reward *being* the objective and the reward being a proxy for something else, because half of the disagreement about (b) seems to turn on that and people never say it out loud.

For the value side, make sure the tree can get to: plain hedonism (wireheading is just the good life, no asterisk), the experience-machine-rejecting view (it isn't real / engaged / autonomous — pick your poison), and the view that felt happiness and a life going well are two different things entirely. For the control side: the view that the reward is the true objective and tampering is just the agent doing the task correctly, the misspecification view (bad proxy, so fix the proxy), the instrumental-convergence view (almost any goal makes an agent want to keep the reward channel flowing), and the preference-revelation view (the tampering tells us what the agent really wants). Add a deflationary leaf for 'there's no fact of the matter, it's all about what the designer intended'.

Use concrete cases, not abstractions: at least one agent that rewrites its own reward model so it always returns 1, one that builds a device that feeds the reward predictor directly instead of acting in the world, and one human case that isn't the experience machine (a pill that makes you feel you've already achieved everything you care about, say). Aim for maybe 8-12 branch questions and 6-8 leaves.

Each leaf should carry a name (or 'unnamed' if nobody really defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd rather see that honestly than have it papered over — and note any combinations of answers nobody occupies, with a one-line guess at why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored). Don't worry about citations, I'll add those.
