I'm in a second-year grad seminar on free will and we just did the counterfactual-intervener stuff — you know, the cases where some scientist or stranger stands ready to make you do a thing if you don't do it on your own, and then you go ahead and do it on your own anyway. The problem is I've been giving verdicts in class for three weeks and I'm pretty sure they can't all be right, and I cant tell which one I should give up. Could you act like a philosopher of action who works on moral responsibility and really press on this? Start by brainstorming the candidate principles on the table (leeway or alternative-possibility principles, sourcehood or reasons-responsiveness principles, the idea that responsibility traces back to earlier choices, whatever else you think belongs), then take my verdicts one at a time and tell me which principle survives the whole set and which of my intuitions has to go. American English please, and don't just hand me a survey — I want the actual adjudication.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Classic setup: a neuroscientist is watching my brain, and if I hadn't decided on my own to hand over the money, he'd have zapped me into doing it. I decide on my own and hand it over. I blame myself completely; the standby zap does nothing to my responsibility. (2) Same setup, except the zap is probabilistic — he's rigged it to fire only 60% of the time if I don't decide on my own. Still on my own, still blameworthy. (3) Same setup, except the way he would have intervened is by putting me under a threat or hypnotizing me, so that in the counterfactual branch where he fires, I would NOT be responsible for handing over the money. I'm still fully blameworthy in the actual branch, and I don't think the fact that the backup would have produced a non-responsible action changes anything. (4) Here's the one thats been bugging me: he's rigged the zap to trigger on the very same neural event that just IS my deciding, so there's no possible world where I decide on my own and he doesn't also zap — the two are nomically glued together. I still want to say I'm responsible, which worries me, because now I can't even point to an alternative possibility in the usual sense. (5) No neuroscientist at all, but my values were manufactured before I was old enough to have a say — a cult, or a lab, built my character from the ground up, and then I act on it. I don't feel responsible here, or at least not fully, even though nobody was standing over me and I could have deliberated differently. And (6) the case that made me start this whole thing: someone pointed out that in (1) I know, at some level, that there's a safety net — that I'll end up doing it either way — and that this knowledge is part of why I feel free to deliberate and choose at all. So my choosing was propped up by the very thing I said was irrelevant. I still want to hold myself responsible, but I can't say the backup is doing nothing anymore.

My own tentative thought is that what's doing the work isn't whether I could have done otherwise but whether the actual sequence ran through my own reasons — the deciding was mine and wasn't bypassed or replaced. But (5) seems to sink that, since my reasons there were also manufactured, and (6) makes me nervous that even (1) isn't as clean as I thought. So: is there one principle that gets all six, or a small set of candidates where each one is motivated by a particular case? State each precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and then tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't given you — one where the backup would have made me act for even better reasons than my own, and one where the only thing standing between me and the act was a coincidence (a gust of wind, say) rather than any agent. Be honest about which verdict I have to drop; I'd rather lose one than keep a principle that's just gerrymandered to save them all.
