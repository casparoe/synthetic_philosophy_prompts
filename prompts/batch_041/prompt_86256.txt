I'm a novelist at work on a book in which one disputed death in a village is told five times, by five witnesses, and the accounts cannot be squared with one another. I want to build the book on Jain anekāntavāda rather than merely gesture at it. Take the role of an expert in classical Indian epistemology who also understands how fiction works: think the problem through carefully, and begin by setting out in your own words the doctrine as you understand it — the doctrine of standpoints (nayas), the seven-fold predication (syādvāda), and the realist commitments that underwrite them — before you draw any conclusions. Then give me the most important implications for how I should build the novel: what the doctrine licenses, what it forbids, and above all what a reader who assumes that many-sidedness simply means every perspective is equally valid would get wrong about the book I would be writing. I don't want a reading list or citations; I have no way to look anything up, and what I need are the conceptual consequences. And if the premise is confused — if anekāntavāda cannot be lifted out of its soteriological, non-violence-centred setting and turned into a narrative device without distorting it — say so plainly rather than humouring me.
