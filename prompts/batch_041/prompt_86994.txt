I care much more about the conceptual carving than about the label, so please do not stop at naming something.

I work on safety inside a large AI lab, and I keep noticing a pattern for which I have no name. It is not the argument I already know, the one about levels: that a lab which spends more on safety than its competitors will fall behind, that the cost of safety is therefore a competitive handicap, and that safety is under-supplied for exactly that reason. I understand that argument and I am not asking about it. What I keep noticing is about composition rather than level, and it seems to happen without anyone deciding it.

Three instances.

First, within a lab, safety proposals compete for the same engineers, the same compute, and the same executive attention as capability work. A proposal that can also be pitched as making the models better survives the budget round. A proposal whose only payoff is that we will understand the system better, with no near-term product benefit, does not. Nobody decides to abandon the second kind of work. It simply loses every round.

Second, take two safety techniques, one that costs a little capability and one that costs a lot. The cheap one gets adopted everywhere and appears in policy documents as evidence of good practice. The expensive one gets adopted by nobody, and the risk it was meant to address stays where it was. The resulting public picture of safety practice describes exactly the subset of safety work that was never in serious tension with the competitive objective.

Third, compare organizations. The outfit that looks most safety-forward by any absolute measure may have the most capability-aligned safety portfolio of all, precisely because only a very well-resourced outfit can afford to do the safety work that pays for itself. The outfits doing the work that does not pay for itself are the ones that shrink or disappear.

The thing I keep circling is this: a competitive constraint of this kind does not merely lower the amount of safety work; it selects within safety work, so that the part that survives is systematically the part that was never really costly. And because the selection is not anyone's decision, it leaves no trace in any decision record, which is why it is so hard to point to in an argument.

So: is there an established name for this? I suspect there is not, and that it is being run together with several nearby things. If there is a name, tell me what it is and what distinguishes it from the alternatives. If there is not, coin one, define it, and show me its edges. In particular, distinguish it from the ordinary level claim about the alignment tax; from adverse selection; from Goodhart's law; from regulatory capture; and from the familiar complaint that safety is used as marketing. If it turns out to reduce to one of these, say so, and say what, if anything, is left over. Then give me a case of the same pattern in a domain far from AI, a case that superficially resembles the pattern but is not it, and a statement of what would have to be true for the selection to be benign rather than corrosive.
