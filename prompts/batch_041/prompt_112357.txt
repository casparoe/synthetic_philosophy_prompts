I have no formal training in statistics or philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time arguing with friends about cases where a comparison reverses when you break the data into groups. I would like to know whether there is a principle behind my judgments, or whether I am just making them up case by case. Here are my verdicts.

1. Two hospitals. Hospital A has a higher overall mortality rate than Hospital B, but when patients are divided by how sick they were on arrival, A has a lower mortality rate in every group. A takes sicker patients. I think A is the better hospital, and the overall comparison is misleading. The severity of the patient on arrival is not something the hospital choice changes.

2. A university’s admissions. Overall, men are admitted at a higher rate than women, but in every department women are admitted at a higher rate. Women apply more often to departments that admit few people. I think the department-level comparison is the one that answers whether the admissions process is biased against women, but the overall comparison is the one that answers what chance a woman applicant has of being admitted. I do not think either number is simply wrong; they answer different questions.

3. A drug. Overall, the drug reduces heart attacks compared with a placebo, but in every age group it increases them. The drug was given mostly to young people, who have few heart attacks anyway. I think the comparison within age groups is the one that tells me whether to take the drug, and the overall comparison is misleading.

4. A job-training program. Overall, people who complete the program earn more than those who do not, but within each occupation they earn less. The program pushes people into lower-paying occupations. I think the overall comparison is the one that matters for whether the program helps people, and the within-occupation comparison is misleading, because the program itself changes which occupation you are in. But if I already know I will end up in a given occupation, the within-occupation number seems relevant.

5. A blood-pressure drug. Overall, the drug reduces heart attacks, but among patients whose blood pressure dropped, the drug looks no better than placebo, and among those whose blood pressure did not drop, it looks worse. I think the overall comparison is the one that tells me what the drug actually does, and breaking the patients into groups by whether their blood pressure dropped is a mistake if the drug works by lowering blood pressure. But if my doctor knows my blood pressure will not drop, I want the number within that group.

6. Exercise and hospitalization. Among people who are hospitalized, those who exercise regularly have worse outcomes than those who do not; overall, exercisers have better outcomes. Exercise prevents hospitalization, and among the hospitalized, exercisers tend to be sicker. I think the overall comparison is the one that matters, and the hospital-only comparison is misleading, because being hospitalized is a result of both exercise and illness.

7. A school. School X has a higher overall graduation rate than School Y, but within every major School Y has a higher graduation rate. Students choose majors after admission, and School X’s advising pushes students into majors with high graduation rates. I think the overall comparison is right for “if I enroll at X, what are my chances of graduating?” but the within-major comparison is right for “does X teach each major better?” I cannot tell whether these are two questions or one.

8. A case where the groups are defined by a cutoff on a continuous variable, such as age or income. Depending on where I put the cutoff, the reversal appears or disappears. I think the reversal is meaningful only if the cutoff corresponds to a real difference in kind, not an arbitrary line. But I cannot say what makes a difference “real” without going in a circle.

My tentative principle is this: compare within the finest groups that are alike in every respect that the choice itself cannot change. If the grouping variable is something the choice can change, do not break the data into those groups; use the overall comparison. But this does not seem to handle cases 2 and 5 cleanly, and case 6 makes me unsure whether the principle is about what causes what or about the question being asked.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures my verdicts. State it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and say what it implies for at least two further cases I have not mentioned. Then tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and why. I would rather have a simple principle that loses one verdict than a complicated one that saves them all by special pleading. If the right answer is that some of my verdicts are not about the same question and cannot be captured by one principle, say that clearly and explain how to tell which question is which.
