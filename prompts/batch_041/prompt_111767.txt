I sit on the board of the Hollowbrook Community House, a small nonprofit in a town of about nine thousand people. We are nine volunteers; I am a retired electrician who never finished college, and I have been turning this question over on my own for the better part of a year. I would like my written opinion to be something the board can act on rather than a reflection, and I have asked you to draft it.

The facts. In 2011, the board then serving adopted a resolution, entered in our minutes and reported in the local paper, that the ground floor of the building would be "kept available, at no charge, to local groups for as long as this organization exists." Three groups moved in: a food pantry, a tenants' association, and a community choir. The choir raised about $38,000 over several years and spent it converting the back room into a rehearsal space with proper acoustics. The same 2011 resolution contained a clause reserving the board's right to revisit the arrangement "if the building's own survival should be at stake." Two of the nine seats were vacant when it passed, and one member who voted for it later told the paper he had always thought it a mistake.

The crisis. The roof, the boiler, and the fire escapes need replacing; our engineer puts the work at $210,000 and we have $46,000. The only offer on the table is a twelve-year lease of the entire ground floor to a pharmacy chain, at a rent that would cover the repairs and leave a small reserve. The three groups have offered to raise $12,000 among themselves, which does not close the gap. Two of our current members were recruited specifically from those groups; the rest of us have no connection to the 2011 promise.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate before our vote on the 14th. I need a recommendation — honor the 2011 arrangement, break it, or something in between — with a time frame and specific terms, and with the reasoning set out so that the members who feel bound by the promise and the members who think a board cannot be governed by a board that no longer exists can each see that their position was taken seriously. What I am really after is the question underneath: what makes a promise binding in a case like this — that people arranged their lives around it, that we gave our word, or something else — and whether a promise can bind an institution whose members are all different from those who made it. If our promise turns out to be weaker than the groups believe, say so; if it is stronger than most of my colleagues believe, say that too. Stay with our facts, and tell me what we should do on the 14th.
