I have come to think that a long-run predictive theory should not be treated as refuted merely because its deadline passed — that a theory can be right but early — and the Malthusian case is my paradigm. The mechanism, I want to say, was sound and the timing was wrong; the escape from the trap is a postponement, not a refutation. Play this straight and tell me what would count against the position.

Sort the defeaters by how much they should move me, fatal to merely awkward, and make the strongest case for each before assessing it. Several, I expect, are internal to the position rather than empirical: whether 'right but early' is a verdict at all or a formula for immunising any long-run claim; whether the delay is produced by a mechanism the theory did not already contain and which carries its own testable consequences (a rescue that adds content) or is merely asserted (a rescue that does not); whether extending the horizon leaves the position predicting anything a rival would not also predict. Where I have to draw the line, be concrete: name the observation, and the date by which it must occur, that would make me abandon the position rather than extend it once more. The familiar Popperian and Lakatosian apparatus may help you here, but the labels are not the deliverable.

The Malthusian case is the test of whether I mean it. Separate the parts that might be right but early from the parts that are simply wrong — the mechanism, the conditional claim about what happens absent preventive checks, and the prognosis of permanence — and say which part the historical record has actually touched. Then tell me whether the distinction I am drawing is a real feature of the theory or one I have manufactured after the fact to save it.

Apply the same treatment to one live case of your choosing — a resource-depletion claim, a collapse scenario, a long-run demographic projection — and say what my position commits me to there. Do not soften the conclusion: if the position turns out to be well insulated, say so, and say whether that is a defect or simply what a long-run theory must be.

Close by saying what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. British English throughout, please.
