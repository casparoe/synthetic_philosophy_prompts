I teach a second-year seminar on action and practical reason, 14 students, and I've got 80 minutes for the session on Buridan's ass — the donkey parked exactly between two identical bales of hay that starves because it has no reason to prefer either one. I don't want to lecture at them about it. I want them to walk into the thing themselves and get stuck.

What I actually care about: I don't want the cheap resolution where someone says well obviously the donkey's an idiot, you just pick one, and everyone nods. I want them to feel that it's obviously fine to just pick, and then feel the floor tilt when the framing changes. So build the activity around at least two framings of the same forced choice. In one, all that's asked is that they act — take a bale, you're done, nobody cares why. In the other, the very same choice is treated as a report about them: which one do you prefer, which one do you want, what does your choice say about you. In the first framing a stall looks ridiculous; in the second the stall feels weirdly right, like the question can't be answered. I want them sitting in that gap and then having to say what the gap is.

I also want to smuggle in two ideas they almost certainly haven't met. First, a distinction between picking and choosing: choosing is selecting something because you take it to be better, or at least because you have a reason; picking is selecting with no reason at all, the way you grab a coin out of a jar. If picking is available, a tied agent can still act, so the donkey has no excuse. Second, the suspicion that the donkey's two bales aren't really equal — maybe they're comparable but neither better, worse, nor equal, the kind of case some people call parity. And under parity, picking isn't obviously free; either way you're leaving something real behind. If the activity can make them feel equal-options and parity-options as different in their hands, the session writes itself.

So: give me the rules, the materials (I can do snacks, tokens, index cards, a timer, nothing fancier, and no real money), and a payoff or forfeit scheme that makes stalling cost something rather than being free. Rounds that move them through equal options, then equal-value-but-different options, then something closer to incomparable options, with the act-versus-report switch landing somewhere in the middle. A running order with timings that leaves me a real debrief, and in that debrief, the mapping from each thing that happened to each piece of the puzzle above. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to patch it on my feet, and be straight with me about which part of the problem the activity just can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself. Don't let them hear the name Buridan until the debrief.
