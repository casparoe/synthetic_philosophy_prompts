I chair the governance committee of a mid-sized research foundation, and after a year of internal argument we have agreed to trial futarchy — the idea that we should settle our values by voting but estimate the consequences of a proposal by betting on them — for our major allocation decisions. We make roughly a dozen binary decisions a year (fund this program or not; adopt this procurement rule or not; continue this grant or end it), each committing a significant share of the budget, and the committee is split between members who trust expert judgment and members who trust markets. We meet monthly, decide within two weeks, have no economist on staff, and every decision needs the board's sign-off. I need something better than a slogan: a decision procedure the committee can actually run, written for people who are not economists.

Give me that procedure. It should set out the steps in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, mark where the procedure terminates in a decision, and mark where it must hand the matter back to a person because the question is one of values rather than prediction. The procedure has to do the conceptual work the slogan hides. At a minimum it should force us to settle, in the right order: what welfare metric the market is supposed to forecast, and who is authorized to choose it; whether the relevant markets are genuinely conditional — paying out on the outcome given the policy, not on the policy's chances — or have quietly collapsed into an ordinary prediction market; how the two branches of the comparison are priced and how the difference between them is read; what threshold of expected gain is required to overturn the status quo, and why that threshold is a values choice the market cannot make for us; how resolution and the measurement of the metric are verified; and what to do when the market is thin, when its price reflects the capital and risk-appetite of the traders rather than their beliefs, or when the estimated difference falls inside the noise.

Handle these hard cases explicitly, and say what the procedure does with each: a proposal whose benefits fall on people outside the metric; a proposal that is popular for reasons the metric does not capture; a metric that the market's own verdict could move, so that the forecast becomes self-fulfilling; and a proposal whose outcome will not be observable for years. Then run the procedure on two or three worked examples — including at least one where it returns an answer I will not like, and say so — and close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and which decisions it cannot be trusted with.

Write it as a procedure, not an essay, with the hard cases and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated marked as such. Where a step is a judgment call dressed up as a rule, say so. And separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material: do not present a contested claim — about whether markets beat experts, about whether manipulation is fatal, about whether a metric can be chosen without smuggling in values — as though it were the consensus.
