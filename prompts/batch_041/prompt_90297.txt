I'm a senior in high school and the editor of our philosophy club's yearly journal; this year's theme is questions we can't stop asking, and my own contribution is a poem about what some philosophers call the meta-problem of consciousness. Not the question of why experience exists, but the question of why creatures like us, whatever we turn out to be made of, keep reporting that their own experience is inexplicable. What interests me is that this question cuts both ways. Before anyone has an account of where the intuition comes from, a plain first-person report of experience reads as evidence that experience resists explanation; once the account is in hand, that very same report starts to read as evidence for the account instead. I want a poem that makes the reader feel that reversal rather than merely be told about it, and I want the form to do the work: write four or five quatrains with regular meter and rhyme, in which the last line of every quatrain is the same refrain, word for word, not a syllable altered. Nothing about that line may change except what it is doing in the argument, so any turn the poem takes has to come from the stanzas around it. Keep the voice as one person who is both the explainer and the thing being explained, and don't let the poem land on a verdict, since the point is that a complete explanation of the intuition may leave untouched the question of what the intuition is about. Plain language is welcome; you don't need to name any philosophers or theories, though someone who works on this should be able to recognize the position rather than just the vocabulary. Please give me just the poem, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and if you want to add a remark or two afterwards, keep it in ordinary continuous prose.
