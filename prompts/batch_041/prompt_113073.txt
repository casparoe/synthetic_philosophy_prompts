I run the substantiation desk at an advertising standards body. We get about forty claims a week — 'clinically proven', 'supports gut health', 'nine out of ten dentists recommend it' — and the caseworkers who screen them have fifteen minutes each and no philosphy. Our rule of thumb at the moment is basically 'is there a study', which is hopless, so I want to rebuild it on something better.

I've been reading about the Nyāya school, which sorted the ways you can know a thing into perception, inference, comparison and testimony, and then spent centuries cataloguing the ways a reason can look fine and be rotten. Turn that into a procedure my desk can actually run: a fixed order of questions for each claim, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a straight no, and where it has to go to a human because it's a value call rather than a factual one. First pass should just be: which of the four ways of knowing is this claim really resting on, and is that one even available here — half our messes are people passing off what someone told them as if they'd seen it themselves.

The bits I want carried over properly: the hidden condition that props up a generalisation (their stock example was wet fuel in the smoke-and-fire case — ours is the confounder, or the fact that every decent trial was run by the manufacturer); the reason that proves too much, since the same reason would equally support the opposite conclusion; the reason that quietly assumes what it's meant to prove (a survey of dermatologists offered as proof the cream works); and evidence that's simply been overtaken. They also distinguish inquiry that's genuinely trying to get it right from arguing in order to win, and I want that in as a step, because a good half of what lands on my desk is the second thing.

Then run it on three: the probiotic, the dentist toothpaste, and a supplement whose whole file is one twenty-year-old study that nobody believes any more. That last one should come out as a no even though there is a study. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire and which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure.
