I'm a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a moral philosopher of a bleak persuasion: she holds that no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything, in the sense that would make a person deserving of blame or praise simply for what she has done. The argument she leans on is the one usually associated with Galen Strawson. Nothing can be the cause of itself; to be truly morally responsible for an action you would have to be, in certain crucial mental respects, the cause of yourself; and to be the cause of yourself in that sense you would have had to intentionally make yourself the sort of person you are, which presupposes an earlier self who did the making — and that earlier self would need the same grounding, and so on without end. What she prizes in the argument is that it doesn't turn on determinism at all: it bites whether the world is deterministic or not, since a choice that is merely undetermined is no more authored by you than one that is caused. I want a poem she could plausibly have written herself, and I want its form to do the argument's work rather than illustrate it.

Please write it as a pantoum — five quatrains, twenty lines — using the form's interlocking repetition, with lines two and four of each quatrain returning as lines one and three of the next, and the final quatrain folding back into the first so the whole poem reads as a circuit with no first link. Two constraints beyond the form. First, each recurring line should come back with its grammatical person shifted — what an I says in one stanza should return as something said about a she, or an it, or a we — so that the poem enacts the displacement of authorship instead of merely describing it; by the end, the one who chose should have become the artifact of an earlier chooser who is herself an artifact. Second, in the fourth quatrain I want the poem to try the libertarian escape: a moment where the agent, torn between two courses, simply throws her weight one way, and the effort is supposed to be undetermined by anything in her prior character, so that the regress stops there and the choice is finally and genuinely hers. Let that move be made in earnest and without irony. Then let the form test it: because a pantoum must return its lines, whatever line is used to stop the regress will have to come back somewhere it can no longer do that work. Don't announce that undercutting or explain it; let the reader feel it. Leave it genuinely open whether the closing loop is a proof or a trap, because I would rather the poem end where the argument actually leaves a person than in a verdict.

A few things to avoid. Don't name Strawson or any other philosopher, and don't use the terms moral responsibility, free will, determinism, or regress — I want the idea rendered, not labeled. Don't let it curdle into a shrug or swell into a sermon; the voice should belong to someone who has lived with the conclusion for years and is not performing despair. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher working on this would recognize the argument and not wince, and fair enough that a libertarian who thinks the regress can be stopped at an undetermined effort would find her own move in the fourth quatrain and feel its pull before the poem complicates it. Keep it in a register that could plausibly come from a philosopher who is not a poet: precise, a little austere, allowed one image that recurs and changes meaning as the repetitions carry it into new mouths.
