I'm taking a seminar on moral philosophy and we just did the Susan Wolf piece on moral saints. Two people in my discussion section kept arguing about it after class and I ended up stuck in the middle, so I'd like an actual ruling instead of more vibes.

Sam's line is that Wolf's objection to the moral saint is just the demandingness objection in nicer clothes. If being maximally good costs you your piano practice and your hobbies, that's a cost of being good, not an argument that the good life is somehow bad. And the "you wouldn't want her at your dinner party" stuff is an aesthetic complaint, not a moral one.

Rae says that's not it at all. On her reading Wolf isn't saying sainthood is expensive, she's saying a life with no room for non-moral projects -- the cello, friendship that isn't also service, a craft you do just because -- is missing goods that aren't reducible to welfare, and a morality that asks you to hand them all over is asking you to be less than a whole person. The saint isn't miserable exactly, she's thin.

Sam came back with two things. First, the saint can keep all of that: being maximally moral just means that when the cello and the suffering child conflict, the child wins. That's a priority rule, not a ban on cellos. Second -- and this is the part I thought was actually interesting -- on any plausible picture of how much good you can do over a lifetime, being a burned-out wreck is counterproductive, so a serious utilitarian is going to spend real time on sleep, health, relationships, her own flourishing, all of it. So the alleged conflict mostly dissolves on its own.

Rae said that if the cello only survives because it's instrumentally useful to your giving, you've already conceded her point: it isn't a good in its own right in your theory, it's a tool, and a saint would drop it the moment the math changed. Sam said that's a distinction without a difference, since what matters is the life someone lives, not the ledger of reasons behind it.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who's right where, rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Is Sam's "it's just demandingness" reading of Wolf fair, or does he lose something real when he reframes it that way? Does the burnout point actually answer her, or does it just relocate the problem? And who's got the better overall position -- or is there something the two of them are both missing about what "maximal moral goodness" is even supposed to mean? I know what Wolf concludes, so skip the summary and give me the adjudication.
