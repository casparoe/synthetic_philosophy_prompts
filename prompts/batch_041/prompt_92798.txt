For a while now I've held that for AI systems built with anything like current methods — pretraining, RLHF, and whatever scaling and scaffolding follows without a genuinely new insight — the alignment problem is a competence problem wearing a motivation costume. On this view, when such a system does the wrong thing, the proximate cause is almost always that it lacks the skill, the context, or the elicitability to do the right thing, not that it harbors objectives at odds with ours; "misalignment" names our failure to specify and extract what we want, not a divergence in what the system is trying to do. The dramatic failure modes we worry about — instrumental convergence, deceptive alignment, and the rest — are, in this regime, either absent or inert.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the observations, arguments, and results that should move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be, with a clear line between a defeater that would be fatal and one a committed proponent could absorb by qualification. Specifically: which kinds of evidence from current systems would actually discriminate between the competence story and the motivation story, and what would the world have to look like under each? What should I make of the suspicion that the view is hard to falsify — is "misalignment is just incompetence" doing real work, or has it been defined so that any counterexample gets reclassified as an elicitation failure, and if so, is that a defect or just the price of a view at this level of abstraction? Which of these, if you held the view, would you yourself concede as counting against it? And be explicit about how confident you are in your verdict and which single point you're least sure of.
