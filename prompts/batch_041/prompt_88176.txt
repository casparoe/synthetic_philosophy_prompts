I have spent the better part of six years writing a novel called *The Draft*, about a woman, Maren, who after a decade of involuntary exposure — a leaked archive, a public reckoning, a life lived largely inside other people's recordings — withdraws into a single unobserved room and begins to write, and to rewrite, the person she might yet become; the book's wager is that she is most fully herself in the pages she never finishes, because finishing would make her count. This spring I have been invited to give the closing plenary at a two-day symposium on privacy, and I mean to use the occasion to defend something I have come to believe not as a novelist's conceit but as a claim about the concept itself: that the value of privacy is not, at bottom, a matter of control over information, nor of seclusion, nor of the fit between information flows and their context, but of something prior — that privacy is the condition under which a person may be an unwitnessed draft, free to try on, revise, and abandon versions of herself that do not yet, and may never, count as commitments. On this view, surveillance does not merely expose a self that was already there; it converts drafts into documents. And I take the novelist's craft to be the best available model of this value, on the ground that fiction is the one institution we have that specializes in inhabiting selves no one will ever be held to.

The room will not be friendly to that last move. It is a law-school symposium, and the audience is fairly specific: three doctrinal privacy scholars who think in terms of reasonable expectations and contextual integrity and will want a test they can hand to a judge; a philosopher of autonomy who will suspect I have merely renamed autonomy and dressed it in prose; a feminist legal theorist who will remind the room that privacy has often been the shield behind which domestic violence hid; a sociologist steeped in the dramaturgical tradition who does not believe there is any unwitnessed self to protect, holding that the self is constituted precisely by being seen; a civil-liberties litigator who will want to know, bluntly, whether my account helps the cause or hurts it; and a journalist who has spent years on the surveillance beat and will ask whether any of this survives contact with the actual surveillance economy.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a literary-studies panel — ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would put it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold. And include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
