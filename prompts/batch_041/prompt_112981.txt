Consider a simple version of the Platonia dilemma. There are N players. Each privately chooses IN or OUT. If exactly one player chooses IN, that player alone receives a prize of value V; if zero or more than one choose IN, nobody receives anything. There is no cost to choosing IN. All players know the rules, know that everyone is an equally good reasoner, and know that everyone knows this.

A friend offers this argument:

1. A rational choice is one that maximizes expected utility, where expected utility is causal: the value of choosing IN rather than OUT is the sum over outcomes of the probability that my choice causally makes that outcome happen, multiplied by its value.
2. Choosing IN rather than OUT changes the outcome only when my choice is pivotal: either I am the sole IN-chooser, in which case I win V, or I am the second IN-chooser, in which case my choice destroys the prize for the one other IN-chooser.
3. In a game with many players, the probability of either pivotal event is negligible, especially if the others are rational and therefore unlikely to choose IN.
4. So the expected causal gain from choosing IN is negligible, and choosing OUT is at least as rational as choosing IN.
5. By the same reasoning, each player should choose OUT.
6. But if every player chooses OUT, the prize is never awarded. A theory that tells every player to choose OUT in a game where the prize could have been awarded is a theory that proves too much: it also tells citizens not to vote, donors not to give to large charities, and individuals not to reduce their emissions, because each such act is causally negligible on its own.
7. Therefore premise 1, or the way it is applied, must be wrong.

I would rather keep premise 1 than accept conclusion 5, because I do not believe that rationality forbids all negligible causal contributions. But I cannot see where the argument goes wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, if I keep the causal expected-utility framework, what exactly must I add or revise so that IN is rational here without also licensing absurd conclusions elsewhere?
