Read Groundhog Day through Schlick's account of the meaning of life, on which meaning is not a purpose or goal lying beyond an activity but is found in play and youth — in activities that carry their own point and value, and in joy as distinct from mere pleasure. Phil Connors begins by treating the loop instrumentally: first as a playground for appetite, then as a machine for advantage, with his repeated suicides as the limit case of a life emptied of intrinsic content. The reading I want should ask whether the film vindicates Schlick. Once Phil learns piano, ice-sculpting, and the ordinary rescues of Punxsutawney, is he at last playing in Schlick's sense, or is he still working — self-improvement as a means to escape and to win Rita? Does the film let Schlick have the last word, or does it quietly restore the purpose-driven picture he rejects by making the last day's decency the condition of the next? Be faithful in both directions: where the film dramatizes something Schlick's essay leaves out — the constitutive role of other people, or the fact that repetition rather than youth is the medium in which Phil finds meaning — say so plainly, and don't reduce the film to an illustration. I am teaching Schlick and want to use the film without misrepresenting either it or him.
