I need comments specific enough that I can return them to the student and defend the grade if it is challenged.

The examination question read as follows:

"Teleosemantics (biosemantics) attempts to naturalize intentional content by grounding it in biological function.

(a) State the core teleosemantic account of what determines the content of a representational state, and explain how, on that account, a state can misrepresent.

(b) The swampman thought experiment asks us to imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an ordinary human being who comes into existence by a cosmic accident, with no evolutionary or causal history of the usual kind. What does teleosemantics imply about whether the swampman's internal states have content? Is that implication an objection to the theory? Defend your answer.

(c) Consider a case not discussed in class. Engineers design, from scratch, an artificial organism (call it the Sentry) with a single sensor that emits a signal S whenever it detects a particular airborne toxin; S is wired to a mechanism that makes the Sentry move away from the source. The Sentry was never subject to natural selection; every component was specified by the engineers precisely so that S would occur in the presence of the toxin and trigger avoidance. On the teleosemantic account, does S have content, and if so, what fixes it?"

The student wrote:

"Teleosemantics holds that a mental state has content in virtue of its biological function: a state represents F when it is that state's proper function to indicate F. Proper functions are etiological — they are conferred by a history of selection, not by what the state currently happens to do. This is what lets the theory naturalize content, since proper functions are a respectable biological notion.

It also lets the theory accommodate misrepresentation. A state can occur when its Normal conditions do not obtain — the frog's bug-detector can fire at a falling pellet — and then it misrepresents. An account that identified content with what a state in fact reliably indicates would leave no room for error, since whatever the state indicates would by definition be its content. Teleosemantics avoids this by tying content to what the state is supposed to indicate, that is, to the conditions under which its consumer can perform its proper function.

The disjunction problem might seem to threaten this, since the frog's detector is correlated with bugs-or-small-dark-moving-things. Teleosemantics answers it by insisting that content is fixed by the consumer mechanism's proper function, not by the extension of the correlation. Fodor's asymmetric-dependence solution, which Dretske adopts, is the standard way of making this precise.

Against the swampman: teleosemantics implies that his internal states have no content, because he has no selection history to confer proper functions on them, and Millikan accepts this. But this is a serious problem for the theory. The swampman is molecule-for-molecule identical to us and will behave exactly as we do; his states play exactly the same roles in his cognitive economy, so they must mean the same things. Since teleosemantics cannot allow this, it must be false.

Finally, the Sentry. Its signal S was never selected for, so it has no naturally selected proper function. But the engineers built it precisely so that S would occur in the presence of the toxin and trigger avoidance, and this history of design confers a derived proper function on S. So S does have content, and it represents the presence of the toxin."
