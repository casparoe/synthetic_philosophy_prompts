Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors — call them the Steward and the Auditor — who disagree about whether pure time preference is defensible. By pure time preference I mean caring less about a benefit or harm merely because it lies further in the future, holding the magnitude, the number of people affected, and every other feature of the case fixed. The Auditor will defend a positive rate of pure time preference; the Steward will deny that any such rate is defensible. Neither may be a strawman: each should offer the strongest available version of their position, concede points the other earns, and respond to the other's actual arguments rather than to a convenient caricature.

Ground the exchange in a decision problem I will call the Two Rivers. A foundation holds a fixed sum that it must spend once, on one of two projects. The first, River A, averts a specified catastrophe — ten million premature deaths from a preventable disease — beginning now and unfolding over the coming century. The second, River B, averts an identical catastrophe of the same size, but it would occur twelve thousand years from now, and this project is the only thing that could ever prevent it. Stipulate away the usual confounders: the figures are known with certainty, the far-future catastrophe is certain to occur if nothing is done, the people it would kill will be as real and as numerous and as well-off as those in River A, and no later actor will be in a position to intervene. Have the two characters work through the following pressure points in turn, and let each one engage the other's replies as they go.

First, the spatial parallel. Suppose the same ten million deaths were instead to occur now, in a galaxy causally isolated from us — no contact, no reciprocity, no shared institutions, no possibility of their helping or harming us, and no possibility of our ever learning of them. The Steward will argue that anyone who would not discount that case cannot discount River B without naming a difference that is genuinely non-temporal. The Auditor should try to name one, and the Steward should test whether the proposed difference is really non-temporal or is pure time preference wearing a disguise.

Second, the uncertainty defense. The Auditor may hold that the apparent discount rate is really a hazard rate: the probability that no one will be left in twelve thousand years, or that any intervention will be undone or forgotten. The Steward should distinguish discounting the expected value of an uncertain outcome from caring less about a person because of when that person lives, and should ask what remains of the Auditor's position once the survival probability is, for the sake of argument, stipulated to be one.

Third, the non-identity and reciprocity defenses. The Auditor may appeal to the fact that future people can owe us nothing and we can owe them nothing under a contractarian test, or that they are not yet determinate individuals with settled interests. The Steward should press whether these considerations would equally license discounting within the present generation, or whether they quietly reintroduce a temporal asymmetry.

Fourth, the arithmetic of consistency. Have the characters confront the fact that under exponential discounting, any positive rate however small eventually drives the weight on a sufficiently distant outcome to nothing, so that the choice is not really between "a little discounting" and "none" but between a zero rate and a rate whose long-run implications are extreme. The Auditor should say whether they accept that implication or whether it reveals a flaw in the way the rate is applied; the Steward should say whether a zero rate generates absurdities of its own — for instance, a present sacrifice of almost any size for a sufficiently large far-future benefit.

Fifth, the metaphysics and the consequences. Let the characters address whether a presentist or growing-block picture of time — on which the far future does not yet exist — gives any independent reason to discount, and what happens to ordinary saving, investment, and intergenerational institutions if either view is adopted wholesale. Each should be willing to treat the resulting picture as either a reductio of their own position or a surprising but acceptable implication, and to say which and why.

Keep the exchange substantive and reciprocal throughout. The dialogue need not end in agreement, but it should end with the two positions in their strongest and most precisely delineated forms.
