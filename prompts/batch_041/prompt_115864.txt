I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy who came into decision theory sideways, from a statistics background, and I keep running into what the people around my department call coherence arguments for expected utility maximization without ever getting a straight account of what they are supposed to establish. Last week a friend in an AI safety reading group — very sharp, but self-taught in philosophy mostly from blog posts and forum threads — gave me his summary of the whole business, and I have been chewing on it since. His line was roughly this. Expected utility theory is not a psychological theory at all, he said, but a consistency requirement on preference: the von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage theorems show that any agent whose preferences satisfy a handful of trivial bookkeeping conditions — no cycles, no gaps, that sort of thing — can be represented as maximizing expected utility, which means the agent's beliefs and desires can simply be read off its choices without anyone ever asking it what it wants. The axioms are just canons of logic, so an agent who violates them is provably irrational; that is why the money-pump argument works, since anyone with intransitive preferences can be bled dry by a sequence of trades each one of which he prefers to the one before. Therefore expected utility maximization is the only decision rule that reason permits, full stop, and once you grant the axioms there is nothing substantive left to argue about. He added, as a kind of coda, that this is also why an incoherent agent is doomed in any competitive setting: a coherent opponent can predict it and strip it of everything it has.

I suspect this is one of those summaries that is right in spirit and wrong in ways that matter — he has absorbed the conclusions of the literature and mislaid the load-bearing qualifications — but I do not know the material well enough to locate the seams myself. If it helps to know my inclinations: I find the representation theorems genuinely impressive and the money pump vivid, but I have a nagging suspicion that the word rationality is doing an enormous amount of work in his last two sentences, and that the phrase a handful of trivial bookkeeping conditions is hiding something. I can handle technical detail; I know what a representation theorem is and I am not frightened of notation. What I am missing are the in-house distinctions.

So what I would like is a sentence-by-sentence audit of the summary above. For each of his claims, tell me whether a working decision theorist would accept it as stated, wince at it as a defensible simplification, or reject it outright; where he has run two distinct claims together, pull them apart and say which one is true; where he has taken a modest representational result for a normative thesis, or the reverse, say so explicitly. I would like to know both what a defender of the coherence program would say in his own defense — which of these sentences the program's own proponents would disavow as a caricature — and which of them are instead the standard objections pressed by critics rather than errors of exposition, since those two categories need different treatment. Then the part I care about most: if the summary is corrected along the lines you suggest, does the conclusion that expected utility maximization is the uniquely rational policy still follow from what remains, or does it require an extra premise that my friend never supplies? I do not need reading lists, citations, or names of papers — I would much rather have the shape of the argument and the exact joints at which it can be pulled apart.
