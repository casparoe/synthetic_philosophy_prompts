I am a software engineer with no training in philosophy, and I have been drafted into writing up an adversarial collaboration that has been running for months between two colleagues of mine. The subject is the tool I keep tripping over whenever I try to read philosophy: the thought experiment. I want the document the two of them could actually both sign, precise enough that I can use it as a checklist the next time someone hands me a case and tells me the verdict is obvious.

The two positions, as they state them.

Position one, the Vindicator: a well-made thought experiment is evidence, not a rhetorical device. It puts a general claim up against a particular case; if the case is described accurately and the verdict on it is stable, the general claim has to accommodate it. Verdicts on cases are data about our concepts and about what is possible.

Position two, the Debunker: a thought experiment is a short story with an argumentative job. Whatever force it has comes from what the author chose to mention, the order of the sentences, the emotional colour of the words, and what the reader already believes. Perturb those and the verdict moves, which means the case tested nothing except the author's craft.

So that they would have something neither had seen before, I wrote a case myself. Ada has a question-answering implant. She asks it a factual question; it answers in her own inner voice, within a second, confidently. In ten years of daily use it has never been wrong, and she has checked its answers against the world many times. It was installed by a firm that will not say how it works: whether it consults a database, polls a hidden panel of people, or simply guesses and has been lucky, Ada cannot tell. The question is whether Ada knows what the implant tells her. I can turn four dials on the case: (1) the firm says the accuracy is by design, versus a credible rumour that every unit is simply lucky; (2) she has verified the record herself, versus she has only read the firm's reliability figures; (3) the question at stake is trivial, versus it decides whether she drives over a bridge; (4) the answering thing is described as a machine, as a stranger on a phone line, or as her own second voice.

I also proposed a procedure, and both of them irritatingly half-agreed with it, which is why I want it argued in writing rather than settled by who talks last. Treat a thought experiment like a specification. Identify the features the argument says are doing the work; then perturb everything the argument says is irrelevant — names, sensory detail, the valence of the wording, the order of presentation, whether the agent is a person or a machine, whether the stakes are high or low — and see whether the verdict survives. Call it invariance testing. A case whose verdict survives is doing work; a case whose verdict moves with the irrelevant features is a puppet. The hole I can see without help is that the specification may not determine the case: a description is finite, the imagined situation is not, and two readers may silently fill in different completions and then disagree about the verdict without disagreeing about anything real.

What I want.

1. A numbered list of claims both positions can sign, covering what a thought experiment is, what role it plays in an argument, what would count as one succeeding or failing, and what each side concedes about the failure modes the other names.
2. The residual disagreements, each stated twice, in the wording its own side would accept for itself, so I can tell a real disagreement from one that exists only because they are using a word differently.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect three. Whether invariance is necessary, sufficient, or neither for a case to count as evidence. Whether a verdict that moves with the stakes is a defect in the method or a discovery about the concept. And whether a stable verdict on a well-specified case can establish a general claim at all, or only refute one. For each crux, the argument or case both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction about how it comes out, recorded in advance.
4. A verdict on the Ada case: which dials are legitimate variation and which are loading, whether the case survives invariance testing, and what each side predicts about the answer.
5. A short section saying which parts of the dispute are verbal, and why.
6. A closing section on what neither position explains.

Constraints. No citations, no names of researchers, no reading lists; name the two positions by their content, not by anyone who holds them. If a canonical case is needed — the two trolley cases, the brain in a vat, the experience machine — describe it in your own words rather than pointing at a source. No agreed claim may be one the other side would read as a disguised concession, and no disagreement may be stated in a way its own side would call a caricature. Skip anything of the form that further work is needed. The document should be usable by someone who has never read the literature.
