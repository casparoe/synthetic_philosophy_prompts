I'm a second-year MA student in history and I've been living in the intake ledgers of a state psychiatric hospital for about eight months — the 1910s volumes, which is what my thesis is on — and something keeps happening in them that I don't have a word for, and I'm starting to think the word matters, because it changes what I think my thesis is actually about.

Here's the pattern, as concretely as I can put it. The admission page has a printed list of about eleven reasons for admission and the clerk circles one. The eleven don't cover what was actually going on in people's lives. So a woman whose husband has left and who has nowhere to sleep goes in under "destitution." A man who's fallen apart after a death in the family goes in under "intemperance," because there's a box for intemperance and no box for grief. That on its own is just ordinary bureaucratic crudeness, and a year ago I'd have shrugged at it. What gets me is the second half. A hundred years later the only surviving thing is the circle: the interview that might have explained it is gone, the clerk is gone, she's gone. And when people write about this hospital now — and this isn't just sloppy work, careful historians do it too — the "destitution" gets cited as a fact about her, and the argument downstream is about rates of poverty among institutionalized women. Nobody is lying. The record is being read exactly as it presents itself. I have two cases where a letter survived in a different collection and I can prove the circle was a misfile, and in those cases the numbers people have published are simply wrong, wrong in the direction of the form's own categories.

So what I'm circling isn't that archives are incomplete — everyone says that, and I can live with incomplete. It's that the archive has a positive capacity to convert: to take something it has no slot for, return the nearest thing it does have a slot for, and leave no trace of the conversion, so the misfiling later reads as a datum. Absence is honest, in a way; this is the opposite of absence, a false presence that looks like a fact. Is there a name for that? I know there's a whole literature on archives and power and I've read alot of it — silences, reading against the grain, all that — but the silence stuff is about what never got written down, and I'm looking at what did get written down, wrongly, and then got quoted as evidence. If it has a name, what is it, and what's the nearest neighbour that people mix it up with? If it doesn't, can you name it and define it so it's actually usable — I want to know what's inside it and what's outside it. In particular: is this different from just saying a source is biased or unreliable? Because bias I know how to handle, and this feels like something else — the source isn't distorting an answer, it's answering a question I didn't ask and can't recover. And does it still apply when there's no malice and no ideology, just a form with eleven boxes and a tired clerk? Half the people I try this on tell me it's basically Foucault, and I don't think it is, but I can't say why. Also — no need to send me a reading list or citations, I just want the concept itself and where its edges are.
