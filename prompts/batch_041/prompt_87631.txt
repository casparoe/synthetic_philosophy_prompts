I'm in a small reading group on the philosophy of pleasure and we hit an argument last week that I can't shake. A friend laid it out like this and at the time it looked airtight:

(1) For a psychological kind to be a genuine kind — for it to earn its place in an explanation — all its instances have to share some common feature.
(2) The things we call pleasures share no common feature. Think of the taste of a cold beer on a hot afternoon, the relief when a headache finally lifts, the click of finally getting a crossword answer, the warmth of a bath, the jolt of someone you love walking in the door. Describe each from the inside and there's just nothing that runs through them all — some are sensory, some are the removal of something bad, some are tangled up with beliefs and meanings, some are barely felt at all.
(3) So "pleasure" doesn't name a genuine kind; it's a grab-bag we lump together for other reasons.
(4) Hedonism makes pleasure the one and only intrinsic good.
(5) So hedonism isn't even false in an interesting way — its central term fails to pick out a kind, so it can't do any explanatory work.

I want to say something's off, because I'd rather not ditch hedonism on the strength of an argument I could reconstruct in my sleep, but every time I try to name the bad step I notice the same reasoning would also knock out "desire" (wanting a glass of water vs. wanting to be remembered after you die — what do those share?), "belief", and honestly "pain" too (burning, aching, stabbing — same problem). And if it takes those down it takes down way to much. The heterogenity in (2) really does seem right to me phenomenologically, so if I have to give up something I'd rather it weren't that. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine before I stared at it, and what does rejecting it cost me — i.e. what do I owe in exchange for keeping (2)?
