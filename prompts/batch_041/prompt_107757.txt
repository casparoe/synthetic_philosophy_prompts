For the better part of a year I have been reporting on the foundations of game theory — on the claim that solution concepts such as Nash equilibrium earn their keep because they follow from assumptions about what the players know. I have interviewed the people who built that program and the people who regard it as a house of cards, and I have hours of tape in which each side is answering a question the other side never asked. I would like to write the document they should have written together.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two positions.

The first is the foundationalist: each player is rational, knows that the others are rational, knows that they know, and so on — and this common knowledge of rationality, together with the structure of the game, is what licenses the equilibrium analysis. On this view the epistemic theorems are the real justification of the solution concepts; the informal "everyone plays the equilibrium because it is the equilibrium" is replaced by explicit premises about knowledge and belief.

The second is the skeptic: the epistemic conditions are either empty or incoherent. Either they presuppose the very equilibrium they are meant to justify, so the theorems are circular, or they cannot consistently hold in the games where the program needs them, so they describe a situation that never obtains. On the skeptic's view the program changes no one's predictions and settles nothing.

What I want from you:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what the formalism says, about what the theorems do and do not establish, about what a solution concept is for, and about what the players are assumed to know at each level. These should be substantive; I do not want a list of platitudes that any economist would sign.

2. A disagreements table. One column per side, each cell phrased in wording its own side would accept as its view — no straw men, and no formulations so hedged that the disagreement evaporates. For each row: the question at issue, the two positions, and what each side would count as settling it.

3. The cruxes, which I expect to include: (a) whether the "rationality" in the common-knowledge condition means that each player's actual choice is a best response, or only that each player would best-respond to their beliefs — and if the latter, whether the condition constrains anything at all, given how little it takes for an act to be a best response to some belief; (b) whether deriving equilibrium from epistemic premises is circular; (c) whether the program's claims are conceptual or empirical, and therefore whether anything could count against them; and (d) the finite-horizon case, where the common-knowledge premises sit awkwardly with backward induction. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If a crux is one where a side holds that no evidence could bear on it — because the claim is conceptual — say so plainly rather than inventing a test.

4. A short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, and why. I suspect at least some of it turns on two senses of "rational," or on what "knowledge" is being asked to do, but tell me where the line falls.

I am not looking for a reading list; I want the arguments and the formulations themselves. And I would rather have the disagreement stated exactly than papered over — the point of the exercise is a document both sides could put their names to, not a compromise.
