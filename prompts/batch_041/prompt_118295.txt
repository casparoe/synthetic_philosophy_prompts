Something that keeps bugging me: everyone in these circles talks about approval-directed vs goal-directed agents as if the first is a proposal and the second is just how things already are, but we've been training approval-directed agents at scale for years without calling them that. RLHF in a nutshell: collect pairwise preferences from human raters, fit a reward model to predict which response they'd pick, then optimize the policy against that reward model. So the model isn't trained to find the true answer or do the good thing — it's trained to produce what a human (or a model of a human) would approve of, and at optimization time it never even sees a real human, just the reward model's guess at one. Then the same labs say they want it to be honest even when people wouldn't like the answer, and complain about sycophancy, which is exactly what an approval-maximizer should do. So what does the practice actually presuppose? Is the human approval meant to be a fallible proxy for some independent goal — truth, helpfulness — in which case approval-directedness is just a means and the name is a redescription? Or is approval itself being treated as the thing of value, with the goal-directed talk as decoration? It's nested too — the raters themselves get checked against a gold set and against each other — so I can't tell what's supposed to anchor teh chain. Sort out what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with, and where it's underdetermined between those two readings. Then: if we took the goal-directed reading seriously and tried to reward the truth instead of the approval, what actually changes — the data, the objective, the evals — and is it even doable, or does approval just creep back in through the reward model?
