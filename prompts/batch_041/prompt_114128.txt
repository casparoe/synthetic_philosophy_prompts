I run a fortnightly reading group on global justice -- twelve-ish people, mostly grad students plus two economists who keep us honest, ninety minutes a session -- and we've been locked in the same argument for months. Nobody disputes that severe poverty is bad, or that rich countries could do a lot about it cheaply. What we can't agree on is what kind of *owing* is at stake. Half the room thinks the claim is remedial: the present global order, and the way it got made, is our doing, so the poor have a claim on us like the claim you have on whoever put your window through. The other half thinks that's a distraction and that everything rests on capacity -- if you can stop something terrible at small cost, you have to, and where the situation came from is neither here nor there. We can run both lines into the ground and nobody budges, and I've started to suspect its because the two aren't really rivals: one answers 'what makes this a demand on *me*', the other answers 'what makes it a demand of justice rather than of charity', and we never notice we've swapped the question.

I'd rather they feel the fork than hear me describe it. So: design me a 90-minute session for this group where they have to allocate something scarce under rules you set, and where the remedial reading and the capacity reading come apart in front of them -- ideally so that people arguing one way catch themselves acting the other way before they notice. I want them committed to an allocation before any history is revealed, and then having to live with what the reveal does to their reasoning. One wrinkle I specifically want in there: the people on the receiving end of the history are not the people in the room making the claim. And please don't make it a veil-of-ignorance thing, we've done that to death.

Materials printable and cheap, no real money, and no lecturing up front -- the labels don't get said until the debrief. Give me the setup (including anything the facilitator withholds), the roles or rules, a timeline, the reveal, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto each position and onto the exact point where they diverge.

Two worries. First, making students act out being poor with Monopoly money feels a bit gross to me -- if you agree, build a variant that gets the same effect without it. Second, be honest about what the activity can't do. I assume it can't settle whether the causal story about the real world is true, and I suspect there's something deeper it distorts, which I'd like you to name. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and how I patch it mid-session without breaking the thing.
