For the past three years I have directed a small community archive — Riversend, named for a working-class neighborhood that was cleared in the 1960s to make way for an interstate spur. We open to the public next month and I have to give the opening remarks. The room will be mixed and, frankly, at cross purposes: the donors are former residents and their descendants, several of whom gave us materials on the understanding that this would finally set the record straight; the city's historic-preservation officer will be there and would like to hear that the city is at last doing right by the neighborhood; and our principal funder, a foundation that supports what it calls community-memory projects, is expecting a warm story about resilience and recovery that it can reproduce in its annual report.

My difficulty is not merely diplomatic. The surviving record is badly lopsided, and in exactly the pattern one would predict. The demolition itself is documented exhaustively — condemnation surveys, permit applications, police incident logs, the highway authority's own photographs — while almost everything in the residents' own hand is fragmentary: a shoebox of unlabeled photographs, a church membership ledger with a nine-year gap, four hours of cassette tape on which the recorder failed partway through, and a set of oral histories I conducted myself, which are vivid and which I have come to suspect are shaped by what the interviewees believed I wanted to hear. I do not want to give a speech that treats the archive as a vessel now filled, or that claims we are giving voice to people who in fact spoke and were recorded only in the categories their adversaries used. I want the thought that an archive is an instrument of power — that its silences are as legible as its holdings, and that the shape of what is missing is itself a finding — to do the structural work of the remarks, not to be a sentiment mentioned in passing. That means the speech has to hold two things at once: enough of a story that the donors feel their materials were honored and the funder is not insulted, and enough honesty about the gaps that nobody leaves believing the record is complete.

Please write the remarks, roughly six to eight minutes read aloud, in a register suitable for a public opening — plain enough for the donors, composed enough for the officials. Then, on a separate page for me alone, tell me how confident you are that the speech strikes that balance and name the single moment in it you are least sure of, with your reason.
