I'm running a session for a reading group that has spent the term on longtermism, and I've claimed Watchmen for it — Moore and Gibbons' graphic novel rather than the film, since the theatrical cut drops the pirate comic that runs alongside the main story and I want the group working from the book. The claim I'd like them to hold the story against is the hinge of history hypothesis: the suggestion that we may be living through a window whose influence on the long-run future is out of all proportion to its length, a short stretch in which the whole trajectory of civilization could be redirected, or locked in, for better or worse, in a way that ordinary decades cannot be. Adrian Veidt believes something of this kind about his own moment, and treats the belief as a licence: he engineers a single atrocity on the assumption that one event can arrest the slide toward nuclear war and settle the peace that follows. I should say at the outset that I am not interested in relitigating whether he was right; the arithmetic of the New York dead is a different seminar. What I want is a reading that is fair both to the hypothesis and to the book.

What I'd like worked out is whether Watchmen dramatizes the hypothesis or diagnoses it. Veidt is the book's hinge-maker, and the confidence with which he acts is itself a datum: are we watching a real possibility being seized, or a fantasy of mattering being indulged, and does the book give us any way to tell the difference from the inside? Doctor Manhattan is the obvious counterweight — the one figure who perceives all his moments at once, which is roughly the vantage point from which the question of whether this is a hinge would have a determinate answer — and his detachment seems designed to drain the urgency out of Veidt's calculation; I want to know what the book is doing with him, and whether that detachment is the book's own verdict or a temptation it is diagnosing. The formal machinery pushes in the same direction: the clock-and-watch imagery that runs through the book, the pirate comic nested inside the main narrative, the narrators who each see only a fragment of what is happening. And I'd like you to press on one component of the hypothesis in particular, namely lock-in — a hinge is supposed to be a moment whose outcome stays decided. Does the ending honour that? Rorschach's journal, mailed to a fringe newspaper and lying unread in a pile of submissions, reopens what looked settled, and I genuinely cannot decide whether that refutes the hypothesis (there are no hinges, only contingencies that keep unravelling) or confirms it in the most uncomfortable way (the hinge was real, and it now hangs on whether one editor opens his mail).

One further thread I'd like in the mix: an objection to the hypothesis that I find difficult to state precisely — that absent special information one should expect to be living in an ordinary stretch of history rather than the decisive one — and whether a book containing a man who is never anywhere in particular in time has any purchase on it. Where the story resists the reading, say so plainly rather than forcing the hypothesis onto scenes that will not bear it, and finish by saying what the graphic novel contributes to this debate that an ordinary philosophical argument about the hinge of history cannot.
