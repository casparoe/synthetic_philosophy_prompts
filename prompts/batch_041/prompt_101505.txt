I'm working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics for a seminar and I've hit a wall at the passage in Book III where he says the good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view...of the Universe, than the good of any other, unless there are special grounds for believing more good is likely in one case than the other. I get the words but not the machinery. Where does the "point of view of the Universe" actually come from — is it supposed to be a standpoint a person can occupy, or just a way of stating the impartiality he's already argued for in the axioms, or something else? And how does he get from that claim to an obligation on me? It reads like a description of a perspective, not a demand, but he clearly wants it to do demanding work.

The reason this is bugging me is the demandingness objection — if the impartial view is really binding, my ordinary attachments and projects start to look like a colossal moral mistake, and Sidgwick doesn't seem to draw that conclusion. He seems to think common-sense morality is roughly okay. Is that just a concession to human nature, or does he think the universal point of view doesn't actually require that much of you? I keep circling a distinction I can't quite pin down: a standard that tells you to give up your projects versus one that tells you the projects were never yours to weigh in the first place. The second feels like the darker one, and I can't tell which Sidgwick's is. Help me untangle this.
