I am a high school student writing a term essay for an interdisciplinary humanities course on order and freedom, and I would like help working out a reading of William Golding's *Lord of the Flies* through the tight-versus-loose framework in cultural psychology. My teacher's handout summarized that framework roughly as follows: societies under chronic threat — disease, famine, war, natural disaster — tend to become tight, meaning strong norms, low tolerance for deviance, and heavy enforcement, whereas societies with more material slack can afford to be loose, tolerating and even rewarding deviation. The handout also made two further claims: that tightness is functional, a way of coordinating people under pressure, and that tight societies require some licensed outlet for deviance, since otherwise the accumulated pressure has nowhere to go.

I would like you to read the novel through that framework and say what it shows, what it complicates, and where it resists the framework altogether. Specifically: is the boys' tightening a response to a genuine threat or to the beast they invented, and does that distinction matter for whether tightness deserves to be called functional? Which regime is actually tight in the relevant sense — Ralph's assembly, which keeps insisting on rules nobody follows, or Jack's tribe, which secures conformity through ritual, face paint, and the distribution of meat? Is there a difference between a society held together because its members share the norms and one held together because the cost of deviating is high, and does the novel care about that difference? The night-time dance in which the boys kill Simon looks like exactly the licensed outlet the framework predicts; if so, why does the pressure discharge into murder rather than into something harmless? And does the ending, with the naval officer arriving embarrassed on the beach while his warship waits offshore, vindicate the tight adult order or expose it as the same game at a larger scale?

Finally, I want the essay to reach the normative question the course keeps circling: how much rule-following should a society demand? Does the novel offer a usable answer, or only a diagnosis? I am not asking for quotations, since I have the book and can locate those myself; I want the argument, and I would prefer it not to settle into the standard civilization-versus-savagery reading, which I could write without thinking. What I really want to know is what the novel contributes to this debate that the cultural-psychology argument on its own does not.
