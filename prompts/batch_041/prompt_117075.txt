I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book where my protagonist has to make one decision that the entire last act hangs on, and I'm stuck, because every version of the scene I write is rigged. I can feel myself stacking the deck so she does what I already think is right, and readers smell that a mile off. So I want to hand the construction over to you.

You're a philosopher who works on decision-making when you're not sure which moral theory is true. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming three or four candidate scenarios — say out loud which ones you're throwing out and why, especially the ones that only work if you stack the deck — then give me the one that survives.

The rules I want the case to run on. Please use exactly these and dont swap in whatever you remember from the literature, because I need the case to be checkable by me:

- Rule 1, "add up the scores." Every theory you take seriously gives each option a score. You multiply each theory's score by how confident you are in that theory, and pick the highest total. This assumes the theories' scores can be put on a common scale.
- Rule 2, "worst case." For each option, look at the worst score it gets from any of the theories you take seriously, and pick the option whose worst score is highest. Credences don't enter at all.
- Rule 3, "back your best guess." Just do what the theory you're most confident in says to do.

Here's the shape I need. Two options, A and B, where A is the ruthless one — the thing my character would be ashamed of and would have to live with — and B is the decent, cautious one. Rule 1 has to come out for A. Rule 2 has to ALSO come out for A, and that's the part I actually care about, because I keep reading that rule 2 is the timid rule and rule 1 is the one that lets big stakes bully you into being ruthless. Rule 3 has to come out for B.

And here's the constraint that makes it hard: the theory she's most confident in has to be an ordinary, humane, recognisable outlook — something like "there are things you don't do to people, whatever the arithmetic says." I don't want rule 3 dissenting because she's 70% confident in some monstrous view. She should be 70% confident in something I'd be happy for my own sister to hold.

Constraints on the scenario itself: contemporary and realistic, one paragraph, a situation a person with a job and a mortgage could actually walk into. No trolleys, no ticking bombs, no transplant surgeons, no organ lotteries, no perfect predictors, no science fiction, and stay away from the four or five cases that get used in every ethics class — if I've seen it before, it's no good to me. And don't make the magnitudes cartoonish. I want to be able to defend every number out loud without sounding like I'm cheating, because she's going to have to defend them.

Then walk me through it:

- the verdict each of the three rules gives and exactly why, in the arithmetic
- which single feature of the case is doing the work — the one thing that, if I changed it, the whole thing collapses
- the smallest change that flips rule 2 over to B, and whether rule 2's answer here depends on it looking at the scores rather than at how the theories rank the options
- how someone who favours rule 3 would attack the case, and whether the attack lands
- your honest read: is this doing real philosophical work, or have I just quietly picked a scale that gives me the answer I wanted? If it's the second, say so — I'd rather know now than after the book is out.

Format: open with the bottom line in your first sentence — the case in a nutshell and who wins — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me.
