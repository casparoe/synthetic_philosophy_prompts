I teach the second half of a graduate seminar on realism and anti-realism, and I am now assembling the take-home final. The unit I care most about is the paradox of knowability, but I have grown dissatisfied with how it gets assessed: my students can reproduce the derivation from memory—the allegedly unknown truth, the appeal to the knowability principle, the two conjuncts that fall out of it—and then recite a stock list of responses without any grasp of which assumption is doing the real work. What I want this year is a question that forces them to reason rather than pattern-match to a familiar result, and that is answerable entirely from the apparatus they already have, since the exam is closed-book and they will not have any reference material at hand.

The question should first ask them to state the knowability principle and to reconstruct the derivation in their own words, naming every inferential step, with explicit attention to the two features of knowledge on which the argument turns—that knowledge is factive, and that it distributes over conjunction. It should then push in two directions. First, it should make them determine whether knowledge is even the relevant feature, by asking them to characterize precisely an operator that is factive but does not distribute over conjunction, and one that distributes but is not factive, and to say what becomes of the paradox in each case; the point is that they should be able to isolate exactly which property produces the collapse. Second, it should make them assess a restriction I plan to attribute to a colleague I was arguing with over lunch: she proposes keeping the knowability principle but confining it to truths that make no claim, even implicitly, about what anyone does or does not know—truths she calls unassuming truths. The students should say whether that restriction blocks the derivation, whether it is independently motivated or merely ad hoc, and whether an anti-realist who accepts it is left with a principle strong enough to do the epistemological work that anti-realism actually needs it to do.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. Build the scheme around five must-hit points worth a total of 20 marks, and give, for each point, a one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost a student marks there. Assume the students command the standard machinery—modal logic, the notion of a factive operator—but have not encountered this particular framing.
