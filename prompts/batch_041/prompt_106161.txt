I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized insurance company and I've somehow ended up chairing our fortnightly conformance review, which is the meeting where we decide what to do when two teams have built two different behaviours against the same bit of our pricing spec. Usually it's fine — one of them is just wrong and we point at the test. But the cases that eat the meeting are the ones where the disputed input is something the spec never mentions and no test ever covered, so both implementations agree with every single thing we've ever checked and disagree only on the new case. It feels like there's no fact of the matter about which one is "really" following the spec, and yet we still have to ship one of them. A colleague who did a philosophy degree told me this is a famous puzzle about rule-following, something about a bloke who's only ever added numbers smaller than fifty and then gets asked to add bigger ones, and whether anything about him settles which rule he was following the whole time. I tried reading a book by Saul Kripke he lent me and it turned into scepticism and community agreement and assertibility and I genuinely cannot work out what I'm meant to do at 3pm on a Wednesday. I should say I have no philosophy background at all — I can just about follow an argument if it's laid out flat.

What I'd like from you is a procedure my review can actually run, not an essay. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure is allowed to stop with a flat "no, we don't ship that", and — this bit matters most — where it has to hand the decision to a person and say "this isn't a question about what the rule means, it's a question about what we want, and someone senior has to own it". Keep it runnable in a 45-minute meeting by people who aren't philosophers, and be honest about which steps are real reasoning and which are just us dressing up a vote as a method. The head of engineering and one product owner have to sign off, and we can't rewrite the spec mid-review, so the procedure has to work with the spec as it is. Also worth knowing: we now have one team in Leeds and one in Bangalore, and they don't always share the same gut feelings about what a word in the spec obviously means, so if your procedure leans on "everyone agrees on the normal case" you need to say what happens when they don't. And our spec has a house clause that says the implementation is authoritative wherever the spec is silent, which I suspect is doing a lot of quiet work, so please say what your procedure does with that.

Then please run it on three cases, and I want the verdicts argued, not just asserted. (1) The spec says round to the nearest penny; nobody ever noticed that it never says what to do when the amount lands exactly on a half-penny, and now the two engines disagree on ties. (2) The spec grants a "30 day grace period" and never says calendar or business days; both implementations passed everything because until now no payment ever landed near a weekend, and they now diverge by up to two days. (3) A customer was underpaid for about a year because of a divergence nobody had thought to test, both behaviours were defensible against the old spec, and now we're deciding whether to refund everyone retroactively. I'd like at least one of those verdicts to be one I won't like, and I'd like you to tell me plainly what the procedure can't settle — the cases where it just outputs "argue about it and then pick" — and name the position you're translating, in one line, so I can look it up without you having to teach me the whole thing.
