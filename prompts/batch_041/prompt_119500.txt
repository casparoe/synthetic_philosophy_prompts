I am a high-school student in a philosophy of science seminar, and I am trying to predict our instructor's ruling before she gives it. Her verdicts so far, on a series of imaginary worlds: in a world whose fundamental generalization is simple, exceptionless, and demanded by the natures of the properties it relates, she says there is a law; in a world where an exceptionless generalization holds only in a highly disjunctive, gerrymandered form, say about objects of one particular size and material, she says there is no law, exceptions or none; a generalization entailed by the laws is not itself a law but a consequence of them; and a simple, exceptionless regularity confined to one small region of space and time is not a law. Next week's world contains a simple, exceptionless, fundamental generalization that is a brute fact: nothing in the natures of the properties requires it, and nothing deeper explains it. Tell me her verdict, the rule she is running, whether it is in the spirit of a Humean best-system analysis or an anti-Humean governing or necessitation view or something else, at least one rival rule that fits all four earlier verdicts just as well, what each rule predicts about the new case, and which further case would separate them.
