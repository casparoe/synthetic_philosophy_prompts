A recurring dispute in the philosophy of cognitive science concerns whether results from computational complexity theory can explain the limits of the human mind. One side treats a hardness result — the task is NP-hard, say — as an explanation: cognition must approximate, and the heuristics we observe are the mark of a mind working around an intractable problem. The other side holds that such a result explains nothing, because worst-case asymptotic complexity is a fact about an abstract model as input size grows, not about the small, finite, specially structured instances an organism actually confronts, so a hardness theorem cannot tell us why a particular cognitive limitation exists. Both sides accept the same theorems, and neither has conceded anything in a long time.

My hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about mathematics but about what an explanation of a cognitive capacity must be: whether an intractability proof is the right sort of thing to cite at all, or whether it is a definition of a formal model masquerading as an empirical claim about minds — and, relatedly, whether citing a complexity class can avoid circularity, given that such classes are defined relative to a machine model and a cost measure, so using one to explain the mind presupposes an answer to how the mind computes. Diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicating it. Is my hypothesis right, or is a hidden empirical question — what problems the brain actually solves, over what distribution of instances, with what resources — doing the work while one side quietly assumes its answer? Are the two sides answering different questions, what any bounded agent can do versus why this particular mind fails here, under a single heading? And on your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a change of formal tools (average-case or parameterized complexity, a different machine model), an empirical result, a conceptual distinction — or nothing, because the question was ill-posed from the start.
