A puzzle in epistemology that I would like treated as a specification problem rather than solved outright. An investigator has come, by ordinary and careful means, to know that the butler poisoned the earl. She can also see that if the butler did it, then any evidence that later appears to exonerate him must be misleading, since knowledge is factive and there cannot be genuine evidence that he did not do it. Combining the two, she now knows in advance that whatever exculpatory material turns up will be misleading, and may therefore set it aside when it arrives — a letter placing the butler in another city, say — without troubling to read it. Every step is one we are inclined to accept (knowledge entails truth; knowledge is closed under known entailment; one need not treat as a reason evidence one already knows to be misleading), and the upshot is that knowledge licenses a standing refusal to reconsider. Variants raise the same trouble in other registers: the reasoning run on justified belief rather than knowledge; on a credence short of belief; on the deliverances of the investigator's own past inquiry, where she can no longer reconstruct what once convinced her; on a case where the apparently exculpatory item is a proof, or a peer's considered judgment, rather than a letter; and on the iterated case, in which she knows that she knows.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate treatment of this puzzle must deliver. I want the list of requirements — the cases it must get right; the verdicts it must be able to give (whether the investigator who ignores the letter is irrational, merely unwise, or fully rational yet criticizable in some other register, such as intellectual character or the norms of inquiry); the distinctions it must not collapse (between knowledge and certainty; between having reason to expect that some future evidence will be misleading and having reason to disregard whatever evidence in fact arrives; between being justified in believing and being reasonable in continuing to believe; between the license as grounded in knowledge and the agent's ability to tell at the time whether she knows); and the constraints it must not violate (factivity, closure, the possibility of losing knowledge, the possibility of knowing without certainty). Give the consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular camps, and say whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — if so, which requirement I should abandon and what abandoning it costs. Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate solutions, and score the main families of response briefly against the list. I am preparing a graduate seminar and want the standard of adequacy in hand before we read any proposals; there is no need to attribute views to named authors.
