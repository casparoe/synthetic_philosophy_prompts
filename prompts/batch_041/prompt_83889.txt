My friend Nadia has very firm opinions about who is and isn't weak-willed, and she's never read a word of philosophy. Her calls so far. (1) The snooze button: sets the alarm for 6, at 6 thinks "I should get up," hits snooze, hates herself. Weak-willed, obviously. (2) A guy who'd sworn off cigarettes, lit up eight days later, was disgusted with himself. Weak-willed. (3) Our housemate, who'd been saving for a deposit, thought it over for a month and decided she'd rather travel while she's young, spent the money, doesn't regret it. Not weak-willed — a change of mind isn't weakness. (4) A guy at work who at breakfast would have told you, sincerely, that he'd rather have the money than a new jacket, and by four in the afternoon was buying an absurd jacket while thinking "I really shouldn't," and never felt bad about it. Weak-willed. (5) A friend with a terminal diagnosis who spent his savings on one last trip, thought it was the right call, would do it again. Not weak-willed.

My brother, an econ postdoc, says she's just tracking hyperbolic discounting: you prefer the later thing from a distance and the sooner thing up close, and you'd flip back if you reset the clock — no judgment about it required. I think she's tracking something else — whether you act against what you, at that moment, think you should do. Every case above comes out the same either way, which is why i can't tell.

So, my cousin Priya. She has never once thought she ought to be asleep early; she thinks the health stuff is overblown and she's fine with it. But she's textbook on the discounting thing: ask her at 6pm whether she'd rather have a well-rested tomorrow or an hour of TV tonight and she'll sincerely say the rested tomorrow, and at half ten she's watching TV. No plan broken, no guilt, no judgment against it, just the flip.

Does Nadia call her weak-willed?
