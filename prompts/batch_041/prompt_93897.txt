Write an essay on the following controversy: whether the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias can be drawn in a principled, non-question-begging way — and whether it deserves the normative weight we routinely place on it.

Begin by taking seriously a structural point borrowed from statistics. There, "bias" is not a property an estimate has in the abstract; it is defined relative to a target, the quantity the estimate is trying to capture. An estimator is biased only relative to what it aims at. Suppose the same holds in morals: calling an attitude a "bias" presupposes a target — the moral truth, the agent's own deeper or "real" values, the output of some procedure, or the verdict of an ideal observer. The interesting difficulty is that every candidate target seems either unavailable or circular. Identify the target with the agent's deeper values and almost any attitude can be described as either surface or depth, depending on where the partition is drawn. Identify it with the moral truth and you appear to have assumed the very verdict in dispute. Identify it with a procedure and you owe an account of why the procedure is not simply a codification of your antecedent sympathies.

In the essay, work through all of the following, in whatever order makes for the strongest argument:

1. Say precisely what the preference/bias distinction would have to accomplish in order to do real work — what it lets us say or do that a bare disagreement about the merits does not.

2. Make the best case that the distinction collapses: that "bias" in morals is a rhetorical label for attitudes we reject, and that the etiological facts usually offered as evidence of bias — socialization, adaptation to deprivation, motivated reasoning, raw affective reaction — are irrelevant to whether an attitude is justified. A genetic story about where a conviction came from seems to leave its merits untouched.

3. Make the best case that the distinction survives: that some attitudes are defective as attitudes, not merely as conclusions — for example by being insensitive to the considerations that would ordinarily count for or against them, by being held only because revising them is costly, or by failing the sort of counterfactual endorsement that separates a commitment from a compulsion.

4. Propose and defend at least one concrete test for sorting preferences from biases. You may invent it; give it a name and a formulation precise enough to apply mechanically. Then run it on two hard cases of your own construction: (a) an attitude with a plainly distorting etiology that nonetheless survives every reflective test, and (b) an attitude with an impeccable etiology that no consideration could dislodge. Report what your test says in each case, and whether the verdicts are costs you can absorb.

5. Address whether the distinction should carry normative weight at all. Does respecting a moral preference and correcting a moral bias require different justifications? Does the answer shift depending on whether the attitude concerns the agent's own good, the treatment of others, or the design of a shared institution?

Your essay must land somewhere. If your view is that the distinction is real only relative to a specified target, commit to which target is the right one, defend that choice, and state what it implies for your hard cases. If your view is that the distinction cannot be made good, say so plainly, explain what is lost by abandoning it, and say where the work it was doing should go instead. If your view is that the distinction is sound but narrower than we usually suppose, specify the narrow scope and defend the boundary. Do not close by listing the options and leaving the choice open, and do not let the conclusion dissolve into the observation that everything depends on the target. Take a position, give your reasons, and treat the opposing arguments as obstacles to be answered rather than alternatives to be catalogued.

Do not cite literature, name authors, or supply a reading list; a reader with no access to any library should be able to follow the whole argument. Argue from the ideas themselves. Roughly 1000–1500 words.
