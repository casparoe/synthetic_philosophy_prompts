I am a retired observational cosmologist. For thirty years I fit models to data, and I have lately been reading philosophy of science in an attempt to understand what I was actually doing. There is one practice from my former field that now strikes me as philosophically strange, and I would like you to reconstruct the commitments it presupposes.

The practice is this. When we estimate a parameter — a rate, a mass, a curvature, a proportionality constant — we routinely adopt a flat or uninformative prior over that parameter and then report the resulting posterior as a neutral, objective summary of what the data say. The flat prior is supposed to let the data speak. It is the default in much of the physical sciences, and it is often described as the expression of a principle of indifference: with no information favoring one value over another, we assign equal prior probability to equal ranges of the parameter.

The trouble is that the practice is not invariant under a change of parameterization. If I put a flat prior on a rate λ, and a colleague puts a flat prior on log λ, we will obtain different posteriors for the same physical quantity, even though we agree on the likelihood and the data. If we try to be indifferent over the predictions instead — choosing the prior that makes the prior predictive distribution as flat as possible — we get a third answer. The principle of indifference seems to underwrite the practice, but it gives no unique rule for which quantity to be indifferent over. I am told this is a version of something called Bertrand's paradox, though the version I know from probability textbooks concerns chords in a circle rather than parameters in a model. And yet the practice persists, and it is rarely treated as a scandal.

I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. In particular:

- What view of probability does it require? Does it commit us to a logical interpretation of probability, on which ignorance has a determinate numerical representation, or is it compatible with a thoroughgoing subjectivism on which any prior is permissible?
- What view of ignorance does it require? Does it presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the correct parameterization, or does it quietly help itself to a privileged description of the system under study?
- Which rival views does the practice rule out? For example, does it rule out a frequentist view that rejects priors altogether, or a subjectivist view that treats the flat prior as merely one arbitrary choice among many?
- Is the practice internally consistent? Or does it harbor a tension between its objectivist rhetoric and its parameterization-dependent mechanics?

Please also assess a proposed fix from a junior colleague. She suggests that instead of being indifferent over parameters, we should choose the prior that makes the prior predictive distribution as flat as possible, so that the model is indifferent over the observable outcomes it might encounter. Is that a coherent way to rescue the principle of indifference, or does it simply relocate the problem? If it relocates it, where exactly does the problem reappear? And would a prior designed to give the same answer regardless of how the parameter is expressed solve the difficulty, or merely trade it for another one?

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we adopted a consistently subjective Bayesian view, and what would have to change if we adopted a frequentist view that rejects priors altogether. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it.

After your analysis, give me a table with the following columns, one row per commitment, and no more than six rows. The first column names the commitment; the second says which feature of the practice reveals it; the third names a rival position that the practice, as practiced, excludes; the fourth says whether the commitment is strictly presupposed or merely convenient, and if convenient, what else would work; and the fifth says what would change in the practice if the commitment were replaced. Keep the table compact, and make the entries specific to the practice rather than generic.
