I sit in a reading group on corruption and clientelism, and there is one member who reaches for the same picture whenever the conversation turns normative. The picture is mechanical: a bureaucracy is an engine, its rules and procedures are the gearing, red tape is friction, and an informal payment is lubricant, a little grease that lets a seized transaction turn. What he does with it is argue that in a badly built engine grease is maintenance rather than sabotage; that officials who demand speed money are doing, at a small scale, what any sensible operator does with a machine that keeps jamming; and that to condemn the practice from the outside, by the standards of a well-oiled administration we happen to have inherited, is to mistake our engine for the only one that could be built. He is not naive about the costs, and I do not think the picture is worthless, but I have never been able to say precisely what work it is doing for him and what it is quietly doing to him, and I would like that laid out rather than gestured at.

So I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on corruption. Begin with the correspondence: tell me what plays the role of the engine, the gearing, the friction, the lubricant, the operator, and the specification of what the machine is for. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some of the breaks I can already half see — a lubricant is inert and indifferent, it does not choose which wheels to favour, it does not accumulate power over the machine, it does not expect the favour returned, and above all it does not manufacture the friction it relieves, whereas a payment is made by an agent with interests of its own, reshapes the incentives of the official who receives it, breeds expectations about future payments, and may be the very reason the obstruction was placed there in the first place, so the friction the picture treats as given may be produced in order to be sold. Others I suspect I am missing. One that interests me in particular: the picture needs a discrete, priced, transactional unit — a lubricant applied at a moment — and I am not sure that unit exists in the clientelist and gift-exchange settings we spend most of our time on, where what changes hands is embedded in an open-ended relation that creates obligations rather than discharging a debt. If the analogy presupposes a kind of act that the target phenomenon does not contain, that seems more damaging than any of the mechanical mismatches. I would also like to know which disanalogies actually bear on his conclusion and which are merely decorative.

Finish by telling me whether his tolerant conclusion — that informal payments can be efficiency-improving, and that condemning them outright is parochial — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole way. Two repairs have been floated in the group and I want them assessed: one is a toll booth whose collector installed the barrier himself, the other is a queue in which paying to jump alters both the composition of the queue and the waiting time of everyone still in it. Which of these does better than the engine, what does each still leave out, and is there a better picture? Finally, the awkward question: his relativist conclusion is supposed to undercut our confidence in judging other arrangements, yet it is reached through a picture of a machine with a determinate function and a single owner. Can the charge of parochialism be made without that picture, or does it need the very image it is meant to discredit?
