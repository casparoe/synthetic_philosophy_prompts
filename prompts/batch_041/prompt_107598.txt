I am writing the opening section of a seminar paper on the wrong kind of reasons, and I have been asked for a case rather than further commentary: the examples everyone reaches for — a demon who will torture you unless you admire him, a cheque for believing a proposition, a pill that manufactures the attitude — have been worked so hard that discussion stops at them. What I want is a new case, built to specification, that does the work those examples were meant to do: some consideration gives an ordinary person a genuine reason to have a particular attitude, and yet, intuitively, it does not make that attitude fitting, so that any analysis which identifies being valuable with being a fitting object of a pro-attitude is in difficulty. The case is for a section whose conclusion I have not settled — whether the right-kind restriction can be drawn from the content of the reason, as the object-given/state-given distinction supposes, or has to be drawn otherwise — so do not build the case towards a verdict. Build it, and then tell me which verdict it supports.

The specifications are meant to be satisfied jointly rather than one at a time. The attitude must not be belief, and not admiration of a person in exchange for a reward, since those are the stock cases; gratitude, loyalty, hope, trust, amusement, or resentment would suit me better, though the choice is yours if you can defend it. Nothing supernatural; no threats to life or limb; no payments, prizes, or penalties attached to the attitude — the reason-giving force has to arrive by some other route, and I would be more interested in a route through what having the attitude would express or reveal about the subject, through what it would mean for a relationship, or through what the subject is entitled to, than in one that runs through consequences. The reason must be a reason for the attitude itself and not merely a reason to want or to engineer that attitude in oneself, since a case that showed only the latter would leave the fitting-attitude analysis untouched. Keep the case to a single paragraph of ordinary prose, with no more than three people in it and no device that exists only because the story stipulates it. Then give me a companion case differing in exactly one respect, in which the same consideration is a reason of the right kind, so that the attitude is intuitively fitting rather than merely advantageous; if you judge that no single change can produce that flip, say so, and say whether that is because right-kindness is fixed by the content of the consideration rather than by anything about the situation, which would itself be a result worth having.

Finish with the audit. Set out the fitting-attitude schema you are working with precisely enough to be applied, and give its verdict on both cases, together with the verdict of the object-given/state-given criterion; say whether the two agree, and if they do not, which one the cases force you to give up. Then the honest part: name the single feature that is doing the work, and say what becomes of the case when it is removed; say whether the case smuggles in its verdict by stipulation rather than generating it from the theory; and give the strongest line of attack open to someone who insists that the reason in your first case is of the right kind after all — what they would say the fittingness of the attitude consists in, and what would settle the disagreement.
