I teach political philosophy and I've somehow ended up on a city committee thats rewriting the rules for our local elections, and the whole thing has me stuck on a question that isn't really about our city at all. Take it as settled that the math people have this one: Arrow showed you can't have a ranked system that's fair by every obvious standard at once, so any real rule has to trade one virtue for another. Fine. Now look at what everyone actually does. Cities and organizations keep adopting ranked-choice voting — you number the candidates, the last-place one gets knocked out, those ballots pass down to the next name, and this repeats until somebody has a majority — and they always sell it in the same language: now the winner will be the one most voters can live with, the one with real majority support, no more spoilers. But the rule doesn't do that. It can knock out the candidate who would beat every other candidate one-on-one, and it does so for a reason: the rule is built so that ranking someone lower can never hurt your favorite, and that very guarantee is provably incompatible with always electing the one-on-one champion. So the pitch and the mechanism pull in opposite directions, and nobody seems to mind much.

What I want from you, as someone who really knows social choice and the philosophy of democracy rather than just the formula: what does this practice presuppose about what a vote is for and what makes an outcome legitimate? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Is a vote a sincere expression of preference that shouldn't be penalized, or an instrument for producing the correct collective answer, or something else entirely? Which rival theories of legitimacy does the practice quietly rule out, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it simply underdetermined, so that several views would license the same rule? Then tell me what would have to change — in how the rule gets justified, or in what we'd have to value — if we took that presupposition seriously and swapped it for its opposite.

Start by brainstorming a few candidate readings of the practice before you commit to one, and think carefully about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what its cheerleaders happen to believe. Be honest and self-critical: tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong, and what argument or fact would make you change your verdict.
