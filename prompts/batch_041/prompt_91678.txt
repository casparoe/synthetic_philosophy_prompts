I have no training in philosophy or mathematics and have been thinking about this on my own, so I will set things out in ordinary words rather than the usual technical ones.

The principle of indifference is the rule that says: if you have no reason to think one possibility more likely than another, treat them as equally likely, and then simply count. Bertrand's paradox is the standard case in which the rule appears to break down. A chord is drawn at random in a circle, and the question is how likely it is to come out longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle. Whether you picture the chord as chosen by its two endpoints on the rim, by its distance from the centre, or by the point at its middle, you get a different answer to the same question, and each of the three pictures looks as sensible as the others. The usual conclusion drawn is that a phrase like at random is ambiguous here and that indifference is not really a principle. I am not convinced that the circle is where the trouble lies. It seems to me the disagreement may have nothing to do with geometry, or with the endless divisibility of a circle, and I would like to find out whether the same failure can be produced without either.

So please build me a case with the following features.

First, no geometry and no continuous magnitudes of any kind. Nothing measured by length, area, volume, weight, duration, concentration, or position on a dial. The possibilities must be finite in number, so that they could in principle be listed one at a time.

Second, it must be one situation described in two ways, not two different puzzles. There must be a single set of ways the world could be, and two ways of counting through it. Under one of those ways, the event whose probability is in dispute must be a single possibility; under the other, it must be several possibilities that have been lumped together. The two counts must give different probabilities for that same event.

Third, both ways of counting must look reasonable on their own. For each, you should be able to state the rule of counting in one sentence, and it must be a rule a person could adopt without already knowing what number it would produce. A way of counting that has obviously been contrived to yield a particular answer does not count.

Fourth, nothing in the setup may settle the question. No coin, die, shuffled deck, or other randomising device, and no symmetry or physical process in the story that would make one of the two ways of counting obviously the right one. Ignorance is all we are to have to go on.

Fifth, make it an ordinary situation with stakes a normal person could face — a drawer, a shelf, a bill, a bet with a friend — not an abstract puzzle about numbers.

Give me the scenario in a single paragraph, and then the analysis. In the analysis, do the following. State the disputed event, the two ways of counting, and the two probabilities, with the arithmetic sketched briefly enough that I can check it. Explain what makes each way of counting feel like the obvious thing to do. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — what is it about the setup that allows two counts to look natural? — and say what happens if that feature is removed, or if one further piece of information or one genuine symmetry is added: does the disagreement disappear, and why? Then tell me how someone who still wanted to defend the principle of indifference would try to rescue it here. They will want to say that one of the two ways of counting is illegitimate, so state the restriction they would need in a sentence, and then say honestly whether that restriction has any justification other than the fact that it delivers the answer they prefer, and whether the same restriction would also have to be applied in the circle case. Finally, tell me whether there is any answer a person should actually use in the situation you built, or whether the honest reply is that the question has no determinate answer until more is said — and what that implies about whether indifference is a principle at all, or merely a name for a family of rules that happen to agree in easy cases.

No reading suggestions or references, please; I want the case and the analysis of it.
