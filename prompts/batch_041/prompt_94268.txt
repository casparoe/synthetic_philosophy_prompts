I'm putting together a handout for a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology on the species problem, and I want a taxonomy rather than a survey: every branch labeled with the question that separates it from its siblings, so that a student who answers the questions lands on a position instead of on a label they have memorized. Build it from the logical space first, and only then place the positions in it: whether "species" names anything in nature at all; if it does, whether what it names are kinds or particular historical lineages; whether there is one correct criterion for drawing species boundaries or only a family of purpose-relative ones; and whether the species category is unified or a grab bag of unrelated things. At a minimum the leaves should include the biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept, the ecological and the morphological concepts, the view that there is one general species concept but many operational criteria for applying it, the thesis that species are individuals rather than kinds, the treatment of species as property-cluster kinds whose members are bound together by underlying mechanisms, the pluralism that says different concepts legitimately answer different questions, and outright eliminativism. Add one leaf for a position I suspect nobody has defended under a name: that the species category is a functional kind whose content is fixed by what conservation law and biodiversity accounting need it to do — say what else would have to be true for that to be a real position rather than a pun. Give one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. Mark the combinations your space leaves empty and diagnose each as incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and flag any leaves the instrument cannot separate, with a note on why. Eight to twelve leaves is right. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would explain it to a colleague on a walk: no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, and mark the forks with words rather than layout.
