Take the role of a philosopher of causation and work through the following carefully; before committing to an answer, lay out the candidate principles you can see and what each one is betting on. I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about causing something by not doing it, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. Here they are. A surgeon skips a standard step and the patient bleeds to death: the skipping caused the death. A parent doesn't feed her child and the child dies: the failure caused it. I forget to water a plant and it dies: that caused it, though it feels like a thinner kind of causing than the surgeon's. But a stranger who never gives to the charity that would have saved a child, or who walks past a pond without throwing the rope that was lying at hand: I want to say those didn't cause the deaths, or at least that they aren't causes in the same way. Here are the two cases that make me doubt the contrast I'm drawing. First, no one ever built the firebreak, and the wildfire runs straight across the line where it would have stood; I want to say the absence of the firebreak caused the town to burn, even though there is no agent here and nobody omitted anything. Second, a doctor who follows every guideline to the letter but declines an unconventional treatment that would in fact have saved the patient; I'm inclined to say that didn't cause the death, even though it has the same shape as the surgeon's case: not doing the thing that would have helped. Is there a single principle that delivers all of these, or do I have to give one up? I keep suspecting that three different things are doing the work here -- that a duty or norm was violated, that the outcome counterfactually depended on the missing act, and that there was an agent who could have acted -- and my cases seem to pull them apart. Please state the principle or principles precisely, show me which of my verdicts each one keeps and which it has to overturn, say what it predicts for a case I haven't given, and be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on causation.
