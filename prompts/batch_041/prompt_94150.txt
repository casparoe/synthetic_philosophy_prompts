I write about philosophy for a serious general readership, and I'm drafting a staged dialogue for a long-form piece. It isn't a transcript of anything real, but I want it rigorous enough that specialists on both sides would recognize the speakers as formidable. The subject is the Frege–Geach problem, though I don't want the conversation to settle into the familiar recital of the embedding objection. My two speakers are an expressivist, who holds that moral sentences express non-cognitive attitudes rather than describe mind-independent facts, and a cognitivist, who holds that such sentences are truth-apt. Make them equals: neither is a fool or a foil, each responds to the other's actual move rather than talking past it, and neither is permitted to win by caricature.

I want the exchange to move past the textbook examples into the harder territory downstream of them, above all the problem of negation. If "lying is wrong" expresses one attitude, what precisely does "lying is not wrong" express, and in what sense are the two contradictories rather than merely different attitudes? Have the cognitivist press the cases that a first-pass expressivist answer will struggle with: a speaker who is entirely indifferent to lying and so neither approves nor disapproves; a speaker who is ambivalent, feeling both attitudes at once; and compound constructions such as "If lying is wrong, then asking my brother to lie is wrong," where the moral clause sits in the antecedent and seems to be doing something other than expressing a present attitude. Add at least one embedding that is less often rehearsed — a moral sentence under a probability operator, say, or under an attitude verb — and make both of them say what work the embedded clause is doing there.

Then let the expressivist turn the tables: the cognitivist owes an account of why moral conviction moves people to act, and of what moral disagreement is disagreement about. Require each speaker to make at least one genuine, unforced concession, and let the dialogue end without a tidy verdict; I would rather the reader see exactly where the pressure lies than be handed a winner. Have them argue in their own voices, without invoking named philosophers, citing literature, or borrowing authority from anyone else's published formulation — the arguments themselves are what I need, not a bibliography.
