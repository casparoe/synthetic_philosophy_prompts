Hey — I'm putting together a little satire zine for our early-modern reading group and i need one more piece for it. The pitch: a fake peer-review packet for a journal that doesn't exist, reviewing a manuscript that claims to have squared the circle and then, using the very same geometrical method, proves that sovereign power has to be absolute and indivisible. It's Hobbes-shaped on purpose. He kept announcing he'd squared the circle and blamed the mathematicians rather than the proofs, and he really did think the thing that makes geometry certain could do the same job in politics. So I want a referee's report plus the editor's decision letter, deadpan throughout, 700-900 words total, no winking at the reader.

The humour has to come from the structure of the position, not from "lol old man bad at maths". Specifically:

- The referee should be competent and scrupulous, not sneering. He should put his finger on where the construction actually fails -- something like the proof quietly assuming the quantity it's supposed to produce, or showing a figure is very close to a square and then sliding from close to equal.
- He should know the thing is provably impossible (the 19th-century result about pi), and be fair enough to concede the author couldn't have known that.
- The kill shot is in the political half. There the author's definitions are stipulations he laid down himself, so every demonstration is really a conditional -- grant me that sovereignty means such-and-such and lo, sovereignty turns out to be such-and-such. The referee should be able to say this without gloating.
- The author's own account of why he's been ignored -- envy, university politics, the awkwardness of his conclusions -- should turn up in the report as something asserted rather than demonstrated, which is funny given what's at stake.
- But the one thing the referee should concede sincerely is that demanding a standard of demonstration in politics isn't silly, and that there's real pull in the idea that we can only demonstrate what we make ourselves: geometry because we draw the figures, the commonwealth because we make it, whereas nature we merely find. End the report there, so a reader feels the position's attraction and not just its absurdity.

The editor's letter should be wearily polite, reject the geometry, and say something about the political corollaries that lands the absurdity of treating political conclusions as theorems without ever announcing that that's the joke.

Two rules. Nobody quotes real published works -- any quotations come from the fictional manuscript itself, and don't try to reconstruct Hobbes's or Wallis's actual sentences from memory, just invent the manuscript's wording freely. And the target is the position, not the man, so no cheap shots about his personality or his politics. Give the referee a name and a title and give the journal a name. Should be readable by someone who's never read Leviathan, but funnier if they have.
