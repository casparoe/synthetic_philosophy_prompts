I recently began writing a short weekly newsletter in which I recommend films and dishes, and two reader responses have left me unsure how much of my authority as a critic is epistemically earned. One reader says she now knows that a particular film is beautiful purely on my say-so; another says that after my remark about a painting's lopsided composition she cannot help but see the painting that way, and credits me with changing what she perceives rather than what she merely believes. The familiar standoff between optimism and pessimism about aesthetic testimony seems too coarse for these two cases.

Please distinguish, in your own words, between two things a critic might transmit: a bare verdict about an object's aesthetic value, and a way of attending to the object that may let the recipient have the relevant experience for herself — I'll call these verdict-transmission and route-transmission. Then set out the main positions one could take about aesthetic testimony, at least four, each named clearly, and rank them by how well each handles the case in which route-transmission succeeds but the recipient's resulting judgment still depends essentially on the critic's guidance.

Return a JSON object with these fields: "distinction" (a paragraph contrasting verdict- and route-transmission and saying which, if either, you think can carry aesthetic knowledge); "positions" (an array, one entry per position, each with "name", "tier" (1 = best), "score" (0-100), "route_case" (how it handles the route-transmission case), "cost" (what it must deny or bite the bullet on), and "worry" (one sentence on its weakest point)); "ranking_rationale" (a short paragraph on why the top and bottom positions land where they do); and "bottom_line" (one sentence on whether my readers can genuinely come to know what I claim on my authority).
