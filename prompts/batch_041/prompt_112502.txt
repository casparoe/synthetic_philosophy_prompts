OK so I need a dialogue for a reading group I'm running, and I want it to be about Aumann's agreement theorem. You know the one — if two people share a prior and it's common knowledge what credence each of them assigns to something, then their credences have to be the same; they can't "agree to disagree" while both being good Bayesians. Both my characters accept the math; the fight is over what it actually shows.

Setup: they've both just read the same short empirical paper and walked away with different credences, say 0.8 and 0.4, that the paper's main claim is true. They know each other's numbers, and each knows the other knows theirs, etc. — as common as common knowledge ever gets in a real conversation. Call them R and S.

R thinks this situation is basically impossible for two ideal Bayesians, so something has to give: one of them is reasoning badly, or they don't actually share a prior, and the theorem shows the disagreement has to be traceable to one of those. S thinks R has it backwards — the theorem is almost empty as an explanation, because the common prior is doing all the work and nobody ever has a real reason to think two actual people share one, so invoking the theorem just relabels the disagreement instead of explaining it.

I want them to really dig in. What does the common prior assumption even amount to — is it an empirical claim, a modeling choice, a regulative ideal? What would count as evidence that two agents share one? And the case I find most interesting: what happens when I'm not sure whether you're a good Bayesian at all — can I keep disagreeing with you while knowing exactly what you think, and if so doesn't that mean the theorem's teeth come from the rationality assumption rather than the common prior? I want R to have to deal with that, not dodge it. Also have them touch the betting intuition — that two people who really disagree should be able to find a bet they both like — and whether that adds anything or is just a restatement.

Keep them both sharp. R shouldn't be a dogmatist and S shouldn't be a relativist who thinks all priors are equally good. They should answer each other's actual moves, point by point. By the end a reader should have a clearer grip on what the theorem does and doesn't deliver, even if nobody wins. Around 1200 words, and don't sweat references, I'll add those myself.
