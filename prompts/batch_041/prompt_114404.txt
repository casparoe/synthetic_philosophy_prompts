I am a third-year PhD student in economics, and this term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of language that I joined for slightly disreputable reasons: I kept losing an argument about vagueness to a friend in the department and wanted to lose it more precisely. We have spent three weeks on the sorites paradox, and I have a reaction I would like you to engage with rather than talk me out of. On the board the paradox looks devastating; in my own field it looks inert. Economists draw lines through vague predicates for a living, and the drawing does not seem obviously arbitrary or embarrassing. The case I keep returning to is the dating of recessions. The rule of thumb everyone learns — two consecutive quarters of falling output — is not the definition, and the body that actually dates American business cycles, the Business Cycle Dating Committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research, does not use it. Its stated definition is "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months," and its practice is frankly discretionary: it weighs depth, diffusion and duration against one another, treats the three as partly substitutable, publishes no algorithm, decides retrospectively and months late, and revises its chronology when the underlying statistics are revised. The rule of thumb fails in both directions. The 2001 downturn is the standard example of a recession with no two consecutive quarters of negative growth, and the recession that began with a February 2020 peak and ended with an April 2020 trough lasted two months, the shortest in the chronology. Then, in the first half of 2022, output fell for two consecutive quarters and there was a loud public argument about whether a recession had begun; the committee never called one — its chronology still ends with the April 2020 trough — and the argument simply subsided, with no one able to say what the correct answer had been.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions rather than a summary of the paradox. First, in the direction my seminar trains me in: say what the sorites machinery actually does to "recession." What is the tolerance principle here, stated exactly — the premise that one more month of slightly lower activity cannot turn an expansion into a recession — and does the forced march from an unmistakable boom to an unmistakable slump go through as cleanly as it does for heaps and bald men, or does something in the case resist it? Take seriously that this predicate looks manufactured in a way that "heap" does not: the quantity being measured is a constructed and repeatedly revised index, resting on weighting and deflation choices, and the question "was there a recession?" is always asked for a purpose — to trigger policy, to assign blame, to date a narrative. Is "recession" vague, or context-sensitive, or interest-relative, or all three, and does the answer change what the paradox has to bite on? I would also like the higher-order question pressed, because the committee's definition is itself built out of vague terms, so the alleged precisification appears to be vague one level up as well.

Second, in the direction my seminar does not train me in, and which I suspect is the more interesting one: what does this episode do to the competing accounts of vagueness? If the committee's calls are treated as authoritative — if once the announcement is made it is simply settled, for practical purposes, that the 2001 downturn was a recession and the 2022 one was not — does that favor the view that there is no fact of the matter and that boundaries are drawn by decision, and if so, does the drawing being constrained, contested and revisable make that position easier or harder to hold? Or does the practice look more like discovering a boundary that was always there and merely hard to see? Or like degree-talk, on which recessions come in degrees and the committee's real job is to report whether some threshold was crossed, with the threshold fixed by the purposes at hand? I want the episode used as a test rather than an illustration: tell me which account it favors, which it embarrasses, and where all of them are simply silent — for instance, whether any of them has anything to say about who ought to be doing the precisifying, or about the fact that the committee decides after the fact and then revises, so that the boundary appears to move in time rather than to have been drawn once and for all. I do not need a survey of the literature or suggestions for further reading; I have the seminar for that. What I want is the mapping, done carefully, and an honest ledger at the end: what the sorites paradox explains about how we date recessions, what it misdescribes, and what the practice of dating recessions shows about the paradox.
