For a column I'm drafting on moral worth, I want to state my own position plainly before I commit to it in print, and I'd like you to check whether it holds together. Here is where I've landed: (1) an action has moral worth only when it is done from duty — the person who helps others out of natural sympathy does something admirable, but not, in the strict sense, morally worthy; (2) doing an action from duty requires that the very consideration that makes the act required be the reason the agent acts, not merely a reliable cause of her acting; (3) an agent need not be able to state or conceptualize that consideration — a steady sensitivity to it can be enough; (4) moral worth must be free of luck, since it cannot turn on a temperament or upbringing the agent never chose; and (5) when an act is overdetermined — when the agent would have done it from duty even had she felt no inclination to — the act has moral worth even though she also wanted to do it. Tell me which of these actually pull against each other and how badly, rank the tensions by severity, and say what would have to give in each case. I would also like to know which of them are only apparently in conflict.
