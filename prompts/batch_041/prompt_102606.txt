For an essay in my critical thinking course, I have been asked to set out my own position on conspiracy theories and then test whether it holds together, so what I want is an audit rather than an exposition. Here is what I believe.

(1) The phrase conspiracy theory does more rhetorical work than descriptive work; a hypothesis is not epistemically worse for the mere fact that it posits powerful people coordinating in secret. (2) Even so, the textbook cases — a flat earth, a faked moon landing — are irrational in a way I can recognise without weighing their evidence at all, because they are held so that nothing could ever count against them. (3) Whether a belief is justified depends only on the evidence available to the person holding it, so I should not call someone irrational for a conclusion that was reasonable given what they could know. (4) I defer to mainstream scientific institutions over any lone researcher, yet I do not think agreement among scientists is itself a reason to believe anything — the data, not the head-count, is what tells. (5) The honest way to answer a conspiracy theory is to take its particular claims seriously rather than to explain away the people who hold it.

Which of these can I keep together? Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and how badly, and which only look as though they do. For each real conflict, say what would have to be given up and what that would cost me, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. I am genuinely unsure which commitment I would abandon, so do not assume that one of them is obviously the weak link.
