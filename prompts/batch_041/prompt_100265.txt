I am setting the final examination for a graduate seminar in epistemology (the logic of belief, acceptance and credence), and I need one essay question for it, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The paper runs 90 minutes; this question carries 20 marks, and candidates are expected to write about 800 words on it. The examination is closed-book, so nothing may turn on candidates naming authors, dates or works, and the model answer should likewise not depend on citation.

The question must not be answerable by reproducing the lottery paradox and the paradox of the preface and then reciting the standard menu of replies to each. What I want tested is whether a candidate can say what the two cases share at the level of structure, whether the resemblance survives scrutiny, and where the pressure of the paradox actually falls. I should therefore like the question built around three cases. The first is the ordinary lottery: a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner, equally good evidence (0.999) for each ticket's losing, and certainty that at least one ticket wins. The second is the ordinary preface: an author who has checked each of 1,000 claims in her book against separate evidence, holds 0.999 in each of them and believes each, and who also believes, on the strength of her record of past error, that at least one claim in the book is false. The third case is my own construction, not something in the literature, and should be presented as such: it is the preface with the fallibility statement treated as one more belief, so that the author assigns 0.999 to "at least one of the claims in this book is false" and holds it alongside the rest.

The third case is the pivot, and the numbers are chosen deliberately. If the claims are evidentially independent, the author's credence that all 1,000 are true is about 0.37, so her credence that at least one is false is about 0.63 and cannot be 0.999; the model answer should run that calculation and should say what it presupposes. I want the question to require three things of a candidate: (a) to state the structure the first two cases share, naming the principles whose joint acceptance generates the paradox rather than merely observing that both turn on long conjunctions of probable claims; (b) to diagnose the third case arithmetically, and then to say whether that diagnosis dissolves the second case, and if not, why not, including whether the author could instead restore consistency by lowering her credence in each claim and what that would show about the scale of the risk being aggregated; and (c) to reach a verdict on whether these are one paradox or two, with an explicit criterion for the verdict, and to rank the available remedies, saying which would be given up last and why.

Please write (i) the question as it would appear on the paper, in three lettered parts whose marks sum to 20, phrased so that it stands on its own without any of my commentary; (ii) a model answer of about 800 words; and (iii) a marking scheme organised as five must-hit moves totalling 20 marks, each with the mark it carries, a note of which part of the question it belongs to, and a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs candidates that mark. The scheme should make the A/B boundary explicit by saying which combination of moves an A answer contains that a B answer typically lacks.

Finally, the remedies in (c) should be described by what each gives up — closure of belief under conjunction, the idea that belief is a matter of a threshold, consistency of the belief set, the thought that each of the many claims is blamelessly believed — rather than by labels, so that a candidate who has memorised the names but not the moves cannot score on them.
