I'm TAing the underdetermination week of a second-year philosophy of science course, and instead of handing out the usual list of -isms I want to give students a decision tree they can actually work through. The trouble is every handout I've found organizes the debate by the names of the positions, which means students sort themselves by which label they already like, and half of them just pick the one the lecturer sounded sympathetic to. So I want the tree built the other way round: start from the bare acceptance of the claim (you've seen the stock examples, two theories that fit every bit of data we have and still disagree about what's underneath), and split on questions that do real work, with every node phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. Some splits I'd want somewhere in the tree, though feel free to rebuild them if they don't carve cleanly, since the questions matter more than my list: is the claim about what I'm personally permitted to believe, about what a scientific community is entitled to accept and build on, or about whether "the theory" was ever a single thing to begin with; is the rival theory supposed to be one someone could write down today, or one we don't even have the vocabulary for yet, or one that's merely not ruled out; is the slack in the evidence or in what we're willing to count as evidence; and is the intended upshot that we suspend judgement, carry on believing, appeal to something non-empirical like simplicity or unification, or quietly change the question. That last one is the distinction I care about most, because I suspect alot of the disagreement in the literature is people running the same premise to four different conclusions.

As for the leaves: I want them to be actual positions, not slogans, so please include the constructive-empiricist line, the pessimistic-induction line with the transient-versus-persistent distinction somewhere in it, the unconceived-alternatives line, and the structural/selective-realist line as a way of dodging the whole thing, plus whatever combinations my splits generate that nobody has a name for. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment, i.e. the thing it has to say about why past theories worked so well or what it has to deny about theory change. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate and say why, I have a hunch the persistent-underdetermination leaf and the unconceived-alternatives leaf are the same leaf wearing two hats. And for branches that come out empty, don't just skip them: say whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. Keep it to something a student could finish in a seminar, so maybe eight to ten leaves and no more than that many branch nodes, and skip the citations, it's a handout and nobody reads them anyway.
