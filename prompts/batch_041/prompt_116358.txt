Last term I took a seminar on self-knowledge and testimony, and we spent three weeks on Moore's paradox: sentences such as "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" are absurd to assert, and the default in the seminar was that the oddity must therefore be pragmatic, since nothing in the world need be contradictory when someone says them. A classmate has since been pressing an argument that the default is simply wrong, and although I distrust the conclusion I have not been able to say exactly where the argument fails. It runs as follows. (1) A sincere assertion of "p" expresses the assertor's belief that p. (2) "p, but I don't believe that p" is a conjunction, and a sincere assertion of a conjunction is a sincere assertion of each conjunct. (3) Hence a sincere utterer of the Moorean sentence expresses the belief that p and, with the second conjunct, the belief that she does not believe that p. (4) What a sincere assertion expresses, the assertor believes; so she believes that p and believes that she does not believe that p. (5) By closure of belief under conjunction, she believes "p and I do not believe that p." (6) There is no situation in which that conjunction is both true and believed: believing it entails believing its first conjunct, which makes its second conjunct false. (7) So the sentence is contradictory, and its oddity is a defect of content rather than of use. (8) Therefore the Gricean and speech-act treatments, which locate the oddity in what the utterance implicates or presupposes about the speaker, are mistaken. (9) The deeper moral is that first-person present-tense belief sentences do not describe a state but give voice to it.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep the weaker claim that the oddity is not a cancelable conversational implicature — nothing one adds, "I'm not saying anything about what I believe" included, makes the sentence acceptable — than keep (7); but I cannot see whether the weaker claim survives the loss of (7). Two further things nag at me. First, my classmate insists that the argument is indifferent to who or what utters the string, so that if a parrot or a language model emits those words the contradiction is still there to be heard; yet it seems to matter a great deal that the speaker be the one whose belief is at issue, and that the tense be present, since "It was raining, but I didn't believe it" and "It's raining, but she doesn't believe it" are entirely unremarkable. Second, if (9) is right, it is unclear what is left of the idea that "I believe that p" can be true or false, and hence of the ordinary practice of correcting people about their own beliefs.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether a pragmatic treatment can be defended without reducing the oddity to a cancelable implicature, and what an account of the absurdity owes by way of an explanation of its attachment to the first person and the present tense.
