I work on the responsible-AI team at a company that sells a risk-scoring tool to hospitals and, separately, to a payments company, and I'm the person who has to sign off before anything ships. Right now our fairness review is basically "run the standard metrics, eyeball them, write a paragaph." I want to replace that with an actual decision procedure my team can run in about two days per model, grounded in the formal results everyone cites about fairness metrics being mutually unsatisfiable -- you know, the ones showing that when two groups have different base rates you can't have a calibrated score and equal error rates across groups at the same time, and that the handful of natural definitions can't all hold together except in degenerate cases. I don't need citations, I know the gist; I need the procedure.

I want it to do the translation honestly: given a model and a protected attribute, which metric pairs are actually in tension for this data (as opposed to only in theory), how to tell when the tension is real versus an artifact of a bad proxy or a tiny subgroup, what to do when it bites, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the choice to a person because the question is "whose errors do we prefer" rather than a fact about the model. Part of what I want is a reframing I've been kicking around: instead of picking one metric off a menu, treat the choice as a question of who bears the errors, and make the procedure force us to write down, for each candidate operating point, whose false positives go up and whose false negatives go up, in plain language, before anyone is allowed to pick. I genuinely don't know if that's the right frame, so tell me where it breaks.

The procedure has to survive the ugly cases: subgroups too small for the estimates to mean anything, a protected attribute that's a bad proxy for the thing we actually care about, a score that's only one input to a human decision downstream, and a customer or a lawyer insisting on a metric the math can't give us alongside another one they also insist on. It should say when to refuse to ship.

Then run it on two: (1) a model that ranks patients for a scarce care-management program, where one group is sicker on average at the same score; (2) a fraud-flag model for a payments company where the flagged group is a tiny fraction of transactions and the base rate genuinely differs between groups. One of these should come out with an answer I won't like -- I'd rather you tell me my tool can't be certified for one of them than paper over it.

Finish by saying what the procedure can't do.
