I teach the causation unit in our phil of science course and I keep having the same fight with the epidemiologists down the hall, so I want to nail down what I actually think. Below are my verdicts on a pile of reversal cases — same shape on the surface, and I don't treat them the same. What I want is the principle behind them, if there is one.

Two clinics treat the same infection. Clinic A cures 86% overall, Clinic B only about 64%. But break it down and it flips: among mild cases A cures 90%, B 95%; among severe cases A cures 50%, B 60%. B's the better clinic, no question — the overall numbers are just an artifact of A getting handed the milder patients. Now the same shape, opposite call: a study of people hospitalized for some condition finds the ones who exercised more did *worse*, even though in the general population exercisers do better. That within-hospital comparison is junk and I'd go with the population numbers — ending up in the hospital is downstream of both exercise and health, so conditioning on it cooks up the comparison. And a third: a blood-pressure drug cuts heart attacks, and it does that by lowering blood pressure. Hold blood pressure fixed and the benefit mostly vanishes. For a patient deciding whether to take it, I want the overall number — the total effect — not the fixed-blood-pressure one; though if a pharmacologist is asking whether the benefit runs *through* blood pressure, fine, then the stratified number is the thing, but that's a different question.

The one that keeps me up is the gender pay gap. I used to think the within-job-title gap was the honest measure of discrimination, and that the overall gap was inflated by women getting sorted into lower titles. Now I half think that's backwards — if the sorting into titles is itself the discrimination, then stratifying by title makes the problem vanish by assumption. And then a case where I'm pretty sure the aggregate just is the answer and nothing causal is even going on: two ballplayers, one out-hits the other in both halves of the season yet finishes with the lower average. Who had the better season? The one with the higher average. The halves are arbitrary, every at-bat counts the same, there's no treatment, no confounder, nothing to adjust for.

So — is there one principle that gets all of these, the clinics and the hospital and the drug and the ballplayers, and tells me what to do about the pay gap? I suspect it has to turn on *why* the third variable is there (common cause, common effect, in-between) and on what question I'm asking, but I can't state it cleanly, and I'd bet at least one verdict above has to go. Give me the principle, or the two or three best candidates, each stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause, plus what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, comparing two school districts' test scores when families sort into districts. Then say plainly which of my verdicts you're overruling. 500 words max, prose only, no table.
