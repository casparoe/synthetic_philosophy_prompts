A friend in my seminar has constructed the following argument against middle knowledge and regards it as decisive. I am half persuaded, but I think it proves too much, and I cannot locate the error.

(1) Middle knowledge requires true counterfactuals of freedom — propositions of the form "If Peter were in circumstances C, he would freely do A," such as "If Peter were in the courtyard, he would freely deny Christ."
(2) If such a conditional is true and its antecedent is realized, then its consequent is true; that step is a matter of logic alone.
(3) The conditional, if true, is true before Peter exists and independently of anything Peter does, and Peter has no control over facts that antedate him.
(4) So if Peter is placed in the courtyard, his denial follows from something fixed before he existed and that he could not have altered.
(5) An action is free only if its agent could have done otherwise and is, in the relevant sense, its ultimate source.
(6) Peter could not have done otherwise, and the source of his act lies outside him.
(7) So his denial is not free; and since the reasoning generalizes, no act covered by a true counterfactual of freedom is free, and middle knowledge is incompatible with creaturely freedom.

What troubles me is the last step. By the same pattern, every act has a true description that predates it and that its agent cannot alter, so the argument appears to show that no one ever acts freely — which my friend, a classical theist who wants God to know the future, does not accept. Which step should she give up, and what does giving it up cost her case against middle knowledge? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (5) than (3), but I want to know what I am paying.
