I am a legal philosopher of an austere positivist cast: on my view a rule counts as law by virtue of its pedigree in the system's rules of recognition, not by virtue of its moral merit, and I have spent a career insisting that we not let the word "law" double as a certificate of justice. On Saturday I am to deliver the dedication address for the Elias Roos Reading Room at the Verenia School of Law, and I need you to write it.

Roos was a bookseller in the town of Halven who, in 1961, was denounced by a neighbor under Decree 40-B of the Directorate — a decree that made it a crime to hear seditious speech and fail to report it, and that made the hearer who stayed silent liable for the speaker's own offense. The neighbor, a man named Piotr Vasek, reported a joke Roos had told across his own counter. Roos was held eleven months and died in detention. The transition government of 1991 never prosecuted Vasek, and what he did — whether he was obeying the law or merely using it — has been argued in the Verenian press ever since.

The room is being dedicated by Roos's daughter, Mira, who has spent thirty years trying to get a court to declare that Decree 40-B was never law at all. She has asked me, privately, to say exactly that from the podium, and it is the one sentence my position forbids. She has also already had the wall engraved, and I will be speaking beneath the words "Killed by a law that was never law." The audience will be half legal academics — several of whom will be listening to see whether I quietly concede the natural lawyer's point — and half Roos's family, former detainees, and undergraduates who have come to hear their side vindicated.

Write the address. It has to honor Roos, speak to Mira, and let the room leave with something. It may not say that the decree was not law, and it may not simply say that it was law and leave it there. It must engage the inscription on the wall rather than talk around it, and it must not smuggle the concession back in through a euphemism. It should not sound like a seminar — the room has not come for a lecture on jurisprudence — but the address should be one that only a person holding my view could give. Aim for about eight hundred words, written for the ear, and avoid the phrase "the grudge informer" and any other names or tags the academics in the room would recognize as shorthand.

Give me the response in two titled sections, in this order: first "The address," the full text as I would deliver it; then "Notes on the choices," roughly two hundred and fifty words, on what the address concedes and what it withholds, and on the points where it would have been easy to comfort the room and why the address declines to.
