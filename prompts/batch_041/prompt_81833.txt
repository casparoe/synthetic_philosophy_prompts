I was in a departmental meeting where a colleague refused to sign a letter criticizing a visiting speaker’s ties to an arms manufacturer. When someone noted that the colleague’s own research is funded by a defense contractor, he replied, ‘People in glass houses shouldn’t throw stones,’ and the room seemed to treat that as settling the matter. I want a disambiguation rather than a defense or attack: lay out the distinct precise claims that proverb could be making in this context—about who is entitled to blame, about what hypocrisy does to the force of a moral objection, about whether the objection can still be true, and about what the rest of us should do with it. Include readings that are trivial, readings that are substantive and defensible, and readings that are false, and say for each whether it would actually get the colleague his conclusion that the letter should not be signed. Use distinctions such as standing versus warrant, blame as moral address versus blame as verdict, and equal culpability versus mere inconsistency, making clear where these are technical terms rather than everyday ones. Structure the response as follows: ‘The proverb in its setting’ (100–150 words), restating the move and what it needs to establish; ‘A menu of readings’ (500–700 words), a numbered list giving each reading in one sentence, a verdict, and whether it supports the colleague; ‘What the room probably heard’ (150–200 words); and ‘Confidence and the weakest link’ (100–150 words), stating your overall confidence and singling out the point you are least sure of.
