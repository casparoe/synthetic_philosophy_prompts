ok so me and my friend spent like two hours arguing last night about whether a reasoner can ever rationally trust its own reasoning, and now I want to write a villanelle about it because I think the form can say something I cant say in prose.

Here's what I want it to actually get right, and I'm spelling it out so you can see what I'm after, not because I think you don't know it: if a system only believes what it can prove, it cant prove that its own proofs are trustworthy. You don't get 'if I have proved P, then P' for free — proving that very claim (for a given P, and a fortiori all at once) would already require having proved P. And the nasty special case is when P is a contradiction: prove that you're consistent and you can prove a contradiction, so you'd better not be able to prove it. The self-trust has to come from somewhere else — an endless tower of levels, each one trusting the one below, or just taking the trust as an axiom, which makes you strictly stronger and so isn't the free lunch it looked like.

What I want from the poem: a villanelle, 19 lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains that rhyme with each other, refrains being the two halves of the self-trust claim — roughly 'whatever I have proved is so' and 'so I may trust the proofs I make' (write them better than that, and make them scan, the two halves have to be worth hearing five times). The refrains should keep circling back the way the two halves keep leaning on each other, and the closing quatrain should set them side by side — I want the reader to feel that the only way both can stand is if you adopt them together and don't try to derive them, because deriving them is exactly the thing that cant be done. Someone who knows Löb's theorem should nod at the right places; someone who doesnt should still find it a real poem about trusting your own mind.

Start with the poem itself, no preface, no 'here's a villanelle', no commentary around it. And dont worry about making it cheerful, its fine if it ends uneasy.
