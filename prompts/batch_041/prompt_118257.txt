I'm a novelist, about 250 pages into a book set in a mill town in the 1840s, and I've hit a wall that's less about plot than about judgment. My protagonist's father pulls his nine-year-old out of school and puts him in the mill; the kid loses two fingers by chapter nine, and I've written the whole thing so far as if it's just what a man in that situation did — no narrator's raised eyebrow, no modern revulsion leaking in around the edges. My writing group loves it, they keep saying let the past be the past, don't impose our standards, nobody in 1840 thought that was a crime. My friend, who did a PhD in early-Victorian social history and now writes for a living, calls this the "house rules" view — every era has its own rulebook and the whole trick is to play by the rules of the era you're writing about instead of showing up with your own. She thinks that's not just difficult but incoherent, and I've been chewing on it all week. So: make the argument for her side, as hard as you can. Argue that there's no such thing as judging or describing the past strictly on its own terms, and that the house-rules view is a fiction we tell ourselves to feel clean.

Do it as an argument, not a survey, and I want two things kept apart and then wired back together, because I keep collapsing them. One, the moral sort of presentism — me, sitting here now, thinking the father is a monster. Two, the descriptive sort — the fact that the very words I have to use to say what he did (child, labour, school, welfare, abuse, "he should of known") are mine and not his. My hunch, which I'd like you to either defend or demolish, is that you can't quarantine the first from the second, because the escape hatch everyone offers — just understand them in their own terms — only moves the problem around: their own terms are always reconstructed by us, out of archives that later hands chose to keep. That last bit is what really interests me, since I'm literally working from a bundle of letters some great-granddaughter decided to donate in 1962, and the silence in them is deafening. But don't let the argument slide into the lazy version where, if presentism is unavoidable, then any present-day verdict is fine — I want the inescapability claimed without the anything-goes ending, and I want to know what the honest alternative to the house-rules view actually looks like for someone like me, who has to put a sentence on the page by Friday. And if my friend is just wrong, say so plainly, because she's definately smug about it and I'd enjoy that.
