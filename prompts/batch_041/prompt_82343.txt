Okay, so I did 34 years of marine chemistry, I'm 68 now, the last grant ran out, and I've swapped lab meetings for a stack of philosophy books. Which is how I ended up in a running argument with my old postdoc, who jumped ship into science-and-society stuff. Her line is that when we call some other community's beliefs unjustified we're just applying our own local standards, and that the reason they can't win an argument with us is that we own the journals and the money, not that we're right. My line was the standard one, that they can't even disagree with us without using our standards -- try to say the other lab is wrong and you'll find you're already giving reasons and weighing evidence, which is our game. She said that test is rigged, that if you define "using it" loosely enough everything looks inescapable, including stuff nobody would call universal, like the rule that you write up your methods in the past tense. And I couldn't answer her, which has been eating at me for a month.

So I'd like you to build the thing rather than describe it. Take the test as: a standard earns its keep for universality only if someone who rejects it is forced to lean on that very standard in the course of rejecting it -- not just on words, or on logic in general, or on the fact that they had to assert something. Two cases please:

(1) One where the test fires but it's a cheat. A standard no outsider has any reason to accept, and yet anyone arguing against it from inside the group ends up leaning on it anyway. Ordinary setting -- a lab, a courtroom, an archive, a school, a fishing crew, whatever, as long as it's somewhere I could actually walk into. No aliens, no brains in vats, no invented languages.

(2) One where the test fires and it isn't a cheat, where the dissenter is genuinely forced to use the very thing they're denying. I can't build this one myself; every version I try turns out to rest on the fact that they had to say something at all, which makes the whole test worthless. And keep it off the usual suspects -- no modus ponens, no induction, no non-contradiction, nothing of the you-cant-coherently-deny-youre-thinking variety.

Then, for the pair:

- one paragraph each, and point to the exact move where the dissenter gets caught in each one;
- name the single difference that makes (1) a cheat and (2) not, then take that difference away and show the two cases collapsing into the same case;
- tell me whether the dissenter in (2) has an exit that isn't just going quiet -- can they make their point by doing something instead of saying something, or by declining to play along? If so, what would the case have to look like to shut that exit?
- and tell me where my postdoc wins: the strongest version of the charge that case (2) is just case (1) with a bigger fence around it, plus your honest read on whether it lands.

No reading list please, I'll definately never get through it.
