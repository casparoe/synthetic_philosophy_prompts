I am preparing a long-form recorded interview with a philosopher who defends an unorthodox account of transformative experience, and I would like a set of questions that engages the account on its own terms rather than inviting a tour of the existing debate. Treat the position as that of an unnamed interlocutor; do not attribute it to any particular living author, and do not ask her to summarise the literature or to supply references.

The account to be interviewed. The guest grants that decisions such as whether to have a child, to emigrate, to convert, to enlist, or to accept a profound and irreversible medical change are hard in a way ordinary decisions are not, but she rejects the usual diagnosis of that hardness. She denies that the distinctive difficulty is the inaccessibility of the experience: we are ignorant of the future constantly, and ignorance by itself does not threaten the rationality of a choice. Her diagnosis is that the difficulty is one of authority. Such a decision must be made by a self whose title to make it is precisely what is in question, since the decision is meant to bind a person who does not yet exist as a chooser and whose preferences the decision may itself replace. Her solution is retroactive ratification: the choice is rational when the person one becomes — now in possession of the transformed preferences, and of an understanding of what the change was like — endorses having made it, and this endorsement is constitutive rather than merely evidential, because it is what makes the earlier decision and the later life the decision and life of one continuing agent rather than of two unrelated ones.

Required questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

1. Why the inaccessibility of the experience is not simply ordinary ignorance. Press with a concrete contrast of your own invention — a choice whose outcome you cannot preview but which leaves your preferences untouched, set against a choice that transforms them — and ask what exactly the second has that the first lacks.

2. Whether the account can avoid ranking lives it has not lived. Ask how it treats a transformation that is neither chosen nor ratified, and whether it can say anything about such a life without treating it as a loss. Follow up on a transformation the transformed person can never ratify.

3. Manipulation and adaptive preference. If an institution, a market, or a relationship shapes the person who later ratifies, does ratification launder the shaping? Ask what distinguishes endorsement from having been brought to endorse.

4. The decision-theoretic dissolution. Ask whether the puzzle is an artefact of modelling choice as expected utility over outcomes, and whether a decision theory that permits preferences over the kind of agent one becomes dissolves the problem rather than solving it.

5. Critical purchase. Ask whether there is any case in which the later person ratifies the choice and the choice was nevertheless irrational. Press on whether the account makes rationality retrospective, and therefore either vacuous or a matter of luck.

Then add two or three further questions of your own, aimed at what the account seems to you most exposed to, and close with one question about what would count as evidence against it. Wherever a question needs a concrete case, invent a small specific one — a named situation, a decision, and an outcome — rather than gesturing vaguely at decisions like having a child. Avoid generic openers, and avoid questions that merely ask her to restate the view.
