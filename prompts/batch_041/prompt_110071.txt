I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of collective memory, and I'm putting together a decision tree for a workshop handout. The literature I work in runs between two poles: the thought that a community owes something to its past and to its dead, and the thought that too much memory is itself a moral hazard. My problem is that workshop participants sort themselves by which pole sounds more attractive rather than by what they actually think about cases, so I want an instrument that classifies them by their verdicts on cases instead.

Act as an expert in moral philosophy with deep familiarity with the ethics of memory, transitional justice, and the philosophy of history. Take this slowly and work in stages: first brainstorm the space of questions, then build the tree, then locate positions within it. I am not looking for a literature review or for references — I want the instrument itself, argued from the cases. Where you are unsure whether a position has a standard name, describe it functionally and mark the label as yours; do not name a source you are not certain of.

The deliverable is a decision tree, together with a leaf table, with five to eight branch questions and six to ten leaves, subject to the following constraints.

Every branch node must be a question that someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to: a question about a case, a claim, or a verdict, never a question about whether one endorses a label.

Every leaf must carry a name (or a descriptive label, plus 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) and one sentence stating the commitment that leaf has the hardest time honouring.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views: first set out the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the positions, then list the combinations that no one occupies and diagnose each emptiness as incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Where two leaves give the same verdict on every case below but are not the same view, flag the pair as inseparable by this tree, and tell me which further case would be needed to separate them — do not manufacture a distinction that does no work.

The following cases must all appear as diagnostic material, and each branch should turn on at least one of them:

1. A town's only marker of a massacre has weathered into illegibility. Restoring it would consume the annual budget of the youth centre the town also wants. The town instead has schoolchildren read the victims' names aloud once a year. Has the town done what it owes, and to whom has it done it?
2. A digital archive of survivor testimony is built to persist indefinitely. Within a century, no one alive will be able to identify with anyone in it; no descendant will exist. Does the obligation to keep it running still bind anyone?
3. A peace agreement trades amnesty for testimony; at the victims' association's own insistence, the testimony is sealed for fifty years and then destroyed. Is the destruction a wrong, and if so, who is wronged by it?
4. A man learns that his grandfather commanded a killing unit. He wants to move on and has decided not to tell his children. Does he owe anything, and to whom — the victims' community, his children, the dead?
5. A survivors' association votes to end its annual commemoration: the ceremony keeps them in grief and their children no longer attend. Is this a failure, a permissible choice, or something admirable?
6. A community's founding narrative of its own persecution is significantly inaccurate in its details, but it is what holds the community together and what motivates its work with refugees. Is inaccurate memory a defective discharge of whatever is owed, or a legitimate form of it?
7. Someone born into a settler family, whose own ancestors arrived after the dispossession, asks what he owes towards a wrong he is not descended from but whose benefits he still holds. Does the obligation run through ancestry, through benefit, or through neither?
8. A survivor refuses ever to speak publicly about what was done to her, and the community's memory is correspondingly thinner. Is her silence a wrong, or is there a duty to let the past be that competes with the duty to remember?

Three things about the shape of the tree matter to me.

First, I want to know which positions can hold that something is owed even when no one remains who could be wronged by the forgetting — no descendants, no community, no rememberer at all. Case 2 is the test.

Second, I want to know which positions can make forgetting obligatory rather than merely permitted, and who is bound by that obligation — the wronged, the perpetrator community, the state, or no one. Cases 3, 5 and 8 are where this should surface.

Third, I have been working with a distinction between thick memory — a community treating a past as its own, with the affective and identity freight that carries — and thin memory — an accurate record available to anyone who cares to consult it. For each leaf, the tree should make explicit whether thick memory is required, optional, or a liability.

The following leaves must appear at minimum: memory as a debt owed to the dead that cannot be waived or transferred; memory as the constitution of a community's identity, so that forgetting is a form of self-loss; memory as a commitment owed to the living wronged, which they can waive or renegotiate; memory as an instrument valued for what it prevents rather than for what it owes; memory as a virtue of persons rather than a duty about facts; forgetting as a good, with excessive memory as a moral hazard; and a deflationary view on which there is no duty of memory at all and memory practices are preferences or politics. You may add leaves if the space requires them.

Format the answer as: your brainstormed branch questions and the answer combinations they allow; the tree itself, with each node phrased as a question and each edge labelled with the answer; the leaf table; the empty combinations with your diagnosis of each; and the pairs of leaves this tree cannot separate, together with the case that would separate them.
