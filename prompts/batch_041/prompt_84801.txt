I am a novelist, and I have a request of a kind I am not usually in a position to make. I have written myself into a corner, and I would like a philosopher's help getting out of it — though what I need is not instruction but comment.

My novel, in proof now under the working title Two Beginnings, concerns a woman named Ines. Its ninth chapter appears twice. The two versions are identical in every sentence, down to the punctuation, except for one detail: which of two sperm reached the egg. At the end of the second version the narrator steps forward and remarks, in effect, that this is one woman told twice. I wrote the chapter believing I was dramatising a claim in metaphysics — that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg, the thesis commonly credited to Kripke, which I had absorbed at second hand from conversation rather than from the book itself. I no longer believe the chapter shows what I thought it showed, and I have drafted a short essay, meant as an afterword but possibly destined for a magazine, arguing for a different reading of my own device. Before it goes anywhere I would like your comments, and I would rather have them unkind than vague.

The draft, more or less as it stands:

> Two Beginnings
>
> When the narrator of this novel tells you that the woman in both versions is the same woman, you believe her. I used to think that was because she is right — that a person's beginning is not the sort of thing that could have gone otherwise, that whoever emerged from that particular pairing was always going to be Ines and nobody else. I now think you believe her for a humbler reason: because you are being told one story, and a story cannot have two beginnings. Nothing in the two chapters distinguishes them except that they are two tellings of one tale. The woman is the same woman because the narration is the same narration.
>
> If that is right, then the intuition that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg is the necessity of narrative mistaken for the necessity of things. We cannot imagine our own second beginning — cannot feel it as anything but a contradiction — because we have already cast our lives as stories with a first sentence, and the first sentence of a story is not optional. The impossibility we feel is grammatical, not metaphysical.
>
> Three things persuade me. First, the twins. Identical twins come from one sperm and one egg, and they are two people. If our origins made us the people we are, the twins would be one person. They are not; so our origins do not make us who we are. Second, nothing available to me from the inside could tell me which sperm I came from, and no conceivable experience could settle it. A question that no experience could ever answer is not a question about anything. Third, our confidence about the necessity of origins is loudest exactly where the origin is most vividly narrated — birth, parentage, the family romance — and quietest where the beginning is diffuse, as it is for a cutting taken from a plant or a lineage of cells. That looks to me like a fact about our storytelling rather than about necessity.
>
> The consequence for the novelist is uncomfortable. If I am right, a character's origin is stipulated and a person's is merely narrated, and the line between the two is thinner than we pretend; my chapter is honest. If I am wrong, the chapter is a cheat and I should cut it.

What I want from you, in whatever order seems best, is four things.

First, accuracy. I am not a philosopher and I have almost certainly misdescribed the position I am attacking; tell me where, in plain terms. I suspect that two of my three supporting points are confused, and I cannot see which two, which is precisely why I am asking.

Second, whether my central claim is a genuine objection or merely a change of subject. If it is the latter, say so and say why — and tell me whether there is a version of it that is not.

Third, structure: what to cut, what to expand, and whether the closing paragraph about novelists earns its place or should go entirely. I have a fortnight and no appetite for reading, so please do not send me to a reading list; I want comments I can act on in the draft itself, not titles. Do not tell me the essay is interesting unless you can say in a sentence why, and do not spare the argument on the grounds that I am a guest in your discipline.
