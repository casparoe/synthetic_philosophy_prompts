I am the incoming chair of a small foundation that funds research on invertebrate welfare, and I have to give the opening remarks at our annual dinner, which is also the launch of a two-year grant program on insect sentience. The room is mixed: about a third are entomologists who have spent careers describing insect behavior without ever needing the word sentience and who will walk out if they think they are being conscripted into a moral crusade; a third are donors who have absorbed the scale argument — the thought that because insects are astronomically numerous, even a very low probability that any one of them is sentient could yield an enormous expected amount of morally relevant experience — and are ready to write checks; and a third are philosophers and welfare scientists who will notice every equivocation. I need a ten-minute speech that does real work: it should make the scale argument vivid enough that the donors understand why anyone would care about insects, but it must not pretend to settle whether insects are sentient, and it must give the entomologists a reason to stay in the room rather than feeling that their epistemic standards are being overridden by arithmetic.

The speech should turn on a distinction that one of our scientific advisors has been pushing and that I want to introduce to this audience: the individuation discount. Her thought is that even if we grant a nonzero probability of sentience to some insect, the scale argument may be too quick in moving from that probability to a number of morally considerable individuals, because the unit of sentience might not be the individual insect; it might be a colony, a developmental stage, a species-typical capacity, or a functional subsystem, and counting every ant as a separate locus of possible suffering could inflate the moral ledger by orders of magnitude. I want the speech to explain that idea without jargon, to show why it cuts both ways — it can reduce the apparent scale, but it can also relocate moral concern to units we have not been counting — and to end with a concrete ask for the grant program that does not depend on resolving the metaphysics. Please write the speech. It should sound like a person, not a position paper; it can use one small concrete image or anecdote (I once watched a wasp do something that has stayed with me, but you can invent a better one), and it should be honest about what we do not know. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular books or papers.
