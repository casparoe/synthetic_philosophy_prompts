My mate Priya and I went at it for like two hours last night about expected value, and I came out of it feeling like I lost. Not that I was convinced — I just couldn't find the exit. She's been reading alot of the longtermism and decision theory stuff and now thinks basically everyones life is a mistake. Her argument, tidied into steps (she wrote it on a napkin, this is my reconstruction):

1. A choice is rational just in case no other available option has more expected value. Take the lower-EV option and you've made a mistake. No hedging.
2. There is no largest amount of value an outcome can have. Whatever you name, something bigger is possible.
3. So for any sure thing G, there's a probability p (bigger than zero) and a payoff worth W such that p times W beats the value of G. Make p smaller, make W bigger; there's always a pair that works.
4. So for any sure thing, some gamble has a higher expected value than it does, and by 1, taking the sure thing over that gamble is a mistake.
5. How small p is doesn't matter. A thousandth, one in 10^50, one in 10^500 — the only thing that changes is how big the payoff has to be.
6. So whatever I'm doing at any given moment, there's a gamble I ought to be doing instead: my savings for a ticket, my job, my friends, my life, as long as the payoff on the other side is big enough.
7. That goes for the ticket too, since there's always a bigger one. So every choice anyone ever makes is a mistake, including the choice to go buy the ticket.
8. Either EV-maximising is the right theory of rationality and everything we do is irrational, or EV-maximising is not the right theory.

Her worked example: a mugger offers you a one in 10^60 chance of something worth 10^70 units of whatever you're counting, in exchange for your wallet. She says the arithmatic isn't close and you hand it over, and if you wouldn't, that's just you failing to be rational. She also says the usual version of the mugger story cheats by using an infinite payoff, and hers doesn't need infinity — everything stays finite, just big. Same reasoning says you should walk away from your patients if someone offers you a one-in-a-googol chance of curing all disease forever.

Here's my problem. I want to keep 1 and 2. I really do think rationality is about expected value, and I don't see how to say value has a ceiling — a ceiling of what, and why there? So my guess is that 2 is the culprit, but I can't bring myself to give it up, and she has an answer to every other escape I tried. Say value is bounded: she says name the bound. Say credences that tiny aren't real credences: she says tell me the cutoff and why it's there, and notice you're drawing a line just to save common sense. Say you should discount tiny probabilities: she says then you've dropped 1 and you're satisficing or making something ad hoc up.

So: which step do I cut, why did it look fine, and what does it cost me? If I drop 1, what exactly do I lose, and is there a principled version of 1 that survives? If I drop 2, what am I buying and what am I stuck with? And is there a move I'm not seeing. Also, when I said fine, but who is offering me that gamble right now, she said I was just restating 3, and I genuinely can't tell whether she's right.
