I work on the experimentation platform at a mid-sized software company. I have no formal training in philosophy or in statistics beyond what the job has forced on me, and I am trying to write down my own position on Simpson's paradox for an internal reading group. Before I circulate it, I would like an audit of whether the following commitments can be held together. I am not attached to any of them in particular and I genuinely do not know which one is doing the damage.

(1) A Simpson-style reversal is never a fact about arithmetic. It is a sign that the aggregate comparison and the segmented comparison are answering different questions, and the correct response is to say which question was meant, rather than to treat the aggregate as the default.

(2) What fixes which question was meant is the causal structure. You compare within strata defined by common causes of the treatment and the outcome; you do not condition on variables that are downstream of the treatment, and you do not condition on variables that are common effects of the treatment and the outcome, because conditioning on those manufactures an association between the two that was not there.

(3) Some reversals survive all of this. When the quantity being compared is an odds ratio, or, I am told, a hazard ratio, the aggregate value is not a weighted average of the stratum-specific values even when nothing is confounded; the two numbers are simply different parameters. In those cases I do not think there is a single correct comparison to recover, and describing one of the two as paradoxical is a category mistake.

(4) When the two comparisons disagree, I report both numbers and let the reader decide which one is relevant to them.

(5) For metrics that are themselves ratios of two aggregates — clicks per session, revenue per user, milliseconds per request — the number that describes the system is the ratio of the totals, not the average of the per-unit ratios, and where those two diverge I go with the ratio of totals.

(6) The sure-thing principle: if a change is at least as good on the metric in every cell of a partition of the possibilities, it is at least as good overall, and any aggregate figure that says otherwise is an artifact to be ignored.

(7) Averages are a legitimate summary of a population. Nothing about the paradox counts against averaging; it shows only that two comparisons can weight the same segments differently.

Please tell me which pairs of these are actually in tension and how severe each tension is, which pairs only look like they are in tension, and, for each real tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision costs. Order the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and finish by naming the single change that would clean up the most, along with what it would leave unresolved.

One more thing about the epistemics. Where a disagreement I have flagged is one on which people who actually work on this disagree, say so and give the shape of the disagreement rather than presenting one side as the settled consensus; and where something is in fact settled, say that plainly. I would rather have a map of what is contested than a confident answer.
