I've got the Molinism slot in our philosophy of religion reading group next week, 50 minutes, mostly grad students who are fine with possible worlds talk and the free will debate but have never touched 16th century theology, plus one philosopher of mind who gets impatient. Don't explain middle knowledge to me, I know what it is. I want the teaching plan.

My worry is that the whole thing hangs on one distinction and if they miss it they'll spend the rest of the hour thinking they've refuted something. So: what order do the pieces have to go in, and what actually depends on what. My instinct is that you have to get them to the point where they see what it would even mean for a would-claim about a free agent to be true before God enters the picture at all, because otherwise the three orders of knowledge just look like arbitrary bookkeeping. Tell me if that's wrong and something else has to come first, or if that's right but not for the reason I gave.

For the carrying example I want to try something other than the usual Capernaum or Peter's denial material. My idea is to use the predictor from Newcomb's problem. The predictor isn't making you choose, isn't reading your mind, just knows what you'd do, and my hope is that the modal structure (known before the decree, true without being caused, act still free) feels natural in that setting instead of magic. But I'm not sure it survives contact: the predictor's accuracy is inductive and probabilistic, the Molinist God's knowledge is supposed to be pre-volitional and infallible, and dragging causal-versus-evidential decision theory into a theology session might just add a second axis of confusion on top of the first. So should the predictor carry it, or should I stick with the boring scriptural case, and either way what does the example have to have and not have? Does it have to involve a creature who doesn't exist yet, or circumstances that never obtain, or neither? And is there a third option I'm not seeing that's better than both.

Then the misconceptions. I've got a list and I want them in the order they'll actually turn up, with the point in the sequence where each one has to be headed off:

1. middle knowledge = God's foreknowledge of what creatures will in fact do
2. so God knows it because he knows the creature's character and the circumstances, i.e. it's just a really good prediction
3. if God knew it infallibly then it was determined, there's no room left for freedom
4. fine, but what makes the counterfactual true in the first place (they'll think they invented this objection)
5. so God just stacks the deck, putting people in the circumstances where they'll do what he wants, isn't this Calvinism with extra steps
6. if nothing grounds these things then maybe they aren't true at all (they've just wandered into open theism)

Tell me if 4 and 5 are in the wrong place, which ones I should let them sit in for a while rather than defusing right away, and where exactly the slide into open theism should get headed off, since that's the one where the smartest student will feel like they've won.

Then what to leave out. I'm skipping the Dominican/Jesuit fight over grace and the congruism business and the fine points about whether the counterfactuals might be grounded in God's will, at least on the day. What else has to go, and what should I say out loud that I'm skipping so nobody thinks I forgot it.

And give me one question for the end that would actually tell me whether the distinction landed, not a vocabulary check, something where a wrong answer is diagnostic. Ideally they have to sort a few claims into the right order of knowledge, say what grounds each one, and say what would change if God had decreed otherwise. This is definately the part I'm least sure about, so if the question I just described is the wrong shape, tell me what shape it should be instead.
