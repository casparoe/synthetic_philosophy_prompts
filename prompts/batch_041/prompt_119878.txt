I am a novelist at work on a book about a translator of Nietzsche who, convalescing in a rented room, decides to live according to the section of Ecce Homo in which Nietzsche treats nutrition not as a medical matter but as a question of self-preservation. I can follow the local moves well enough: he runs together what look to me like three quite different things — what one eats, where and in what climate one lives, and what one takes in as recreation — and he insists throughout that the organism's requirements are discovered by instinct rather than by rule. What I cannot find is the argument's spine. Is he making a physiological claim, that a particular way of eating and living suits a particular kind of constitution; an aesthetic claim, that a regimen is a style, a mode of self-fashioning; or a polemical claim, that modern diets and modern moralizing about them are symptoms of decline? And why does he seem most contemptuous of exactly the people who follow a regimen correctly and visibly benefit from it? I keep wanting to read the dietary material as an allegory of reading — of what a mind can and cannot assimilate, of incorporation as an always-selective act — and I cannot tell whether that is a projection of mine or something the text genuinely licenses. He wrote this in 1888, within months of his collapse, which I suppose is either irrelevant or the whole point. I have only a translation and no German, and I care about the thought rather than the wording. Help me see how these strands hang together, and if they do not, how the section still functions as an argument.
