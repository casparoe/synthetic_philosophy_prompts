Last night I had a long argument with a friend — he read mathematics at university before drifting into software, and he is generally the person I go to when I want the view that is two steps more sophisticated than the one I would have reached on my own — about whether a machine could in principle do everything that a human mathematician does. At some point he appealed to the Church–Turing thesis, and when I asked him to spell out what it actually says and how we know it, this is roughly what he gave me: 'The Church–Turing thesis is the claim that anything computable by any means whatsoever — by a person following a rule, by a machine, by any physical process you care to name — can be computed by a Turing machine. Turing and Church proved it by showing that all the rival attempts to formalise computability — λ-definability, general recursiveness, Turing machines — pick out exactly the same class of functions; and since that identification has been proved, the thesis is a mathematical theorem and no future discovery can overturn it. Its empirical support is that nobody in the decades since has found a counterexample, and that every computer ever built has borne it out. The one thing that could still break it is quantum computing: because a quantum machine explores many computations in superposition at once, it may well compute things that no Turing machine can, and that would be the first genuine counterexample.'

I did not push back at the time, partly because I could not have said which part I wanted to push back against. I do understand that the thesis is meant to be about what is computable at all rather than about how quickly, which is why the quantum remark struck me as slightly odd even as it was being made — but the rest of the paragraph sounded like the sort of thing one is not supposed to disagree with, and I have been turning it over ever since. My suspicion is that it is more or less the standard account with a joint slipped somewhere, and then a further claim hung on the slipped joint.

What I would like is an audit rather than a verdict on my friend. Go through the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each, what someone who works on computability theory would happily accept, what they would wince at, and what they would call simply false. In particular, I want to know whether the two halves of his conclusion can really sit together, since a thesis that has been proved as a theorem does not seem to be the sort of thing a new kind of machine might refute. And I would like to know how much of the weight he went on to put on the thesis in the rest of our argument it can actually bear: if his use of it was too strong, tell me what the honest version of the appeal would have been. I have no attachment to the conclusion that he is wrong — as I say, he is usually the better-informed of the two of us — so please do not soften a point merely because it happens to be his.
