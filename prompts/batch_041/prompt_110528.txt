I do the analytics for a state health department — I'm the one who signs off on the annual hospital comparison report, and I also field the ad hoc requests that come in from the commissioners office, from reporters, and from hospitals that are furious about their numbers. So I'm making calls on this maybe ten times a month, usually with someone waiting on the other end of an email.

What keeps biting me is Simpson's paradox, and I'm tired of working it out case by case from scratch. Someone hands me a table, the pooled number says one thing, every stratum says the other thing, and I have to decide which one goes in the report. I've been going on instinct for two years and my instincts have started disagreeing with each other.

So: write me a decision procedure I can actually run, in order, from the moment a table lands on my desk. The questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and just says no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person — the medical director, the commissioner, or a biostatistician we have to pay for — because the question turns out to be about values, or about a causal assumption that no amount of data will settle. It has to be runnable in about twenty minutes, by me, without a stats package. And it has to be honest about which steps are just judgment calls with a lab coat on. The medical director has to sign the report and the commissioner's office reads the summary line, so I need to know which parts I'm allowed to defend on my own and which ones I shouldn't.

Things it has to handle, because these are what I actually hit:

- The obvious one. Hospital A has worse overall mortality but better mortality in every stratum, because A takes the sicker patients. I assume the answer is "adjust," but I want the procedure to say why, and to tell me what to do when I can't get a case-mix measure for one of the hospitals.
- The one where the stratifying variable is something the hospital did. Last spring we looked at a sepsis protocol and whether the patient was moved to the ICU within six hours turned the comparison inside out — but the protocol is what causes the early ICU move. Adjusting for it would be the wrong move even though the reversal is real. I want the procedure to catch that automatically, not because I happened to remember it that day.
- The one that's really been bothering me. I redid a comparison using risk differences instead of odds ratios and the gap basically vanished. Then I did the same thing on a different dataset and the gap sat there unchanged. A few years ago someone told me that adjusting for more variables isn't the safer choice, it's just a different choice, and I nodded and didn't understand. So there needs to be a step that separates confounding from something about the measure itself — and tells me when I can settle that from the numbers and when I can't.
- Aggregation level. Half my requests are "compare the counties" when the data is people, and the other half are the reverse.

Then run the procedure start to finish on three realistic made-up cases:

1. A: 100 low-severity patients with 5 deaths and 300 high-severity with 75 deaths (80/400). B: 400 low-severity with 40 deaths and 100 high-severity with 30 deaths (70/500). Every stratum favors A; the pooled rate favors B.
2. The sepsis protocol, where early ICU transfer is the stratifier and the protocol causes early ICU transfer. Invent the numbers.
3. Two hospitals that look balanced on everything I can measure, each subgroup showing an odds ratio around 2.0 for the same intervention, but the pooled odds ratio is 1.3 — and the risk difference is about 8 points in every subgroup and pooled. The commissioner wants one headline number out of this.

On at least one of those three I want the procedure to give me an answer I won't like. Don't sand off the corners. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire, and which questions I should stop trying to answer myself and hand to a biostatistician even if it costs me a week.

Tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, and point to the single step you're least sure of — the one most likely to send me wrong in practice.

Then give me the whole thing again as a JSON object I can paste into our SOP wiki, with these fields: "procedure", a list of objects with "step" (number), "question", "what_counts_as_an_answer", "if_not_met", "action" (one of "continue", "stop_no", "hand_off"), "hand_off_to", "judgment_not_fact" (true/false); "worked_cases", a list with "case", "verdict", "report_this_number" (one of "aggregate", "stratified", "both", "none"), "what_the_procedure_rules_out", "confidence_0_1"; "where_it_hands_off"; "where_it_misfires"; and "least_confident_step" with "step" and "why".
