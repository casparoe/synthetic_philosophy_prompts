My position, which I want you to defend, is that self-deception is plainly possible and the paradox is an artifact of two bad assumptions — that a mind has a single belief store which either contains P or doesn't, and that deceiving yourself has to be a thing you do in the way that lying to someone is a thing you do. Here's the mechanism I keep pushing on them, and I want you to make it do real work rather than just name-drop it: split the mind into a belief-former and an attention-policy. The attention-policy can't write beliefs directly at all. What it can do is pick which questions get asked, which evidence gets gathered, how a question gets framed, when inquiry stops, what gets rehearsed nightly and what never gets thought about again. Over years, a policy like that can walk a mind to a conclusion it would never have reached under unsteered inquiry, and the resulting belief will feel like something that simply happened to it.

So: argue that this is genuine self-deception, that we should call it that and not something anodyne like motivated reasoning or biased evidence selection, and that the paradox dissolves once you accept the architecture.
