I'm in a seminar on logical empiricism, and we've just finished the Neurath material — the anti-metaphysical programme, the physicalism, the encyclopedia idea, and the visual-education work that grew out of his direction of the Social and Economic Museum in Red Vienna, where the Vienna Method of pictorial statistics (later renamed ISOTYPE) was developed with Gerd Arntz. Our instructor showed slides of the housing and unemployment charts, and I've come away with a reading of the whole project that is coherent but, I suspect, wrong; I would much rather be told where it breaks than be left holding it. My reading is this. Neurath had diagnosed ordinary verbal language as hopelessly loaded with theory and metaphysics, so he built a picture language in which social and economic facts could be displayed rather than described — an array of standardized, repeated figures whose meaning is grasped at a glance because the figures resemble the things they stand for. I took this to be, in effect, the idea that a proposition is a picture of a fact, transplanted from logic into statistics: a notation that shows the world instead of talking about it, and that any citizen can read without training and without a technical vocabulary. When I read that he defended protocol sentences as the basis of science, it seemed to confirm the reading — I assumed the charts were the pictorial counterpart of those basic observational records, the visible given on which the rest of knowledge could be built.

But that reading now seems to contradict nearly everything else I know about him: that scientific knowledge has no secure foundation, that even observation reports are revisable in the light of further claims, that we are always rebuilding the ship while afloat, and that the unity of science was to be an encyclopedia rather than a system. If the picture language were meant to be a self-evident pictorial given, it would be exactly the kind of rock-bottom foundation he spent his life denying; and if it were not, I don't see why he would have bothered inventing a notation at all, since words would have served. The evidence I keep tripping over is that the charts themselves are full of words — titles, legends, numbers, explanatory text — which I had been treating as a concession to the public rather than as part of the method, and that the museum plainly expected visitors to be taught how to read the displays. So: which of my assumptions has to go, and what was the picture language actually for, if not to ground knowledge in the immediately visible? I would also like to know whether the resemblance-to-the-world idea does any real work in his project or whether I have simply run together two unrelated senses of picture. I realize the historical record may leave some of this genuinely open, and I would rather have the open parts marked as open than have them smoothed over.
