I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long piece about whether the conventional dividing lines of European history—the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution—are discoveries about the past or artifacts of later historiography, and I've been rereading the opening of the second part of Jacob Burckhardt's The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (the Middlemore translation), because it's the locus classicus for the claim that something genuinely new in human self-consciousness appeared in Italy around the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The passage I keep circling is this: "In the Middle Ages both sides of human consciousness—that which was turned within as that which was turned without—lay dreaming or half awake beneath a common veil. The veil was woven of faith, illusion, and childish prepossession, through which the world and history were seen clad in strange hues. Man was conscious of himself only as a member of a race, people, party, family, or corporation—only through some general category. In Italy this veil first melted into air." What I want help with is not the general thesis—I know the general thesis—but the specific rhetorical and conceptual machinery of this paragraph. When Burckhardt says the medieval person was conscious of himself "only through some general category," is he making an empirical claim about medieval psychology that could in principle be tested against sources, or is he doing something closer to stipulating a contrast term—constructing a "Middle Ages" that exists mainly to be the un-individuated foil against which the Renaissance can appear as an awakening? And if it's the latter, does that make the periodization circular, or is there a defensible reading on which the stipulation is legitimate because it's really a claim about the *conditions of possibility* of a certain kind of self-description rather than about what any particular medieval person felt?

Put more sharply, my confusion is about the direction of explanation. Burckhardt needs the Middle Ages to be a period of submerged individuality so that Italy's emergence of the individual can be an event; but the evidence he elsewhere adduces for medieval corporate consciousness (guilds, feudal ties, the "spell of race") seems to me equally compatible with people having vivid interior lives that simply weren't recorded in the genres he privileges. So I'd like you to walk me through how a sympathetic contemporary historian of periodization would defend or reject this paragraph: whether the "veil" is meant literally as a description of experience or figuratively as a description of available self-representations; whether the contrast with Italy is doing causal work or merely classificatory work; and what, if anything, would count as evidence for or against the claim that a form of self-consciousness was absent before it was named. I don't need a bibliography—I need the argumentative anatomy of the passage, and a clear-eyed verdict on whether its periodizing move is a piece of history or a piece of conceptual legislation.
