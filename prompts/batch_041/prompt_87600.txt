I work as a forecasting engineer: my day job is building and maintaining long-horizon time-series models — energy load, freight volumes, that sort of thing — so the vocabulary I actually think in is stationarity and non-stationarity, structural breaks and regime changes, distributional shift, exogenous versus endogenous inputs, backtesting and out-of-sample error, and the gap between a fitted trend line and an invariant mechanism that survives intervention. I have no real training in the philosophy of history, but I have been sitting in on a reading group working through Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and the more I read the more it feels like a forecasting argument wearing a toga. I want to know how much of it actually survives being said in the only vocabulary I really command.

The position I want translated is, I think, a package. First, the target: the historicist holds that there are laws of historical development — lawlike regularities governing the trajectory of a whole society — from which the broad shape of the future can be read off, the way an astronomer reads off an eclipse. Second, the refutation, which as I understand it runs: the future course of history is shaped in part by the growth of human knowledge; we cannot predict the growth of knowledge, since to predict a discovery would be to have made it; so we cannot predict the course of history. Third, a distinction he keeps pressing between laws and trends — a trend holds for a period and depends on its initial conditions, whereas a law is universal and conditional — with the historicist charged with mistaking the one for the other. Fourth, an argument that a society is not a natural unit whose totality can be studied, so that any description of the whole is necessarily selective and the attempt to grasp it is what tips people into prophecy. There is also, at the end, a practical punch line about preferring piecemeal reform to large-scale utopian planning, though I confess I cannot see how he gets there from the earlier points.

So: restate that package in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. I want to know which parts are already sitting there under other names — I have hunches about regime instability, about the way a model's own forecasts feed back into the process being forecast, and about non-stationarity that arises from inside the system rather than from outside it — and whether those are the same ideas or only cousins. I want to know which parts survive only by being distorted: my suspicion is that the law-versus-trend distinction, which Popper seems to treat as a difference in kind, flattens into a difference in degree in my world, namely how robust a fitted relationship is to intervention, and I would like you to say whether that flattening is a loss or a clarification. And I want to know what has no counterpart at all: nothing in my vocabulary gives me a grip on the claim that the whole of history is the thing at stake, and still less on the jump from "we cannot predict" to "we ought to prefer piecemeal reform," since my models have no room for a therefore-we-ought. Finally, tell me how to read the residue — whether it marks something forecasting genuinely cannot see and Popper can, or whether it is a sign that he was smuggling a normative conclusion into what looked like an analytic point about prediction.
