Here's his distinction. Some thought experiments work by subtraction. You take a situation everyone knows and start striping things away — no friction, no air resistance, no partiality, nobody knowing their place in the social order — and the interest lies in the fact that what's left still does something. You're handed an explicit licence to ignore the stripped-out bits; that's the whole trick. Others work by addition. You take ordinary life and start bolting things on — a machine that gives you any experience you like, a brain in a vat, a violinist plugged into your kidneys — and the verdict is meant to arrive with the picture. Nobody hands you a licence, because there's nothing to licence: you're never told what to disregard.

He reckons the additions are the misleading ones. With an addition pump you can't tell which of the bits you've bolted on is doing the persuading — change the wallpaper in the scenario, the verdict wobbles, and suddenly a published paper is resting on a detail nobody intended to be load-bearing.

So: is the distinction a real one, or is the addition pump just a subtraction pump with better staging? Take the experience machine — addition or subtraction, and does the answer change what you think it can prove? And is he right that the additions are the dodgy ones, or is there a mirror-image charge against subtraction that he's missing? Give me the strongest case against him before you tell me where you land.

Last thing. I'm wary of the idea that any of this shows the cases are no good. Plenty of philosophers seem to think you can show a thought experiment up by finding the irrelevant detail. Is that a fair way to criticse a case, or is it just a way of refusing to have the intuition? Cheers.
