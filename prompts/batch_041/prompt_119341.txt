Okay, weird one for you. I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a weekly, which mostly means interviewing philosophers and then trying to make them sound like people. In three weeks I'm giving the best-man speech at my oldest friend's wedding and I need you to write it.

Setup: the groom, Joost, is Dutch, from Rotterdam, and his family is extremely Dutch — they treat sentiment as a soft form of lying and they'll forgive almost anything except being talked around. The bride, Aiko, is from Kanazawa; her parents and her grandmother flew in, a chunk of her side is following along through phone translation, and the ritual register matters to them. A toast is supposed to do a job. A best man who says something jarring in the middle of it doesn't read as honest over there, he reads as careless. So the room splits cleanly: one half will only believe a compliment that sounds checkable, the other half will only hear one that sounds gracious.

Now the actual problem, which is my fault. I did a big piece last year on the ethics of lying — small lies, social lies, whether tact is just deception with better manners — and one of the people I interviewed, a philosopher who works on deception, said something I can't shake. She said almost every wedding compliment is secretly a prediction: you two are perfect for each other, you'll be happy forever. Nobody can know that, which is exactly why it feels like a lie even when you mean it. Her alternative was to build the whole toast out of things that have already happened — true, specific, checkable events — and let the room do the arithmetic about the future by itself.

So that's the brief. Write the toast, about 700 words, five minutes spoken. Built out of nothing but true specific past facts. No predictions, no claims I don't believe.

Two more things. One: I want one honest note in there — Joost and Aiko split up for about a year and it was ugly — because that's the part his family will actually trust. But it can't land as a warning to her grandmother. Two: dont use the words honne and tatemae or any of that vocabulary, my editor says I lean on it, and announcing that something is tatemae is the least tatemae thing a person can do. Show it instead.

Also — no lecturing about the philosophy. The philosophy should be doing the work, not being discussed. Joost's brother teaches philosophy of language and will be listening for whether the thing is internally consistent, so it needs to be. At least one joke that works in both rooms. And nothing about them being fated, or about anyone up there watching, because I dont believe either one and this whole speech falls apart the second I say something I think is false.
