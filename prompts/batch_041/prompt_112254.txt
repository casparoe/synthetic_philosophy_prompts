The proposition. Call a view *pure expressivist* if it holds that a declarative sentence containing a thin normative predicate (for example, "lying is wrong") conventionally expresses a non-belief, non-representational state — disapproval, a plan, a norm-acceptance — and does *not* express a descriptive belief with a truth-apt normative content. A pure expressivist owes an account of at least three things: (i) *negation and inconsistency*: why "lying is not wrong" is something more than the absence of disapproval, and why sincerely accepting it alongside "lying is wrong" is incoherent rather than merely a change of mind; (ii) *embedding and validity*: why arguments whose validity turns on normative sentences occurring inside conditionals, disjunctions, "unless", and "it is true that" come out valid; and (iii) *attitude and factive embeddings*: what "Sasha hopes that lying is wrong", "I doubt that lying is wrong", "she wonders whether lying is wrong", and "he regrets that lying is wrong" mean, given that hoping, doubting, wondering, and regretting take propositional objects — and that factive ones appear to presuppose the truth of their complement.

The question. What is your credence that the Frege–Geach family of problems has a solution that is *pure expressivist* in the above sense — that is, that (i), (ii), and (iii) are jointly satisfied without the theory's assigning truth-apt descriptive content to normative sentences and without its collapsing into a form of cognitivism?
