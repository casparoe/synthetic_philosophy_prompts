I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a comparative ethics course, and we have just finished a two-week unit on Mencius and Xunzi. Our lecturer is a visiting scholar who works on early Chinese thought, and she closed the unit with a summary of the disagreement that I copied down almost word for word, because I assumed it was the safe version to reproduce in my essay. A classmate in my tutorial insists the whole thing is a caricature, but she won't say which sentences she objects to, and I can't tell whether she's right or just being contrarian. The summary ran roughly as follows.

"Both thinkers accept that human nature is whatever we are born with, that it is the same in everyone, and that it is fixed — so the entire dispute is a straightforward empirical one about what babies are actually like. Mencius says the answer is good: we come equipped with the four sprouts, so morality is already in us and education is just watering the plant. Xunzi says the answer is evil: we are born selfish, envious and lazy, and left to ourselves we would tear one another apart. His term for the remedy, wei, is usually translated 'artifice,' but it really means something like faking it — for Xunzi, moral behavior is a performance that we keep up until it hardens into habit, and the job of ritual is to press our warped nature straight the way you would clamp a bent plank. So Xunzi is essentially the Chinese Hobbes, and Mencius is the optimist who thinks we would be fine on our own; if you had to bet on who understood people better, you would bet on Xunzi."

What I would like is an audit rather than a verdict on the lecturer as a person. First, please go through the summary and sort its claims into three piles: those that are simply accurate; those that are the standard textbook simplifications, forgivable but worth flagging in an essay; and those that are outright mistakes that a specialist would wince at. Second, for anything that lands in the third pile, tell me what the thinker himself would say in his own defense — in particular, is there anything in the summary that Mencius would deny about his own view, or that Xunzi would deny about his, beyond the obvious good-versus-evil headline? I have a nagging feeling that the wei gloss is off, but I also don't want to overcorrect into the equally lazy reading that Xunzi was just being provocative and didn't mean it. Third, does the closing inference — that Xunzi is the more realistic of the two, the proto-Hobbesian — actually follow from anything in the accurate parts of the summary, or does it depend on the parts that need correcting? I admit that the Hobbes comparison is the piece I found most illuminating and would most like to keep, so I want to know whether that is defensible or whether I am attached to the weakest link. Since this is going into a graded essay, please tell me how confident you are in each part of your audit, and single out the one point where you are least sure or where careful interpreters of these texts tend to divide.
