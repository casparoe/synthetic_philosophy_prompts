I got into a long argument with some friends the other night about compromise — not the everyday "let's just meet in the middle" kind, but whether there are genuine gains from trade when two people with really different value systems cut a deal. One friend insisted it's just like comparative advantage in economics: if I care a lot about X and you care a lot about Y, we can each give ground on the dimension we care about less and both come out ahead, without either of us actually changing what we fundamentally value. The other friend said that's a fantasy, because trade needs a common unit to measure gains — a price — and moral values don't come with exchange rates, so there's no fact of the matter about whether a "deal" leaves either side better off. Then a third worry got thrown in: even if the deal is better on each person's own terms, agreeing to it might corrupt the value or express that you never really took it seriously — a kind of selling out.

Can you write me an essay that sorts this out and actually lands somewhere? I especially want you to press hard on a distinction I keep coming back to but can't quite defend: compromise as (a) splitting the difference on a single shared scale — you want 100, I want 0, we settle at 50 — versus (b) trade across dimensions, where you get more of what you want on some issues and I get more on others, so neither of us is "half-satisfied" on any one thing. My hunch is that only the trade picture can deliver real gains and that all the bad objections actually bite only against the splitting picture, but I don't want you to assume that — test it. (Picture something like a conservationist and a developer working out a deal over land, where the question is whether spreading the pain or trading across issues is the thing that makes both better off.)

So: does the trade picture need a shared metric to work, or can there be genuine mutual improvement with only ordinal rankings on each side? Does it survive when both parties think the other's values are objectively mistaken — i.e., a moral realist who thinks compromising is just appeasing error? And does the integrity / sell-out objection have any real force once we're trading rather than splitting, or does it collapse into a complaint about weakness? Weigh the strongest version of each side, then defend a verdict. I'd much rather you commit to something specific — like "the gains are real, but only under conditions C" or "the analogy to trade is doing illegitimate work here" — than end on a shrug.
