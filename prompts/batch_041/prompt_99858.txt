The book's summary runs roughly as follows: Dennett holds that the self is an illusion. There is no inner thing that thinks and feels; the brain merely tells itself a story, and the self is that story. Like the center of gravity, the self is a useful fiction: it helps us predict behavior, but nothing in nature actually answers to it. Since the self is only a narrative construction, it is not a natural kind, and Dennett concludes that the traditional notion of a responsible agent is a myth we could discard. I suspect this is not entirely fair, but I do not know enough to say where it goes wrong. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what Dennett would accept, what he would qualify, and what he would reject. In particular, does the center-of-gravity analogy support the book's conclusion that the self is unreal, or does it make the self an abstract object with a genuine kind of reality? Does Dennett posit an inner storyteller, or is the narrative authorless? And does his view really undermine responsibility, or does it relocate it? I would like to know which parts are accurate, which are standard simplifications, and which are misreadings.
