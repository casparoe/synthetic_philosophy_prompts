You are to act as a philosopher of the historical and social sciences with a solid working knowledge of both the archaeology of complex societies and the conceptual disputes over how to describe large-scale social change. I am assembling a graduate seminar on the concept of collapse, and before we read any particular account of why societies fail — ecological, institutional, climatic, or skeptical — I want the specification first: what any adequate theory of societal collapse would have to deliver. Think this through carefully before you commit to anything.

Begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, the different jobs the word "collapse" is asked to do in scholarship and in popular writing, and the different sorts of episode it gets stretched over. Then turn that into a clean desiderata list. Work from what you already know; do not give me citations, reading lists, or named schools, since I am asking for the criteria rather than the contenders.

Fix the purpose as follows: the theory is to be adequate for a comparative inquiry that must both adjudicate whether a given historical episode counts as a collapse and explain why it happened. With that purpose in view, the list should cover at least:

- the cases it must get right — among them the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and any case you take to be a decisive counterexample to a popular generalization;
- the distinctions it may not flatten: collapse versus decline, versus dispersal, versus replacement, versus the mere loss of a written record; and the prior question of what the unit of analysis is — a polity, a civilization, an exchange network, a mode of subsistence;
- the empirical results it cannot contradict, drawn from archaeology, paleoclimate, and demography, and — this is one I especially want you to press on — the fact that the evidence for collapse is itself a product of the phenomenon, since collapsing societies stop producing records, so the theory must not be circular with respect to its own evidence base.

For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

Then do the two things I most want. First, identify any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, I expect a tension between the demand for a thin, cross-culturally comparable metric — energy capture, social complexity, institutional capacity — and the demand that the theory respect what the people in question valued, since on some accounts a change counts as a collapse only relative to a standard the actors themselves need not have held. If the list is jointly unsatisfiable, say which requirement you would sacrifice and what that costs. Second, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Finally, briefly — a short paragraph or a small table — indicate how four broad families of account fare against the list: resource or ecological overshoot, elite and institutional breakdown, external shock (climate, disease, invasion), and the skeptical view that "collapse" is largely an artifact of how we periodize and narrate. Keep this section short; the criteria are the deliverable.
