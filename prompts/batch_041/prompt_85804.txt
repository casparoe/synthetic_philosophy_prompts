Please compare the absent-minded driver problem with Newcomb's problem. I am not after a restatement of the textbook evidential-versus-causal contrast; I want to know whether the two cases press on the same commitment or on two different ones, and whether either of them survives the fix that is usually offered for the other.

To keep the comparison sharp, separate two features that both cases might be trading on. The first is act–outcome correlation: what you do is correlated with something outside your control, so the value of an act depends on facts that are not downstream of it, as with the predictor in Newcomb's case. The second is act–occasion indistinguishability: you cannot tell which of several occasions you are at, so a single choice has to serve for all of them, as with the driver's two exits. I want to know whether the driver problem is exactly the second feature without the first, and if so, whether it is a genuine counterexample to choosing act by act rather than a disguised instance of the first feature. Along the way, address the objection that the driver, at the moment of deciding, can read his own standing dispositions as evidence about where he is, which threatens to make the case Newcomb-like after all, and say whether that move works. Then give the verdict for someone who wants one decision theory that handles both, and say whether the act/policy distinction here amounts to a conflict between two senses of rationality — the rationality of a choice versus that of a disposition — that arises even with no exotic assumptions.

Close by stating how confident you are in your overall verdict and singling out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
