I have been keeping, for my own use, a ledger of verdicts about ground — not the positions I defend in print, but the ones I actually hold when I am not being careful — and I have reached the point where I suspect they cannot all be had at once. I would like you to reconstruct the principle they add up to, or the small family of rival principles, test each against the cases, and tell me which of my verdicts has to go.

By ground I mean the non-causal, explanatory relation that holds one way between facts: the fact that A obtains in virtue of the fact that B. I take it to be irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive, and I take fundamentality to be simply holding without grounds. Here are the verdicts.

1. The singleton whose only member is Socrates: it exists in virtue of Socrates existing, and I want to add that the singleton is nothing over and above Socrates. Socrates is not grounded in his singleton.

2. A set with two members is grounded in the two members taken together, not in either alone; and neither member is grounded in the set.

3. A true disjunction is grounded in its true disjunct alone, the false disjunct contributing nothing. A true conjunction is grounded in both conjuncts.

4. The fact that there is no elephant in this room is grounded — in the complete inventory of what is in the room, together with something to the effect that the inventory is complete. I accept this, though it makes absences look like positive items.

5. The fact that there are no unicorns anywhere is not grounded in anything. It simply holds. I cannot say what distinguishes it from the elephant case, and I suspect nothing does.

6. The fact that I did not telephone my mother yesterday is grounded in the facts about what I did do instead — but not in the totality of such facts, since that would ground every omission I ever commit in the whole history of my conduct.

7. A determinate grounds its determinable: the ball's being scarlet grounds its being red, not the reverse, even though the scarlet fact entails the red fact and the red fact does not entail the scarlet fact. So grounding is neither entailment nor mere necessitation.

8. The fact that Hesperus is Phosphorus is grounded in nothing. Identity holds in virtue of nothing at all, though it is necessary if it is true. Contrast this with case 4, where I was content to supply a ground.

9. The fact that the number of chairs in this room is two is grounded in the chairs. The fact that two is even is grounded in nothing. I am unwilling to call it fundamental either; the equation of ungrounded with fundamental seems to me to fail precisely here, and I cannot say why.

10. The fact that this piece of paper is a banknote is grounded in the paper's physical properties together with the practices and attitudes of a community, and it is nothing over and above them — which I find uncomfortable, since the paper by itself is not money.

11. The fact that A grounds B is itself grounded in the natures of A and B; the natures are not further grounded, so the regress stops there.

I have a candidate of my own, which I offer for testing rather than endorsement: A grounds B just when B is nothing over and above A, in the sense that B adds nothing to being beyond what A supplies, with the relation required to be asymmetric, transitive, and explanatory rather than causal. I want to know whether this survives contact with the list, clause by clause — which verdict motivates each clause, and which clause does no work.

Three things, then. First, the principle or principles, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with each clause traced to the case that forces it. Second, the verdicts of the principle on cases not on my list: (i) the fact that the average household in the country contains 1.8 persons — is it grounded in the facts about households, and if so, in which ones? (ii) the fact that the panic caused the collapse — is causation itself a grounding relation, or is the causal fact grounded in non-causal facts, or is it ungrounded? (iii) the fact that the empty set exists. And (iv) a case of your own construction that separates the rival principles. Third, an honest accounting: which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, whether a repair would save it at the cost of a clause that earns its keep elsewhere, and which single verdict you would advise me to abandon if I want a principle that is more than a list of my tastes. Give me the argument rather than a survey; assume I know the standard positions and do not need them summarized, and do not supply citations.
