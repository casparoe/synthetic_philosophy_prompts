I belong to a reading group that has spent several weeks on Hofstadter's strange-loop picture of the self — the idea that the 'I' is a self-referential symbol, a loop in a symbol system that turns back on itself, rather than a substance, a thing, or a ghost. Two members, Ruth and Marek, have now had the same argument three sessions running, and the rest of us cannot settle it. I have no view of my own and have tried to reproduce their exchange faithfully rather than as either of them would restate it.

They agree on the background claim: a self is a pattern a system builds of itself, a representation that is part of what it represents, so that the system's model of itself feeds back into the machinery that produces its behavior.

Ruth: This is a deflationary story, whatever its author intended. A symbol that denotes itself is still only a symbol, and the loop explains how a system could come to have a self-symbol, not how a self could do anything. On this picture the 'I' is real the way a center of mass is real: a convenient bookkeeping device for a pattern that the underlying parts fully determine.

Marek: The center-of-mass analogy helps me more than it helps you. Centers of mass are not fictions; they are the quantities you have to compute with to predict how a body moves, and there are domains in which the micro-level description is useless. The self may be like that: there are things a person does — deliberating, keeping a promise, refusing a temptation — that you cannot explain or predict without the level at which the person's self-model constrains the rest of their cognition.

Ruth: Then you owe me a case in which the loop does work. Take two systems with the same physical history. If the loop is doing causal work, there should be a situation in which the loop-level difference explains a difference in behavior over and above what the physics explains. If there is no such case, then talk of the self constraining the cognition is a redescription, and the explanation is being paid for twice.

Marek: There can be no such case, because the self-model is part of the physical history. But that is true of any high-level property you like, including the center of mass. You are asking the loop to make a difference the physics does not already contain, and no special science can meet that demand.

Ruth: That is my worry sharpened. Your loop exists only relative to someone's reading of the system as self-referential. Self-reference is a property of a description, not of a mechanism. Without a reader inside — something that takes the symbol as being about the system — there is no loop there.

Marek: Self-reference need not be interpretive. A system whose outputs are routed back as inputs to the very machinery that produced them is self-referential in the mechanism, not in our description. Feedback is a physical fact.

Ruth: Then you have changed the subject. Feedback is not self-reference. A thermostat has feedback and no one thinks it has an 'I'. The 'I' was supposed to be a tangled hierarchy, a loop at the level of representation, not a thermostat.

Marek: The difference is that in the interesting case the loop runs through the system's model of itself — the model is part of the modeled. And if you press the question of who reads the model, you get the same regress for any account of representation whatsoever; it is not an objection to this one.

Ruth: One more thing. The account also tells us that selves come in degrees: a dog has a thinner 'I' than a person, and a person can be partly present in someone else's head. If that is right, then whatever we call a self is something we can have more or less of, and I do not see how a matter of degree can support the moral weight people want to hang on it.

Marek: It was never meant to carry that weight. It is an existence proof that a physical system can house a self, which is enough to defuse the objection that a naturalistic self is impossible.

Ruth: But notice what has happened. Your story now divides into two claims: that a loop makes a self possible in a physical world, and that a loop makes a self efficacious. You need only the first to answer the question of how a self could be physical, and the first is compatible with everything I have said. So the interesting claim — that the 'I' does something — is quietly dropped.

Marek: The two are not so separable, and in any case the objection to the first claim has to fail for the illusionist line to work. If a self can be a pattern in a physical system, then saying the self is an illusion loses its force, and that is all I have been defending.

Ruth: Then we agree that calling the self real means that there is a useful level of description at which a system can be called a self, and we should stop saying the 'I' resisted the temptation, or held firm, or changed its mind — as though a level of description could be brave.

Marek: That is a verbal quarrel. Every special science uses that idiom and no one calls it fiction.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, in the order it occurred. For each move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, sound as far as it goes, or clearly right, and justify the rating briefly; where a move contains more than one claim, separate them. Then say which of Ruth's central contentions and which of Marek's survive and which do not. Identify anything both of them are missing — a distinction, an objection, or a reframing neither has considered — including whether the possibility/efficacy split can be drawn as cleanly as Ruth draws it, and whether the appeal to special sciences establishes what Marek needs it to establish. Finally, say whether their last exchange is a genuine disagreement or a merely verbal one, and who, if either, has the better of the dispute overall.
