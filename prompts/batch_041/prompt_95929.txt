I'm running a one-off session for my reading group next month on the Kripkenstein rule-following thing — you know, the plus/quus setup, where every sum you've ever actually worked out is equally consistent with your having meant a made-up operation that matches addition everywhere you've ever checked and then goes its own way past the largest sum you've ever done, and the question is what fact about you makes it true that you meant addition. Mixed group, about a dozen, mostly lawyers and a couple who work on machine learning, no philosophy background, 75 minutes, one whiteboard. Don't explain the argument to me, I know it. I want a plan for teaching it.

First the spine: what has to be in place before the skeptic's challenge lands, and in what order, so that the challenge feels forced rather than cheap. My recurring problem is that people shrug and say well obviously I'd say 68, and I need the bit that makes that reply beside the point to already be in their heads before anyone can say it — tell me exactly where that bit goes.

Then the carrying example. Is the arithmatic the right vehicle, or does the maths make them hear it as a trick about maths? A friend of mine heard it and said it's just overfitting, which I'm fairly sure isn't it but I couldn't say why at the bar. If the arithmetic isn't right for this crowd, what is, and what does the replacement have to have and lack — I half want to use something from the lawyers' world, like what makes a new case count as the same case as a precedent, but I suspect that quietly hands them the answer. And the ML people will want to ask whether a model that predicts the next token is following a rule or just continuing a distribution: let that in or shut it down, and where?

Then the misconceptions. I can already guess a few: that the community view means meaning gets decided by majority vote; that this is scepticism about other minds that more evidence could fix; that it only bites for maths; that it shows meaning is arbitrary; that Kripke is just reporting Wittgenstein. What else will they form, and for each one, do I head it off before it's said or let it land and then take it apart?

Finally, one question to put to the room at the end that tells me whether the arguement landed rather than just impressed them, and what I should openly refuse to go into at this level — I'm thinking the attribution fight and the private language stuff, but say so if I'm cutting the wrong things.
