For the past two years I have been a volunteer curator at a small regional museum, and I have just been put on the committee rewriting the labels for our permanent gallery on prehistoric burial practice. The labels we inherited are confident about what people believed: a particular arrangement of grave goods is said to express a belief in an afterlife; a deliberately smashed vessel is said to reflect a ritual of destroying objects for the dead; a scatter of imported beads is said to show long-distance exchange, and a large earthwork is said to show centralized authority. A colleague trained in archaeological science has been pushing back, and I find the general shape of her objection hard to answer. Everything we can actually observe, she points out, is a depositional pattern — what is where, what is broken, what is worn, what is made of what — and she insists the gap runs in both directions. Not only can a single set of beliefs leave very different traces depending on circumstance, but a single trace can be produced by beliefs we would never think to posit, or by no beliefs at all. She calls this the two-way gap, and I have come to think it cuts well beyond wall text, since our decisions about cultural affiliation, about whether an object counts as ceremonial for the purposes of a repatriation claim, and about what we teach our students all seem to rest on exactly this kind of inference.

Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of this gap for the practical work of museums and heritage bodies — how we should write, what we should stop claiming, how object-based evidence should be weighed in affiliation and repatriation decisions, and what it means for how we train the next generation of curators. I want the implications ordered by how much they should actually change what we do rather than by how intellectually interesting they are, and I want you to say plainly which of the implications people commonly draw from this kind of argument do not in fact follow. I have argued about this over dinner with friends who work in law and in medicine, and they both thought the obvious lesson was some version of never claim anything about past minds, which strikes me as too quick and possibly self-defeating for a museum.

If you think the argument is weaker than my colleague and I suppose — for instance, if there are principled ways to rank competing belief-hypotheses that survive the two-way gap, or if the practical stakes of affiliation and repatriation decisions quietly supply constraints that the purely inferential problem lacks — please say so and explain where it fails, since I would rather have a defensible label than a timid one.
