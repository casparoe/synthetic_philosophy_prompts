Ok so I've been reading Hobsbawm and Ranger's Invention of Tradition and Anderson's Imagined Communities for a seminar and now I cant stop seeing this stuff everywhere, so I want to turn it into a piece of satire. Here's what I want.

Write a straight-faced press release from the (fictional) Ministry of Heritage and National Memory of some small invented country — call it Valdoria, or whatever sounds right — announcing that it is "restoring" the nation's 1,400-year-old Festival of the Unbroken Chain, which will henceforth be celebrated on the third Tuesday of every September. Maybe 600–700 words, full bureaucratic deadpan. No winking, no jokes with a little flag on them — if a reader can tell where the joke is before the punchline lands, I've failed.

The funny has to come from the theory being *right*, not from inventing nonsense. So the festival gets described as ancient and unchanging, while every concrete detail of it turns out to be datable to the last few years: the "traditional" costume was designed by a steering committee, the "time-honored" anthem carries a copyright line, the "ancestral" recipe was workshopped by a food consultant. Let the ministry do the two classic moves at once — it insists the tradition is organic and descended from time immemorial, and it also openly describes having manufactured the whole thing last fiscal year (a budget line, a logo, a print run, a subcommittee). It should treat rival nations' traditions as obviously fabricated while its own is the real thing.

Work in, without ever naming them as such, the things Anderson and Hobsbawm actually stress: that the tradition is a response to a brand-new situation dressed up as continuity with an old one; that it spreads through print and mass reproduction; that the census, the map, and the museum arrive together as instruments for "imagining" the community; that the nation is imagined as both limited and sovereign. Give it a "Fact Sheet" section where the founding date keeps being restated with absurd precision. And let it quietly expose the standard objection — if every nation is invented, what's so bad about inventing one? — by having the spokesperson insist that only the *other* nations' traditions are invented, and that Valdoria's is simply... real.

Don't quote Hobsbawm or Anderson, and don't put words in their mouths — I don't want fake quotes floating around. After the press release, tack on a short separate note (clearly not part of the piece) about where the satire takes liberties with their actual claims or leans on a contested reading, say how confident you are in that note, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
