I'm a third-year graduate student in cognitive science, writing a dissertation on whether the special sciences earn their keep by tracking patterns that are real in a sense the physical sciences cannot do without. My advisor is a committed Dennettian; at reading group last week a physicist friend remarked that a real pattern is just a useful fiction with better public relations, and I found I could not say precisely what an account of patternhood would have to deliver in order to answer him. So before I write another word, I want the specification on the table: the desiderata that any theory of what it is for a pattern to be real must satisfy. Work within the three stances — physical, design, intentional — as the framework, but do not assume any particular answer about how they relate to one another; I want that relation to be something the theory is required to settle, not a premise it may take for granted.

For every requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it. I want, first, the cases the account must get right: the temperature and pressure of a gas; a person's beliefs as opposed to the firing of her neurons; a chess-playing program; the orbital regularities of the planets; a statistically robust but wholly accidental regularity; and a pattern that is unmistakable to one observer and invisible to another. Second, the distinctions it must not collapse: real versus merely useful; a pattern versus a mere aggregate or list; a pattern's identity versus its salience; being relative to a stance versus being dependent on a stance in a way that makes the pattern arbitrary; and a pattern that predicts well, one that explains, and one that merely co-occurs with what it seems to explain. Third, the results from outside philosophy that it must be consistent with: that any compression or description-length measure is relative to a chosen description language, so that no compression scheme is privileged; that multiple realization is the norm rather than the exception in the special sciences; and that the predictions generated from the intentional stance are, as a matter of fact, strikingly accurate. Then flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect there is at least one, and it is the part of the exercise I care about most — and tell me which single requirement, taken seriously, eliminates the greatest number of otherwise attractive accounts. If it is cheap to do so, add a few lines on how a compression-based account and a causal-explanatory account fare against the list; keep that part brief, since the criteria are what I am after and I do not need a reading list.
