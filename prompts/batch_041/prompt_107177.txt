I am a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I have constructed an argument that I now find too strong in a way I cannot diagnose. It generalizes two ideas I am committed to. The first is the Darwinian inversion: adaptive competence can be produced by a blind selective process that comprehends nothing, so that a competence is explained without presupposing comprehension anywhere in the explanation. The second is the Turing inversion: the competence of reasoning can be produced by an uncomprehending mechanical procedure. I want to keep both. Here is what I built from them.

(1) Stated at full generality, the two inversions license this: for any competence a system displays, there exists a mechanistic process — selection, computation, or the like — that produces that competence without itself comprehending what it does.

(2) Comprehension, understood as the capacity to represent reasons and to be answerable to them, is itself a competence; it is something a system does, and does well or badly.

(3) Applying (1) to that competence: there exists a mechanistic process that produces comprehension without itself comprehending.

(4) A capacity produced by a process that does not comprehend cannot be genuine comprehension; at most it is the appearance of it. Nothing can confer what it does not possess, and a non-comprehending process has no comprehension to confer.

(5) Hence whatever we call comprehension in ourselves — understanding a proof, grasping a reason, seeing an inference to be valid — is produced by a non-comprehending process and so is not genuine comprehension.

(6) Hence no one ever comprehends anything; comprehension is an illusion.

(7) The conclusion then bites on the argument itself: I can follow steps (1) through (6) only by comprehending the premises and seeing that the conclusion follows. If (6) holds, no such comprehending occurs, and the argument cannot be grasped by anyone, myself included.

Run the same pattern on vision, or on a chess engine's evaluation of a position, and it shows those are not genuine either, which is absurd.

A colleague tells me the fault lies in (1): that I have inflated two existence results about particular competences into a universal law, and that the inversions were never meant to reach comprehension. I suspect the fault lies later, at (4): that I have confused a fact about the producing process with a fact about the product. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would like to keep both inversions. If (4) is the culprit, describe the defensible replacement and say what it obliges me to claim about how comprehension can be real while still arising from something that does not comprehend. If (1) is the culprit, tell me precisely what the inversions entitle me to and what the generalization was smuggling in. Finally, tell me whether the self-application in (7) is a genuine difficulty or a cheap trick, and why.
