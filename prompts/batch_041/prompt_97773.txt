I'm not an academic, I just read philosophy on my own, and there's a line I keep bumping into about Hobbes: that he wanted to do politics the way Euclid did geometry — prove the state like a theorem, so that someone who disagrees about politics is making a mistake of the same kind as getting a sum wrong. I've seen it used to explain why he's so confident, and why he seems to think that once you've accepted the definitions there's nothing left to argue about.

Take the role of someone who knows Hobbes's actual texts well and also knows how geometry was understood in his day. Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm the candidate correspondences out loud — I'd rather see you float a few and then pick than just assert one.

Here's what I want. (1) Lay out the mapping: if politics is supposed to run like geometry, what plays the role of the axioms, the definitions, the drawing/construction, the proof, the thing proved, and the reader who checks the proof? (2) Tell me where the mapping breaks. I have a hunch you'll need to say something about whether Hobbes thought the definitions were just stipulations we get to set, because if we invent them, that seems to wreck the picture of a proof forcing itself on you.
