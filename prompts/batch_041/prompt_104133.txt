I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar — 14 students, 90 minutes, ordinary seminar room with movable chairs, no tech — and the Grice session is coming up. I don't want to stand there and list the maxims while they nod. I want to run something for the first 55-60 minutes where they do it with their hands, and spend the rest mapping what happened onto the theory. Here's the shape I've been picturing; I want you to design the actual thing, including the parts I havent thought through.

Round one: everyone gets a private state card (something the others can't see — the bridge is out, the parcel is in the wrong locker, the results are bad, we're out of coffee) and a handful of coloured stones. They have to get a partner to take a specific action. In this round I want them to succeed without anyone meaning anything: I'll set the room up so the stones are already lying in patterns that track the states, so a partner can just read the situation off the environment, the way you read measles off spots. Nobody intends anything and the right action still happens.

Round two, I take that away. The environment no longer determines the state, and the only way the partner can get it is by reasoning that the speaker put the stone there because they wanted them to know. I want the room to feel the difference between those two rounds before anyone says the word meaning.

Then the traps, which is where I need the most help. I want at least one case where a partner infers the right state from a stone that got there by accident, or from a plant who isn't trying to communicate at all — right answer, no meaning. And at least one case where a speaker really does intend to convey something, the partner lands on the right content, but lands on it for the wrong reason (a pattern, a coincidence, a private hunch) rather than by recognizing that the speaker meant it. I have a vague plan for operationalizing by-recognizing-the-intention: whenever a partner acts, they fill in a little reason slip, and the pair only scores if the reason cites the speaker's intention. Help me make that rule actually work in a room full of people, because I can see it going bureacratic and turning into paperwork.

Round three, the literal sentences come in. Give them a small fixed menu of things they're allowed to say out loud, most of them technically true and useless, plus the stones. The interesting moves should now be choices among things that are all true, where the choice itself carries the message. And I want a rule where someone can add a tag along the lines of 'and I don't mean to suggest that...' and the partner has to decide whether the suggestion was ever there in the first place — I want them to find out that some of these inferences can be taken back and some can't.

I also want a deliberate-breach move: someone is allowed to break the rules of the game on purpose, obviously, and the partner is meant to treat the blatant breach as itself informative — as against a quiet accidental breach, which should just produce confusion. I want them to feel that difference too.

Debrief: map the moments onto the natural/non-natural meaning distinction, the reflexive-intention story about speaker meaning, the gap between what's said and what's implicated, calculability, cancellability, and the flouting/violating contrast. They've done the reading for this week but we won't have discussed it, so the activity has to land before the vocabulary does. Don't let me name any of it up front.

What I need from you: the materials (I'd rather use stones, cards and paper than an app), the exact rules and payoff scheme with no real money, a running order with times for the 60 minutes, how many plants I need and how to brief them without tipping the room, the debrief questions in the order I should ask them, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do when it does, and an honest note on which part of the picture this can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. I already suspect that a game where nobody hears you is a game where nothing happens, and that Grice doesn't think meaning needs an audience at all — tell me whether that's the right thing to worry about, and what else the activity will quietly get wrong.

One more thing: they will almost certainly try to just agree on a code in round two. I don't know whether to let them, or how to stop that from swallowing the point. Advice please.
