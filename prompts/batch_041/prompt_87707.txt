"Frankfurt's target is the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, which says that a person is morally responsible for what they did only if they could have done otherwise.

His counterexample runs like this. Black wants Jones to perform a certain action, say to vote for a particular candidate. Black has secretly wired Jones's brain so that he can both monitor Jones's deliberations and, if it comes to it, take over. The arrangement is conditional: if Jones is about to decide against voting for the candidate, Black will step in and make him decide in favor. If Jones decides in favor on his own, Black does nothing at all. As it happens, that's exactly what occurs. Jones weighs his reasons and decides to vote for the candidate himself, and the device is never triggered.

Still, Jones could not have done otherwise. Given the setup, the option of deciding against the candidate was never genuinely open to him; had he leaned that way, Black would have intervened and produced the very same decision. So we have a case where someone is morally responsible for something even though he could not have done otherwise. That's a direct counterexample to PAP, and Frankfurt concludes the principle is false.

I think the real significance of this is that it shows determinism is compatible with moral responsibility. If responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise, then the worry that our actions are causally determined by events in the distant past doesn't undermine responsibility, and the incompatibilist's case against compatibilism falls apart.

The best-known reply is the flicker of freedom defense. According to it, cases like this never really strip away all the alternatives, because even if Jones couldn't have decided differently in the situation as described, there's a nearby possible world in which Black never installed the device and Jones was free to decide either way. So the alternative possibility is still there, just not in the actual scenario, and PAP survives. But this seems to me to just restate PAP rather than defend it — of course a world without the device contains alternatives, that's precisely why we added the device — so the objection never gets off the ground.

In the end I'd say the counterexample is a strong challenge to PAP but not a knockdown, since it doesn't really tell us anything about the free will problem as a whole."
