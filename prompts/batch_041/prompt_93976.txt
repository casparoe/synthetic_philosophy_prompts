I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for our department's decision-theory reading group, and I want to build it around the Pasadena game. Here is the game, so we are working with the same object: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on the n-th toss, the prize is 2ⁿ/n dollars, paid to you if n is odd and taken from you if n is even. Summing the probability-weighted payoffs in the natural order gives the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, but because the terms are not absolutely summable the same sum can be made to come out at any real value you please by reordering it, and the game's expectation is therefore undefined rather than infinite. Almost surely you receive or pay a finite amount; the trouble is entirely in what it means to evaluate the game in advance.

The audience will be philosophers and economists who are entirely comfortable with expected value and with the St Petersburg game, and who will have seen the Pasadena game mentioned but not worked through it. Do not explain the paradox to me; plan how I should present it. I want the dependency structure worked out: which idea has to be in place before the next one can even be stated, and why that order rather than the obvious one, given that these people already believe they understand expectation. Tell me which single example should carry the session and what it must have and lack; if the Pasadena game itself is not the right carrying example, say what is, and if the St Petersburg game should be kept in reserve rather than used as the warm-up, say so and why. Map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form -- I am expecting things like 'an undefined expectation just means the game is worth zero', 'undefined means we cannot decide, so any preference is permissible', and 'we would simply simulate it and the sample average settles the matter' -- and identify precisely where in the sequence each needs to be headed off. I would also like a second, structurally analogous case from outside probability, in which a quantity looks perfectly well defined until you notice that the order of an operation was doing illicit work, to be introduced late as a mirror; choose it yourself and justify the choice. Finish with one question I can put to the room that would show whether they have grasped the distinction between a value that is infinite and a value that is undefined, and a short list of what I should explicitly refuse to pursue at this level and say so out loud. Assume no board beyond a whiteboard, and no handouts.
