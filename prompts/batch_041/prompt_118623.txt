I sit on the ethics committee at a mid-size hospital and we're stuck on a case that has turned into a fight about a metaphor, and I'd like your help taking the metaphor apart.

Patient is in his late 70s, moderately advanced dementia. Mobile, recognises his daughter, and on a good day seems to be enjoying himself. Years ago, fully lucid, he signed a directive refusing heroic measures and specifically refusing antibiotics for pneumonia — he'd watched a parent die that way and was adamant he didn't want to live like that. He now has pneumonia. He can't follow the question. He's not in distress. Yesterday he said something that his daughter hears as "I don't want to die," and honestly none of us can tell if it's that or he wants the IV out.

Our chair keeps invoking Ulysses and the mast. He lashed himself down before the sirens started, the crew's job is to row on and not untie him no matter how he carries on, and that's what a directive is. The conclusion she draws is that the directive settles it, period, and that what the family wants is beside the point.

I want to know if that story can carry that much weight. Give me the mapping first — what plays Ulysses, the sirens, the ropes, the crew, Ithaca — and then where it breaks. What I keep coming back to is that the chair is importing features of the poem without noticing: Ulysses is tied up in order to reach a goal that the man on the far side of the song still holds (he wants to have heard it and got home), and the song is a passing temptation rather than what he becomes. Neither of those obviously holds here. There's also the fact that in the story the crew are just hands — they aren't being asked to decide anything about him — whereas our nurses and I are.

Then: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? And tell me which reading of a directive the Ulysses picture actually supports — that it's a promise he made to his future self, or just evidence of what he'd probably have wanted, defeasible when the evidence runs the other way — because half the committee is on the second reading and it changes the vote. If there's a better comparison for what's really going on here, I'd like that too.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate the case back to me.
