I've been going through the General Scholium at the end of Newton's Principia with a small reading group — we started it because someone in our Discord kept insisting Newton was basically a deist who tacked God on at the end, and I wanted to actually look at what he wrote rather than relitigate it from vibes. Most of it I can follow at the level of "the solar system is too tidy to be an accident". But there's a stretch near the end where he starts talking about the word "God" as if it were a job title, and I genuinely can't tell whether I'm missing an argument or whether the argument is just odd. Here it is, in the English translation I've got:

This Being governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all; and on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God παντοκράτωρ, or Universal Ruler; for God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants. The Supreme God is a Being eternal, infinite, absolutely perfect; but a being, however perfect, without dominion, cannot be said to be Lord God; for we say, my God, your God, the God of Israel, the God of Gods, and Lord of Lords; but we do not say, my Eternal, your Eternal, the Eternal of Israel, the Eternal of Gods; we do not say, my Infinite, or my Perfect: these are titles which have no respect to servants.

What I want help with is the logic of the thing. He seems to be saying that God's being God — being Lord — is constituted by dominion, and that dominion is a relation to servants, so that "God" is a relative word in something like the way "king" is; and then he treats that as a reason to prefer "Lord" over "Eternal", "Infinite", "Perfect", on the grounds that the latter titles "have no respect to servants". Is that supposed to be a point about how we name God, or a point about what makes God God? And if it's the latter, what happens to the divine attributes when there's no one to rule — does Newton have an answer to that, or does he just not feel the pull of the question? I also can't tell what he's rejecting when he says God governs "not as the soul of the world". I have a vague sense there's a target there — some view on which God is to the universe as the soul is to the body — but I can't tell wether he's objecting to pantheism, to a specific way of making God immanent, or to something about how the world's order gets explained. Could you walk me through the passage at that sort of grain, and flag any places where I'm importing a modern distinction (use and mention, essence versus relation) that he wouldn't have had? I don't need a reading list, I just want to understand the paragraph. British English please.
