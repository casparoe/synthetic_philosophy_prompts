In my school's philosophy club we have been reading an excerpt from Wittgenstein's lectures on religious belief, and one of the members has turned the central idea into a debating move. Whenever another student suggests that some scientific finding counts against a religious claim, he answers that a religious belief is not a hypothesis — that it is not the sort of thing evidence could ever touch — and that science therefore cannot refute it. He sometimes adds that it is a way of life rather than a theory. He says it as though nothing further could be said, and I am never sure whether I ought to be convinced.

What I want is not a defense of the line or an attack on it, but a map of it. It seems to me that the sentence a religious belief is not a hypothesis could be doing several quite different jobs at once: saying something about what a believer is doing when he believes, something about what religious sentences mean, something about what may count as a reason for or against them, or something about whether religion and science are answering the same question at all. Please separate these into the distinct, precise theses the line could be stating, and say of each whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false.

For each thesis I would also like to know what it costs to hold. Is it consistent with the fact that believers argue with one another, treat their beliefs as true, and regard those who disagree as mistaken? Does it leave room for a religious belief to be true or false at all, or does it protect the belief by emptying it of content? Does it allow anything to count in favor of the belief, or does it only prevent anything from counting against it? And is the thesis a description of how religious language actually works, or a recommendation about how it ought to be used?

Finally, tell me which of these readings the conclusion — that science cannot refute religious belief — actually requires, whether that reading is among the defensible ones, and whether my classmate's argument only appears to work because it slides from a modest reading of the premise to a much stronger one. Lay the readings out as a numbered list, each with its verdict and its price, so that I can take it to the next meeting.
