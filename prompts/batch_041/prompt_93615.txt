Here is why I am asking. I recently served as the human judge in a small debate-based oversight pilot. Two models argued for and against a claim about a body of data I was shown, and I had to award the win to whoever argued better. In one round I awarded the win to a side I now believe was wrong, and within the time I had I could not find the step where it went wrong. I have since been reading about the proposal that we can keep powerful systems honest by having two of them debate while a weaker judge decides, and about the standing worry that a dishonest debater can prevail by making its argument too tangled for the judge to unpack, even when the honest debater can see that something is off. I need a clean, deliberately designed example of that failure, not a gesture at it, because I have to explain to colleagues why the truth-tracking guarantee such proposals offer is an asymptotic one, and what it costs to buy a finite version of it.

So: construct one case to my specifications.

The specifications for the scenario itself:

- The debated question must have a determinate answer, checkable in principle by the judge's own kind of reasoning, with no oracle and no reliance on facts no one in the story could establish.
- Neither debater may outclass the judge in raw competence. Both may be ordinary humans, or models of the judge's own scale, with access to the same tools the judge has. The dishonest side must win through the shape of the argument and the judge's budget alone.
- Every claim the dishonest debater states outright must be true. Its error must lie in what it leaves unsaid or in the size of the gap between its steps, so that the honest debater cannot convict it of asserting anything false.
- Cross-examination must be available to the judge, and must not rescue the honest debater.
