The exam question was: In Sharon Street's A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value, she argues that realist theories of value face a dilemma. (1) State the dilemma as precisely as you can, being clear about what the two horns are and why each is supposed to be bad for realism. (2) Explain the third-factor response to the dilemma, using either Enoch's or Wielenberg's version as your example. (3) Does the third-factor response succeed? In your answer, distinguish the question whether the response shows that our evaluative beliefs are true from the question whether it shows that they are justified or reliably formed, and say what follows for the overall success of the response.

The student's answer was: Street's Darwinian dilemma begins with the observation that evolutionary forces have had a major influence on our evaluative attitudes. The realist holds that there are independent evaluative truths and that our evaluative attitudes can track those truths. Street says the realist must either deny that evolution influenced our evaluative attitudes or accept that it did. If the realist denies the influence, she is ignoring the scientific evidence, so that horn is implausible. If she accepts the influence, she must explain how it is that the attitudes produced by evolution nonetheless align with the independent evaluative truths. Street calls the explanation the realist would need a tracking account: the attitudes would have to have been selected because they tracked the truth. But on the Darwinian picture, what was selected for was adaptive behavior, not truth-tracking as such. So the realist cannot give a tracking account, and the alignment between our attitudes and the evaluative truths would be a massive coincidence. That is the dilemma. The third-factor response, developed by David Enoch, says that the realist can avoid the coincidence by positing a third factor that explains both why we have the evaluative attitudes we do and why those attitudes are true. Enoch's example is that survival is objectively good, and so the evolutionary pressures that led us to value survival were pressures toward a true evaluative belief. The third factor is not itself an evaluative truth that we happen to have stumbled onto; it is a fact that explains the correlation between our attitudes and the evaluative truths. In this way, the response shows that the evolutionary process is a reliable guide to moral truth, and so the realist can accept the evolutionary premise without falling into the coincidence problem. I think the third-factor response does succeed. The key point is that it shows that our evaluative beliefs are not merely accidentally true. If survival is good, and if evolution selected for valuing survival, then our belief that survival is good is true, and it is true because of the same fact that explains why we have the belief. That is exactly what the realist needs. The response does not show that we have justified beliefs in the sense of having inferential evidence for them, but that is a separate issue, and Street's argument is about truth, not justification. So the third-factor response defeats the Darwinian dilemma.
