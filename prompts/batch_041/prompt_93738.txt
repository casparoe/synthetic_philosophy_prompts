I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on animal minds and ethics, and I keep running into something I can't square. Our welfare law and regulation extend protection against suffering only within a recognized class of animals: vertebrates almost everywhere, and within the last decade or so a few jurisdictions have brought octopuses and certain crustaceans in as well, on the strength of evidence about their nervous systems and behaviour. Insects remain outside the class entirely, and no one is seriously proposing to bring them in. Yet the same agricultural system that regulates in detail how a few thousand pigs are stunned and slaughtered kills insects in numbers many orders of magnitude larger than the entire vertebrate population of the planet -- through pesticides, harvest machinery, and land clearing -- and no welfare statute, no regulatory impact assessment, and no farm assurance scheme treats those deaths as a welfare cost at all.

I want you to tell me what view of moral status this pattern presupposes. Is it a threshold view of the kind the usual criterion implies -- that what matters is the capacity to suffer, and insects are presumed to fall below the line -- or is it really a kind-based view that treats "animal," or resemblance to us, as the morally relevant category? Or is it an epistemic view (we have no idea what an insect's inner life is like, so nothing follows), or an administrative one (the deaths are unavoidable and untraceable, so they drop out of the calculus)? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and tell me whether the two halves -- the careful inclusion of a cephalopod and the total silence about insects -- are actually consistent with one another, or whether they only look consistent because the numbers are never stated together.

Then apply a proposal from our seminar: a scoring rule under which regulatory impact statements would carry an invertebrate welfare term, computed as (probability of sentience) x (number of individuals affected) x (assumed intensity and duration), with the probability stipulated somewhere between one in a hundred and one in ten thousand. Under that rule, what would have to change in the existing practice, and does any part of it survive? I'd like a committed answer rather than a survey: say which theory you think the practice actually commits to, whether the line it draws is defensible once the arithmetic is on the table, and what you would change if it isn't.
