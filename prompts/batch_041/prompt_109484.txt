ok so I've been on a bit of a Blade Runner kick lately (the 1982 one, obviously) and I keep getting into dumb arguments about it, and I want to think it through properly instead of just vibing. I've been reading rationalist blogs for like a decade now, and one thing that always bugs me in those circles is that people treat "what the author meant" as either obviously irrelevant or obviously decisive, with not much in between — the whole endless fight about canon and whether Word of God counts. Blade Runner seems like a stress test built specifically to break every simple theory of interpretation.

Here's the situation as I understand it. Ridley Scott has said Deckard is a replicant, with the unicorn dream (added in the director's cut, kept in the final cut) and Gaff's little origami unicorn at the end as the tells. Harrison Ford has said he played him as a human, and that the point was that Deckard doesn't know. The theatrical cut has the studio-mandated voiceover and the drive-off-into-the-countryside ending, which I'm pretty sure was literally leftover helicopter footage from The Shining, which is funny and also weirdly relevant. Underneath all of it there's Philip K. Dick's novel, scripts by Hampton Fancher and David Peoples, a studio that wanted something more legible, an actor who by some accounts sandbagged his own narration on purpose. So "the author's intention" isn't one thing here, it's like five things pulling in different directions, and they aren't even the same *kind* of thing — a director's retrospective claim, an actor's performance choices, a screenwriter's draft, a studio's commercial panic.

What I want is a reading of the film through the philosophy of authorial intention: the Barthes/Foucault death-of-the-author side on one hand, and the actual intentionalists on the other — the ones who think meaning does depend on what the author intended, including the versions that soften it with "hypothetical" or "modest" qualifiers. No bibliography needed, just the ideas and roughly who they belong to.

Things I actually want answered:

- Does the film have a determinate answer about whether Deckard is a replicant, or is the indeterminacy itself the intended effect? Can both be true at once without contradiction?
- Whose intention counts, and why? If Scott's claims are retrospective and possibly shaped by what fans wanted to hear, do they have the same authority as what's actually on screen in 1982? And does it matter that Ford's contribution is a *performance* rather than a statement — is acting a kind of authorship or just an instrument of the director's?
- Which cut *is* the film? Picking the final cut feels like it begs the question by letting the director's preferred version be the one that reveals his intention. Is there a non-question-begging way to choose, or is "the film" just whatever we stipulate?
- The film's own subject matter is that you can't tell a replicant from a human, and Deckard's job is basically reading texts — photographs, memories — for signs of authenticity. Does that make the interpretive problem part of the content, so the film is about exactly what we're doing to it? I have a hunch this is right but I can't tell if it's a real argument or just a cute symmetry.
- Where does the film resist all of this? There's got to be some residue — a performance choice, a shot, a bit of production design — that doesn't reduce to anybody's stated intent, and the honest critic just has to say so rather than pretend it fits.

And at the end: what does Blade Runner add to the philosophical debate that the papers don't? My suspicion is there's something here about authorship being distributed across a production, and about how we experience the thing as unified even though it was made by committee — which is sort of the opposite of the usual literary case where there's one person and one text. Tell me if I'm just pattern-matching.

Don't quote lines at me as evidence, I know the film well enough. And don't just tell me the author is dead and anything goes — I want the version of this a careful intentionalist could actually live with.
