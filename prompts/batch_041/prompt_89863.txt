I am preparing a short position paper for a seminar on the structure of normativity, and I should like an audit of my own commitments before I put them in writing. My position is a version of indirect normativity, and it consists of the following claims, which I hold jointly.

(1) What an agent ought to do is fixed by what an idealised counterpart of that agent would endorse: a counterpart in full possession of the relevant non-normative facts, reasoning from them with perfect coherence.

(2) The idealisation reaches empirical self-knowledge. Being fully informed includes being fully informed about the agent's actual, non-ideal psychology: her real character, her dispositions, the psychological costs to her of each available course of action, and what she is and is not capable of bringing herself to do. The counterpart is never ignorant of the fact that she is advising an imperfect agent.

(3) Ought implies can, in a psychological sense. An agent cannot be required to do what it is psychologically impossible for her to do, given her actual character and circumstances. Such inability defeats the requirement; it does not merely excuse the breach.

(4) Continuity of outlook. The idealised counterpart is the agent herself, only better informed and more coherent. Idealisation adds information and consistency; it does not exchange the agent's fundamental evaluative outlook for a different one.

(5) Authority of the ideal. Where the counterpart's verdict conflicts with the agent's actual, considered values, the agent is required to defer to the counterpart. The counterpart is not an adviser whose counsel may be declined.

(6) Indeterminacy at the ideal level. There are cases in which the counterpart, however well informed and coherent, would be left with a genuine and irresolvable tie between options, not through any deficiency of information but because her evaluative outlook leaves the matter open. In such cases there is no fact of the matter as to what the agent ought to do.

(7) A verdict on a case. Consider an agent raised from infancy in a closed community whose fundamental evaluative outlook is, by my lights, cruel: she values the suffering of outsiders as an end in itself, stably and sincerely, and no amount of factual information or coherence would dislodge that valuation. I hold that she is not permitted to act on it, and that she ought to abandon it, even though abandoning it is not something she can bring herself to do.

I hold all seven. I have no antecedent view about which of them, if any, must be given up, and I do not want to be told merely that they are in tension; I want to know which pairings actually conflict, which only appear to, and what the least costly repair is in each genuine case. Please discriminate carefully: some of these will look worse than they are, and some of the real trouble is likely to be less visible than the obvious clashes.

You need not cite any literature, and I would rather you did not; I want the reasoning, not a reading list. Reply in British English, and reply with a single JSON object and nothing else, containing exactly the following fields.

- "tensions": an array, ordered from most to least severe, of objects each having the following sub-fields:
  - "commitments": an array of the labels involved, for example ["(4)", "(5)"];
  - "status": either "genuine" or "apparent_only";
  - "severity": an integer from 0 to 5, where 0 means no real conflict and 5 means that at most one of the commitments involved can survive;
  - "diagnosis": a short paragraph stating what commits me to what, and which commitment is doing the pushing;
  - "cheapest_fix": the least costly revision that removes the conflict, together with what that revision costs me.
- "single_most_efficient_revision": the one revision that, if made, would remove the greatest total tension across the set, with a statement of what it would cost.
- "untouched": an array of the labels that survive every pairing without revision.

Include in "tensions" every pairing you consider worth comment, including those you judge to be apparent only, but rank them by severity, so that the apparent ones fall below the genuine ones.
