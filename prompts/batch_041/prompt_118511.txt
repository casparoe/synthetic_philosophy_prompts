I have been chairing, in an unofficial capacity, a long-running departmental dispute between two colleagues and I would like to force them to write the document they should have produced a decade ago instead of another round of replies. The first holds the broadly Kripke–Putnam line: proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid designators, true identity statements couched in them are metaphysically necessary, and that necessity is discovered a posteriori, with no a priori route to it; the second holds the broadly Jackson–Chalmers line: once the epistemic (primary) and the subjunctive (secondary) intension of a sentence are kept apart, the 'necessary a posteriori' turns out to be a surface phenomenon, since for any such truth there is a corresponding epistemic reading that is knowable a priori, and the appearance of a deep category is an artefact of running the two readings together. They have been talking past each other for years and I suspect at least part of the disagreement is verbal. Please draft the joint statement they could both sign. I want a numbered section of agreed claims — covering rigid designation, the Twin Earth and Hesperus/Phosphorus material, what 'a priori' and 'a posteriori' are here being used to mark (including whether the intended sense is 'justification independent of experience', 'knowable from the armchair once the actual-world facts are fixed', or the stricter 'knowable with no empirical premise at all'), and what both would accept as a discovery in this area; then a section setting out the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept; then the cruxes; and, for each crux, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

To keep the exercise from becoming a rehearsal of the standard literature, I have asked them to include their predictions for three cases the three of us invented together, and these are to be part of the document. (1) Melin: a substance term introduced by ostension to a liquid drawn from a spring; chemistry later finds that the liquid is a fixed-proportion mixture of two compounds, A and B, that every causal power the community ever attributed to 'melin' is a power of the mixture rather than of either component alone, and that A and B also occur separately elsewhere in nature, where they behave quite differently. Does 'melin' designate the mixture, one component, or nothing with a well-defined essence; and what is the modal and epistemic status of 'melin is a mixture of A and B'? (2) Testimony: someone who has never performed any chemistry and accepts the water/H2O identity purely on authority — what, if anything, does that person know, and is it a posteriori knowledge? (3) Revision: a community later abandons the chemistry that fixed the reference of one of its kind terms and adopts a replacement, while continuing to use the old word for everyday purposes; did it discover that its earlier identity claim was false, or did it change the subject? Finally, please say which parts of their dispute you take to be merely verbal and why, and flag any point on which you think neither side has yet earned the right to a verdict.
