So there's this long-running fight about how to explain people who do terrible things in groups, the Browning vs Goldhagen thing. One side says ordinary guys, dropped into the right situation, become killers; the other says no, it took a specific culture of hatred to make them willing, and you can't wave it off as just the situation. And then there's the character angle, which is kind of the boring old view: some people are sadists and the situation just gives them cover. I've read the standard versions of all three a hundred times. What I want is for you to run them on a case they weren't built for: Abu Ghraib.

Map it out carefully. Who even counts as the perpetrator here, the night-shift reservists, the military-intelligence people giving the nudges, the chain of command above them? What were the actual situational features, the boredom, the fear, the lack of supervision, the pressure to soften detainees up for interrogation, the sense that the usual rules didn't apply there? Then do the culture reading, the whole war-on-terror framing, the dehumanization of Arab detainees, the idea that these were the worst of the worst. Then the character reading, and be honest that some of these people look like they were enjoying themselves.

Then the part I actually care about: where does each reading break? Abu Ghraib isn't genocide, so the annihilation logic the culture story needs isn't there, does the theory even apply, or is that a sign it was always narrower than it looked? And the photographs. They posed, they smiled, they traded the pictures around. None of the three readings handles that well. What does that fact do to the situationist story in particular? Does the case help the situationist, hurt it, or show that the whole debate was really about the Holocaust rather than atrocity in general? And say plainly where all three go silent instead of forcing an answer out of them.
