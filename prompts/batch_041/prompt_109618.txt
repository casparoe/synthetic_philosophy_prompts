I am preparing a graduate seminar on Indian epistemology, and I have been working through the Jaina chapter of the Sarva-darśana-saṃgraha, the fourteenth-century compendium attributed to Mādhavācārya, in the Cowell and Gough translation. I want to devote one session to the seven-limbed predication and the doctrine of many-sidedness, but I keep getting stuck on a stretch of the chapter and would like help before I teach it.

The passage I need help with is one I have rendered into my own English from the Sanskrit, smoothing the syntax so that students with no Sanskrit can read it; treat the wording as mine rather than the translators'. My rendering runs roughly as follows. Every real thing possesses an infinite number of properties, and it is apprehended only under some standpoint, so that no single assertion exhausts it. Of a thing, under one standpoint, we may say that it is; under another, that it is not; under another, that it both is and is not; under another, that it is inexpressible; and then, combining these, that it is and is inexpressible, that it is not and is inexpressible, and that it is, is not, and is inexpressible. All seven modes are true, each in its own respect, and whoever asserts any one of them without the qualification in some respect falls into one-sidedness. Applied to a pot: as clay it is, as a pot not yet made it is not, and when one tries to assert both at once without qualification, what one is asserting is said to be inexpressible.

What I cannot get straight is the fourth mode. If the qualification in some respect is doing all the work in the first three modes, then the fourth seems to be either a claim about language, namely that we cannot say everything at once, or a claim about the object, namely that the object taken as a whole resists predication altogether. Which is it, and how does the idea of a thing with infinitely many properties support the reading you favor?

Relatedly, what distinguishes the fourth mode from the seventh? The seventh appears to combine exactly the content of the first, second, and fourth, and I cannot see whether the combination does any work beyond conjunction or whether it is mere bookkeeping.

I am also worried about triviality. If every predicate can be asserted in some respect and denied in some respect, what would it even be for a predication to fail? What constrains which respects are admissible, and what stops the scheme from making every assertion vacuously acceptable?

A colleague in the logic group has suggested that the seven modes are best read as a seven-valued paraconsistent logic, with the inexpressible values functioning as truth-value gaps or gluts. I am suspicious that this is anachronistic, but I cannot articulate why. Is there a defensible reading along these lines, and if not, what is the scheme doing that a many-valued logic is not?

Finally, I keep sliding between three things: many-sidedness as a thesis about the object, the seven-limbed predication as a logic of assertion, and the standpoints as perspectives. I suspect that sliding is itself the source of my confusion about the fourth mode. Can you disentangle them?

I would like a reading I can present to students with no Sanskrit, and I would like you to flag where you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting one, and which parts of my rendering are doing the damage. No citations needed; I want the argument.
