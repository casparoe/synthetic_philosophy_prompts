I am a retired physical chemist who has spent the past six years reading philosophy, and I have been asked to contribute one essay question to the final examination of an upper-level seminar on conceivability and modality. The students will have read the standard material on whether conceivability can serve as a guide to possibility, so a question that can be answered by summarising the received positions would be useless to me. I want them to do some epistemology of their own.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and should not require the students to cite any sources.

I would like the question to turn on the following challenge, which I have come to think is the real crux and which I want the students to confront directly. Treat conceivability as though it were an instrument: when one can conceive of a proposition's being true, the needle registers possibility. Now ask what it would take to calibrate such an instrument — what would have to be true before we could attach any reliability to its readings. It seems we should need independent access to some stock of propositions whose modal status we already know, so that we could compare the needle against the truth. Yet if we had independent access to modal facts of that sort, it is unclear what work the instrument would be doing; and if we have no such access, it is unclear how the instrument could ever be calibrated. The students should decide whether this argument shows that conceivability cannot be a guide to possibility at all, or whether it shows only that conceivability must be understood as something other than a fallible instrument — for instance, as partly constitutive of the modal facts, or as a defeasible heuristic answerable to a posteriori constraints. I want a verdict, not a survey.

The question should require at least one worked case, and I would prefer a case from the natural sciences rather than from the philosophy of mind. The conceivability of a world with different laws of nature — as distinct from merely different values of the constants — strikes me as a good candidate, though you may substitute another scientific case if it serves the calibration challenge better. What matters is that the case tests that challenge, and not merely the familiar distinction between what is conceivable and what is metaphysically possible.

The model answer should show what a first-class script looks like, and the marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a second-class one, the marks each move is worth, and the typical errors that should cost marks. Please write the question, the model answer and the marking scheme in British English.
