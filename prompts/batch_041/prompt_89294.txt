I am a third-year PhD student in early modern philosophy, and I need a piece of sustained satire for a graduate workshop on philosophical genres. The target is not Leibniz the man but the theodicy move as a genre of argument: the strategy of answering objections to particular evils by appeal to the perfection of the whole. What I want is a deadpan parody in the form of a formal pleading — a complaint filed in a divine tribunal by an invented finite soul against the sovereign, complete with a case caption, a docket number you invent, numbered paragraphs, a prayer for relief, and a disposition. Set it in the procedural register of an early modern chancery pleading, or of a modern civil complaint; choose one and hold it throughout. Roughly 900 words for the pleading. The humour has to come from getting the doctrine right, so that someone who works on Leibniz laughs rather than winces: no winking at the reader, no slipping from irony into ordinary grievance, and a reader who knows no Leibniz at all should still be able to follow the case.

The pleading must be answerable, and answered, on Leibniz's own terms, so that the reader feels the cost of the doctrine instead of being told that it is cruel. Four doctrines should do the work, and I would like you to place each of them deliberately before you begin drafting:

First, compossibility. The tribunal's standing rule is that a plaintiff may not plead merely a better world but only a compossible one — a world all of whose constituents can exist together at once. Whatever the plaintiff proposes as an improvement must be shown not to cost something he also wants to keep, and the court should find that it does.

Second, the distinction between an antecedent and a consequent will: what God wills considered in itself, prior to the whole, favours the good of every creature, while the total decree takes the entire series into account. The court should concede the first and rest its judgment on the second, and the plaintiff's reply should make that concession feel like a denial rather than a consolation.

Third, the distinction between metaphysical evil — the imperfection inseparable from any limited creature — and the physical and moral evils actually pleaded. The plaintiff must not be allowed to win by running the three together, but the court must not be allowed to make real suffering vanish into the word imperfection either. The crux of the piece should be a grievance that Leibniz's system genuinely has to concede is a real evil rather than a mere privation.

Fourth, the contingency of the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. On Leibniz's account that claim is a contingent truth, reachable only by an analysis that never terminates, so no finite mind can carry the demonstration through. Let the disposition turn on precisely this: the plaintiff's finite catalogue of particular sufferings cannot discharge a burden that only an infinite analysis could discharge. Then end the piece without telling the reader what to make of that.

Some further constraints. No named living persons. Do not attribute verbatim quotations to Leibniz or to any real text — there are no sources to hand, and invented quotations are worse than none; let the court report the defendant's position in paraphrase. Keep the plaintiff's advocate competent: his arguments should be ones a Leibniz scholar would recognize as serious rather than straw men, and the court's answers should be the answers Leibniz actually has available to him.

After the pleading, add a note of about 200 words, in your own voice and no longer satirical, that (a) says which doctrines you compressed or bent to make the piece work, (b) identifies the reading of the best-possible-world claim on which the judgment depends — in particular whether you treated it as an optimizing claim or a comparative one, and whether the permission of evil is being asked to do more work than the texts can bear — and (c) says what would change your mind about that reading: which argument or which part of the system would make you describe it differently. If you conclude that the complaint cannot be made to fail on Leibniz's own terms, say so there rather than rigging the judgment; a piece that exposes a real gap is more useful to me than one that pretends the system is seamless.

You are a philosopher with a specialist's command of early modern metaphysics and of the theodicy literature. Think this through carefully before you write: spend a few lines working out how each doctrine enters the pleading and where the plaintiff's case is strongest, then discard the plan and write the document.
