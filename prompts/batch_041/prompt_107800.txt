For the past few months I have been running a fortnightly evening discussion group attached to our local philosophy society, and the next session falls to me. The audience is mixed — mostly non-philosophers, a few with physics or engineering backgrounds, a couple of theology graduates — and they are argumentative enough that anything hand-waved will be picked apart within a minute. I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, so I can hold my own on the metaphysics, but I have never had to teach this material to people who arrive without any of the distinctions. The topic I have been given is whether suffering could be located in fundamental physics: whether, if something in the vicinity of panpsychism were true, there would be anything in the physics itself that could amount to a harm. I do not want you to explain the topic to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it in fifty minutes with a whiteboard and no slides, in the form of a teaching plan.

What has to come first, and what genuinely depends on what? I am fairly sure the group cannot even hear the question until they have a working contrast between experience and the functions that usually get confused with it, and until they can separate the claim that experience is fundamental from the further claim that it is ubiquitous, and both of those from the claim that it is unified. But I would like the dependency structure set out properly, with the reason each piece has to precede the next, because I suspect my own instinct about the order is wrong at one point. Then the carrying example: I had planned to build the whole session around an electron, on the grounds that it is maximally simple, but I suspect maximal simplicity is precisely the wrong property for a case meant to bear the weight of suffering, and I would like you to say what the example must have and must lack, whether the electron can be made to work at all, and — if not — what should replace it and what that replacement buys. The misconceptions I expect: that panpsychism is animism, or that it means rocks have opinions; that if experience goes all the way down then moral status automatically follows; that micro-experience and a unified subject are the same thing, so that the combination problem can be waved away or else sinks the whole view; that physics is supposed to report suffering the way it reports charge; and that the ethical question is settled the moment the metaphysical one is. Name the point in the sequence at which each of these should be headed off rather than merely corrected at the end, and say which one I should let them commit to out loud before dismantling it.

One thing I want doing real work rather than being tacked on at the close: what I have been calling the valence gap — the distance between having experience of some sort and having experience that can go better or worse for the creature having it, which seems to need negative valence and perhaps some integration across time and some unit for whom things can go badly. The group will happily grant that a fundamental entity might have something it is like to be it, and then leap straight to the conclusion that the fundamental level is full of victims; I want the plan arranged so that the gap is visible before the leap is available, and I want to know where it sits relative to the combination problem, since I am not sure which of the two should be used to discipline the other. Finish with one question I can put to the group at the end that would distinguish someone who has grasped the shape of the problem from someone who has merely learned which positions are respectable, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — and how to refuse it without the refusal looking like evasion.
