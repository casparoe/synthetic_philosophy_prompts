I'm a TA for a 300-level seminar that's basically an argument about whether history can be a science, and next week it's my turn to run the whole two-hour session myself — 16 students, a mix of history and econ people, several of whom are openly skeptical that anything about the past can be quantified. The reading that week is Peter Turchin's flavor of macrohistory: secular cycles, the structural-demographic picture in which pre-modern agrarian states swing between an integrative phase and a disintigrative one over roughly two or three centuries, driven by population growth pressing against a fixed land base, elite overproduction (far more people with elite aspirations than there are elite positions for them to fill), fiscal strain on the state, and then a violent crisis that clears the board and lets a new elite re-consolidate. I don't want to lecture at them. I want an activity, and I want them to feel two things, in this order. First, that a cycle of that shape can be an emergent consequence of a few mundane local rules — nobody has to intend the cycle, or even notice it while it's happening; it just falls out of people having children, wanting status, and competing for a small number of slots. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: once they've watched the cycle fall out of the rules, I want them to feel how fragile that result is. If you'd nudged one or two of the parameters the oscillation vanishes entirely; and they, sitting inside it and living one round at a time, can't tell which phase they're in until I show them the aggregate graph at the end. The historiographical analogue I'm chasing is the gap between seeing cycles looking backwards over centuries and the people who lived them having no idea, plus the worry that a model which reproduces a cycle may simply have had the cycle built into its assumptions from the start.

So: take the role of an expert in simulation-based teaching and in the philosophy of the historical social sciences, and design this properly. I need roles, materials (assume no computers — index cards, dice, a whiteboard, photocopied state sheets), a round structure, the rules for how land, food, revenue and elite positions get allocated each round, and a timeline that fits two hours with real room for the debrief. Then give me the debrief itself: how to map specific moments of what actually happened onto the parts of the theory, an honest note about what the activity cannot reproduce (I assume the total absence of technological change, contingency, and anything like genuine agency is going to be a problem — tell me how bad it is and how to say so out loud), and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the failure mode where a sharp student spots the cycle around round four and starts gaming it. Start by brainstorming a few candidate core mechanics before you commit to one, and think carefully about the second act, because that's the piece these activities usually omit — I don't want them walking out thinking we've shown that history is predictable.
