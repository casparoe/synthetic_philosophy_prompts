I am the newly appointed director of ethics and compliance at a mid-sized fertility clinic in a US state where commercial gestational surrogacy and compensated egg donation are both lawful, and about a third of our intended parents come from countries where one or both are restricted, criminalized, or effectively unavailable. Since taking the post three months ago — a change that followed my own family's experience with a cross-border arrangement, which is what turned this from an academic question into a weekly one — I have had to rule on one or two such cases a week, usually with under an hour of my time, no philosopher on call, and staff who are clinicians and coordinators with no ethics training. I need a procedure my team and I can actually run.

Build it out of whatever the philosophical literature on commodification, exploitation, reproductive autonomy, and the sale of bodies and body services actually establishes in a form a non-specialist can use. I do not want a survey of positions; I want the load-bearing distinctions — the ones that change the answer rather than decorate it — translated into something operational. Then give me the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

It needs to survive four things I keep running into. First, the cross-border dimension: intended parents who are residents of, or are flying in from, a country where paid surrogacy is banned, and the question of what, if anything, our obligations are to a law under which we do not operate, and whether so-called reproductive tourism is morally distinct from ordinary medical travel or just the same thing with a worse name. Second, the money dimension: the line between compensating a surrogate or donor and inducing her, whether a donor's poverty or debt changes the analysis or merely the optics, and whether the language of altruism does any real work when side payments exist. Third, consent and reversibility: the differences between donating gametes and gestating a child, what a carrier's right to change her mind amounts to, and where enforceability should enter the procedure at all. Fourth, the parties' relationships: relatives and friends as carriers or donors, and single or same-sex intended parents whose home jurisdictions will not recognize the resulting parentage.

Run it on three cases: an intended father who is a resident of a country that criminalizes commercial surrogacy and who wants a gestational carrier in our state; a twenty-three-year-old woman applying to donate eggs for the third time, candidly motivated by the money, which would clear her student debt; and a couple who want the wife's sister as gestational carrier for a payment described as a gift to help her buy a house. At least one of those three should come out against what I would personally prefer, and the write-up should say so rather than bury it. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — the cases it will misclassify, the inputs it needs and will not get, and the point past which I am simply deciding, not applying a rule.

Open with the procedure's bottom line in a single sentence — the one test that does the most work — and then give the reasoning and the full procedure, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
