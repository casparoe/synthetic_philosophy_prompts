I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and I am trying to build a piece that takes the long-running dispute over Occam's razor somewhere genuinely new rather than re-rehearsing the usual opposition between those who treat simplicity as a deep clue to the structure of the world and those who treat it as a merely pragmatic preference dressed up in Bayesian or minimum-description-length clothing. What I want is an essay pitched at the level where the argument actually bites. Solomonoff's universal prior ranks hypotheses by the length of the shortest program that generates them, and the invariance theorem is supposed to make that ranking objective by showing that any two universal machines assign description lengths differing by at most an additive constant. The objection I want taken seriously is that this constant is doing all the work: it is machine-dependent, uncomputable, and in practice plausibly far larger than the complexity differences between any hypotheses we could ever entertain, so the agreement between machines is asymptotic in a limit no working scientist can approach. Worse, one can construct universal machines that invert the ranking of two hypotheses for the first 10^100 bits of evidence, which is to say forever as far as any observation is concerned; the razor would then be pointing the wrong way for the whole history of the universe.

A friend of mine, a machine-learning researcher, has proposed what he calls a two-level razor, and I would like the essay to give it a fair hearing. Rather than fixing a reference machine and minimising description length relative to it, one minimises the sum of the description length of the machine itself and the description length of the hypothesis given that machine, jointly over all machines. He thinks this dissolves the objection, because a machine engineered to make some pet hypothesis cheap is itself expensive to specify, so the arbitrariness pays for itself. I am not convinced, and I suspect the proposal may simply relocate the problem rather than solve it, but I would rather have the argument made properly than have my hunch flattered.

Please write the essay. Set out the controversy, treat my friend's proposal as a serious contender and work out what it really amounts to, and reach a definite verdict on whether Occam's razor can be given an objective, machine-independent justification — or whether the most we can honestly claim is that it is a discipline we impose on inquiry rather than a fact we discover about the world. No survey, no even-handed non-conclusion: drive the argument to a verdict and defend it against the strongest counter-argument you can construct. Roughly 1200 to 1500 words, in British English. Finish with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states your conclusion in one sentence.
