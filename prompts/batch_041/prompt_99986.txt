I'm running a four-week reading group for people who work in archives and digital preservation -- smart, no philosophy background, and they signed up mostly because they secretly want to argue about whether we should be keeping everything forever. Week one is Nietzsche's second untimely meditation, the one about history being useful and harmful to life, with the three flavours (monumental, antiquarian, critical). Don't explain the essay to me -- plan how I should teach it in ninety minutes.

Here's the wrinkle I want the whole thing to hang on: I want them to come out seeing the three modes as three different answers to the same question -- what do we do with the past, given that remembering everything is paralysing -- so the three modes land as three ways of forgetting on purpose, not three hobbies you might have. Is that a fair way to put it, or am I bending him? I'd rather you tell me it's a distortion than let me teach a distortion.

So: what has to be in place before that framing even makes sense to them? What single case should carry the whole thing? I was going to use a local example -- a 1970s council housing estate that's up for demolition, where one camp says it was a triumph of social housing (that's the monumental pull), one says it's heritage and you can't knock it down (antiquarian), and one says it was a monument to bad planning and should come down (critical). Does that case actually have what it needs, or do two of the modes collapse into each other? If it's no good, tell me what a carrying case has to have and what it has to lack.

Which misconceptions will they bring -- I'm guessing "critical history just means being critical of your sources," and "antiquarian means you like old things" -- and at what point in the sequence do I head each one off? And what should I refuse to explain at this level and say so -- I'm thinking the whole Hegelian backdrop, and anything about the eternal return.
