I'm a science journalist at work on a feature about self-deception, and the more reporting I do the more I suspect that the standard ways of framing the problem are framed around the wrong question. My anchor case is a man — call him R — who for about two years has been failing to notice that his business partner has been quietly moving money out of their joint accounts. R avows, sincerely and repeatedly, that the partner is trustworthy, and he is not lying when he says it; there is no audience he is performing for. Yet he declines to bet on the partner's honesty even at generous odds, he "forgets" the meetings where the numbers would be put in front of him, and he has begun quietly keeping copies of documents he insists are unnecessary. Two of my sources read R in opposite ways, and I'd like a dialogue between them that I can actually learn from.

The first holds that R's case only counts as self-deception if R both believes the partner is untrustworthy and believes he is trustworthy — an outright contradiction lodged in a single mind — so all the interesting work lies in explaining how one mind can host that contradiction, whether by splitting into subsystems, by oscillating between the two beliefs over time, by holding the unwelcome one to only a low degree, or by some similar device. The second holds that the contradiction is an artifact of an over-intellectualized notion of belief: on his view R never believes the unwelcome thing at all, he merely registers it, acts on it in some contexts, and keeps it out of the material he is willing to reason with in others, so the interesting work is in saying what registering without believing amounts to and why it should not simply be called believing. I have a half-formed suggestion of my own that I'd like them to take seriously before they dismiss it: that we should stop treating self-deception as a state a person is in and treat it as a practice a person maintains — a standing policy, roughly like keeping a room presentable by never opening the closet — and that the two-ledger picture captures more of R than either of their views does on its own: one ledger recording what has been witnessed, a second recording what is officially on the books, and a settled refusal to audit the gap between them.

Please write the dialogue. Both speakers should be first-rate, and neither should be a strawman or a mouthpiece; they should answer each other's actual points rather than trading positions, and they should flag which parts of their disagreement would survive if they simply agreed on how to use the word belief. I'd also like them to say what I could look for, in how R behaves when no one is asking him about his partner, that would tell me which of them has the better account of him — and to be honest about whether R's case is self-deception at all, or just ordinary motivated inattention that we have given a moralizing label.
