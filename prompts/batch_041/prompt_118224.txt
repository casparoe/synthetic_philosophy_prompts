You are an expert in axiology and the formal structure of value — the kind of philosopher who cares as much about the shape of a value ordering as about the verdicts it issues. I want you to help me assemble the desiderata for a family of theories, and I want you to reason carefully and show your work rather than reach for a stock answer. Begin by brainstorming the candidate requirements and the cases that motivate them before you commit to a final list.

Here is the territory. Consider a view I will call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU), stated as a family rather than a single doctrine so that we do not get stuck on labels. LTNU holds that pleasures and pains lie on a single scale of valence — that they are commensurable, at least locally — but that the scale is not homogeneous. Below some threshold of suffering, positive and negative valence trade off at a finite exchange rate: enough pleasure can outweigh some pain. At or above the threshold, the exchange rate becomes infinite in one direction: no finite amount of pleasure, however great, can compensate for even a tiny increment of above-threshold suffering. Above the threshold, the disvalue of suffering is lexically prior to all positive value.

I am far less interested in defending LTNU than in knowing what any theory in this neighborhood would have to deliver in order to be adequate. So please produce the desiderata — the specification a theory of this kind must meet.

I want the list to cover, at minimum:

- The cases the theory must get right, each with the case that motivates it. Include the two-dials case (a subject whose pleasure and pain are separately adjustable) and the ledge case (intense short-lived suffering for one against mild protracted suffering for many).
- The distinctions it must not collapse: between the amount of suffering and the number of sufferers; between a value being lexically prior and its merely being weighted more heavily; between a genuine threshold and a merely steep but continuous exchange rate; between axiological priority and decision-theoretic priority.
- The formal constraints it must respect or explicitly reject: transitivity and completeness of the betterness relation, representability by a real-valued value function, continuity or the Archimedean condition, and the possibility of ranking options under uncertainty.
- The puzzles it must dissolve rather than stipulate away, with each puzzle stated: the threshold-arbitrariness problem (where the threshold lies and why not elsewhere); the zero-point or commensurability problem (what makes pleasure and pain quantities of the same kind at all, and what fixes their common unit); the lexical cliff (if any above-threshold suffering defeats any finite pleasure, is that a feature of the view or a reductio?); aggregation across persons; and the lottery case (a minute chance of above-threshold suffering against a certainty of enormous pleasure).

For each requirement, say what is behind it — the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp.

The part I most want to see is the tension analysis. Identify any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue for the incompatibility rather than merely gesturing at it. I have a hunch that lexical priority sits badly both with the demand for representability by a real-valued function and with the demand that the theory be usable under uncertainty, and that the commensurability requirement may be in tension with the very idea of a threshold; but do not defer to my hunch — tell me where the real fault lines are, and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories.

Finally, briefly score the standard contenders against your list: classical utilitarianism, prioritarianism, a two-scale incommensurabilist or parity view, and a finite-but-enormous exchange-rate view that refuses true lexicality. I care about the structure of each view, not about who held it, so do not bother with names, dates, or reading lists.
