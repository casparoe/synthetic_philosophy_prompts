I am a novelist, and I have spent the past four years writing a book about Frank Ramsey — the Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who died in 1930, at twenty-six, after doing foundational work in logic, probability, economics, and decision theory, and who translated Wittgenstein's Tractatus as an undergraduate before talking Wittgenstein back into philosophy. King's College, where Ramsey was a fellow, has asked me to give the memorial address at this year's gathering on the anniversary of his death. The address will be delivered in the chapel. That is the first difficulty, and I would like you to write the address with it fully in view: the man was, by his wife's account, a militant atheist, and his younger brother Michael became Archbishop of Canterbury. I do not want the chapel either ignored or made into a stunt.

The room will be split, and the split is the second difficulty. Half the audience knows the technical Ramsey in detail — the combinatorial theorem, the treatment of general propositions and causal laws, the account of conditionals that later philosophers named after him, the subjective-probability framework, the optimal-saving rule, the paper on universals. The other half are family, students, and readers who know only the one page of his that everyone knows, the page about the world drawn in perspective rather than to scale, the foreground occupied by human beings, the stars shrunk to the size of small coins, and the far future's collapse discounted to almost nothing in present value. Some of them come to that page for comfort. I need an address that does not lecture the first half and does not lose the second.

The philosophical content must do structural work, not decorate. What I take from Ramsey is this: meaning and value are not functions of cosmic scale; the perspective is not a distortion to be corrected but the only frame in which anything matters at all; the reflex of consolation that zooms out to the size of the universe is the very thing he was mocking when he said he felt no humility before the vastness of the heavens, since the heavens cannot think or love; and his deflationary remark that philosophy's chief proposition is that philosophy is nonsense — with the insistence that we not then treat it as important nonsense — should govern the ending. The address must not close on an ineffable gesture. It must close on something sayable.

Three further constraints. First, do not undo him at the last minute: no being part of something larger, no living on in his work if he could not have assented to it, no borrowed Christianity. Second, the brother is available to you, but handle him as a novelist would — as a fact about two brothers, not as a sermon. Third, my novel contains an invented scene in which Ramsey and Wittgenstein walk at night and quarrel over whether the stars have any claim on us. The address should set that scene up, use it, and then explicitly step back from it: I want one moment where I, speaking as the novelist, distinguish what I made up from what he actually held, and admit what it costs me to do so in front of people who knew him.

Write the address at roughly eight to nine minutes of speaking pace, in a register that can be spoken aloud in a chapel without either piety or flippancy. Give it one concrete object that stays in view from beginning to end — not an abstraction — and let the largest thing in the room remain a person.
