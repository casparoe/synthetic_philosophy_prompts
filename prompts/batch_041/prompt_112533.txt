I run a ninety-minute session in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: sixteen students, all of whom have had one course on the mind–body problem and none of whom has read anything in consciousness science. I want the session built around an activity rather than a lecture, and I want the students to have run into the theory's central difficulty with their own hands before they hear any of its vocabulary.

The theory is Giulio Tononi's integrated information theory. Three of its commitments need to be felt rather than explained. First: consciousness, on this view, is a matter of a system's causal structure being irreducible to the causal structure of its parts. The measure Φ is defined as the difference between the cause–effect repertoire of the whole system and the repertoire of the least damaging way of cutting that system in two, its minimum information partition; a cut that loses nothing yields Φ = 0, however intricate the system looks from outside. Second: this makes a purely feedforward arrangement come out at zero. Signals run one way, no unit takes and makes a difference to the past of another, some cut between the layers costs nothing, and so on the theory's own terms there is nothing it is like to be that system — even when it produces exactly the same output as a recurrent system of the same size doing the same job. Third: the exclusion postulate allows at most one subject per piece of matter. Of all the nested and overlapping candidate systems you can carve out of a substrate, only the one with the greatest Φ is conscious; a smaller loop inside a larger one is a perfectly real mechanism and not a subject at all.

Please design the activity, with:

- Rules, roles, materials, and a per-round protocol, all buildable from index cards, tape, a whiteboard, and a cardboard screen. No computers, and no one calculates Φ or does arithmetic beyond counting.
- A first phase in which two teams are wired differently — one feedforward, one recurrent — and asked to perform an identical input–output task, so that from the outside the two teams are indistinguishable and neither looks cleverer or more coordinated than the other. Then a cut test: I screen off one part of a team from another and re-run the task. The teams should discover for themselves that one of them has a cut that changes nothing about what the system does, and that this is fatal in a way they did not expect.
- A second phase that makes the exclusion problem concrete: with the recurrent team as the substrate, the students must decide which of the nested candidate systems is the subject — the whole team, the two-person sub-loops inside it, the team plus me standing at the board — and should find that the theory returns a definite verdict and that no one's introspection has anything to say about it. Include the grain question: whether the units are counted as people or as pairs changes which candidate wins.
- A debrief that maps what happened onto integration, information, the minimum information partition, the feedforward consequence, and exclusion, and that supplies the name of the theory and its terms only at that point.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and a frank note on which features of the theory the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
