I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs and forums — I arrived for the Sequences and stayed for the long book-club threads — and I've been trying to use fiction as a stress test for the tidy liberal stories I absorbed as an undergraduate. The tidiest of those stories is the one about John Stuart Mill: the harm principle, experiments in living, the whole liberal package, with the inconvenient fact that Mill spent the better part of his working life as an examiner of Indian correspondence at East India House, defended the Company's administration of India when the Crown took over after the 1857 rebellion, and argued in his writings on representative government that British rule in India was justified as a trust exercised on behalf of a people he did not think were yet fit to govern themselves — a despotism he held legitimate precisely insofar as it aimed at the improvement of the governed. I want to read E. M. Forster's A Passage to India against that theory. The novel is set in a provincial Indian town under the Raj, among British officials who are petty and frightened and Indian characters who are by turns deferential and furious; it turns on the expedition to the Marabar caves, the accusation against Aziz, the trial that follows, and the friendship between Aziz and Fielding that the book pointedly refuses to resolve. My question is not the standard one — that Forster criticizes imperialism — but something sharper: does the novel dramatize a refutation of Mill's colonial theory, or does it instead confirm Mill's own internal warning that a dominant race, far from improving those it governs, is corrupted by its position into exactly the callousness Forster shows?

I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the Millian framework seriously, and that is honest in both directions rather than treating the author as a mouthpiece. Where does the book actually engage Mill's questions — the test of good government as the improvement of the governed, the value of an active and cultivated character, the thought that despotism can be a legitimate instrument of tutelage, the worry that colonial administrators govern in the interest of the settlers rather than the natives? Which scenes or choices carry the weight — the Club and its code, the caves and their echo, Mrs. Moore's collapse of faith, the trial, Fielding's compromises, the closing ride where the landscape itself seems to deny that the two men can be friends yet or there? And where does the reading fail? My instinct is that the caves and the novel's mysticism resist being cashed out as a lesson about colonial policy at all, and that this resistance may itself be making a point Mill's framework cannot accommodate — that something exceeds the political, or that the novel refuses the very notion of a moral ledger his utilitarianism requires. Say so if that is right, and distinguish the places where the novel genuinely complicates the theory from the places where I am simply reading my own interests into it.

Finally, tell me what the novel adds to the argument that Mill's prose and the modern commentators on colonial liberalism do not — whether there is a truth about the colonial relation that only a novel could deliver, and what it is. My book club is reading this next month and I'd like to be able to point to three or four specific scenes rather than waving at the whole thing, so be concrete about which passages do the work, without quoting at length — the text isn't in front of me.
