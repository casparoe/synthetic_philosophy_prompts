I chair the clinical AI review board at a hospital network, and I have to circulate the written opinion that frames Thursday's vote on whether to license a vendor's sepsis-alert model for our wards. The evidence resists a headline number. The model's predictions are good — on the vendor's held-out data it alerts earlier and misses fewer deteriorating patients than our current protocol — but our audit shows that within the training hospitals its score is driven largely by a feature recording that a clinician had already ordered a lab test, that is, by prior suspicion rather than by physiology; its written explanations of individual alerts read as sound step-by-step clinical reasoning, yet feature-attribution checks show those explanations do not track what actually drives the score. The vendor's remedy is to reward the reasoning and not only the final answer, and it has offered a version trained on step-level human ratings of its rationales; in our pilot that version produced rationales our clinicians rated as markedly better while its predictions were no better, and the rationales drifted toward whatever the raters reward. One faction wants to approve the outcome-trained model with monitoring, one wants to require the process-trained model, and a third holds that the distinction is a red herring because at the bedside only the alert matters.

Please draft the opinion: a concrete recommendation and a time frame, what we should require of the vendor and of our own post-deployment monitoring, and an honest statement of the strongest case against the course you recommend. It should also say plainly whether a board like ours can coherently demand process-based supervision of a system whose stated reasoning is not what produces its answers — and if it can, what exactly it would then be supervising, and whether an alert that arrives early by a route we would not endorse should count as the system working.
