I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small town that, after a period of political violence, has adopted an unusual civic practice: each year, on the anniversary of the violence, the town holds a ceremony in which a single name from the conflict is publicly retired — struck from the memorial, removed from the school curriculum, and no longer mentioned in official histories — on the theory that the town cannot heal while it keeps every wound open. My protagonist is the daughter of one of the retired names; she is also the town's archivist. She begins to suspect that the practice, whatever its therapeutic value, has made it impossible for her to know whether her father was a perpetrator, a victim, or both.

I have been reading Avishai Margalit on the ethics of memory and David Rieff on the case for forgetting, and I want to think through what follows from each side for the novel I am writing. Please describe the most important implications of that debate — at its strongest, not its most caricatured — for three decisions I have to make. First, should the town's practice be presented as a genuine moral achievement, a self-deceiving evasion, or something that cannot be classified without further distinctions? Second, is my protagonist's desire to recover the truth a duty she owes to her father, to the town, to herself, or to no one in particular? Third, is the novel's form — a first-person narrative that withholds the father's identity until the final chapter — itself an ethical choice about memory or merely a technical one? I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want the implications that a thoughtful defender of each position would insist on, and the points at which the two positions turn out not to be in disagreement at all.
