I'm writing a seminar paper on Kierkegaard's social criticism — the course is mostly Fear and Trembling and Sickness Unto Death, but the part I actually care about is the socio-political stuff: the review of Gyllembourg's novel (Two Ages, where he sets out "the present age") and the later war with the Danish press, the Corsair business. Here's the reading I'm working with, so you don't have to reconstruct it: his age is one of reflection rather than action, everyone has become a spectator and a judge rather than an agent, and the press manufactures a thing called "the public" which is a pure abstraction — no one is the public, you can't point to it, it has no situation and no simultaneity, and for exactly that reason it does the work of "leveling": a silent, abstract process that flattens every distinction, leaves nobody answerable for anything, and can't be argued with because there's no one there to answer. His remedy is the "single individual" — the one who takes a stand in earnestness instead of dissolving into the abstraction. There's a line I've been quoting at people all semester: "There is no more action or decision in our day than there is perilous delight in swimming in shallow waters."

The case I want to run through it is not the Boston Marathon bombing itself but the week after, April 2013. The FBI puts out photos on the Thursday; within hours there's a mass amateur investigation underway, mostly on Reddit (some subreddit set up for the purpose — don't quote me on the name), with thousands of people listening to the same police scanner feed at the same moment and cross-referencing photos. They settle on a name: Sunil Tripathi, a Brown University student who had gone missing a month earlier and whose family had been putting up posters and running a Facebook page trying to find him. The name gets amplified by big accounts and by actual press outlets — the New York Post had already put two entirely innocent bystanders on its front page as suspected bombers (the headline isn't the point, the innocence is; they later sued). Tripathi's family, still searching for him, got flooded with abuse. His body was found in the Providence River a few days later; he had died before any of this and had nothing to do with it. The real identification came from the FBI, not the crowd. And the week ends with the city locked down and an actual gunfight in Watertown — a genuine action, performed by the state, while the crowd watched.

I want the analysis to run both directions: what the lens actually illuminates in that week — who or what plays the press, whether the people doing this were a public in his sense or a crowd, where leveling shows up (the amateurs treating their own inferences as weight-equal to the Bureau's, the glee of being the one who names him) — and then where the lens fails or misdescribes. Things I can't get past: the crowd had real-time simultaneity, which he says the public can't have, so does a live scanner feed plus a subreddit finally produce the public he said was impossible, or just a faster abstraction? The affect doesn't fit either — he says the age is passionless, and this was nothing like passionless. And the abstraction turned out to have a paper trail: some identifiable actors were legally answerable, the phantom itself wasn't, which looks like half a vindication and half a problem. I also definately want the reflexive question taken seriously — he wrote this while he was himself a public laughingstock in a press feud he did not exactly stay above, so is the theory partly a man's resentment dressed up as diagnosis? I don't need a bibliography and please don't pad with citations; I want the argument. Some of this is genuinely disputed among people who know Kierkegaard well (how much of Two Ages is score-settling, whether leveling is diagnostic or providential, whether the single individual is a politics at all) and some of it isn't, so keep those seperate and don't hand me a contested reading as consensus. Structure it as: "The facts I'm using" (~150 words, flagging anything I've got wrong or that the argument shouldn't lean on), "The mapping" (~500 words), "Where the lens breaks" (~400 words), "What the case does to the theory" (~350 words — confirmation, anomaly, or a case he never anticipated), and "Bottom line" (~150 words, with the settled-versus-contested note at the end).
