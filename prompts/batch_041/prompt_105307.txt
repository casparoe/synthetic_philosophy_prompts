ok so ive been reading rationalist blogs and lurking in forums for like a decade, and the units of selection thing is one of those debates I figured I had basically nailed down (genes, duh, the organism is just a vehicle) until I tried to actually use it to sort out some real cases and everything went mushy.

Heres what I keep bumping into. I think we're asking the phrase 'unit of selection' to do two pretty different jobs, and nobody seperates them:

Job A, call it the counterfactual ledger view. A unit of selection is whatever entity you can meaningfully run the counterfactual on: ask what the world would've looked like if this thing had been different, holding the rest fixed, and the answer shifts trait frequencies over generations. So the unit is just whatever shows up as an accounting entry when the bookkeeping is done properly. Being a unit is a formal property of a model, not a fact about biology.

Job B, call it the design-positing view. A unit of selection is whatever entity you have to posit as the beneficiary when you give a teleological explanation. Wherever you say 'this trait exists because it did X for Y', Y is your unit. Being a unit is a fact about where the design in nature is aimed, and the bookkeeping just has to catch up to it.

These two usually agree, and I suspect they come apart exactly where it gets interesting. So: compare them head to head. What does each get right that the other misses, where do they genuinely diverge, and which one is doing the actual work in the classic gene vs organism vs group fights? Please at least hit meiotic drive and other selfish genetic elements, eusocial insect workers, parent-offspring conflict, and one case of your own choosing where the two views disagree. Then the meta question: if one person held Job A and another held Job B, would they make different predictions or would they just be talking past each other?
