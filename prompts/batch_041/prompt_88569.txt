My opening move. You know the technique they use for women with faulty mitochondria, the so-called three-parent baby stuff. Here's one version of it: they take one of the mother's eggs and one of the father's sperm and fertilize it the ordinary way. Then they do the same thing to a donor egg, with sperm from the same man, so now there's a second fertilized egg sitting there containing the donor's own DNA plus the father's. Then, before the two sets of parental chromosomes have fused, they pull those chromosomes out of both cells, discard the donor cell's own chromosomes and the mother's original cell along with them, and drop the couple's chromosomes into the donor's cell body. That reconstituted cell is what grows into the kid. So the kid's nuclear DNA is entirely from the two parents, but the actual cell that became the kid is not the cell that the mother's egg and the father's sperm made — that cell got assembled and then taken apart, and its contents got moved into a different cell. Now: is that kid the same individual as the kid those two people would have had if they'd just conceived normally — same egg, same sperm, no donor egg, no swap? I think everyone says yes. Same parents, same genome, she's the one who was going to be born either way. But on your view, you are the particular cell you started as. So if the swap substitutes a new cell with the same nuclear contents, then either (a) the cell was never the essential thing, in which case the gametes being this egg and this sperm wasn't the essential thing either and I want to know what actually is, or (b) the kid born after the swap is a different individual from the kid who'd have been conceived without it — the procedure is, in the literal sense, a baby swap — and the cell that got disassembled was somebody who no longer exists. So which is it? And whichever you pick, tell me what makes one single cell the same cell as, or a different cell from, another one, in a way that doesn't just collapse into 'this one, because it's this one.'
