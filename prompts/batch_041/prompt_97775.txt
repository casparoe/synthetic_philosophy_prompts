I'm a novelist — four books, decent reviews, a living made by building people out of sentences — which means I spend my working life deciding what a character would do, what they cannot bring themselves to do, and which of those two things the reader should be allowed to see. A friend who teaches philosophy recently told me, with some relish, that there is a long-running quarrel in ethics about whether the thing I do all day — inventing people with stable dispositions that explain their actions across an entire novel — corresponds to anything real in human beings. On his telling, the virtue ethicists and their Aristotelian descendants hold that good character is a set of deeply ingrained dispositions, cultivated over years by habit, that reliably issue in good action, with the particular case sorted out by a kind of practical wisdom; and the situationists, working from social psychology — the obedience experiments, the study in which seminary students in a hurry walked past a person in distress — counter that actual conduct is driven far more by the situation than by any stable trait, and that the robust character trait is mostly a piece of folk psychology that the evidence does not support.

Here is what I want from you. Restate that dispute — virtue ethics as the position, the situationist critique as the challenge to it — entirely in the vocabulary I actually think in: character, motivation, want and obstacle, arc, scene, dramatic logic, foreshadowing, showing versus telling, interiority, the unreliable narrator, and the persistent gap between what a person is and what the plot requires them to do. Be as faithful as you can manage, and be honest about the seams. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which of the virtue ethicists' claims and which of the situationists' claims survive the translation intact, which survive only in a distorted form, and which have no counterpart at all in the way a novelist thinks about persons. I have no formal philosophy training beyond one undergraduate ethics course, so do not assume I will catch you importing the situationists' assumptions and calling it translation; if some step only works because you have quietly decided that behavior is situation-driven, flag it as such rather than letting it pass.

And tell me, at the end, whether the residue looks to you like a genuine gap in my vocabulary — something the craft of fiction simply cannot say about people — or like evidence that one side of the philosophers' quarrel was less clear than it sounded. My own suspicion, which I would like you either to confirm or to demolish, is that the novel is a standing argument for the virtue ethicists: the author controls the situations, so the characters can afford to be consistent.
