Two friends of mine went at each other for about forty minutes at a party on Saturday and I've been chewing on it since. I was just standing there with a drink, so I've got no dog in this. Ravi's in a philosophy PhD, June does stats, and somehow the conversation ended up on money pumps. As best I can reconstruct it, in order:

(1) Ravi: picture someone with a cycle — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. That can't be rational, and here's the proof. I hold A. I offer to swap her B plus a penny for her A, then C plus a penny for the B, then A plus a penny for the C. Each trade on its own makes her better off by her own lights, so she takes all three. She ends up holding exactly what she started with and three pennies poorer. That's a sure loss, arranged in advance, with nothing but her own preferences as the lever. Anything that lets you be made worse off by your own standards is irrational. So intransitivity is irrational, done.

(2) June: fine, but look at what you assumed. You assumed she takes each deal. She could also look at the whole sequence, see it ends with her back at A minus three pennies, and decline to start. And declining isn't some outside force acting on her — it's just her, not trading. So the pump shows that a myopic chooser with a cycle gets fleeced. It doesn't show the cycle is irrational. Fix the chooser, keep the cycle, the pump stops working.

(3) Ravi: that's a dodge. Her preferences say B beats A. So if I offer her B plus a penny for A and she says no, she's making a mistake by her own lights. You're saying she's rational as long as she ignores her preferences whenever ignoring them is convenient, and at that point what are the preferences even doing? Same structure as a Dutch book — nobody says the incoherent credences are fine, the agent just shouldn't bet. Being offerable the bad deal is the defect.

(4) June: the Dutch book comparison is where I think it goes wrong, and I couldnt get him to see it. In a Dutch book, each bet is one she'd call fair on its own, nothing she'd disavow at the moment of taking it; it's the combination that's bad. Same with the pump, granted. But that's the point — what's bad is the preferences plus a policy for choosing, and I can keep the preferences and swap the policy. He wants the defect to live in the preferences by themselves, but his own example only ever exhibits it in the pair. And "she'd be making a mistake by her own lights" only follows if her lights are strictly per-moment. If she has preferences about whole trajectories, then refusing the sequence is what her all-in preferences recommend, and there's no conflict left to point at.

(5) Ravi: okay, here's a cleaner test and I'll bite whatever it forces. Call it the no-free-loss test: a set of preferences is irrational if there's a finite run of trades, each one the agent prefers at the moment it's offered, that leaves her strictly worse off by her own lights, and no strategy open to her at the outset avoids it. Cycles fail the test, transitive preferences pass, and the line lands exactly where I want it. Give me a version that still does some work and doesn't just let everybody off with "she could always have refused everything."

(6) June: I don't think I can, and I think that's the tell. "She could always refuse everything" is open to every agent with every preference structure, so the test never fires for anybody — unless you build in that she acts on her preferences, which is a claim about her, not about the preferences. And once you build that in, alot of ordinary agents start failing too, including ones with perfectly transitive preferences. A mugger can make you worse off by your own lights as well; nobody blames that on your preferences. Exploitability only means irrationality when the exploit runs through a defect in the attitudes, and he's shown it runs through the attitudes-or-the-policy while quietly treating the policy as fixed and blameless. It's myopic. Myopia's the bug. Cycles might just be a weird but harmless feature.

(7) Ravi: the mugger thing is cheap, he's not using her preferences as a lever. But grant the rest — myopia is irrational, fine. Myopia is defined relative to the preferences; a myopic chooser is one who follows her preferences too literally. For someone with transitive preferences, following them literally is optimal, that's basically what the standard representation results say. So myopia isn't a free-standing vice. It only becomes one when your preferences can lead you around in a loop. So the cycle is still the thing doing the work.

That's where they stopped, because June thinks that last move is either trivial or circular and can't tell which, and Ravi thinks it's neither.

So: referee. Go move by move — for each numbered move, what's right in it, what's overstated or wrong, and how much weight it should actually carry. Then the two things I want settled. (a) Is there any version of the no-free-loss test that survives June's objection, i.e. that doesn't either never fire or smuggle in a premise about the chooser? And if there isn't, does that mean intransitivity is actually fine, or only that the pump argument is doing less work than people think it is? (b) Does it matter that all of this unfolds over time — is she irrational at each moment, or only in the pattern across moments, and is diachronic rationality a genuinely separate requirement or does it collapse into something synchronic? Finally, who's got the better of it overall, and what are they both missing? Don't just split the difference — if one of them is more right, say so.
