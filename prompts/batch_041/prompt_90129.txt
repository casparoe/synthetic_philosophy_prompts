I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or law, and I serve as a lay member of the disciplinary appeals panel of a national professional association for software and systems engineers. Our panel hears appeals from members who have been sanctioned for failing to comply with directives issued by a government regulator — I will call it the Authority — that prescribe how engineers must build and operate systems. A member's usual defence is that the directive was not really law and therefore imposed no duty of obedience. We do not decide constitutional questions in the abstract; we decide only whether a directive is to be treated as binding law for the purpose of disciplining one of our members. The sanctions are serious — they can end a career — so the stakes of getting the rule right are high.

I am worried that we have been applying a rule we have never stated, and that we are about to contradict ourselves. The Authority's charter is our only explicit source of validity rules. It provides that a directive is valid once the Authority's board adopts it by majority vote, and it says nothing about publication, retroactivity, vagueness, impossibility of compliance, or the justice of a directive's content.

Our relevant decisions are these.

Registry directive. Required engineers to log and report the ethnicity of users of certain systems. It was adopted by a board majority, but was published only after the Authority had begun enforcing it. We held that it was not law and overturned the sanction.

Regional directive. Required engineers to certify that products originating in a region populated by a despised minority contained no security vulnerabilities of any kind — a guarantee no one could give — and applied the requirement to products already shipped. It was adopted by a board majority. We held that it was not law and overturned the sanction.

Faction directive. Required engineers to give procurement preference to firms owned by members of the governing party. It was adopted by a board majority, published well before it took effect, stated clearly, and entirely possible to comply with. We held that it was law, upheld the sanction, and recorded that although we considered the directive unfair, its unfairness did not affect its status as law.

Colleagues on the panel describe the underlying dispute as one between legal positivism and natural law, but I do not understand the labels well enough to say which we are following, and I would like the answer in plain terms.

The case now before us, the Silence directive, requires engineers to build systems that covertly suppress safety warnings for members of one religious group. It was adopted by a board majority, published well before it took effect, is general, is stated with complete clarity, is entirely possible to comply with, and has not been amended or withdrawn.

What I want:

1. Identify the several rules that fit our three past decisions equally well, and say for each what it predicts about the Silence directive. I am particularly interested in whether our record forces us to choose between a rule that turns on how a directive was made and a rule that turns on what it commands, or whether both can survive.

2. Predict how our panel will decide the Silence directive, and separately say how we should decide it if our aim is consistency with our own precedents. Give reasons for your best guess and say how confident you are.

3. Say whether any version of the view that a directive's validity depends only on how it was adopted survives our record, or whether our past decisions have already ruled it out.

4. Name the single future case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, describe it concretely, and say what each rule would decide about it.

5. Flag anything our record leaves genuinely undecided, and any place where our stated reasons are doing less work than they appear to.

Please reason in plain language and avoid assuming I know the technical vocabulary; if you use a standard name for a position, define it in a sentence.
