In a seminar on grand strategy, a colleague used the line 'history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes' to license a Munich-style reading of a present-day standoff — treating the rhyme as sufficient reason to carry the lesson of 1938 over to a case that differs in many details — while another colleague pointed to Vietnam and the so-called Thucydides trap as instances where a borrowed lesson did more harm than good. What can the rhyming line actually be claiming? Set out the distinct precise theses it might express — that historical events literally recur; that they share underlying causal mechanisms; that they share only surface resemblances; that the dispositions of states and statesmen are stable enough to be relied on; that the 'rhyme' is an artifact of the analogist's own vocabulary and interests — and for each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which of these readings the line has to carry if it is to do the work my colleague wanted, and what further premises the transfer of a lesson such as deterrence or credibility from one case to another would require. I would like this as a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single recommended view.
