I have been puzzling over a small family of sentences that go wrong in a distinctive way, and I would like to know what principle explains when they go wrong. I came to this on my own, so I may be missing something obvious, but I want the underlying rule, not a label for the phenomenon. It began with a sentence I tried to say out loud and found I could not: "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The strange thing is that it does not seem to be false. People can be mistaken about the weather, and the two halves could, I think, both be true at once. Yet there is something self-defeating about saying it that I cannot put my finger on. I have since collected a number of neighboring cases, and I have opinions about each one. I want the principle behind the pattern, and I want to be told which of my opinions it forces me to give up.

Here are my verdicts.

1. "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." Self-defeating. I could not say this in earnest.
2. "It rained yesterday, but at the time I didn't believe it was raining." Perfectly fine.
3. "It rained yesterday, but I don't believe it did." Just as bad as (1). This surprised me, because the rain is in the past; what changed between (2) and (3) is the tense of the belief, not the tense of the rain.
4. "It's raining, but you don't believe it is." Fine.
5. "It's raining, but I don't know it is." Much weaker than (1); I might even say it. It lacks the locked-up feeling.
6. "It's raining, but I'm not sure it is." Fine.
7. "It's raining, but I doubt it is." Fine, or nearly so.
8. "It's raining, but I hope it isn't." Fine.
9. "It's raining, but I don't care one way or the other." Fine.
10. "I believe it's raining, but it isn't." Self-defeating too, but in the opposite direction from (1).
11. "It's raining, and I don't believe it's raining." Just as bad as (1). So the word "but" is not what is doing the damage.
12. "It's raining, but I have no opinion about whether it's raining." Bad, in the way (1) is bad. I did not expect this, since having no opinion sounds neutral rather than like a denial.
13. "If it's raining but I don't believe it is, I should still take an umbrella." Fine. "Suppose it's raining but I don't believe it is." Also fine. The trouble seems to vanish the moment the sentence is not actually being asserted.
14. Silently thinking to myself, "It's raining but I don't believe it." Still feels locked-up, though I am less confident here, since nothing is being said to anyone.
15. A novelist writing of a character: "It was raining, but she did not believe it was." Fine.
16. "I'll be at the meeting, but I don't intend to be there." Self-defeating, like (1). "I intend to go, but I won't." Self-defeating too.
17. "It's raining, but I don't believe it will last." Fine, even though the second half is still about my beliefs, because it is about a different claim.
18. "Nobody believes it's raining, but it is." Fine, even though I am somebody. But "I don't believe it's raining, but it is" is not fine. So it matters that the belief in question is mine.

My tentative principle is this: when I assert something, I put it forward as something I believe, so denying that I believe it contradicts what I am doing in asserting it, rather than contradicting the content of what I assert. I would like you to test that against the list, repair it where it fails, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If a single principle will not cover both the belief cases and the intention cases in (16), say so and give me the smallest set of principles that will. Please also apply whatever you settle on to two or three cases I have not listed, so I can see it working on new ground. I am not looking for references or background reading; I want the rule itself, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each part of it. End your reply with a single final line beginning "Principle:" that states the principle in its finished form in one sentence.
