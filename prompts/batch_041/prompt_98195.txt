My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether morality can be captured in rules at all, and by the end I had noticed something in his position that I cannot name and am no longer sure has a name.

Consider two things he did, both within the same hour. Early on he told me, without hedging, that lying is wrong — not a rule of thumb, an actual requirement. Later he mentioned that he had told his brother a relative's surgery had gone well when in fact it had gone badly, to keep him from panicking on a long drive. When I said that was a lie, he said it was not really a lie, since a lie requires an intent to deceive for one's own advantage and he had meant only to spare his brother. Then he told me that you must keep your promises; he also described backing out of a commitment to help a friend move because a better offer came up, and defended it by saying the promise had been conditional on nothing better coming along, though nothing of the kind had been said when he made it.

What struck me was not that he made exceptions — everyone does — but that each exception arrived with a redefinition of the rule itself, so that by the end no rule had been broken; the rule had merely turned out to mean something narrower than I had taken it to mean, and narrower each time it was threatened. He was emphatic that he believes in moral rules and that I was the one who wanted to throw them out, and in a sense he is right: he never abandons a rule, he shrinks it. And the shrinking was asymmetric. When my conduct was at issue he applied the widest version — my broken promise to him was a broken promise, full stop — while the narrow version was always available for his own.

A rule that contracts at exactly the points where obeying it would cost the holder something does not seem to be doing what a rule is supposed to do, but I could not say precisely what it is doing instead, and that is what I want from you. I have heard the word "defeasible" used about moral principles, and I know there is a long-running dispute between people who think morality needs general principles and people who think judgment is always case-by-case, but neither of those is what he is doing. He is not saying the particular case comes first, and he is not saying his principle admits of exceptions; he is saying the principle is binding and then quietly rewriting its content. Is there a name for this? If there is, I need more than the name: what it is, and what it is distinguished from — in particular how it differs from ordinary defeasibility, from the case-by-case view, and from simply redefining a term to win an argument, since it feels adjacent to all three and identical to none. If there is no name, propose one, and tell me where its edges are. Is the rewriting necessarily self-serving, or can it be innocent and even correct? After enough of it, is it still the same rule, or a different rule wearing the same name? And what could a defender of my friend say on his behalf — is there a description under which he is being consistent and I am the one who is confused?
