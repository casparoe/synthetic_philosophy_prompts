Two of my flatmates got into a long, slightly drunk argument on Friday and I got dragged in as the referee. I couldn't settle it, so I'm outsourcing the verdict to you. Please give me your answer in British English.

The set-up was this thought experiment they'd both been chewing on. A stranger knocks on the door and offers you a deal: hand over £20 now, and if a certain quantum event goes one particular way -- he says the chance is about one in ten to the thirtieth -- he'll give you and everyone you care about ten to the fortieth years of bliss. Otherwise you're just out twenty quid. Then they started going at it, and it went roughly like this.

Round one. Dan said you obviously pay. The expected value is astronomically positive; any decision rule that tells you to refuse a positive-EV bet is broken. Sam said no, because the probability is just asserted by some bloke on the doorstep, and if you let 'maximise EV, however tiny the probability and however huge the payoff' be your rule, then literally any con artist can mug you by promising something enormous. The rule has no teeth.

Round two. Dan said that's a complaint about where the probability came from, not about the principle. Suppose the probability really is ten to the minus thirty and the payoff really is ten to the fortieth -- then EV maximisation says pay, and if you want to refuse you have to say what's wrong with the arithmetic, not just that it feels mad. Sam said fine, grant the probabilities. Then the problem is worse, not better: expected value is only well defined when the relevant sum converges. If payoffs can be arbitrarily large, the sum can diverge or fail to be well defined, and then 'maximise expected value' doesn't rank anything at all -- you can't even say which of two enormous-payoff options is better. And the whole thing hangs on how we assign values far out in the tail, where we have no evidence whatsoever.

Round three. Dan said that's a technicality about infinite cases; for big finite numbers the principle is perfectly fine, and anyway if you refuse it you're just being a fanatic about not being a fanatic -- letting a horror of large numbers override the actual reasons. Sam said then here's a principled fix: put a floor under probabilities, treat anything below, say, one in a googol as zero, or discount tiny probabilities hard. That kills the mugging without giving up on EV for realistic stakes.

Round four. Dan said the floor is arbitrary -- why one in a googol and not one in a googol plus one? -- and that a floor can be exploited: someone can offer you a long series of bets, each of which you write off as negligible, and the series as a whole can ruin you, so the fix buys sanity at the price of a money pump. Sam said maybe, but the alternative is a rule that tells you to hand your wallet to anyone with a big enough promise, and at least a floor is a rule you can actually live with.

Neither of them would budge and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Can you go through it move by move -- rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification -- and tell me who's right about what? I'd also like to know what both of them are missing, and whether the real disagreement is about the principle of expected-value maximisation itself or about the probabilities and values being fed into it, since I suspect they kept talking past each other on that point.
