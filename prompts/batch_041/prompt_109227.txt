I recently became the person responsible for reconciling the probability forecasts my research group produces each month — we put numbers on things like whether a compound will replicate in a larger trial, whether an anomalous result is real, whether a grant will be funded — and I have been trying to replace our habit of simply averaging everyone's numbers with something more principled. In the course of reading about the foundations of disagreement I came across Aumann's agreement theorem, which appears to settle the matter in a way I did not expect: two agents who are both Bayesians, who share a prior, and whose posterior credences are common knowledge cannot disagree. That seems to tell me that disagreement of the kind we have in the group is itself evidence of a failure of rationality somewhere, because my colleague and I have done everything the theorem asks. We start from the same base rates and the same background papers, we are both committed Bayesians, we each stated our credence out loud, and we each walked the other through the evidence and the likelihoods we used. We still differ, on one forecast by close to thirty points, and neither of us has budged after two meetings.

What I would like is a procedure for turning the theorem into an audit. If the conditions really are met, and the theorem therefore says our credences must coincide, then the fact that they do not must mean one of us has misapplied the axioms, and it ought to be possible to work backwards from the gap to find the defective step: reconstruct the common prior from our shared background, recompute what each of us should have concluded from the shared evidence, and identify whose chain of reasoning departs from the Bayesian ideal. Once the audit locates the irrational party — and I take it the theorem leaves no room for two rational agents to differ, so it must be exactly one of us — I would also like to know what the rational one owes the other: is there an obligation to keep re-explaining until we converge, or is it defensible to hold one's ground and let the disagreement stand?
