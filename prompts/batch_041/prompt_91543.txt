I have been asked to give the opening address at a two-day workshop my institute is hosting under the title "Why Britain and Not China?" The title was fixed by the steering committee before I was invited, and I accepted knowing that I now believe the question, as phrased, is not well posed — not because it is unanswerable, and not because the scholarship it produced was wasted, but because of what kind of question it is.

The room will be split three ways. Roughly half are historians of late imperial and early modern China, several of whom have built careers on what is generally called the Needham question and who will hear any challenge to its framing as a challenge to their life's work. A second group are quantitative economic historians who study wages, coal, and the Atlantic economy, and who regard the whole "why not China" apparatus as a parochial hangover from an older historiography. The remainder are graduate students, two visiting philosophers who work on explanation, and a dean who will leave after twenty minutes.

Write the speech. It should run five to seven minutes as delivered.

Its structure has to be governed by the contrastive view of why-questions — the idea that "why P?" is almost never the whole question, that a why-question carries with it a contrast class and a relevance relation, and that different contrasts make different facts relevant. On that view, "why Britain rather than Holland?", "why Britain rather than the Yangzi delta?", "why Britain rather than Japan?", and "why Britain rather than anywhere at all?" are four different questions with four different answers, only some of which are well posed. This is not decoration; it is what determines what the speech may assert. It must not say that China failed, since failure presupposes a target that was never set; it must not treat European development as the default against which everything else is measured; and it must not announce a verdict on the causes of the divergence that two days of papers could not support. But it also must not collapse into the fashionable conclusion that the question was simply a mistake. I want the speech to commit, in plain language, to a position on whether the title question is well posed, ill posed, or merely under-specified — and to earn that position in front of people who will not accept it on authority.

Constraints:

- No citations, no reading list, no quotations. This audience knows the historiography better than I do; do not summarize their own field back at them.
- Explain the difference between a question that is unanswerable and a question that is under-specified without borrowing the vocabulary of any particular philosophical tradition, and show which one this is by example rather than by assertion.
- Choose one image or case that will carry the point for the dean, the graduate students, and the two philosophers at once.
- Do not insult the senior China historians and do not pretend their question was idle. Grant whatever should be granted to it, concretely and by name.
- Close by setting up the two days: what this workshop can settle and what it cannot.

After the speech, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "occasion": one sentence.
- "audience_segments": array of objects with "segment", "what_they_came_for", and "what_they_will_not_accept".
- "thesis": the single claim the remarks turn on, in one sentence.

- "prohibitions": array of strings — claims the speech deliberately does not make, each with the reason.

- "carrying_case": the one image or case, with a note on what it had to have and what it had to lack in order to work.
- "unsettled": what the speech explicitly leaves for the two days.
- "coffee_break_objection": the most likely objection from the floor, in one sentence, and "reply": the one-sentence answer.
