You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of harming and saving, and who knows both the trolley-problem literature and the doctrine of double effect well. I am drafting a position paper and would like a consistency audit of my commitments before I put them in print. Please think carefully and at length. Begin by listing, for yourself, every pair of commitments below that might pull against one another; then decide which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be. Report only the audit, not the brainstorming.

I am not a consequentialist — I think deontic constraints are real — though I do not think constraints exhaust morality. Here is where I stand.

1. Intention and foresight differ morally. Bringing about a person's death as a means to one's end stands in greater need of justification than bringing about that very same death as a foreseen but unintended side-effect of achieving that end.

2. One may never intentionally kill an innocent person, not even in order to save a larger number of other innocent people.

3. Bystander: a runaway trolley is heading for five people. I may divert it onto a side track, where it will kill the one person standing there.

4. Footbridge: I may not push a large stranger off a footbridge into the path of the trolley in order to stop it, even though doing so would save the five.

5. Transplant: I may not kill one healthy person and distribute his organs so as to save five others.

6. Loop: the trolley is heading for five. I may divert it onto a side track that curves back and rejoins the main track before the five; the diverted trolley will strike the one person on that side track and be stopped by his body, so that the five are saved. I may divert.

7. An agent is fully morally responsible for every consequence of her action that she foresaw, whether or not she intended it.

8. A moral theory should not return different verdicts on two cases that differ only in features that are morally irrelevant — for instance, whether the agent acts with a lever or with her own hands.

Now: which of these are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each? Rank the genuine tensions from most to least serious. For each one, say precisely which commitment has to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Which pairs only appear to conflict, and what exactly dissolves the appearance? Finally, if I make exactly one revision, which single change clears up the most, and what does it leave unresolved? Please do not conclude that the whole set is consistent, and do not treat every pair as equally problematic: the point of the exercise is to discriminate. Argue for your ranking rather than merely asserting it.
