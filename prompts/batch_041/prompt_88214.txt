I'm a novelist — third book, and the first one that's supposed to be grown-up. It's set inside a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been hanging around one (a week of interviews, plus a friend who works there reads my drafts and tells me when I've got the jargon wrong). I'm stuck on something I can't unsee and I don't have the philosophy to know what I'm even looking at.

Here's the practice. The lab wants to train models on questions that are too hard for any human they can afford to check — is this protein fold plausible, does this chunk of code do what its comment says, is this claim in the report actually supported by the source. So instead of checking, they run a debate. Two copies of a model get the same question, get assigned opposite answers, and take turns arguing to a human judge. The judge is not an expert; that's the entire point. The judge is a contractor with maybe twenty minutes, and the house rules say each side must, whenever it can, point the judge at something the judge can check for themselves — a line of code, a number in a spreadsheet, one sentence in a document. The judge picks a winner. The winner's answer goes into the training set and the model gets fine-tuned on it; the loser's answer gets binned, and in some runs the losing copy has its weights nudged away from the winner's, or gets retired.

Now the part thats eating me. The lab's own internal write-up — I have a copy, they're not hiding it, it's basically a wiki page — says flat out that the judge "cannot verify the truth of the winning answer" and that all the judge certifies is that one side was more persuasive within the rules. Fine. But three paragraphs later the same document calls the winning answer "ground truth," and the pipeline treats it exactly like the answers they got by hand-checking. And they never penalize the side that lied — there is no lie-detection step anywhere — they penalize the side that lost. And when a question is safety-critical they don't use debate at all, they hand-check a small sample; but nobody has written down what to do when the hand-checked sample and the debates disagree about the same question. One researcher I talked to said, cheerfully, that the failure mode they worry about most is a debater who wins by making its case too long and fiddly for the judge to follow — and then said there's nothing in the protocol that stops it, because the judge just decides.

So what I want from you is the philosophy hiding in there. Not "is debate a good idea" — I want to know what this setup has to believe in order to make sense to the people running it. Concretely:

- What does it have to assume about truth, and about the relation between winning an argument and being right? Is there a view of knowledge or justification on which "the judge picked this one" is any reason at all to think it's true, and what does that view have to claim about deception — that lying is more costly than truth-telling, that it's more checkable, that it's harder to keep straight?
- What does it assume about language — that a hard question can be seperated into little claims a non-expert can check one at a time without losing the thread of the big claim? And what does it assume about the judge: that non-expertise is neutral, or that being fooled is roughly symmetric between the two sides?
- Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? I want the ones it genuinely can't live with, kept apart from the ones it merely happens to sit next to.
- Where is it underdetermined — where would several different theories license the exact same protocol, so that I can't have a character say "ah, so you must believe X" without being wrong?
- Then the fun part: if you swap the hidden view for one of its rivals, what actually changes on the ground — the rules, the paperwork, who signs off, what the judge is told, what happens to the loser? I need at least one version of that where the change is small, ugly and administrative rather than a grand reframing, because that's the kind of thing my book is about.

Finish with a table I can tape above my desk, one row per commitment, with these columns exactly: Commitment | What in the protocol shows it | Rival view it excludes | What would break if the rival were true | Which of my characters would notice, and in what scene. Keep that last column to one line per row, and dont be nice about my characters.
