So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on structural realism after a few blog posts and a skim of the encyclopedia entry on it. I think I mostly buy it and I'd like you to try to talk me out of it, or at least tell me what should.

The version I hold, in my own words: when physics replaces a mature theory, the old equations keep working as a limiting case of the new ones, even when the old picture of what's really there gets thrown out entirely. Fresnel's equations for reflection and refraction came back as a limiting case of Maxwell's, ether gone. So what we actually know when a theory succeeds isn't the furniture, it's the structure. The equations are the API, the ontology is the implementation, you swap the implementation and the API holds. I'll take the strong version: the old equations should survive as a genuine limiting or special case, not just agree with old data to within error bars.

So what would count against it? Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones structural realists would concede themselves versus which ones only critics care about. Things I'd want you to hit:

Cases where the math got junked too. Everybody reaches for the ether, probaly because it's the cleanest. But what about things that were treated as structural and then turned out not to hold at all, like parity in the weak interaction? Is that a broken structure, or does the structural realist get to say symmetries were never part of the structure in the first place?

The retrofit worry, which is the one that actually scares me. If you only ever identify the preserved structure after you already know the successor theory, aren't you reading it off backwards? In software I can refactor something until not one line survives and the behavior still matches. How do I tell preservation from reconstruction, and if I can't, is the view unfalsifiable? And if it is, is that fatal or just the price of a thesis this general? Related: does it predict anything at all, or is every claim it makes retrospective?

Whether it quietly collapses into something weaker, like just saying we should trust the predictions we can check. If it does, what's left that's distinctive?

Whether it only works where there's math. Chemistry and biology don't obviously have an API. Is this a thesis about science or just about physics?

And the one I'd personally find most convincing against me: a clean case where a theory's central equations were kept for a while and then shown to be an accident that doesn't correspond to anything in the successor, not merely approximate but wrong in a way the limiting-case story can't absorb. Does a case like that exist, or am I asking for something that couldn't exist?
