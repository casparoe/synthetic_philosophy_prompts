I am a philosophy professor, and next month I have to run the fourth session of an interdisciplinary workshop on evidence and policy: ninety minutes, about twenty people, a mix of philosophy graduate students who have had a semester of normative ethics and economics and public policy students who do cost-effectiveness analysis for a living. The topic is the problem that has come to be called cluelessness: the thought that a consequentialist theory tells us to act for the best, while for the overwhelming majority of our choices there is no defensible way to say which option is best, and not merely in the ordinary sense of being uncertain, but in the sense that there is no principled way to attach probabilities to the downstream consequences or to weigh heterogeneous consequences against one another once they arrive. I have watched this idea fail to land twice before: the philosophers hear it as the demandingness objection in a new costume, and the economists hear it as a routine call for more research. I do not want an explanation of cluelessness; I want a plan for the session.

Give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and why each depends on the one before it. In particular, tell me whether the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure has to be laid out first or whether the room has to arrive at it themselves, and whether the causal and the evaluative sources of the trouble should be separated at this level or run together for as long as possible and prised apart only at the end. Then tell me the one case that should carry the whole ninety minutes. It needs to be mundane and small in scale, with a first-order effect everyone can see and second-order effects that fan out past any hope of enumeration; it must not be the far-future or existential-risk case, which makes the problem look like the private hobby of a subculture, and it must not be a case with a clean probability distribution, which makes it merely risk. Say what the case must lack as well as what it must have, and if you think the obvious candidates, the charity donation and the butterfly-effect anecdote, are the wrong carriers, say so and say why. Then the misconceptions, in the order the room will actually form them, each with the exact point in the sequence at which I should head it off; I am expecting at least these four: that this is just uncertainty and that Pascal settled it; that it is the demandingness objection or the repugnant conclusion wearing a new hat; that it shows consequentialism to be false, rather than showing something about the relation between the theory and the agent who must act on it; and that the remedy is more empirical research. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to announce out loud that I am leaving it out. And give me one question to put to the room in the last ten minutes that would reveal whether they have understood the problem rather than merely felt its discomfort.

Do not hand me a survey of the positions one could take; commit to one and give your reasons. My own inclination, which I want you either to endorse or to overrule, is that cluelessness is a defect in the decision procedure rather than in the criterion of rightness, and that the session should land on that distinction rather than on any verdict about consequentialism itself. Tell me where I am wrong. End with a final line beginning Verdict: that states in one sentence the position the session is designed to land.
