I am a retired field scientist — forty years in ecology and environmental statistics — who has spent the first years of retirement reading moral and decision theory, and who has lately been made chair of the grants committee of a small environmental charity. We meet monthly and decide on perhaps a dozen proposals. Each asks for a modest sum and each promises outcomes that are genuinely uncertain: a restoration technique that may or may not establish, a monitoring programme whose value is knowledge that may never be used, a field trial with a small chance of a large advance and a large chance of nothing at all. Our trustees are not specialists, we have about two hours and one page per proposal, and I sign off on the recommendations.

The committee keeps making these calls by intuition, and a particular failure has come to bother me. We describe ourselves as risk-averse, but we have no idea what we mean by it. We cannot say whether we are discounting the good outcomes because we doubt they will happen, because we secretly value them less than we claim, or because we simply dislike gambles as such. Reading around, I came across a framework that separates these things, usually called risk-weighted expected utility. As I understand it, one first orders an option's possible outcomes from worst to best and assigns each a utility; then, rather than weighting those utilities by their raw probabilities, one weights them by a transform of the cumulative probability of doing at least that well. A convex transform, which pulls the cumulative chances of the better outcomes downward, makes one risk-averse; a linear transform collapses the whole thing back into ordinary expected utility. The attraction, for me, is that one's attitude to risk becomes a separate dial rather than something smuggled into the curvature of a utility function.

Please turn this into a procedure the committee could actually run on a proposal. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact. It must say how to elicit something like a risk function from trustees who will never read the theory, what to do when we cannot put numbers on the outcomes, and — this matters to me — which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three proposals: a wetland restoration with roughly a four-in-five chance of a modest, well-understood gain and no chance of harm; a long-shot method-development grant with perhaps a one-in-ten chance of transforming how we monitor a whole class of species; and, third, a proposal with a small chance — say one in fifty — of serious ecological harm and otherwise a solid expected benefit. On that last one my instinct is to refuse anything with a catastrophic tail, and I suspect an honest application of the framework, with the mild risk aversion we would actually endorse, may tell me to fund it. If so, say so; do not bend the procedure toward my gut. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do.
