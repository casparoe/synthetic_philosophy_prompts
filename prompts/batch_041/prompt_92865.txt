You are a philosopher of law specializing in statutory interpretation, and I am asking you to diagnose an argument for me. Work carefully: before you write anything final, use a scratch space to list the premises, mark which transitions carry logical weight, and sketch two or three candidate diagnoses; then commit to the one you can best defend and tell me what the others would have cost. I want reasoning, not references -- do not give me citations, a reading list, or the names of cases and articles, since I care about the structure of the argument rather than its pedigree.

Here is the setting. Consider a fictional jurisdiction whose environmental statute contains this provision (I have invented it; do not hunt for it):

"No person shall cause a discharge of a pollutant into the waters of the Commonwealth except under a permit issued by the Director."

A fertilizer plant keeps a holding pond for its runoff. During an unusually heavy storm the pond overtops its berm and the runoff reaches a river. No operator turned a valve; the release was a mechanical consequence of rainfall against a fixed design. The regulator seeks a penalty. A textualist judge says the plant did not "cause" a discharge in the statute's sense, because "cause" in ordinary English implies an agent's act, and no act occurred here. A purposivist judge says the statute exists to keep pollutants out of the waters, and a design that guarantees occasional releases defeats that aim, so liability attaches.

A colleague of mine has built an argument that the textualist's method is impossible in principle. He states it as follows:

(1) The meaning of a text is fixed by the linguistic conventions of the community that produced it.
(2) A legal term carries its ordinary linguistic conventions unless the statute stipulates a special meaning.
(3) Whether an ordinary term applies to a novel case is settled by whether that case is relevantly similar to the paradigm cases the community associates with the term.
(4) Similarity is never relevant in itself; a case resembles a paradigm only relative to some purpose or interest that the classifier brings.
(5) Therefore, applying any legal term to a novel case requires appealing to a purpose.
(6) Therefore textualism -- which purports to apply terms without appeal to legislative purpose -- is impossible.
(7) Therefore every interpretive dispute is at bottom a dispute about purposes, and purposivism is the only coherent method of interpretation.

I resist the conclusion, which hands purposivism the victory by fiat, and I suspect the argument proves too much even on his own terms. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) through (3) than keep (7), but I want to know what I would be paying. So: which step should I reject? Tell me whether the argument fails by a specific flaw -- an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a slide from one kind of purpose to another -- or whether it is in fact valid and the real task is to pick the least costly premise to abandon. For whichever step you reject, explain why it looked persuasive in the first place, then price the alternatives: for each premise a reasonable interpreter might surrender, say what giving it up costs, what a textualist could put in its place, and whether the replacement leaves textualism a genuinely distinct method or quietly converts it into purposivism under another name. Finally, consider whether the argument also turns against its own conclusion, and if so, exactly where the self-undermining happens.
