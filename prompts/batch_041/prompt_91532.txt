I write about economics and business for a general-audience magazine, and I've reached a passage in a profile that I can't resolve on my own. My subject is a procurement manager at a mid-sized manufacturer who is about to renegotiate contracts with her two principal suppliers. To prepare, she says she first writes down every opening offer she might make and every response each supplier might give, then crosses out any offer that is never the best thing to do against any single response. With those gone, she scans again and crosses out any offer that has now become never-best, and repeats until a single opening offer remains, which she treats as the one a clear-headed negotiator has to make. Each individual step I can follow, and I can see how it might be defended.

What I can't place is her final move. She also strikes an offer that, as best I can reconstruct her reasoning, is not worse than any of her surviving offers against any one supplier response; it is worse only than a deliberate fifty-fifty coin flip between two of them. She treats this crossing-out as just as decisive as the earlier ones, and calls the whole procedure a demonstration that the offer left standing is the only sensible choice.

I want to describe her method accurately — neither overselling it as ironclad nor dismissing it as intuition dressed up. Is the coin-flip step legitimate? Does it really take that offer off the table, or does it quietly help itself to something about her beliefs, or about her being able to randomize on purpose? And is the earlier, step-by-step part genuinely airtight, or does its force rest on assumptions about how the suppliers reason that I would be wrong to present as settled? Give me the distinctions I need, in plain terms, so I can write the paragraph with a clear conscience.
