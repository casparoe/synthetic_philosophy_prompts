I have been trying to sort out a family of principles that share a single form, and I want to know whether one criterion separates the ones that can be held from the ones that quietly destroy themselves. The form is this: for every truth, there is some state or activity that can take that truth up — every truth can be known, or believed, or proved, or the like. What interests me is that some of these principles seem to entail a much stronger claim, and I cannot tell in advance which will.

My cases, with my verdicts.

1. Every truth can be known by someone at some time. Verdict: untenable. Take a truth T that in fact no one knows. By the principle it can be known, so it is possible for someone to know the conjunction of T with the fact that no one knows T. Now assume, as I do here and revisit below, that knowledge is never of what is not the case and that one who knows a conjunction knows each of its parts. Then that possible knower would know T and would know that no one knows T, which would make it true that no one knows T while they themselves know it. So there can be no truth that no one knows; the principle entails that every truth is already known by someone. It has to be given up.

2. It could have been that every truth is known by someone. Verdict: this survives. It says something about a possible arrangement of things, not about each truth taken separately, and I see nothing impossible in it.

3. Every truth can be verified by some possible observation. Verdict: collapses as in case 1 — one cannot verify what is not the case, and verifying a conjunction verifies each part.

4. Every truth can be believed by someone. Verdict: harmless. Even if every truth is thereby believed by someone, that is not absurd, and I doubt anything follows from it at all.

5. Every truth can be remembered by someone at some time. Verdict: I think this collapses too, which surprises me, since memory strikes me as a humbler thing than knowledge; but you cannot remember what did not happen, and remembering a conjunction appears to carry both halves.

6. Every truth can be perceived by someone at some time. Verdict: undecided. Perception is never of what is not there, so by my provisional rule below it ought to collapse; but I cannot settle whether perceiving a conjunction counts as perceiving each of its parts, and without that step I do not see the argument going through.

7. Every truth can be proved in a formal system I have in mind, one that is consistent, rich enough for arithmetic, and all of whose theorems are true. Verdict: I want to say this is a different matter altogether, since Gödel has already shown that not every truth is provable in such a system, so the principle is simply false and nothing collapses. But I suspect I am confusing two things.

8. Every truth can be known by someone who knows everything. Verdict: harmless, and instructive — the conclusion is built into the premise, which suggests that the collapse in case 1 is not by itself a reductio, and becomes one only because the conclusion it delivers is independently unacceptable.

My provisional rule is that a principle of this form collapses when the state in question cannot be wrong about its object, and is safe when the state can be wrong. Case 8 suggests the rule is incomplete, and case 7 suggests I may be applying it to the wrong thing.

What I would like is a criterion, stated precisely. What must be true of the state for a principle of this form to collapse, and what must be true of it for the principle to be safe? When the principle entails the stronger claim, is that a reductio or only a discovery of what the principle already meant? Please apply the criterion to cases I have not listed, including a state that is never wrong about its object but whose objects are simple rather than compound, and a state that is never wrong about its object and takes compound objects without taking their parts. Tell me what becomes of case 1 if one denies that knowing a conjunction carries knowing its parts. And tell me which of my verdicts the criterion forces me to abandon — I would rather lose a verdict than complicate the criterion. If this family of arguments has an established name, I would like to know it, though the name is not what I am after.
