I am designing a ninety-minute internal seminar for a mixed group of ML engineers, policy analysts, and one philosopher. All of them know the basic proposal for AI safety via debate, but none has thought carefully about the obfuscated arguments problem. I want you to act as an expert in AI safety, computational complexity, and the pedagogy of technical philosophy, and to produce a teaching plan rather than an explanation. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the last; the single carrying example that should run through the whole session and what it must include and leave out; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a closing question or exercise that would show whether they have understood the problem rather than merely learned its name; and the topics you would explicitly refuse to cover at this level, with a sentence on why. Please begin by brainstorming the dependency structure before you commit to an order.

To make this concrete, build the seminar around the following invented case, which I want you to treat as the carrying example unless you can argue that a different one would teach the dependency structure better: a judge with a fixed context window and no ability to run code must adjudicate a debate over whether a model has a hidden backdoor. The dishonest debater offers a two-hundred-step argument that the backdoor is harmless; exactly one step is invalid, but the invalidity only becomes visible if the judge expands a definition that would consume most of the remaining context. The honest debater can name the flawed step but cannot make the judge see the flaw within the round. The audience will likely think the problem is just lying, or just computational complexity, or just a matter of giving the judge more time. I want the plan to separate those. In particular, the sequence should make clear how obfuscation differs from ordinary deception, why a debate protocol can be sound in the limit yet useless under bounded resources, and what the judge's limitations do to the burden of proof. End with the one thing you would want the audience to be able to reconstruct on a whiteboard a week later.
