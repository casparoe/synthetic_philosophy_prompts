I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar all term, run by a philosopher of mind I've written about a couple of times, and I'm trying to work out wether she actually has a rule or is just improvising. Her whole apparatus is a distinction she takes from Dennett's Consciousness Explained — Orwellian versus Stalinesque. Orwellian is when the record gets rewritten after the fact, so the experience itself is edited in light of what came later and what you were conscious of at the time wasn't what the stimulus actually delivered. Stalinesque is when the show trial is rigged in advance: the experience was perfectly fine in real time and it's only the memory or the report that got falsified afterwards. Dennett's own line, as I understand it, is that the two are empirically indistinguishable, because both need some finish line in the brain where unconscious processing becomes conscious and there isn't one. She thinks that's a cop out — she thinks you can usually tell which one is going on — and half the room thinks she's cheating. I'd like to be able to say in print what her test actually is, because I'm writing a feature on postdiction and I need her to be a character and not a mush.

Her record so far, as I've got it in my notebook. (1) Two dots flashed in different places a bit more than a tenth of a second apart, which everyone sees as a single dot travelling across the screen and, when the colors differ, changing color about halfway along the path — a moment when the second dot hasn't been shown yet. Orwellian, she says, and she'll tell you the edit is being done way upstream, in the visual machinery itself, before anything reaches memory. (2) A bar sweeps across a screen and at one instant a stationary flash goes off right where the bar is; you see the flash lagging behind the bar, so the bar looks like it's already further along than it can be. Orwellian again, same reasoning, and again she locates the correction inside the visual system rather than after it. (3) A faint-dot detection task where the only thing that changes is the payoff for saying yes; people report seeing more dots, and nobody, her included, thinks the dots got brighter. Stalinesque — the edit is at the level of the decision and the report, the experience was whatever it was. (4) The one where you show someone two faces, ask which is more attractive, then hand them the other one and ask them to explain their choice; most people don't notice and cheerfully explain why they picked the face they didn't pick. Stalinesque, and for her it's obvious: the story is being written at the moment of the question and nothing about the original experience got revised.

Subjects watch a short video of a car on a country road, then read a written summary of the video, then a week later are asked what they remember seeing. The summary mentioned a barn beside the road that was never in the video. About 40% of the subjects later report the barn, and they can tell you what color it was and where it sat in the frame. So: Orwellian or Stalinesque, in her mouth — and be careful here, because I think her two tests come apart on this one. Give me the rules that fit her four verdicts so far, what each of them predicts about the barn, which rule you think she's actually running and why, and what case you'd have to put in front of her to make those rules disagree on something she can't wriggle out of. And if there's a third reading of her record that fits all four verdicts and predicts something different again for the barn, I want to hear that too. Keep it tight, I'm on deadline, and put your call at the very end on its own line as \answer{Orwellian} or \answer{Stalinesque} — or \answer{undecidable} if that's what you think she'd say, with the rule in parentheses after it.
