I've got no formal philosophy background at all — I did a marketing degree and these days I run a small bike-repair shop — but I've been going down a rabbit hole about privacy ever since I realised how much my smart speaker was hoovering up, and I've ended up with a pile of opinions that I honestly can't tell apart: are these a coherent position, or just a load of things I happen to like the sound of? I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on the ethics of privacy and tear it apart properly. Please work through it step by step: start by restating each of my commitments below in your own words as a clear claim, and say what each one is actually committing me to, then compare them pairwise before you say anything that sounds like a verdict. Be slow and specific rather than impressive, and please don't just tell me it's all fine or that it depends.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) Privacy is fundamentally about control — you have privacy over something to the extent that you get to decide who else gets access to it.
(2) But I don't think that's the whole story, because when a company buries the opt-out six menus deep and counts my not-clicking as agreement, I've lost privacy even though I technically went along with it. What's gone wrong there feels more like my information leaking out of the setting I handed it over in — my GP knowing my blood pressure is fine, my GP's software supplier flogging it to an insurer isn't.
(3) The value of privacy is derivative rather than intrinsic. What's good about it is the stuff it protects: room to be odd, to change your mind, to have a row with your partner without an audience, to think badly out loud.
(4) And yet I'm pretty sure that if some agency watched everything I did, never once acted on it, I never found out, and I behaved and felt exactly the same as I would have anyway, I'd still have been wronged. Not just unsettled. Wronged.
(5) I also think privacy is collective: mine depends on other people's, because a world where everyone else is transparent is eventually a world where I am too. Concrete version — our street has a WhatsApp group that shares doorbell-camera clips of anyone who walks past. The neighbours who joined it can hardly complain. The one neighbour who refused and never consented has still been made less private by the rest of us, and I can't shake the feeling that matters.
(6) Which pulls me the other way, because I do think the individual can waive it: if my mate wants to livestream his entire life, that's his business, and a "community privacy" that overrides him is just paternalism wearing a nice hat.
(7) And I'm confident privacy isn't secrecy — you can have privacy in a crowded kitchen, and you can keep secrets about things everybody already knows. Which is part of why (4) bugs me: if nothing's been hidden and nothing's been leaked, I can't put my finger on what's actually been taken from me. Maybe that's not a clash at all and I'm just muddling two different things, but I can't tell which.
(8) Though honestly I sometimes suspect their's no single thing privacy is at all — that it's a cluster of different goods that happen to share one word, and everyone arguing about it is talking past each other. Which would be a relief, except it might also be the cop-out.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each clash really? Rank them by severity, and for each one give me the cheapest repair and exactly what that repair costs me — I want to know what I'd have to give up, not just what I'd have to add. Then finish by telling me which single change would tidy up the most mess for the least cost, and be straight with me if two of them are just flatly incompatible.
