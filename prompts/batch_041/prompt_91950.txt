For some years I have been drawn to a reception-first account of art, and I would like to know what would count against it. The view, roughly, is this: an object is a work of art just when there exists a practice of appreciative attention — a practice whose governing norm is to treat objects as meriting sustained attention for their own sake — under which the object counts as a proper candidate for that attention; and I am tempted by the further thought that this makes art status fragile and revisable rather than stable, so that an object can become art, or cease to be, as the relevant practices wax and wane. I take the account to handle the readymades better than either the institutional theory or any aesthetic-functional account, since it seems to need neither a conferring act nor a perceptible aesthetic merit, only a standing practice of reception. What I want from you is a map of its exposure.

Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be: the ones that should make me abandon the view, the ones that would force a serious revision, and the ones that are merely awkward. Draw them from the view's natural defenders as much as from its critics, since I want to know what a committed reception-first theorist would concede rather than only what an opponent would press. I suspect several cases are load-bearing and want you to work through them carefully: the forgery or the perfect duplicate, where the appreciation seems identical in kind yet we want to say something has gone wrong; the object no one ever attends to, or one made and immediately destroyed, which the view seems to call art only by counterfactual courtesy; the first art, before any practice existed to receive it; the conceptual work deliberately built to resist the very attention the view requires; and the reverse case, a community whose practice of reverent attention is directed at something we would not call art at all, so that uptake alone seems to make too much art. Say which of these a defender can absorb with a qualification and which expose a genuine commitment the view cannot shed.

I also want an honest verdict on whether the account risks anything, or whether it has been quietly insulated. If the "appropriate" practice turns out to be just whatever practice confers art status, the theory may be true by stipulation and so unfalsifiable; tell me whether that is a real defect or simply the fate of any constitutive account of a practice-dependent kind, and whether the fragility-and-revisability claim helps or hurts, given that it commits me to saying a work could stop being art if the practice died out. Where a rival account does better on some of these cases, say so and say why, and where the view is simply silent rather than wrong, say that too. Throughout, keep what is settled apart from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a long and careful letter.
