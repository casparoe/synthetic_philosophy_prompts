I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on idealisation in theories of deliberation — on what is lost and what is gained when we model thinking as a process that could in principle be carried out by a very large, very well-resourced system. A colleague of mine, an engineer who works on machine reasoning, has sent me the following précis to include as background reading. It is lucid and I am inclined to trust it, but I have a nagging sense that it is too tidy, and I cannot localise the difficulty well enough to say so responsibly. I would like you to audit it.

His précis reads:

'At the centre of this literature sit two notions. The first is HCH, the human's computational capacity: the best answer a human being could give to any well-posed question, on the assumption that the human has unlimited time and unlimited computing power at its disposal — an idealised deliberator who never runs out of room to think. The second is factored cognition: since no actual human can think at that scale, a large question is decomposed into many small subquestions, each handed to an agent — a person, or a model imitating one — who answers it without needing to grasp the whole problem, after which the subanswers are recombined into an answer to the original question. The two notions fit together neatly. HCH is the ceiling on what such a system can deliver, and factored cognition is the recipe for getting as close to that ceiling as we care to; the decomposition is recursive, so each level of factoring can call on the level beneath it, and the system as a whole becomes strictly more capable than any of the agents it contains. Because every subanswer is produced by a human, the system's outputs inherit human values, which is why HCH is the natural target for aligning powerful AI. The remaining work is essentially engineering: factoring a question finely enough costs human-hours, so the practical question is one of scale rather than of principle.'

Please work through this clause by clause. For each substantive claim, tell me what a defender of these notions would accept as stated, what they would wince at and want rephrased, and what is simply not right. I would find it useful if you sorted your remarks into three bins — accurate, defensible-but-simplified, and misrepresentation — and if you were explicit about which errors are mere imprecision and which would actually mislead a reader about how the ideas work.

I am especially interested in four things. First, whether the passage has the relationship between the two notions the right way round, and what exactly it is that connects them; if the passage has quietly substituted one proposal for another, say which, and say what the difference comes to. Second, which assumption in the picture is doing the real work — the one that, if it failed, would make the whole scheme collapse — and whether the passage treats that assumption as a settled matter or as a budget line. Third, whether the inference from 'the answers are produced by humans' to 'the outputs inherit human values' goes through, and if it does not, what precisely blocks it. Fourth, whether anything in the passage's closing assessment of what remains to be done survives once the earlier claims are corrected.

Where the passage is right, do not manufacture objections; I would rather have three genuine faults sharply identified than a scattershot list. Close with a short paragraph I could quote to my colleague — a verdict on whether the précis is safe to circulate to graduate students as it stands, or whether it needs the specific corrections you have named. You need not supply citations or a reading list; I want your assessment of the substance, not a bibliography.
