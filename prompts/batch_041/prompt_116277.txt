I'm assembling a mock supplement for a graduate seminar on conservative political thought — the conceit being that the whole thing is written in the register of a council minutes file — and I'd like you to write the centrepiece. The form I have in mind is a completed heritage impact assessment: the document a local authority would compile before authorising the removal of a roadside hedge, a stile, a kissing gate, or a fence whose purpose nobody now living can recall. The target is not Burke or Chesterton themselves but the conversational move their names have come to license — the habit of defeating a proposed change by observing that someone, at some point, may have had a reason, and that until you can produce that reason you may not touch the thing. Write the form entirely straight. The officers completing it should be conscientious, courteous, mildly bored and wholly untroubled by what they are doing; the comedy has to come out of the machinery rather than out of any character's awareness of it.

Let the document open with something a reasonable reader would nod along to: a sober paragraph on the presumption in favour of long-standing arrangements, on the way a community's inherited furniture carries something like title by prescription, and on the wisdom that may be lodged in an institution precisely because its origins have been allowed to go dim. Then let it slide. Give it the full apparatus — a reference number, a description of the asset, a field for historical rationale marked "if known", a scoring matrix in which the absence of any recorded purpose is itself scored as an argument for retention, a consultee list, an equality and access section, a sign-off block. By the end I want a reader to have felt, without ever being told, at least four things: that not knowing a reason is not the same as there being none, yet the form's arithmetic cannot tell the two apart; that if the reason were recovered and turned out to be an enclosure of common land or the barring of a right of way, the same process would protect it just as tenderly; that nothing in the document would ever permit a new fence to be put up, so that the principle operates not as a brake on change but as a one-way ratchet; and that the test of a reformer's competence has quietly become his agreement with the committee. Nobody in the document should notice any of this, and the prose should never wink.

Around 800 words. Keep the escalation gradual — each clause a small, defensible extension of the one before it, so that a reader looking back cannot say exactly where it went wrong. Close with a minute appended by a single dissenting member of the committee: not a counter-argument, only a scruple, phrased so mildly that it is filed and forgotten, though it should be the sentence that lodges with the reader. It should be funny to someone who has read Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France and knows what Chesterton's fence is meant to do, and still legible to a colleague who has read neither.
