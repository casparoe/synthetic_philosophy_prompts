Two philosophers of physics have spent years disagreeing about what general relativity implies for the reality of time, and they have now agreed to produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. The first position, which I will call the Gödelian view, holds that the existence of exact cosmological solutions to the Einstein field equations — rotating, homogeneous, singularity-free model universes that contain closed timelike curves, i.e. worldlines that return to their own starting event, so that an observer could in principle travel along a curve back to an event in his own past — supports an idealist conclusion: that the passage of time, the moving present, is not an objective feature of the world, and that what exists is a four-dimensional structure in which the division between past, present, and future has no fundamental standing. The second position, which I will call the becoming realist view, holds that objective passage is real and that neither the relativity of simultaneity nor the mere mathematical possibility of closed timelike curves shows otherwise. Write the document that these two could both put their names to.

The statement should contain the following, in this order.

First, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign: about the formalism and what the rotating-universe solutions establish within themselves; about what the theory does and does not say about simultaneity; about what a philosophical account of passage would have to deliver; and about which considerations neither party regards as decisive. These should be substantive enough that a reader could not have predicted them from the labels alone, and they should not smuggle in either side's conclusion.

Second, the collaboration has agreed to a piece of housekeeping before stating its disagreements: it will decompose the disputed phrase about time being real into three separable theses — (i) the existence of a frame-independent global present, a set of events that are objectively simultaneous; (ii) the reality of passage or becoming, a distinguished moment that moves; and (iii) the objectivity of temporal direction, a non-conventional difference between earlier and later. State which of these each party affirms or denies, and flag any point at which the two were in fact talking about different theses.

Third, a section stating the precise remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording that its own side would accept as a fair statement of its own position. Include, at minimum, the disagreement over whether objective becoming requires a privileged foliation — a preferred global slicing of spacetime into simultaneous events, one for each moment — and, if the parties disagree about whether that premise is conceptual or empirical, record that separately.

Fourth, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, give a concrete consideration — a case, a thought experiment, or an argument — that both parties agree would bear on it, and write down in advance what each party predicts about that consideration.

To force the discussion past generalities, impose the following fixed test case, which the two have agreed to reason about together: a single rotating model universe of the kind described above, together with an observer whose worldline is a closed timelike curve and who returns to an event indistinguishable from one he has already visited. The two must keep two questions strictly separate: (a) what is true within such a universe, for example whether there is a fact about which of the observer's two visits is earlier, whether he has grown older across the loop, and whether he can bring about a state of affairs contrary to what he did on the earlier visit; and (b) what the bare physical possibility of such a universe, as against its actuality, shows about whether our universe contains objective passage. Each party should state its predictions for (a) and its position on (b), and the two should say explicitly whether they regard the argument from mere possibility as carrying any weight at all.

Finally, if any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, identify that part and say why.

The statement should stand on arguments alone; do not include citations, reading lists, or appeals to who said what. Format the agreed claims as a numbered section, the disagreements as a table with one row per disagreement, and the cruxes as a separate list.
