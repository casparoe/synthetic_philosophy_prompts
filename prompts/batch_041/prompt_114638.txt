I'm in a metaphysics reading group and we've gotten stuck on the identity of indiscernibles, specifically the old two-spheres-in-an-empty-universe case, and I've realized I have alot of gut verdicts about it that I can't fit under one principle. The setup is a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, same size, same mass, same surface, same intrinsic properties all the way down, placed symmetrically so that swapping them makes no observable difference. My first verdict is that there are definately two spheres, not one. If someone says there is only one sphere and the second is a redundant description, I feel like they've changed the subject. But then if you swap the two spheres, I think nothing has happened at all, and if you ask which one is which after the swap, I want to say there's no fact of the matter. So I seem to think the count is two even though no description distinguishes them.

I have other verdicts that may or may not fit. Take one sphere and have a machine make a perfect atom-for-atom copy in another room: obviously two, and not only because they're in different places. The copy has a different history, it was made and the original wasn't, and I think that history is enough to distinguish them even if it isn't intrinsic to either at the moment. But my friend Priya described a variant where the two spheres have identical histories down to the last detail and the whole universe is symmetric under swapping them, and I still feel there are two, though I can't say what makes them two. My friend Tom gave me a quantum case: two electrons in the singlet state. They're identical in all intrinsic respects, and swapping them gives a minus sign rather than a new state. I feel like there are two electrons there, but also that they're not individuals in the way the spheres are, and I can't tell if that's a different verdict or the same verdict in different clothes. I also made up a case: a computer simulation runs a perfect copy of my brain for one second, deletes it, then runs the exact same state again. My gut says two conscious episodes, but if the simulation has no memory between runs and no external time-stamp, I lose my grip on why it isn't one episode repeated. And two identical twins feel clearly like two people to me, even though I can't name an intrinsic property that seperates them, so maybe relational or historical facts are enough after all.

My tentative principle is that two things are numerically distinct just in case there is some true description that applies to one and not the other, even if the description is relational or historical. But the symmetric empty universe seems to have no such description, and I still want two spheres. Maybe the right move is to say that counting is primitive in that case, but then the swap case and the quantum case start to feel different from the sphere case, and I don't know if I'm allowed to have it both ways. Can you tell me what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what does it say about a case I haven't listed: a universe with two spheres that are identical in all intrinsic respects, where the two regions of space they occupy are qualitatively identical, and the whole universe has a topology that identifies those two regions after a 180-degree rotation. Is that two spheres, one sphere, or a case where the question has no answer? I'd rather have a principle I can actually use on new cases than a taxonomy of everybody's positions.
