I should like you to take a single, concrete episode and use it to interrogate the theory of invented tradition, running the analysis in both directions: the theory should illuminate the episode, and the episode should be allowed to embarrass the theory. The episode is the choreography of George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822.

Let me set out the facts I want you to work from, since you cannot consult anything and I would rather you argue from these than from a half-remembered version. The visit was the first by a reigning monarch to Scotland in nearly two centuries. Walter Scott, already famous for the romanticised Highlands of his novels, effectively stage-managed it, and it is chiefly remembered for having lifted the tartan kilt into the centre of Scottish national identity. Some of the salient details: Highland dress had been proscribed by statute after the Jacobite risings and the ban repealed in 1782; the so-called small kilt was itself a relatively recent innovation of the early eighteenth century rather than an immemorial garment; the Highland Societies that sprang up after the repeal were largely landowners' clubs that required members to appear in the old dress, and their "improvements" were bound up with the Clearances; for the visit Scott had the king outfitted in a bright Royal Stuart tartan, presented him as a Stuart and a Jacobite at heart, and brought the chiefs and societies into a plaided pageantry whose settings and "revived ancient dresses" were the work of a professional actor-manager. Two further facts matter. First, the clan tartans that the pageant presumed were largely fixed only later, by a volume published in 1842 that claimed to reproduce a fifteenth-century manuscript, was almost immediately denounced as a forgery, and was brought forward by brothers who falsely claimed descent from Bonnie Prince Charlie, yet whose invented designs passed into general and then official use. Second, Scott himself, in letters of 1829, rejected the authenticity of that manuscript and went further, denying that the clans had ever been distinguished by tartan at all, calling the whole idea a fashion of modern date.

Now the apparatus, stated so that we are working with the same tools rather than with whatever summary you happen to recall. Hobsbawm treats invented traditions as ritual or symbolic practices, governed by repetition, that seek to establish continuity with a suitable past, and he distinguishes them from customs, conventions, and mere routines; he holds that they characteristically serve three ends — social cohesion, the legitimation of institutions and authority, and the inculcation of values and beliefs — and that they flourish especially in the construction of the modern nation, which typically presents itself as the opposite of novel and the opposite of constructed. Anderson's imagined community is a different instrument, and part of what I want from you is a judgement about which instrument cuts better where; note in particular his insistence that imagined is not a synonym for fabricated or false. And keep in view a third strand, which I take to be genuinely unsettled: within folklore studies the criterion of authenticity has itself been disputed, with the charge that an emphasis on origins rather than on ongoing practice mislocates what makes a tradition a tradition, and that the very opposition between tradition and modernity is itself something of an invention.

I want the following, in order.

First, a disciplined mapping. Which of Hobsbawm's three functions does the 1822 pageant serve, and does it belong to his category of genuine invention or of revival dressed up as ancient? Who are the agents — is Scott an inventor, a stage-manager, a bricoleur, or something the vocabulary does not name? And what precisely is the object of invention: the garment, the pattern, the clan, or the monarch's Scottishness? Be exact about who is inventing what for whom.

Second, where the theory misdescribes the case. My suspicion, which I want you either to confirm or to correct, is that Hobsbawm's framework is too top-down and too instrumental, treating invention as an elite imposition in the service of cohesion and legitimation, whereas here the king was himself a dupe, the presiding genius was a sceptic about the very tradition he staged, and the tradition was taken up from below — by popular taste, by a diaspora, by clubs — in ways no one controlled. I am also struck that the forged tartans became official, which suggests that the theory's distinction between the authentic and the invented is doing less work than it advertises. Say whether these are real difficulties or only the expected texture of any large social process.

Third, and this is what actually interests me, the normative question. Grant the whole debunking story. Does the inventedness of the Highland tradition have any bearing on its value, its authority, or its claim on us? Is the inference from a disreputable origin to a diminished present standing simply a genetic fallacy, or does genealogy carry genuine critical force here? Be precise about what kind of thing a tradition is such that its origin could or could not matter to its authority — and if your answer is that it depends, say on what.

Fourth, what the episode does to the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? And does Anderson's vocabulary outperform Hobsbawm's at any point? Consider, too, whether the theory of invented tradition is not itself a candidate for the treatment it applies to others.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Name the consideration that, if it turned out one way rather than another, would move you off your verdict, and say where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
