I teach a unit on applied ethics every year, and the session on killing versus letting die has turned into a ritual. The students split into the usual camps — those who think the difference between doing something and merely letting it happen carries independent moral weight, and those who think it is a proxy for something else, whether intention, causal proximity, or the salience of the agent's contribution — and every year the same arguments get made and nobody is persuaded. What strikes me is that almost no one disputes the cases. We agree that switching the trolley feels different from pushing the fat man off the footbridge, that turning off a ventilator feels different from administering a lethal injection, and that the standard examples pull our intuitions in several directions at once; the fight is rarely about the facts, and only partly about the intuitions themselves. It is about what those intuitions are tracking. I have stopped believing that another clever case will break the deadlock, and I would like you to tell me why it will not — or why it might.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than take for granted, is that both camps are using the single phrase "the doing/allowing distinction" for two questions that need to be pulled apart. The first is a metaphysical or causal question: what did the agent actually do — did her body set a fatal process in motion, or did a process she could have interrupted simply run its course? The second is a normative question: does either causal role, just as such, generate a permission or a constraint that the other factors on the table do not already supply? If that split is right, I want to know whether the impasse dissolves once the two questions are separated, whether it merely relocates into the normative half, or whether a genuine substantive disagreement survives the separation — and if it survives, exactly what it is a disagreement about.

So please give me a diagnosis, not a verdict. Say which parts of the dispute are verbal, which are disagreements about which facts count or about what the intuitions are evidence for, and which, if any, rest on starting points that neither side can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, a case that actually discriminates between the camps rather than trading on some other difference, a formal result, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-posed — or, if you think no progress is available, why the debate can be expected to run forever without either side being unreasonable. Ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually say about the cases, and be candid about the parts of the disagreement you cannot resolve.
