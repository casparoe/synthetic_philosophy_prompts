I write fiction rather than political theory, and I have reached a scene I cannot finish until I have settled a question of vocabulary. My novel moves between Boston and Lyon. In one scene a man who would call himself, in English, a liberal says as much to a Frenchwoman, who receives the word as an accusation of doctrinaire free-market zeal; in a later scene, reversed, a German politician of a market-friendly party calls himself a liberal and an American diplomat hears a left-winger. I suspect I am exploiting a translation failure that is more interesting than a mere failure, and I would like a careful comparison before I commit to either scene.

Please compare what liberal means in American political speech with what its cognates mean in European political speech — not as a tour of dictionary senses, but as an analysis of two concept-clusters that happen to share a spelling. I am interested in the following, in whatever order best serves the argument.

First, content. What is a self-described liberal in each setting actually committed to, with respect to the market, the welfare state, religion, civil liberties, the nation, and the proper reach of the state? Where the two usages agree in substance, say so; where they diverge, describe the divergence precisely enough that I could write dialogue around it.

Second, the internal variety on the European side. My impression is that the European sense is not one thing: the French cognate carries a different charge from the German, and the British case is complicated by a party that owns the word. Say where the term lands hardest as an insult, where it functions as a proud self-description, and whether it is fair to claim that in parts of Central and Eastern Europe it has become chiefly an epithet deployed by the right.

Third, a test I can use. Give me pairs of concrete political positions such that the same person counts as a liberal under one usage and not under the other — ideally a case in which each usage would classify the other's paradigm liberal as something else entirely: a conservative, a socialist, a libertarian.

Fourth, the history, briefly. Both usages descend from a single nineteenth-century liberalism. Which one moved, when, and why? Is the American sense the drift and the European sense the retention, or is that too tidy a story?

Finally, the conceptual question, which is the one I actually need answered. Is this one word with two senses, or two concepts wearing one word, or a single concept whose content is fixed by the community that uses it? Would it help to describe liberalism as an essentially contested concept — W. B. Gallie's notion, from the mid-1950s, of a term whose users cannot stop disputing its proper use? And if the two political vocabularies carve the terrain differently, is there a residue that does not survive translation, something my characters could feel but never say?

On that last point I want your own judgment rather than a survey of opinion, and a practical answer to the novelist's question: in each of my two scenes, is the misfire a genuine disagreement, or two people who agree in substance and are separated only by a word? No sources or reading lists, please — I want the analysis itself.
