I'm a novelist — fourth book, embarrassingly overdue, don't ask — and for about a year now I've been running a monthly evening workshop for writers at our local arts centre. Fourteen of us, mixed bag: two published, most not, one woman who writes nothing but shipwrecks, and a retired GP who mainly comes for the biscuits. Lately I've got a bee in my bonnet about dreams, and not the symbolism — the metaphysics. Whether a dream is an experience at all, or whether it's something we only ever encounter afterwards, as a tale we suddenly find we can tell once the lights are on. One philosopher friend insists dreams can't be experiences in the ordinary sense: on her view, to have dreamt is just to be disposed to produce the report on waking, so nobody is ever really *in* a dream while it's going on. Another friend thinks that's mad, and that dreams are experiences we simply fail to store properly, so the waking account is a reconstruction rather than the thing itself. I'm told these are roughly Malcolm's and Dennett's corners of the argument, but I don't want a lecture on either of them — I want the group to walk straight into the problem and only find out afterwards that it has a name.

So please act as someone who designs experiential learning for philosophy — half games-designer, half seminar leader — and build me a runnable activity for these fourteen, roughly a hundred minutes, no kit beyond paper, envelopes, pens and somebody's phone as a timer. I've a hunch about the shape: the whole thing should turn on the fact that the only evidence any of us ever has about a dream is the waking report, so I'd like them to spend the middle of the session trying, and failing, to sort the genuine dreamers from the fabricators. My instinct is to hand sealed cards to a few of them telling them to invent a dream they never had — something they only thought up on the way in — with a bottle of something for whichever invented report the group judges most convincing, and to run the rest as a structured interview in which the only permitted questions are about what the dreamer can now do with the material (re-tell it, answer questions about it, sketch the layout, say what would have happened if...). But don't just take my hunch — brainstorm two or three possible structures first, tell me why you're rejecting the others, then commit to one and think it through carefully before you settle on the mechanics.

Give me the full thing: rules, roles, materials, the interview protocol, a minute-by-minute timeline, how the scoring works so that fabricating is genuinely worth attempting, and a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the two positions I sketched above, without my ever having to say "and this is called...". Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing someone will refuse to fabricate on principle, someone else will oversell it and give the game away, and the quiet ones will get steamrollered — and how to handle each of those. And be honest about the one thing the activity definately can't reproduce: my suspicion is that it can stage the evidential symmetry between a real dream and a merely reported one, but never the first-person fact of whether anything was experienced at all. If that's right, say so plainly, and tell me what I'll have to supply myself, in my own voice, at the very end.
