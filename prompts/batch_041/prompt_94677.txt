I sit on the acquisitions committee of the small university art museum where I teach — six of us, mostly faculty, plus the director and the curator of works on paper, and we meet Thursday to decide whether to buy a group of eleven works (mostly gouache on board, two large canvases) that our curator saw at a fair abroad last month. She came back lit up: says they're the best things she's seen in a decade, that they'd anchor the twentieth-century gallery, and that the price — 400k out of a restricted acquisition fund that expires at the end of the fiscal year — is once-only, because another buyer is circling. The problem is that none of us has stood in front of any of them. What we have is a dossier of photos, a condition report, and forty minutes of her talking at us. Our charter, drafted in the 70s by nobody knows whom, says the committee shall not acquire works "not experienced by the committee," which everyone has always treated as a formality for donated prints, but two members are now brandishing it. Their line, and they're not fools, is that a borrowed judgment of taste isn't a judgment at all — you can't know a painting is good the way you know Lisbon is in Portugal, the most you can know is that someone you trust thinks so, and a museum shouldn't drop 400k on "someone thinks so." They keep invoking something they call the acquaintance principle, which they say is standard in aesthetics. A third colleague has floated a compromise she calls the borrowed-eyes rule: we may buy on the strength of a report from a viewer we've vetted for expertise and disinterest, provided the report is rich enough to be argued with and the viewer stands behind it in writing.

Two wrinkles I should mention. The curator has wanted this artist in the collection since her dissertation, and the seller is her former student — she disclosed both, unprompted, and nobody thinks she's lying, but it's in the room. And the funder requires a one-page written justification of any purchase over 250k, filed with the minutes, which is basically the document I'm asking you for.

So: draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday. I need a concrete recommendation — buy, don't buy, or buy conditional on something specific, with a time frame that respects the other buyer and the fund deadline — and reasons the two holdouts could actually engage with, not a restatement of the charter or a lecture about how committees delegate. Three things I'd especially like you to sort out: (1) can the committee approve the purchase while being honest that it is not itself judging the work good, or is that a fudge; (2) is the borrowed-eyes rule a real principle or just a way of letting us buy things, and if it's real, what would the vetting and the written report actually have to contain; and (3) does it change anything that the testimony comes from someone with an interest in the outcome, or is that just a discount rate we can apply. Give me the strongest version of the holdouts' case somewhere in there, because I have to read this out loud to them.
