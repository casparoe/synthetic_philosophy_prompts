I am a retired natural scientist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy, and I have run into a methodological puzzle that I cannot settle on my own.

The practice I have in mind is the one by which historians of the historical Jesus decide which sayings attributed to him are authentic. One assembles the sayings tradition, runs it through a battery of criteria — a saying is judged early if the first Christians would have found it embarrassing to invent, if it is attested in several independent strands of tradition, if it is unlike both the Judaism of the first century and the Christianity that followed, if it coheres with material already accepted, if it preserves a Semitic idiom — and treats whatever survives the filter as the authentic core. In one well-publicised project of the 1980s and 1990s the judgements were even put to a vote, the tallies being published as a summary of the evidence.

Two features of this puzzle me. The criteria do not look independent of one another, and some appear to pull in opposite directions: a saying stripped of everything Jewish and everything Christian by the criterion of dissimilarity can hardly be expected to cohere with much else, and the criterion of embarrassment seems to rest on a substantial empirical claim about what a community will and will not preserve. And in my own field, agreement with the result one already expects is a reason for suspicion, whereas here coherence with the accepted core is counted as evidence for a saying's authenticity.

So I should like you to tell me what theory of historical inference this practice presupposes — about testimony and its transmission, about the independence of the evidence, about whether a recoverable authentic core exists at all. What must it assume about the psychology of the communities that preserved and shaped the tradition? Where is the method philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that rival accounts of what the historian is up to would license precisely the same procedure? And if the criteria were replaced by an explicitly probabilistic treatment, what would have to be supplied that they silently assume?
