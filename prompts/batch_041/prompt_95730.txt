I am preparing a recorded interview for a student-paper series with a philosopher who has spent several years defending a graded conception of sentience, and I need questions that will press her well past her rehearsed formulations. Her position, in outline, is this. Sentience is not a binary property but a magnitude, constituted by four functional markers: the presence of valenced states, multimodal sensory binding, a temporal window that unites states into episodes, and a minimal self-model. Moral status is a separate magnitude, obtained by multiplying the degree of sentience by a stake factor, understood as the degree to which a creature's states can go better or worse for it. She also holds that the markers combine non-compensatorily and with a floor: a system scoring near zero on valence or on binding has no moral status whatever, while above that floor the remaining markers trade off continuously against one another. She is practiced at deflecting challenges with the remark that she is offering a measurement program rather than a metaphysics, and I want questions that make that deflection progressively harder to use.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each accompanied by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At a minimum, I want one question on each of the following matters:

1. whether the floor in her account is a sharp line or a region of indeterminacy, and what she would say to the charge that introducing a threshold merely relocates the difficulty it was meant to resolve;
2. how the non-compensatory structure handles a system that scores maximally on valence and minimally on temporal thickness, and what she would conclude if such a system outranked one scoring moderately on every marker;
3. whether a purely functional realization of the four markers suffices, and precisely where her account draws the line between a thermostat-like artifact and a marginal animal;
4. what the account implies for insects, and how she would answer the worry that the markers cannot be independently measured well enough for the account to be empirically testable rather than merely adaptable to any verdict;
5. how the stake factor is to be identified without tacitly presupposing the moral status it is supposed to explain;
6. whether a scalar conception of moral status can accommodate the aggregation of interests, that is, whether one being of high degree may be sacrificed for many of low degree, and on what principle;
7. how the markers are to be weighted, and whether the weights are a substantive discovery or a conventional stipulation;
8. one question designed to make the measurement-program deflection as difficult as possible to deploy.

The questions should turn on the internal commitments of her view rather than on its motivation, and should not be answerable by restating the view. Where a question admits two likely replies, supply a follow-up for each. Please do not ask how she came to hold the view, and do not ask her to recommend readings or to name her critics.
