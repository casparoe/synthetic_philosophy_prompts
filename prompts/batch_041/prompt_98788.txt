Many states adopt, and deliberately publicize, a policy of refusing to pay ransoms or make other concessions to kidnappers, and the publicity is meant to do the work: the rule deters only because it is known in advance. Consider what the rule demands when it is most costly. A single hostage could be freed today by an exception that would never become known; instead a rescue is attempted and some hostages die. Officials defend the refusal by saying that conceding once invites more kidnappings, so the right act is the one the policy dictates rather than the one that saves this particular hostage. What theory of rational choice does that defense presuppose? Does it require something like an updateless or policy-based standard — choosing as though the same decision procedure were run in every relevantly similar situation, and refusing to treat the fact that one has reached this particular decision point as new information that licenses re-optimizing — or can ordinary precommitment, rule-consequentialism, or a deontological rule explain the practice without it? Note that officials often offer two different defenses: that paying would fund and encourage future kidnapping, a claim about the consequences of this act, and that the rule must be exceptionless to stay credible, a claim about the policy. Do those defenses presuppose the same theory, or does the practice quietly mix an act-based with a policy-based standard? Is publicity essential to the presupposed theory or merely an implementation detail? And is the same state's willingness to bargain in other hostage and criminal matters — sieges, plea deals, ordinary negotiations — an inconsistency, or a sign that no single implicit theory was ever in play? Reconstruct the implicit decision theory as carefully as you can, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, name the rival theories it does and does not rule out, and say what would have to change if the state replaced it with a straightforwardly act-based view.
