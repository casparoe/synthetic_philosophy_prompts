I'm a PhD student in philosophy putting together a 90-minute session for an upper-level seminar of about twenty students, and I want an activity that makes them feel the force of Lewis's account of convention before they've read a word of it — the parts that are easy to nod at but hard to feel: that a convention can arise with no agreement and no authority; that it is arbitrary in the sense that another regularity would have served equally well; that it is nonetheless self-enforcing and gets treated as though it were justified; and that it can be stubbornly hard to move even when everyone would prefer a different arrangement. Design a runnable game for me: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, the sequence of rounds, and the shocks I should build in — at minimum a moment where a newcomer who never witnessed the initial convergence behaves differently, and a moment where the payoffs shift so that the established regularity is no longer the best one — so that the group runs into the coordination problem, the arbitrariness, the self-enforcement, and the stickiness rather than being told about them. Keep the word "convention" out of the room until the debrief. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the pieces of the account, an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — in particular the gap between shared behavior and common knowledge of shared behavior, and whether anything in the game earns the normative "we ought to" that people attach to conventions — and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. In the debrief material, mark which points are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on convention — whether conventions are exhausted by coordination, whether they require common knowledge or only mutual expectation, and whether the arbitrariness is as thoroughgoing as the account suggests — and don't present any contested reading as the consensus.
