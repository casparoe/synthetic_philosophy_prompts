I write the science-and-ideas column for a general-interest monthly, and for our forthcoming issue on the theme of categories the editors have asked me to assemble a self-scoring "final examination": one hard essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme readers can use to grade themselves. The topic is the one that has quietly occupied both type designers and cognitive scientists for decades — what makes every 'a' an 'a'.

I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the received lines, whether that a letter is a Platonic form, that 'a' is simply whatever a language community agrees to call 'a', or that letters are family-resemblance categories and leaving it there. So I have invented a concrete case to force the issue, and I would like the question built around it.

The case: a fictional foundry, Fenwick & Voss, releases a display face called Gable, whose brief is to push a single letter to its breaking point. In Gable, one 'a' serves both the uppercase and lowercase positions, so the case distinction is abolished. The 'a' is single-storey in the caption weights and double-storey in the display weights, so a reader meets two quite different constructions under one name. And the 'a' and the 'e' are exact 180-degree rotations of each other, so the same contour reads as 'a' or 'e' depending only on which way up it is printed. A fictional critic for a design magazine has already pronounced that Gable's 'a' is not really an 'a' but an 'e' lying on its side.

I would also like the question to hand students two tools I have coined for the piece, so that the analysis has somewhere to bite. Call the first the erasure test: a feature of a glyph is genre-defining only if removing it changes which letter a competent reader sees. Call the second the swap test: a feature is style-defining if transplanting it onto a neighbouring letter preserves that letter's identity while altering the family resemblance. Part of the exercise should be to show where these tests strain or come apart — for instance, on letters like 'i' and 'j', which share a body and differ only by a dot, or on the near-collision of 'l', 'I', and '1' in many sans-serif faces, where identity seems to rest on context and system rather than on any feature of the mark itself.

Please write: the essay question, aimed at an educated general reader with some design or cognitive-science interest and answerable in about ninety minutes; a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words; and a marking scheme of five must-hit moves totalling 20 marks, with a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs candidates each mark. The question should require a defended verdict on whether Gable's 'a' is an 'a', and it should force the reader to say whether the verdict is a matter of fact, of convention, or of the perceiver's learned habit — and what evidence would settle it.
