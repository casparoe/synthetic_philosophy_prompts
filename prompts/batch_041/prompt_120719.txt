I retired from experimental physics three years ago and have spent much of my retirement catching up on philosophy, in the company of five or six others who meet fortnightly. Our next subject is the sea battle, and I have been asked to produce the discussion aid — largely, I suspect, because I am the only one of us who finds it natural to say that the state of a system at one time fixes its state at later times, and yet cannot see how a sentence about tomorrow could already be true today. What I want from you is a decision tree rather than a list of names: an instrument that takes a reader from an initial commitment to a labelled position on whether statements about the contingent future have truth values.

Begin from whichever commitment you judge the rest to hang on; my own starting point, if it helps, is that I do accept that a sentence about tomorrow can be true today, without being able to say what that commits me to. Each node should be a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and wherever possible it should turn on a concrete case rather than a slogan. I would like the following cases to appear somewhere on the tree: the sea battle itself; a written prediction later vindicated by events; a prediction that helps bring itself about, and one that prevents itself; a prediction about a choice the agent has not yet made, together with the complaint that if the prediction is already true there is no point deliberating; a statement about the future that no one will ever utter or entertain; and a statement about the future that is fixed by present laws and initial conditions. The tree should also separate, if they are genuinely separate, the claim that the future is unsettled from the claim that it is merely unknowable, and the claim that a true statement about the future requires something in the present to make it true from the claim that it does not.

I would like these views to emerge as leaves, named as you see fit, with any leaf that has no standard name marked as unnamed: the view on which future contingents are neither true nor false; the view on which they are all false; the view on which they carry ordinary truth values fixed by what actually happens; the view on which truth value is relative to the standpoint from which the statement is assessed, so that a vindicated prediction counts as true when we look back at it but not when it was made; the view on which such statements have truth values but no truthmakers; the view on which their truth value is settled by God's knowledge of what his creatures would freely do in circumstances that never arise; and the view on which they have truth values simply because the future is already settled. If two of these turn out to be one leaf under two descriptions, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction.

Give the tree as a numbered, indented outline, no more than six levels deep, so that I can trace a path aloud at the meeting, and add for each leaf one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Then, separately: which combinations of answers your tree permits that nobody appears to occupy, and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored; which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and why; and which questions you deliberately left off, with a note on what including them would have cost.

Please dispense with attributions, citations and reading lists — I have no library to check them against, and I would rather have the structure than the credit. Kindly write in British English.
