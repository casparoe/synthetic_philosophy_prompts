Ok so I'm makeing a handout for a grad reading group on intentionality and I need a dialogue written out. Set it up as two people arguing — call them A and B — where A holds something like Searle's line that there's a real difference in kind between original/intrinsic intentionality (what minds have) and derived intentionality (what words, pictures, thermostats, and vending machines have), and B holds something like Dennett's line that the difference is at most a difference in degree, complexity, or history, and that once you've got a system whose states can be reliably predicted from the intentional stance, you've got intentionality, full stop, with no extra ingredient left over.

Have them work through the two-bitser case in real detail. A vending machine is designed in the US to accept quarters; it gets shipped to Panama (by mistake, in one version) where it happily accepts Panamanian quarter-balboas, which are physically indistinguishable to it. B says: look, the machine's internal state is now tracking balboas, or at least it's about whatever it's responding to, and its content is fixed by its causal/functional role, so the US/Panama distinction can't be a difference in kind. A says: no, the state doesn't mean anything at all in the intrinsic sense — it has whatever meaning we assign it, and the design history plus our interpretation is doing all the work. That's the whole point.

Then make them push into the weirder variants, because that's where I actually want the dialogue to earn its keep:

1. The machine is shipped to Panama deliberately, by its manufacturer, as a cost-saving move, with a manual that says it accepts quarter-balboas. Does that change anything about what the state means? A will want to say no, still derived; B will want to say the history now makes balboa-content the proper function.
2. A Panamanian engineer takes the machine apart and builds an exact duplicate from scratch, but the duplicate is never used for money — it's used as a doorstop, and by coincidence a balboa rolls into the slot every morning. What does its state represent, if anything? Does the answer differ from case 1?
3. The machine is fitted with a little screen that prints I detected a quarter in English, and a second version that prints I detected a balboa. Both have identical internal wiring. A and B should disagree about whether the screen output is a report of the machine's content or just a label we've stuck on it.

I want A to make the strongest Searlean moves — the syntax/semantics point, the observer-relativity point, the you've only described what we can predict, not what the state is about point — and I want B to make the strongest Dennettian moves — the intentional stance as predictive strategy, the continuity with thermostats and immune systems, the where exactly is the magic line? challenge, the appeal to selection history and proper function. Neither should strawman the other; when one makes a point, the other should actually engage with it before moving on, and I'd like at least two places where they concede something small.

End the dialogue with them identifying one concrete case where their disagreement would cash out in a prediction or a verdict that differs, rather than just trading intuitions. If they can't find one, have them say so honestly and explain why the disagreement might be merely verbal. Keep it maybe 1200-1800 words. No citations needed, no as Searle says on p. X stuff — just the argument.
