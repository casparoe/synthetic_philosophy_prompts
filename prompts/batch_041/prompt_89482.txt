Ok here's one I've been chewing on. Karoshi in Japan — the fact that a rich country had to invent a word, and a whole legal and medical compensation category, for people worked to death, and the public reckoning that followed. I want you to run that episode through Weber's Protestant work ethic thesis. The interesting snag is that Japan is about as non-Protestant as it gets, so don't just wave at the obvious fit; map it properly. Which parts of the Weberian machine actually transfer — the anxiety about idleness, worldly asceticism, work as a sign rather than a means — and which parts clearly don't (predestination, the specific religious engine)? And then tell me whether karoshi is evidence for the thesis, against it, or a case the thesis never imagined. Then flip it: what does karoshi actually show about whether there's a duty to work at all, and whether leisure is a right — is the work-ethic framing even doing moral work here, or is it just laundering labor-market power? One thing I want you to press on: wether the thing being enforced is really a duty to labor or only a duty to be visibly burdened, and whether that distinction matters for the ethics of the whole thing.
