Im a novelist — third draft of a book about a public defender who has some kind of moral crack-up, so I've been mainlining the free will and punishment literature on the side, mostly so I dont embarass myself in the courtroom scenes. Anyway, I was at a going-away party last weekend and two people I know went at each other for a solid forty minutes, and it was honestly the most useful thing thats happened to the book so far. Ruth teaches criminal law. Dev is a philosophy grad student who's fully in on the free-will-skeptic side of the whole Dennett-Caruso thing. It went roughly in rounds:

1. Dev: if nobody has the kind of free will that grounds basic desert — the idea that suffering is fitting just because of what you did, apart from deterrence or keeping anyone safe — then retributive punishment is out. All we get is a quarantine model: we confine the dangerous the way we isolate carriers of a contagious disease, no resentment attached, and we let them out when they stop being dangerous.

2. Ruth: quarantine can't carry that weight, because waiting for someone to actually offend is arbitrary if safety is the whole point. The person we can reliably predict will offend is a better candidate for confinement than the one who already did. Your model gives me no reason to wait for the crime.

3. Dev: the reason to wait is epistemic, not moral. After an offense I know what I'm dealing with. Predictions about people who've never done anything are garbage in the real world, and the state shouldn't cage people on garbage.

4. Ruth: then whether it's wrong to cage an innocent person depends on how good our predictions happen to be. If the neuroscience got good enough — a scan that's basically never wrong — you'd have to say it's fine to lock someone up for what he's going to do, and that's the reductio. And look at what got thrown out on the way. Blame and gratitude aren't just behavior-modification levers. Resentment is how we take someone seriously as a person, and if you spend a few generations telling everyone that nobody is ever really responsible, the practice of holding each other responsible erodes, and you've lost something you need.

5. Dev: that whole speech is an argument about consequences, not about desert. Defending desert-talk because of what it does isn't showing that anyone ever deserves anything. And taking people seriously cuts both ways — it might be more respectful to say we're holding you because we have to, not because you deserve to suffer. Also, if you're only keeping the language of desert for its effects, aren't you a fictionalist about it, telling the public something you think is literally false because it's useful? And for that matter your own compatibilism can't get you basic desert anyway: being responsive to reasons is one thing, being someone it's fitting to make suffer is another.

By the end they were talking past each other, I think, and I honestly couldn't tell whether that's because they really disagree or because the word deserve is doing two jobs in there. So: can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what? Rate all five from clearly mistaken to clearly right, a sentence or two of justification each, then say who has the better of it overall and whether the fight is really substantive or partly verbal. American English, and please dont dumb it down — I'd rather look up three words than get a summary.
