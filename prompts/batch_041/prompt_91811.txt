I never took a philosophy class, but I've gotten kind of obsessed with the "greatest conceivable thing" arguments and I've been keeping a notebook of my verdicts on the versions I run into, mostly because I want to know whether I'm being consistent or just reacting to how fancy the wording is. Heres what's in the notebook:

1. The classic one. God is the thing than which nothing greater can be conceived. You've got it in your mind; but a thing that exists only in your mind could be conceived to be greater, namely by also existing for real; so the greatest conceivable thing must exist for real. Verdict: no good. The move that does all the work is that existing for real is a bonus a thing can be missing, like a feature you add on. But existing isn't a feature sitting on top of a thing, it's what makes it have features at all.

2. The island version. Same argument, "island" instead of God. Verdict: no good, and it fails in exactly the same spot, which I take to be the whole point of it.

3. The one where existence is called a perfection, and God is supposed to be perfect in every way, so existing has to be part of what God is. Verdict: no good. Same trick, fancier word.

4. The modal one. First move: it's possible that a greatest conceivable being exists. And since a greatest conceivable being couldn't exist in only some worlds, it exists in all of them, ours included. Verdict: no good, but this one bugs me, because it doesn't seem to make the mistake in 1-3 at all. What it does instead is start from a possibility claim that's exactly as strong as the conclusion. And if I'm allowed to say "it's possible" like that, I can say "it's possible that no greatest conceivable being exists" with the same confidence, run the same machinery, and get the opposite answer. So nothing was actually settled, the argument just restates the fight.

5. Same as 4, except the first move is "nobody has ever shown it's impossible," which I think is just 4 with the burden of proof hidden inside it. Verdict: no good, same reason.

Now a friend of mine who actually reads this stuff showed me a version that's basically all formal logic, and I can't tell what to do with it. Here's what it says as best I understand it:

- Properties come in two kinds, positive and not. Every property is either positive or its negation is positive, but never both.
- If a positive property entails another property in every possible world, that other property is positive too.
- A being is "God-like" if it has every positive property. And being God-like is itself one of the positive properties.
- Positive properties dont involve any contradiction, so if a property is positive then it's at least possible that something has it.
- Necessary existence (not existence — the kind of existence where the thing couldn't have failed to exist) is positive.
- Then he uses the rule that if something is possibly necessary, it's necessary, and out comes: a God-like being exists, and exists necessarily.

His pitch is that this one never says "existing is a bonus" and never just asserts "it's possible" — the possibility comes out of the other rules, and the only thing it assumes about greatness is which properties count as positive. My gut says it's items 1 through 3 in a tuxedo, but a gut isn't an answer.

So here's what I want. Going by my notebook, what rule or rules am I actually running? Does the new one pass or fail on each of them, and which rule should I go with, and why? And is there some small change to the new argument — a tweak to one of its rules, or to how it defines God-like — that would make the different answers come apart, so I'd know which one I really believe? I care way more about whether my notebook is coherent than about whether God exists.
