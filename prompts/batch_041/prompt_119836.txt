I've been working on a long feature about the republican theory of freedom — the neo-Roman crowd who insist you're unfree not only when someone actually interferes with you, but whenever you live at the mercy of someone who *could*, so the slave with a kind master is still a slave, and the point of free institutions isn't just to minimize meddling but to make sure nobody is in a position to meddle arbitrarily. I'm a science and ideas writer, so I'm supposed to have a view on this, but the more I report the less sure I am, and I got into a green-room argument after a podcast taping last month that I can't stop chewing on. So I want you to pin yourself down to a number, and then show me the machinery behind it.

Here's the setup. Imagine a single global AI custodian — call it the Custodian — with the technical capacity to intervene in literally any human affair, which never has to ask anyone's permission and answers to no one, but which is also, by its architecture, incapable of wanting to harm us or to treat us unfairly. Benevolence isn't a policy it adopted; it's baked into what it is. There's no version of it that turns on us, and no coalition of humans could ever push it to, bribe it, or vote it out. It publishes its reasoning, but there's no appeal, no exit, and no mechanism by which any person or assembly could contest or replace a decision. My colleague's read: the republicans should be fine with this. Non-domination just means nobody has the *capacity* to interfere on an arbitrary basis, a necessarily-benevolent Custodian's power is never arbitrary, so nobody is dominated, and its subjects are free in the fullest sense even though they can't contest a single thing it does. I think that's wrong, but I want your credence rather than a vibe. So: what's your credence that a necessarily-benevolent, wholly unaccountable sovereign does *not* dominate its subjects — that my colleague's verdict on the Custodian is the right one?

Don't just give me the number, decompose it. Roughly what I'm after: your probability that "arbitrariness," in the republican sense, is really about the absence of any check by the governed rather than about the presence of a live risk that power gets misused; your probability that the relevant *capacity* to interfere is a modal property, so that whether the Custodian dominates turns on whether there's a nearby possible world where it abuses someone; your probability that the Custodian actually *promotes* positive liberty, in the sense of giving people the material and psychological conditions for self-mastery, and whether that should count for anything in the verdict; your probability that even if the concept comes out my way, the concept isn't the thing we should care about anyway, so the real disagreement is about what non-domination is *for*; and whatever else you think belongs in the sum, each with a number and a line of justification, then the combination. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most — an argument, a case, a bit of history, whatever. Two side notes: I'm at about 0.85 that the Custodian's subjects are dominated and my colleague's at 0.15, so I'd also like your guess at where the real seperate disagreement lives, in the analysis of arbitrariness or further upstream in what freedom is for; and please check your Custodian verdict against the obvious contrast case, a chaotic elected government that interferes with people constantly but that anyone can criticize, organize against, and throw out — because if you tell me the meddling democracy is non-dominating and the benevolent Custodian is too, I want to know whether you're actually running one rule or two.
