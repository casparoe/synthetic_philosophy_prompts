I am a doctoral candidate working at the intersection of tort theory and the metaphysics of causation, and I want to use the House of Lords decision in Airedale NHS Trust v Bland (1993) as a stress test for theories of causation by omission. I have the standard facts in mind: Tony Bland was crushed at Hillsborough in 1989 and left in a persistent vegetative state; the hospital, with his parents' support, sought a declaration that it might lawfully discontinue life-sustaining measures, including artificial nutrition and hydration; the Law Lords granted the declaration, reasoning that the discontinuation was properly characterized as an omission rather than an act, that a doctor is under no duty to continue treatment that is not in the patient's best interests, and that continued feeding therefore could not be in his interests; he died shortly afterwards. I am not asking for a doctrinal survey or a reading list. I want the metaphysics, run in both directions.

Analyze the case through two accounts of omission causation: the counterfactual-dependence account, on which an omission causes an outcome when the outcome depends on whether the agent acted, and the duty-based account associated with Hart and Honoré, on which an omission counts as a cause only where the agent was under a duty to act. For each account, say what it says the doctors faced, what it identifies as the cause of Bland's death, and which features of the decision it explains and which it misdescribes or cannot reach.

Then put pressure on the case from the theory's side, addressing at least these points. First, at the level of bodily movement, disconnecting a pump or declining to replace a tube is something done; the court's characterization of it as an omission appears to depend on a normative baseline about what a doctor is required to do. If the causal status of the omission depends on a prior judgment about duty, and the duty depends on a judgment about the patient's best interests, is the causal claim doing independent work, or is it a moral conclusion restated in causal vocabulary? Say where, precisely, the theory gets its footing.

Second, on the counterfactual account, continued feeding would have prolonged Bland's life, so the withdrawal makes a difference and is a but-for cause, yet the court denies that it is a killing. Does the case show that counterfactual dependence is insufficient for causation, or that legal cause is simply a different concept from metaphysical cause? Be explicit about which claim you are defending.

Third, the agent question. The omission is attributed to the doctors, but the decision was made on the family's application and ratified by a court. If omissions are causes only relative to a duty, and the duty-holder is the doctor, do the family and the court drop out of the causal story entirely, and is there a principled way to say whose omission it was? If the answer is whoever bears the duty, the theory needs an independent account of how duties are assigned, and the case suggests that account will be normative throughout.

Fourth, withdrawal versus withholding. The court treats discontinuing artificial nutrition as equivalent to never having started it. Is that equivalence a consequence of the theory or an assumption the theory imports? If there is a genuine asymmetry between removing an obstacle and failing to provide one, the case may be an anomaly for any account that treats them alike.

Finally, tell me where the theory is silent. If the best account of the case makes the act/omission distinction turn on a substantive judgment about interests, does the metaphysics of causation have anything left to contribute, or is it a vocabulary for restating a moral conclusion? If it does have something to contribute, describe the kind of case that would actually decide between the counterfactual and the duty-based accounts, and say what the Bland decision would look like under each.
