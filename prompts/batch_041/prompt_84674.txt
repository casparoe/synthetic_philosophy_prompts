I'm teaching Mill's Utilitarianism in a grad seminar next week, and every time I get to the middle of chapter 2 I realize I don't have a clean story about what Mill is actually up to. Here's the stretch I keep circling:

Human beings have faculties more elevated than the animal appetites, and when once made conscious of them, do not regard anything as happiness which does not include their gratification.

and a few lines later:

It is quite compatible with the principle of utility to recognise the fact, that some kinds of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others.

My problem is that I want to read this as an early instance of idealized deliberation — the standard of value is fixed by what a suitably idealized or well-placed judge would endorse, and the experienced judges who've tasted both kinds of pleasure are standing in for that idealized judge. But I can also read it as a much more defaltionary empirical claim about what people who've tried both actually prefer, which then makes the whole quality-of-pleasures doctrine hostage to a survey. And I gather there's a third reading where the judges are just an epistemic device and the real normative work is done by the principle of utility itself.

Could you walk me through the passage and help me see which of these is the most defensible? I'm especially interested in whether Mill's move here is really continuous with the later ideal-advisor / ideal-observer tradition in indirect normativity, or whether that's an anachronistic overlay that flattens what he's doing. And if the judges are idealized, what does the idealization consist in — experience only, or something more like full information and coherence?

I want to be careful about the difference between what's actually settled in the Mill literature and what's genuinely contested. So please flag which parts of your answer are things pretty much everyone who works on this agrees on, and which parts are live disagreements where you're giving me one side. I don't want to walk into seminr and present a contested reading as if it's the consensus.
