I am a high-school student in my final year and I run the philosophy-of-history strand of my school's philosophy club. We have spent the term on the question of what it means to explain a historical event, and I have been asked to set the mock paper that will show whether the group has genuinely understood the disagreement or has merely memorised the labels. I would like you to write the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Some background you should use. We read about the covering-law model of explanation, the view usually traced to Carl Hempel, on which to explain an event is to show that a statement of it follows deductively from general laws together with statements of initial conditions, so that explanation and prediction are two uses of the same logical structure. We then read the objection, common among philosophers of history, that historians explain by telling narratives rather than by subsuming events under laws, and that a narrative can be explanatory even when it contains no general law at all.

The problem I want the paper to reach is one I have not seen stated cleanly in anything we read, and I would like the exam to turn on it. There seem to be two different things one might want from an explanation of a historical event. The first is an account of why the event happened at all. The second is an account of why it happened rather than the alternative that was also open. The covering-law model looks well suited to the first and poorly suited to the second, since deducing that the event occurred tells you nothing about why the other outcome did not. Narratives, by contrast, often seem to explain precisely by showing that the outcome was contingent, that it could have gone otherwise; and yet we would normally say that a narrative which says only that it could have gone otherwise has explained nothing. I would like the question to force students to confront this, rather than to rehearse the covering-law debate in general.

To make it concrete, use the following case, which I invented for the paper. Two students explain the same event: the collapse, in 2019, of our school's debating society, which had run for thirty years. Explanation A runs: whenever a voluntary society depends on a single organiser and that organiser leaves without a successor, the society collapses; the organiser left without a successor; therefore the society collapsed. Explanation B runs: the organiser had been the society's centre for a decade; the two members most likely to succeed her had fallen out over the previous year; the school's new timetable moved the society's slot to a lunch hour that clashed with the orchestra; and a single autumn recruitment drive failed by two members, when three would have been enough. Explanation B closes by noting that had that drive succeeded by one more member, the society would have survived.

I would like the exam question to be answerable in 45 minutes and roughly 800 to 1000 words. It should not be answerable by summarising the covering-law model and its critics; it should require the student to say what each explanation is doing, whether Explanation B contains an explanation that Explanation A lacks, and what the two would have to disagree about for the dispute to be a real one rather than two people answering different questions.

Please supply: first, the question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper; second, a model answer of roughly 600 words; third, a marking scheme out of 20, built from five things a top answer must do, with the marks for each, one line on how candidates typically get each wrong, and an explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer.

Two things about the answer itself. First, do not treat the debate as settled: where philosophers of history agree and where they genuinely disagree, say which is which, and do not present any one resolution as the consensus. Second, wherever the model answer takes a side, mark it as a side rather than as the answer.
