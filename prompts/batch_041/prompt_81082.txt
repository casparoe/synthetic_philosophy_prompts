I'm recording an episode of our department's podcast with a visiting philosopher who has spent years defending a version of the moral equality of combatants that departs from the familiar Walzerian defence. Her line is that combatants on both sides are equally permitted to kill one another, but not because the justice of the war is irrelevant to them: she locates responsibility for a war's justice in the political leadership and responsibility for conduct in the military, and she holds that individual combatants are rarely in a position to assess the former. She also accepts that a combatant is liable to be killed only if he is responsible for an unjust threat. Draft eight interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on (a) whether the equality she defends is an equality of permission, of liability, or merely of excuse, and what turns on the difference; (b) what her division of responsibility implies for volunteers who enlist to fight a war they have reason to think is unjust, and for citizens of a democracy that voted for it; (c) whether the uncertainty that grounds the equality also loosens the constraints on conduct, since an unjust combatant who cannot know his cause is unjust likewise cannot know whether his targets are proportionate; and (d) what she takes to be the strongest consideration against her view. Skip anything that invites her simply to restate the position, and press her where the revisionist literature presses.
