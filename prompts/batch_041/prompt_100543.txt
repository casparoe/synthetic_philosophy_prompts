Design a runnable activity for a ninety-minute session in a graduate seminar on the ethics of emerging technology. There are eighteen participants, who will work at three parallel tables of six. Assume they know the broad shape of the debate over autonomous systems but have not thought carefully about responsibility; do not build the session around any particular author or text.

The goal is for participants to feel a specific difficulty rather than be told about it: that an autonomous system can produce a serious harm for which no human being is blameworthy, even though competent adults designed it, approved its deployment, operated it, and profited from it. The session should leave them able to distinguish two things that are easily run together. The first is backward-looking blameworthiness, which normally requires that an agent had the capacity to control or foresee the relevant outcome. The second is forward-looking answerability, the question of who must now account for the harm and change the system. The activity must make these come apart in the participants' hands.

Build it as a structured role-play or simulation, not a debate. Supply the following.

Roles. Six per table, including a designer, a procurement or deployment decision-maker, an operator, an external auditor or regulator, a frontline worker who deals with the system's outputs, and a person who bears the harm. Give each role private information and a private objective, and arrange the incentives so that each participant has a sincere, non-cynical reason to disclaim responsibility for the outcome. The disclaimers must not be evasions; each must be defensible given what that role knew and could do.

Mechanism. Choose a concrete setting in which an autonomous system allocates a scarce, life-relevant resource across a population, and let the harm be a distributional one that emerges from the interaction of decisions made by different roles, none of whom could have foreseen it alone. The mechanism should produce the harm through the ordinary, well-intentioned operation of the rules, not through anyone breaking them.

Two gaps. The design should let participants discover, in sequence, the difference between a gap that is merely epistemic, where someone in principle could have known what the system would do, and a gap that is a genuine gap in control, where the outcome was not within anyone's power to determine. It should also force them to separate a genuine responsibility gap from the mere diffusion of responsibility across many hands. Include a moment where the group tries to close the gap by assigning blame anyway, and a moment where it tries to close it by shifting to forward-looking accountability, and let them see what each move costs.

Structure. Give a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, the materials to hand out, the rules of play, and one hidden feature of the setup that participants are not told about in advance and that the debrief later exposes. No real money should be required, and no technical expertise beyond what a philosophy student has should be assumed. Do not lecture at the start, and do not name the concept until the debrief.

Debrief. Map specific moments in the activity onto the distinctions above: where the epistemic gap was, where the control gap opened, where the group reached for blame, and where it switched to accountability. Be explicit about which participant's disclaimer was strongest and why.

Failure modes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, and how the facilitator should handle each case. At a minimum, address the tendencies to personify the system, to reach immediately for the largest organization in the room, and to treat the gap as a puzzle about who to blame rather than a problem about what responsibility can mean here.

Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. Say which feature of the responsibility gap the facilitator must supply in discussion because the rules cannot generate it.
