I am a third-year PhD student in political theory, and in a fortnight I have to lead a seventy-five-minute session for my department's interdisciplinary reading group. The audience is mostly historians, with a couple of lawyers, an economist and two sociologists; none of them works on liberalism, and all of them are quietly convinced they already know what public reason is. The nominal topic is religious arguments in politics, and my fear is that we will spend the entire session on the version of it everyone has met a hundred times: the claim that religious citizens should keep their faith out of the public square, followed by the indignant reply that this is illiberal, followed by mutual incomprehension. I want to get them somewhere considerably more interesting than that, and what I need from you is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself.

So please plan the explanatory arc. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, and in what order, given what depends on what — I take it that the fact of reasonable pluralism and some account of why coercive law needs a justification its subjects could accept must come before anything about reasons and their admissibility, but tell me whether that is right and what else the sequence requires. I can already predict the misconceptions, and I would like them mapped to the sequence rather than listed: that public reason simply is secularism; that it forbids religious citizens from speaking religiously in public at all; that it is a legal restriction rather than a moral duty of civility; that it governs every political utterance rather than only the justification of constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice; and, subtler, that it is a rule about what people may believe or be motivated by rather than about what can serve as a justification for coercion. Tell me the order in which these will actually surface, where in the arc each should be headed off, and which one is the hinge — I suspect the last, and that the distinction between the grounds of a law and the motives of those who vote for it is what the whole session turns on, but I would like to be told if I am wrong about that.

Second, the carrying example. Which single case should bear the weight? I am tempted by a parliamentary debate over assisted dying, or alternatively over extending marriage to same-sex couples, in which one member's argument is frankly religious and another's is frankly public; tell me which to use, what the case must contain and must not contain for the hinge of the session to turn on it, and why the other candidate is the wrong vehicle. I want them to leave with the distinction between the wide and the exclusive view of public political culture, and with the proviso that a religious argument may be introduced provided that public reasons are supplied in due course; and I want them to see that this proviso can look like a generous concession while being either empty or extremely demanding, depending on how the follow-up is understood. The colleague who convened the group is a Catholic legal philosopher who will certainly object that the whole framework unfairly burdens believers, and I would like the sequence to have absorbed that objection before he makes it. Do not send me readings — I know them and this group will not do them. Finish with the check for understanding that would tell me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been parroted back, and with what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so. Please write your answer in British English.
