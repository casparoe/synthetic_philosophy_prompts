I teach a 90-minute session of a third-year metaphysics seminar (14 students) on laws of nature, and I want them to run into the identification problem themselves before they hear anyone's proposed solution. Design a runnable activity, not a lecture, and no reading list.

Build it around the following material, which I will supply to the groups. Each group receives a dossier for a small fictional universe: a complete list of everything that happens in it, together with a deck of candidate regularities. The group must write a law-book of at most five axioms, scored by a metric I stipulate — coverage counts only regularities that rule out at least one observation the dossier does not contain, and every axiom costs one point. I will then hand out a second dossier whose observation list is identical but whose underlying story differs: in one, the striking regularity is sustained by something that would break under intervention; in the other, it holds by coincidence. The groups produce a law-book for each. In a final round they may buy a primitive law-making predicate at a cost, and must justify which regularities receive it without simply restating their intuitions.

Give me the rules and materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the staging of the reveal, and a debrief that maps what happens onto the choice between treating laws as the best summary of what happens and treating them as something over and above the regularities — including the views that posit a second-order relation between universals and those that derive laws from the essences of properties. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. Close with a note on which consideration would lead you to abandon this design for a different one, and where the design is most likely to fail.
