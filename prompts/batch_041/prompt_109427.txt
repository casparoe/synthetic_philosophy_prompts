Ok, so I'm TA'ing a grad seminar that mixes CS and philosophy students (topic is roughly "measurement, optimization, and alignment") and I have to write the take-home final. I want one essay question on Goodhart's law / specification gaming that a student can't pass by reciting "when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure" plus the usual four-flavor taxonomy they all memorized off a blog post. So I want the question to force them to actually run a toy setup I've been using in class, and to say what's really doing the work when a proxy fails. Here's the setup, please build the question around it:

Let there be a space of "situations" s. There's a true objective U(s) — think long-run welfare, or "what we actually cared about". There's a cheap observable feature vector phi(s). Somebody fits a proxy P(s) = E[U | phi(s)] by regression on a baseline distribution D0. So by construction P is a *calibrated* estimate of U: on D0, whenever P says x, U is on average x. Then we hand the agent the task "find the s that maximizes P" and let it search.

I want four parts in the question:

(a) Get them to show that an optimizer maximizing P can drive U down even though P is perfectly calibrated on D0, and then say precisely what was wrong with the phrase "P is calibrated". The point I want them to reach is that calibration is a property of a joint distribution, not of a function, so it gives zero guarantee once the thing being measured is being selected for. Make them construct their own two-state or two-feature example rather than quoting one.

(b) Then the obvious rescue: "fine, so after the agent shifts the distribution, retrain P on the new distribution and iterate until it's stable." I want a case where that makes things worse, and an account of why. (I've been calling this the reflexive-metric problem in class — the metric is partly an equilibrium object produced by everyone's behavior including the agent's, so retraining can chase the agent instead of correcting it.) They should be able to say when retraining helps and when it's a treadmill.

(c) Now make them separate specification gaming from Goodhart, using the setup. Give them the task of producing (i) a modification where the failure is a genuine specification error — the proxy doesn't track U even at baseline, someone just wrote the wrong thing down — and (ii) a modification where the specification is as good as it can be and the failure is still Goodhartian. Then say what that pair shows about "just write a better spec".

(d) Finally: what would it take for P to be optimization-robust? Is that achievable in general, or is there a tradeoff between a proxy being informative and being invariant under optimization? I want them to state the tradeoff in their own words and commit to a view.

Please give me: the exam question (as the students would see it, so it has to be self-contained and not assume they read the class notes), a model answer of around 700-800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. For the marking scheme I want the must-hit moves that seperate an A from a B named explicitly, the marks each part is worth, and a one-liner for each part on how students typically fluff it. Don't make any part of it depend on knowing who said what or citing anything — I want it answerable from the setup plus thinking.
