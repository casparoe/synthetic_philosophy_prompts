I have been trying to write down cleanly an argument I keep half-making in conversation, and I have reached the point where either the argument is sound or I do not understand my own field. It concerns the alignment problem, and I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

(1) To align a system is to make its behavior answer to human values rather than to a proxy for them.

(2) Human values are not handed to us as a finished specification. They are latent in our choices, judgments, and reactions, and they are context-sensitive: what we would endorse depends on features of a situation that no finite description can anticipate.

(3) Therefore, for any finite specification S that we could hand a system, there is some situation in which behavior conforming to S diverges from what we would endorse. (This is the ordinary point behind talk of specification gaming, reward hacking, and Goodharting.)

(4) So no specification S achieves alignment.

(5) Alignment must therefore consist in the system's inferring our values from our behavior, rather than being told them.

(6) Any such inference proceeds from a prior or inductive bias. Among the many hypotheses about our values that are consistent with the behavior we exhibit, the system selects those that its prior favors.

(7) Therefore the values the system ends up with are a joint product of our behavior and its prior; wherever our behavior underdetermines the answer, the prior decides.

(8) But the prior is not itself answerable to us. It is a feature of the system we built, not a fact about us.

(9) So there is no fact of the matter about which of two systems is "aligned to us." Each is aligned to the projection of our behavior through its own prior. Alignment, as something we could deliberately aim at, does not exist.

I can see that (9) is too strong. Some systems plainly behave better than others by our lights, and "we cannot guarantee alignment" is not "alignment is incoherent." And the argument proves too much elsewhere: run on parents and children, or on a teacher and a student, it seems to show that no one ever transmits their values to anyone, which I take to be false. But I would rather not reject (9) by intuition alone. Where exactly does the argument break? If I insist on keeping (1) and (2) — I am not willing to give up the thought that our values are latent and context-sensitive — which of the later steps has to go? I also have a stake in keeping (3), since I think the Goodharting point is genuine and not an artifact of bad engineering, so tell me what I pay for keeping it, and whether the argument can be repaired into something true that preserves what is right in (3) without arriving at (9).
