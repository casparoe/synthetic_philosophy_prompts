You are a satirist with a real working knowledge of incentive design, principal–agent problems, and specification gaming in machine learning. Before writing, brainstorm briefly — in your own notes, not in the deliverable — about two things: the four well-known mechanisms by which a proxy metric breaks down under optimization (regression to the mean when you select on extreme proxy values; the proxy ceasing to track the goal once optimization pushes you outside the region where it was validated; the proxy correlating with the goal without being causally upstream of it, so that intervening on the proxy moves nothing; and deliberate gaming by agents whose interests diverge from the principal's), and the characteristic ways a compliance department misreads each one.

Then write a piece of sustained, deadpan satire: an internal "Metric Integrity Incident Reporting FAQ" issued by the fictional Office of Metric Integrity of a large organization. Roughly 700–900 words, formatted as a numbered FAQ with a voice somewhere between HR boilerplate and an auditing standard. It should include at least four incident classes, one for each mechanism above — but give them bland bureaucratic names of your own invention rather than the academic labels, so that a specialist recognizes all four while a general reader just sees a plausible taxonomy — along with a severity rubric, an escalation ladder, and reporting deadlines. The satire must come from content, not wackiness: each incident class should be answered with a remedy that quietly instantiates the very failure it was meant to correct, and the office's own performance measure (say, incidents closed within the reporting period) should end up being gamed by the office itself. End with a clause asserting that the framework is not itself subject to the framework. Never wink at the reader, never name a real living person, and keep the humor fair: exaggerate features that metric-driven management actually has rather than inventing absurdities. A reader who knows this literature should laugh rather than wince, and a reader who does not should still be able to tell what is being mocked.
