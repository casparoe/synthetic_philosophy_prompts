I'm a second-year in a philosophy PhD program and I'm stuck on something from a Moore seminar that I built myself, so I can't even blame the reading. It's an argument that only uses moves Moore would've accepted, and it lands somewhere I really don't want to go. Here it is. (1) Moore's two great intrinsic goods are the enjoyment of beauty and personal affection, i.e. friendship, love between two people. (2) He's also a hard-line impersonalist about value: a state of affairs is good simpliciter, not good-for-this-person-or-that-one, and its value doesn't budge when you swap out who's involved. (3) So a world containing A's and B's mutual affection is exactly as good as a world containing C's and D's, other things equal. (4) His isolation test makes this vivid -- a universe with only that one friendship in it is better than an empty one, and nothing in the test cares whose friendship it is. (5) So if I can either keep my own friendship or bring about an equally rich friendship between two strangers, the two are worth exactly the same; and if dropping mine would free me up to underwrite two stranger-friendships instead, the arithmetic says I should drop it. (6) But that proves way too much. Friendship is supposed to be the paradigm of a partial, particular good -- the whole point is that it's *them*. A theory that treats my friendships as interchangeable tokens of an impersonal good, so that I might even be obligated to liquidate them into a larger stock of other people's friendships, seems to have thrown away the exact thing it was trying to praise, and Moore obviously thought affection was the best thing going.

I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (2), but I want to know what I'm paying and whether there's a version of impersonalism that survives. Also whether the isolation test is a neutral tool or whether it's already smuggling in the impersonalism it's supposed to test.

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion.
