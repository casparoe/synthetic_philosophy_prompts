I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep circling the Buridan's ass case — the idea that an agent facing two perfectly equal options has no reason to choose either and so, on some tellings, starves. I'd like a poem that takes the symmetry seriously instead of treating it as a punchline, and I want the form to do the philosophical work. Specifically: about fourteen lines, arranged as a line-level palindrome, so line 1 matches line 14, line 2 matches line 13, and the two middle lines are identical — the poem should have no privileged starting point, so that reading it in either direction breaks the tie no more than the ass could. Fold in the thing I notice constantly in my own work, which is that real decision procedures never actually stall: ties get broken by something that isn't a reason at all, a clock tick, a random seed, the order the options happened to load. So I want the poem to hold two thoughts at once — that indifference supplies no ground for preferring either side, and that any actual chooser gets moved anyway by a cause that isn't a preference — without collapsing the second into a smug resolution of the first. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the debate hears the difference between having no preference and merely having a tie-breaking procedure, and it should work as verse, not as an argument in costume.
