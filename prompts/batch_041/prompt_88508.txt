I've been attending a monthly reading group that's been working through the "was civilization a mistake" genre — the line of argument that runs from the old anthropological thesis that foragers were the original affluent society, working short weeks and eating better than the first farmers, through to the more recent popularizers who use that thesis to say we traded health and relative equality for hierarchy, surplus, and the state. I've read enough of the rationalist canon to flinch whenever an argument proves too much, and a friend in the group offered one last week that I've been trying and failing to kill. It runs like this:

(1) Whether an episode in human history was a mistake depends on whether it left the people living through it worse off than they would otherwise have been.
(2) On the best evidence we have, the shift from foraging to farming lowered per-capita nutrition, raised the burden of infectious disease and famine, and increased the workload of the average person, while enabling storable surplus and, with it, durable hierarchy and the state.
(3) So the people who lived through the transition were, on average, worse off than the foragers who came before them.
(4) Therefore the agricultural revolution was a mistake.
(5) Nothing in this reasoning is peculiar to farming. Any transition that lowers the average welfare of a population while increasing its numbers is a mistake on exactly the same grounds.
(6) But that description also fits medicine, or the demographic transition, or simply having children under conditions of scarcity — each raises the number of people alive while doing little or nothing for the average welfare of those already here.
(7) So if we accept (5) we are forced to call those mistakes too, which I can't bring myself to do. Something in the argument must be wrong.

My instinct is that the trouble sits somewhere in the middle: that (3) glides past the fact that the farmers and their descendants are not the same people as the foragers we are comparing them to, so that "worse off than they would otherwise have been" quietly presupposes a fixed population when what we actually have is two different populations, one of which exists only because of the very thing we are condemning. But I can't tell whether that is the real fault or whether I am just reaching for the least costly escape hatch. So: which step do you reject — the inference from (2) to (3), the move from (3) to (4), the generalization in (5), or the principle in (1) — and what does each rejection cost me? I am especially interested in whether (5) is doing illegitimate work, that is, whether the "proves too much" objection actually refutes the conclusion about agriculture or only the sweeping generalization, since I suspect it might be possible for farming to have been a genuine mistake for the people who lived it while the universal principle is the thing that has to go. And if I keep (4) while dropping (5), I'd want to know what independent reason I have for treating farming as a special case rather than simply special-pleading.
