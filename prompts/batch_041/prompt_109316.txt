I serve on the science panel of a small foundation that funds methodology work on consciousness, and we are deciding whether to make method a standing theme for the coming cycle: roughly six three-year postdoctoral fellowships, plus a workshop budget, which is enough to commit the field to one direction and not much more. Several of us are drawn to heterophenomenology — the third-person method associated with Dennett, in which the subject's reports are recorded, purged of the analyst's own inferences to yield a cleaned-up text, and then interpreted much as one interprets a work of fiction, so that the analyst ends up with a heterophenomenological world for the subject and explains why the subject says what they say, while officially refusing to grant that the reports describe inner events as they seem to. Our difficulty is that we cannot tell which disagreements about the method are substantive and which are merely about its application, and I would sooner fund the questions that other questions depend on than the ones that are merely current.

What I want from you is a defended agenda: the three or four open questions or lines of work within heterophenomenology that most deserve a few years of a philosopher's or methodologist's attention, ranked, together with the reasoning behind the ranking — what each would unlock, how tractable it is without new empirical data or a settled theory of consciousness, and whether it is upstream of the field's other disputes. Two proposals have been circulating among us, and I would like you to say where each belongs in the ranking and whether it is a genuine open question or a confusion. The first, which we have been calling the textual-subject proposal, is to test whether the method is a general method or an artifact of the human case by applying it to systems whose reports are generated text rather than speech — a large language model answering questions about its own processing, say — and asking what heterophenomenology would license us to infer there, given that it already declines to take any report at face value. The second, the silent-subject proposal, is to extend the method to subjects who cannot report at all, such as infants, animals, and patients with disorders of consciousness, and to ask whether it can be adapted to them or whether it simply presupposes reportability. For each, tell us what would have to be true for it to count as a real research question, and what argument or evidence over the next two or three years should move it up or down the list. We are not asking for a reading list; we want the agenda itself and the case for its ordering.
