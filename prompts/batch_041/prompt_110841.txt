I am trying to determine what I actually believe about honoring sunk costs, and I would like the principle—or the smallest set of principles—that reproduces my verdicts. My verdicts, case by case, are these.

1. You have placed a non-refundable deposit on a week's rental, and a friend then offers you a better week elsewhere. I think you should take the better week and write off the deposit; traveling to the rental in order not to waste the deposit is a mistake.

2. At mile twenty of a marathon your knee is injured, and stopping would cost you nothing but the finisher's medal you wanted. You finish because you have come this far. I think this is defensible, and not only because finishing feels good afterwards: the endurance is part of the achievement, so the miles are not merely spent, they are partly the point.

3. A founder keeps funding a venture that she and everyone else now believe will fail, citing the money and years already committed. I think this is plainly irrational.

4. Wanting to attend a talk you would otherwise skip, you deliberately buy a non-refundable, non-transferable ticket for it, and it works: you go and are glad. I think both the purchase and the going are rational, even though the only thing that moves you is a cost you cannot recover.

5. A family keeps a failing farm running because the family has worked that land for generations. This feels unlike the startup to me, but I cannot tell whether the generations are a reason or merely a sentiment dressed as one.

6. Two hundred pages into a novel you are not enjoying, you finish it because you have read two hundred pages. I think this is irrational, and closer to the startup than to the marathon, even though all three run through the thought that you have come this far.

7. You keep at a proof because the weeks already spent, and the fact that you are still gripped by it, strike you as evidence that the problem is worth solving. I cannot decide whether this is a good reason or a rationalization.

My tentative principle is that a sunk cost is a reason only when it is either evidence about the future or a device that binds your later self to your earlier one, but I suspect it fails on the marathon. Please state the principle precisely, name the case that forces each clause, tell me what it predicts for at least two cases I have not listed, and say plainly which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should give that verdict up. Open with the principle in no more than two sentences, and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
