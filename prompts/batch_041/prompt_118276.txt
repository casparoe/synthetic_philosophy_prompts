I retired from a physics department about six years ago and have been filling my mornings with philosophy since then, mostly philosophy of mathematics and logic, which is close enough to my old trade that I can fool myself into thinking I follow it. Every so often I hit something where I suspect I only think I understand it, and thats where I am now.

I'm stuck on a passage in an essay about non-constructive existence proofs. The essay gets to Zorn's lemma and I copied the statement out like this: take a partially ordered set P; if every chain in P (a subset in which any two elements are comparable) has an upper bound in P, then P has a maximal element, meaning an element with nothing strictly greater than it in P. Then, a bit later, the essay gives what it says is an older formulation of the same principle: every partially ordered set contains a chain that cannot be extended to a larger chain.

The formal statement I can grind through. What stops me is the sentence right after it in the essay, which says something like: here mathematics stops producing objects and starts merely reserving a place for them, since the proof guarantees the object exists without ever telling you which one it is. And then the essay notes, in passing, that this principle is equivalent to the axiom of choice, as if that settled something.

Three things I'd like help with. First, what is the chain hypothesis actually doing? Why is the condition about chains rather than about all subsets or all pairs of elements, and what goes wrong if you weaken or strengthen it? Second, why is a maximal element not a maximum, and where does that gap really earn its keep? I've seen the lemma used to prove that every vector space has a basis and that every ring with identity has a maximal ideal, and I want to see the exact step where maximal-rather-than-maximum is load-bearing rather than a technicality. Third, is the essay's flourish anything more than a flourish? Does the lemma create the object, or only report that the universe of sets is already fat enough that one must be sitting there, and what would it even mean for the universe to be fat enough in advance? While I'm at it, I'd like to know how the chain-extension formulation and the upper-bound formulation are related, and whether one of them is the real content and the other a convenience.

One request: if my framing is off — if I've mangled the lemma's logical shape, or if one of these questions is really a confusion of mine dressed up as a question, or if the thing I'm asking for doesn't exist — please say so straight rather than working around it. I'd rather be told I've misunderstood something basic than get three smooth paragraphs that quietly assume I haven't. And I dont want a reading list; I have too many books already.
