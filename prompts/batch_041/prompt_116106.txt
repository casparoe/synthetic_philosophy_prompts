I'm giving a paper at a small workshop next month - it's nominally on the epistemology of testimony, but they always have one poor soul doing scepticism, and this year it's me. I've got a draft that I think has a decent idea in it, but I've been staring at it so long that I can't tell anymore whether the central distinction is doing any work or whether I've just relabelled something everyone already knew. Before I send it round I'd rather have it knocked about a bit. So: comments, please, as brutal as you like. Here's the draft, roughly as it stands.

Moore's proof is familiar enough that I won't dress it up. He holds up a hand and asserts that there's one hand, then the other, and concludes that at least two external things exist, and so that there is an external world. He thought this was a proof in the fullest sense: the premises differ from the conclusion, he knows the premises, and the conclusion follows. The standard reply is that it begs the question, since the sceptic's hypothesis - dreaming, a deceiving demon, a vat - is meant to be compatible with all your evidence, including whatever evidence you have that you have hands. Moore's counter is that the sceptic's own premise, that not being able to rule out dreaming means not knowing you have hands, isn't more certain than the claim that he does have hands. So it comes out as a comparison of certainties.

What I want to argue is that this standoff is misdescribed, and that the interesting thing about Moore's proof is a structural feature I've been calling collateralized certainty. The thought is that the certainty attaching to the judgement that here is a hand isn't a self-standing property of that judgement. It's collateralized by the general reliability of perceptual judgement - which is precisely what the conclusion is supposed to establish. Moore treats the certainty of the premise as an asset he already owns and can spend; I want to say it's on loan, and the lender is the conclusion. The proof isn't circular in the narrow sense, since he doesn't assume the conclusion, but the epistemic wherewithal that makes the premise knowable is downstream of the conclusion, so nothing gets carried through.

I do two things with this. First, I use it to sort the sceptical arguments. Take the dream argument, the demon, the vat, and then a case I invent for contrast: someone says I can't know I have ancestors, because for all I know I was assembled yesterday with fake memories. That one has no traction, and I claim the reason is that it doesn't collateralize - the certainty of the judgement that these particular people raised me doesn't lean on the general reliability of memory in the way the hand judgement leans on perception in general. So collateralization is supposed to explain a difference in intuitive force that the bare charge of begging the question doesn't. Second, I try to turn the tables: if foundational certainty is always collateralized, then the sceptic's demand - know the premises without leaning on the conclusion - can't be met, but needn't be, because no cognition could meet it, including the sceptic's. Moore wins not by having the better proof but by declining the game. I end by suggesting the proof is really a reminder: it puts the certainty back where it already was. That last move is the bit I trust least - it feels like a cop-out wearing a suit.

What I want from you. Is collateralized certainty a genuine structural feature of the proof, or is it the old charge of epistemic circularity with a new name? If it's the old charge, tell me which version of it, because I count at least three going around and I don't think they all apply. Does the ancestors case do the work I need, or does it collapse into just not being scary? If the distinction is real, does it apply to the vat in the way I say, or does the vat collateralize differently - I have a nagging feeling it does and I've been avoiding the thought. Tell me where I've got Moore wrong, because I'm working from memory of the lecture and I'd rather be corrected now than in front of an audience. And if your answer to any of this is that it's just Wittgenstein on hinges, please say exactly which part and where mine parts company, because I've had that said to me twice now and nobody would spell it out.
