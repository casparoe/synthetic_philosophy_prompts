My two collegues in the reading group have been fighting about the prisoner's dilemma for close to two years now and I've told them we're not leaving the room until they produce a joint statement. So: draft it.

Here's the split. Camp 1: rational choice attaches to acts. In a one-shot PD with no repetition, no reputation, no communication and no way for my choice to reach yours, defecting is the rational act, full stop; when people cooperate in these setups it's non-standard preferences, or confusion, or a psychology calibrated for repeated life misfiring in a lab. Camp 2: the unit is the decision, not the act. If each of us asks 'what should we do?' rather than 'what should I do?', cooperating is what comes out, and the fact that you're running the same reasoning I am is itself a reason, not a sentimental add-on.

Organise the statement around this, and if it's a dud say so instead of humouring me: I think 'one-shot' is doing three jobs and the two of them have been trading on the ambiguity. (i) no shadow of the future -- this encounter doesn't affect later payoffs; (ii) no causal channel -- nothing I do can causally affect what you do; (iii) no shared reasoning -- we aren't agents whose choices are correlated by having run the same deliberation. Camp 2 needs (iii) to be false. Camp 1 can grant it and deny it matters. My hunch is a lot of the fight is (ii) and (iii) getting run together, but test that rather than assuming it.

Two cases I want both of them to take a position on, because I think they pull in opposite directions:

The twins: one shot, anonymous, no communication, standard matrix, but each player knows the other was picked by the same process and is working the same problem with the same reasoning, and each knows the other knows this. Nothing either does can causally reach the other. What's the rational move, and why?

The shared cause: same one-shot anonymous game, but both players were exposed to a hidden common influence beforehand -- the same primes, or assignment by a coin neither of them can see -- so their choices end up correlated without either causing the other. Cooperating here is a decent predictor that the other cooperates. Does that change whats rational? Either they agree on an answer or they say exactly why they can't.

And the iterated side, because this can't be settled on the one-shot alone: in the repeated game, with observable actions and enough patience or a low enough stopping probability, cooperation is sustainable, and both of them know it. Does that mechanism -- my cooperating now makes your cooperating later more likely -- belong to Camp 1's toolkit, a plain causal link making cooperation instrumentally rational, or is it the same thing Camp 2 is pointing at when it talks about correlated reasoning? If they think it's the same mechanism, that's an agreed claim and I want it stated as one. Same for the evolutionary side: a well-mixed population where higher-scoring strategies spread tends toward all-defect, and cooperation hangs on only when something adds structure, assortment, repetition or partner choice. Both of them should say what that fact is evidence for.

For every crux, give me something both of them accept as bearing on it -- an experiment they'd both run, an argument they'd both count, a formal result they'd both recognise -- with both predictions written down in advance. In particular: is there a design that separates 'team reasoning' from 'social preferences' from 'the psychology misfiring', and if there isn't, say so. And each of them should say what would count as evidence against their own side, plus what would actually move them.

No citations, no reading list, no literature survey -- they don't agree on which ones count anyway. What I want is a document they'd both sign. Numbered agreed claims first. Then a disagreements table with these columns: Disagreement | Camp 1's own wording | Camp 2's own wording | Substantive, verbal, or empirical | What actually turns on it. Then the cruxes with their tests and predictions. If part of the dispute is verbal -- about the word 'rational', or about whether 'one-shot' should be read as (ii) or (iii) -- say which part and why, instead of papering over it.

Then, for the dataset, a compact machine-readable index after the document: a JSON object with agreed_claims (array of one-sentence strings), disagreements (array of objects with topic, camp_1, camp_2, kind, stake), cruxes (array of objects with crux, camp_1_prediction, camp_2_prediction, shared_test), verbal_disputes (array of strings), open_questions (array of strings). No prose outside the JSON in that block.
