I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy — my exposure is a few popular science books and long arguments with friends — and I keep running into a puzzle at work that I suspect has an established name, though every search I try lands me in either the free will debate or the mind-body debate, neither of which is quite the thing. Here is the pattern. When we debug an incident, we explain it at the level of the program: a cache held a stale entry, so the request handler priced the order off last week's data, so the invoice went out wrong. That is the explanation we act on; we change the invalidation logic and the bad invoices stop. But the same episode also has a story told in electrons, and that story is, as far as I can tell, complete: given the initial conditions and the laws, the electrons were going to do what they did regardless of whether anything was, in the program's vocabulary, stale. So the program story appears to make no additional causal difference. My colleagues split into two camps. Some say the program story is the real cause and the electron story is just bookkeeping; others say the electron story is the real cause and the program story is a useful fiction we tell to keep our jobs. I do not see why I have to pick, and I suspect the dilemma is an artifact of something.

The wrinkle that stops me from settling for a stock answer is this. The two stories do not always agree about what caused what — they are not simply a coarse and a fine picture of the same thing. In a race condition, the program-level explanation is that two handlers wrote to the same record and the second overwrote the first, and that is the explanation that fixes the bug; a physical-level explanation would instead cite a particular interleaving of clock edges. Neither story is a redescription of the other, because the program-level story is compatible with a huge range of physical stories, and, worse, the same physical system will run any of infinitely many different programs depending on what is written on the disk. I also notice that we never intervene at the physical level: we set a breakpoint, flip a flag, deploy a patch, and the effect propagates reliably. The level we can push around is the symbolic one, and it pushes back reliably, which is a strange thing to say about a fiction.

What I am after is not a label but a carving, and I would rather you reason carefully and show your work than reach for the first impressive term. Please answer in the voice of a philosopher who works on causation and on the special sciences, and begin by brainstorming a list of candidate concepts — anything from the metaphysics of causation, the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of the special sciences, or the philosophy of explanation — before you commit to anything. Then do two things. First, tell me which established concept is nearest to what I am describing and be specific about where it comes apart from my case: my pattern is not exactly the familiar worry that the higher-level story is doing no work at all, since the two levels sometimes disagree about causes rather than merely coarsening one another, and it is not exactly the ordinary distinction between a cause and a background condition, since that distinction is about selecting one factor within a single story rather than about two stories that return different verdicts. Second, if nothing fits, coin a term, define it precisely, and mark its edges: say what would have to be true for the pattern to disappear (for instance, does it depend on the higher level being producible by many different lower-level arrangements, or on the two levels being related by design rather than by emergence?), tell me which of the standard positions in this area are live options, and give me one or two boundary cases where it is genuinely unclear whether my pattern is present. I would also like to know whether this is one puzzle or two — whether the stale-cache case and the race-condition case are the same phenomenon in different clothing.
