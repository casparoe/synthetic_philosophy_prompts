My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether artifacts can have politics, and we ended by trading examples past each other rather than settling anything; I would like to settle it by construction. Her position is that a technology is a tool and nothing more, so whatever politics shows up belongs to the uses people make of it, not to the thing; mine is that the arrangement can be built into the artifact and then hold itself up without anyone intending it. Build me a thought experiment that decides between us.

The case should come in two parts, both set in ordinary workplaces — no speculative machines, no smart cities, no future tech, nothing science-fictional. In the first part, hold the artifact fixed and vary the social arrangement: one machine, the same model in the same condition doing the same work, under two different regimes of who does what and when. The distribution of authority over the pace of the work and over who bears its risk must come out different under the two regimes, and must come out different because of the arrangement itself, not because any person intends that result. In the second part, hold the arrangement fixed and vary the artifact: the same rules, the same staffing, the same task, but two versions of the machine differing in a single material feature — a guard, an interlock, the placement of a control, whatever you choose — such that the same distribution of authority now comes out different. In this second part the difference must not be traceable to a designer's intention, to a legal or regulatory requirement, or to a difference in the task the machine performs; it should be the sort of difference that could have arisen by accident, drift, or an unrelated engineering convenience. Keep each part to a paragraph, and be explicit about what is held fixed in each, since the whole point is the isolation of one variable at a time. Not the well-worn example of the low overpass built to keep buses out; I want a case that isn't already in every syllabus.

After the two parts, do the analysis. State what each of three positions implies about each part and where each is embarrassed: (a) hard technological determinism, on which the technology drives the social arrangement; (b) the neutrality thesis, on which technologies are politically inert and only their uses have politics; (c) the thesis that the artifact itself carries a politics apart from both use and intent. Then tell me which single feature distinguishes the two parts and is doing the real work — I suspect it is something about whether the constraint needs a person to keep enforcing it or keeps enforcing itself, but I want your judgment, not mine. Say what happens to the case if that feature is removed: for instance, if the material feature in the second part turns out to require a supervisor to sustain it, does the case collapse back into the first part? Finally, give the strongest reply available to a defender of whichever position fares worst, and tell me whether any of the three survives in weakened form — in particular, whether the case shows that a distribution of power can be self-sustaining without anyone intending it, which is the claim I was trying to make last night and could not put properly.
