I am a retired experimental biologist who has spent the past three years attending a philosophy department's reading group. In a recent session on comparative cognition, a colleague closed a long exchange about whether a new information-theoretic measure—one that scores an organism by how efficiently it compresses the contingencies of its ecological niche—actually tracks intelligence with the observation that intelligence is whatever intelligence tests measure. I have since heard the same line used to dismiss questions about machine benchmarks, about whether cephalopods and corvids are intelligent in the same sense, and about whether general intelligence names a natural kind. I would like a disambiguation rather than a defense or a refutation. Please set out the distinct precise claims that the line could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, state what it commits one to, whether it is plausible, and whether it would actually license the dismissal in the setting where I heard it. I am especially interested in readings on which the line is not a definition at all but a prediction, a decision procedure, or a claim about the social function of testing; and in whether any reading can survive the observation that we sometimes revise a test because we have decided it was measuring the wrong thing. Please give me a menu I can bring back to the group, with a verdict on each item and a note on which reading my colleague most likely intended.
