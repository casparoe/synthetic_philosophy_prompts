I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing has a courtroom at its center: two artificial systems argue a single question before one human arbiter, who must rule on which of them is telling the truth. To keep the scene honest, I have been reading the technical literature on "AI safety via debate," and what strikes me is how little the argument has moved in years. The proposal, as I understand it, is this. Rather than trust one system's answer, you have two systems answer adversarially, and a weaker judge — a human, or a smaller model, working under a fixed budget of time and attention — decides which answer is better. The hope is that a false answer can always be exposed by the opposing side, and that checking an answer is easier than producing one, so that truth wins even when the judge could never find it alone. The objection I keep meeting is the obfuscated-arguments problem: a dishonest system can give an answer that is wrong in a way the judge cannot detect, and the honest system, though it is right, may be unable to make the flaw visible within the judge's budget. The dishonest answer and the honest one can look equally rigorous; the difference lies at a step the judge cannot check. Picture a system claiming that a particular training procedure cannot be gamed by some rare input; the honest side holds the counterexample, but the counterexample is an execution trace of ten thousand steps whose defect sits at step six thousand two hundred and thirty-one, and the judge has four minutes.

What confuses me is that the two camps seem to agree about the mechanism and disagree about what the mechanism is meant to prove. One side says the obfuscated-arguments problem shows the method fails; the other answers that if debate cannot do the job, then nothing else can either, because every oversight scheme still ends in a human judgment of something the human cannot fully verify, so the failure is not debate's but oversight's as such. Meanwhile I keep hearing a third line, that the honest debater need not reproduce the whole proof — it need only point to one checkable link — and a fourth, that the judge could be trained on debate transcripts until it is no longer the weak party in the exchange. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not really about whether debate works but about two different questions wearing one name: whether debate is a sufficient method, and whether it is the best method available. I would like that hypothesis tested rather than assumed.

Do the sides differ about facts — specifically, whether a winning honest strategy actually exists, which sounds like a formal question about the power of the judge — or about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading, and if so, which questions? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? Ground each claim in what the sides actually say, and treat my hypothesis about the two questions under one name as something to be confirmed or refuted, not accommodated.
