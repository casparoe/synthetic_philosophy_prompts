Ok, I need a dialogue for my decision theory reading group, and I'd rather you write it than me.

Take the absent-minded driver: you're driving home down a road with two intersections that look exactly alike, and when you reach one you can't tell which of the two it is. Turn off at the first and you get nothing, turn off at the second and you collect, sail past both and you crash. Two people in the group know the case inside out and neither is fuzzy on the arithmetic, but they've ended up in completly different places, and I want them going at each other.

The first says the driver should settle on a policy before she sets off -- flip a coin at every intersection she reaches, say -- and that this is the answer even though it guarantees she'll sometimes bail out at the wrong one. Her thought is that the only thing the driver ever really chooses is a rule for what to do at an intersection, and that a theory telling her to look at the intersection she's standing at is ignoring the fact that she has no clue which one it is.

The second thinks that's a cheat. As she sees it, the driver is always at one of the intersections, and the question that matters is what to do now, given what she knows now; you can't answer it by asking what she'd have picked before getting in the car, since that perspective isn't available to her at the node and the first one needs it to be. She'll also press that the case may not be a well-formed decision problem at all: what the driver should think about where she is seems to depend on what she's going to do, and a decision problem isn't supposed to be circular like that. Her verdict comes out differently, and I want them fighting about why rather than about the number.

Then have them try one more case: the driver's memory loss is partial, so with probability q she does remember wether she's already been through the first intersection. Does the first one's answer survive as q slides from 0 up to 1? Does the second's? If that needs pinning down before it can be answered, have one of them say so and say how they'd pin it down.

No strawmen -- both are smart and both get real replies. Finish with two or three sentences of your own: where is the dialogue most likely to be unfair or to get the philosophy wrong, and what would have to be true -- which argument or case -- to make you think the other one has the better of it?
