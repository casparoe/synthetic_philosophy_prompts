The setup first, so the numbers are concrete: a four-move centipede, pot starts at $8, each pass adds $2 and hands the pot to the other player, whoever takes gets two thirds of the pot with the other getting the rest, and if the fourth player passes as well the pot is split down the middle. So if we both keep passing we walk away with $8 each, but if I take on move one I get about $5.30. Now his argument. (1) A rational player at any point does the best she can given what comes after her move. (2) At the fourth move, taking pays her two thirds of $14, about $9.30, while passing pays half of $16, $8, so she takes. (3) At the third move I can see that passing leaves me with a third of $14, about $4.70, since she'll take, while taking hands me two thirds of $12, $8, so I take. (4) The same comparison repeats at every earlier move, and nothing in it depends on how many moves there are, so I take on move one. (5) And nothing in it depends on the game either, since any interaction with a known and near end has the same shape, so a partner in a firm with a fixed dissolution date should start stripping it on day one, and the last soldier sent up a hill in a war with a scheduled end has no particular reason to be the one who goes. (6) But a theory of rationality on which trust, gratitude and keeping your word at a known cost are never rational, and on which the first move in every finite cooperation is to defect, isn't a theory of rationality, its a theory of why nobody would ever have a friend. (7) So one of (1)-(4) is wrong.

I'd rather keep (1) than keep (5) or (6) if I have to choose, but I want to know what I'm paying either way, and my hunch is the slip is somewhere around (3)-(4) though I can't say where.

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up: which step goes, why that step looked fine to me, what it costs me to drop it, what the best repair looks like, and what his argument still gets right.
