I am a retired natural scientist — I spent nearly forty years on evolutionary ecology and animal behaviour — and since retiring I have been trying, late and haltingly, to get straight on a philosophical tangle that nagged at me through my entire working life. I would like you to draft a document, and because I have watched enough well-intentioned disputes in my own field, I will say at the outset what the document is and for whom, since all its value lies in the drafting.

The dispute concerns reasons without reasoners, or what are often called free-floating rationales. On one side — call them the realists — is the position that functionally organised systems genuinely stand in means-end relations: that organisms, their organs and behaviours, and by extension any system shaped by selection or by deliberate design, have ends that are objective features of the system, and that descriptions citing those ends can be literally true even though no one, anywhere, entertains them. On this view the rationale floats free of any reasoner without ceasing to be real, and it is this that is thought to license a naturalised teleology, a naturalised notion of function, and perhaps even a naturalised normativity. On the other side — call them the reasoner-required camp — is the position that a rationale is by its nature something that could be a reason for a subject, a consideration that could figure in reasoning or justify a choice; strip away every subject and there remain only causes, dispositions and statistical regularities, so that the means-end vocabulary is either a compressible shorthand for those facts or a stance we adopt for prediction, neither of which warrants realism about reasons. Normativity, on this view, does not survive the removal of the norm-user: to say that a malformed heart ought to pump, or that an insect has been deceived, is true only relative to a purpose that we or selection have supplied.

I would like the joint statement that these two camps could put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please give me, first, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the phenomena to be explained, about which descriptions are indispensable in practice, about what is not in dispute, and about what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own position. Then a disagreements table, each row phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording of its own view and the other would recognise it as a fair statement of what it rejects. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux, state the case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Where you judge that part of the quarrel is really about words, say which part and identify the ambiguity.

There are two things I would particularly like the collaboration to put to the test, because they are the points at which my own intuitions flip.

The first is what a rationale rests on: a history of selection, or merely the kind of goal-directed organisation that a competent engineer can build from nothing. Consider a purely engineered goal-directed device with no evolutionary history whatever — a servomechanism, say, or a policy learned by trial and error rather than by descent. Does it possess a free-floating rationale? If it does, the rationale has come loose from selection altogether; if it does not, then organisation alone never suffices and something historical is doing the work. I would like both camps to state their position here and to say what they would expect.

The second is misrepresentation without a mind. Take an evolved response that tracks a cue which no longer serves the end it once served — an organism drawn to its death by a signal that formerly meant a mate or a meal. Is that a false representation, or only a mismatch between a response and the world, with no content to be false? Here, I suspect, the two camps will diverge sharply, and I would like the divergence stated so exactly that neither side could complain of caricature.

You may draw on any examples from biology or engineering you find apt, provided each is set out fully enough to be assessed without appeal to anything outside the document. The document should read as something the two camps might genuinely have drafted together — not a verdict on which of them is right — and it should be addressed to a reader like me, a scientist who wants to know precisely where the disagreement lives.
