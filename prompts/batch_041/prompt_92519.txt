I'm a population geneticist — quantitative genetics, mainly, so I'm fluent in heritability, breeding values, selection gradients, transmission kernels, drift, and the branching-process treatment of genealogies, and I'm used to asking what the distribution of a trait looks like over generations given some rule of transmission. I want the current debate about inheritance taxation, dynastic wealth and equality of opportunity restated in that vocabulary as faithfully as it can be, and then I want the residue report: what the translation carries, what it distorts, and what it cannot say at all.

Take the main positions one at a time. There's the egalitarian case for a heavy tax on bequests and dynastic transfers, which rests on the thought that a person's life chances should not be fixed by the family they were born into; there's the entitlement case that what one has justly acquired one may dispose of as one chooses, and that taxing a bequest at death is not different in kind from taxing the earner; there's the family case, that parents advancing their children's prospects is continuous with ordinary parenting, so that a polity which suppresses it suppresses something valuable; and there's the more empirical claim that dynastic wealth is self-perpetuating, so that without a corrective the same families sit at the top of the distribution generation after generation.

For each, do the translation as precisely as you can. If the egalitarian case becomes a claim about the transmission kernel and its stationary distribution, say what the kernel has to look like and what quantity is being equalised; if equality of opportunity becomes something like the absence of a correlation between parent and offspring position, tell me whether that is a well-defined target in my formalism or whether it silently presupposes a social welfare function that genetics does not contain. Where the debate turns on a distinction between advantage that is earned and advantage that is merely received, say plainly whether my vocabulary can represent that distinction at all, or whether it has only the single notion of transmission fidelity — and whether fitness can be made to do any of the work, or whether that is a category error.

I'm particularly interested in two things. First, whether the branching-process picture of ancestry bears on the question: ancestry is a graph rather than a tree and it converges in a way that makes the dynasty framing harder to state than it looks, so if the philosophical claim is about lineages rather than individuals, tell me what happens to it. Second, whether multiplicative growth changes the answer. I'm aware that in a multiplicative process the typical trajectory grows at the logarithm of the ensemble average, so a tax that reduces the variance of transmitted increments can raise a typical dynasty's growth rate even while it lowers the mean, and I want to know whether the ethical argument looks different once that is taken seriously or whether it is simply irrelevant to the question being asked.

Finish with the residue. Which parts of the philosophical debate survive translation into my vocabulary intact, which survive only as a distorted image, and which have no counterpart at all — and for that last category, say whether the gap is a defect of my framework, in the sense that it is missing something a theory of inheritance ought to have, or a sign that the original claim was doing rhetorical work a precise formulation would not support. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or tables — the way you would put it in a long letter to a colleague.
