Two people in my Thursday reading group have been going at each other for like three weeks about Moore's paradox, and I got asked to referee. Honestly I can't tell who's winning. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Setup: someone brought up the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining," and everyone agreed it's weird. Then Dana and Marcus started fighting about why.

Round 1 — Dana: The weirdness is entirely pragmatic. The proposition (it's raining, and I don't have a belief that it's raining) is perfectly consistent. People fail to believe true things all the time. So the sentence can be true, and it can even be believed. What you can't do is assert it, because asserting the first half expresses your belief that it's raining and the second half denies that same belief. It's a speech-act trainwreck, not a logical one.

Round 2 — Marcus: That can't be the whole story. Look at embedding. "It's raining and Marcus doesn't believe it" is fine. "If it's raining and I don't believe it, I'll get soaked" is fine. "It was raining and I didn't believe it" is fine. So it's not the content, and it's not the first person as such — it's specifically the present-tense first person. And it isn't just about talking to other people: write "It's raining but I don't believe it" in your own private diary, where you're not asserting anything to anybody, and it still reads as broken. So the absurdity has to live in the relation between me and my own present beliefs, not in what I'm doing with my mouth. Maybe belief is transparent to the believer — you can't get a gap between "p" and "I believe p" from the inside.

Round 3 — Dana: I'll give you the diary point, but the transparency stuff sounds like mystery-mongering. Here's a case that cuts against you: "It's raining, but I'm not sure it's raining" is not absurd at all — it's just hedging, people say it constantly. Same first person, same present tense, same relation to my own mind. So whatever norm assertion answers to, it requires belief but it does not require certainty. That's a fact about the norm, and you can state it with no transparency talk at all.

Round 4 — Marcus: Fine, then try the belief-on-belief version. "It's raining, but I don't believe that I believe it's raining." That one has no punch for me — it sounds more like someone reporting an odd bit of self-ignorance than like a contradiction. Whereas "It's raining, but I believe it isn't raining" sounds worse than the original, even though that's the one where the two halves actually do contradict each other. So the degree of absurdity isn't tracking logical contradiction at all, which is your whole criterion, and I think that's a problem for you.

Round 5 — Dana: Or it's a problem with your ear. And notice we also have "It's raining but I don't know it's raining," which sounds exactly as bad as the belief version. If your story is that assertion demands belief, you have to say the knowledge version is absurd for the same reason the belief version is, which seems like a big coincidence. If assertion demands knowledge instead, then the belief version is absurd because belief is implied by knowledge, and now the knowledge version is the basic one. Either way one of us is explaining something the other is just relabeling. Ugh.
