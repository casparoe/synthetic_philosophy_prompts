ok so I've been going round in circles on this for weeks and I want the spec before the theories. You're a philosopher of mind who works on collective and distributed cognition -- and please actually think this through rather than giving me the standard tour. Brainstorm for a bit, then commit to a list.

The question is whether an ant colony can have a mind. You know the setup: the colony (the one people nickname Aunt Hillary) does all the things we'd normally take as marks of mentality -- it forages, it allocates labour, it "decides" to move the nest, it recognises intruders and mounts a response, it can be fooled. But no individual ant has anything like a mind; each is a few hundred thousand neurons running local rules, and there's no boss, no queen handing out orders, no central place where it all comes together. And yet my brain is also, on some descriptions, a colony of cells with no boss. So everybody picks a favourite case, argues from it, and I've stopped learning anything.

Before I read another theory, tell me what a theory in this area actually has to deliver. Give me the desiderata list. For each one: the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and whether you think its non-negotiable or just a house preference of one camp (the functionalists, the enactivists, whoever you think is in the room). Here are things I'd at least want you to consider putting on it, though you should add and cut freely:

- the cases it has to get right. Human brains, which should come out as minds on any view that lets colonies be minds -- otherwise we've built a theory that excludes the only uncontroversial example. Corporations, churches, states: things made of minds that we cheerfully say believe and intend and decide. Thermostats, bacteria, plants, immune systems, and an ordinary computer program, all of which track and respond to the world but probably shouldn't count. Slime moulds that solve mazes. Split-brain patients, where one skull seems to house two of something.
- a case I made up that I want you to take seriously, because I think it breaks the obvious answers: imagine a species whose colonies periodically fragment in a drought and later re-fuse, and whose ants are freely exchanged between colonies whenever their trails cross -- so a colony is more like a pattern than a membership list. If you ship half the ants to another continent, is that the same mind, a copy, or a death? If two colonies' trails merge, do we get two minds sharing a body, or one mind with a stroke? A theory that has nothing to say here isn't doing work, it's just restating the intuition we started from.
- the distinctions it can't collapse. Between the colony's states being about something and merely co-varying with something. Between the colony having a point of view and the colony merely being usefully describable as if it had one. Between collective mentality and very good coordination. Between having a mind and having one experience rather than a committee of them. Between mind and life, so we don't quietly give up and let anything alive count.
- constraints from the biology it can't contradict: ants are individually dim; coordination is local and stigmergic (ants leave traces in the environment that other ants respond to, with no one reading a global plan); there is no central controller; and colony-level behaviour changes with colony size in ways no individual ant's behaviour does.
- a meta-requirement I care about most. Whatever the theory says, it must not be satisfiable by everything. If your criterion is feedback, or information integration, or goal-directedness, I can point it at a thermostat, a forest, the economy, or a rock in a stable orbit and it lights up. If the theory can't draw the line somewhere, the line isn't doing any work.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most damage -- the one that knocks out the most otherwise attractive theories -- and whether any two of your requirements can't be had together. That last part is what I most want: if the list itself turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, then the list is the result. And if you think "can a colony have a mind" is the wrong question to have asked, fine, say so and tell me what the right one is -- but only after the list.
