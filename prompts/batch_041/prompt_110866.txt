Argue for a definite position on the following question: can al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, as he develops it in the seventeenth discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, give an adequate account of causal selection — the fact that we single out some conditions of an event as its causes while relegating others to the status of background conditions or mere occasions? Take a side and defend it rather than surveying the options. In the course of the argument, address at least the following three points. First, distinguish the epistemic claim that we cannot perceive a necessary connection between a putative cause and its effect from the metaphysical claim that no such connection exists, and say which of these Ghazālī is best read as asserting. Second, consider whether occasionalism can preserve the asymmetry of causation — the fact that contact with fire explains the burning while the burning does not explain the contact with fire. Third, examine the role of the idea that God acts according to habit or custom, and say whether that idea can do the explanatory work required of it without collapsing into a merely descriptive report of observed regularities, on the one hand, or imposing a constraint on God's freedom, on the other. Reason from the ideas themselves rather than from external sources, and flag any point at which your reading of Ghazālī is a contested one.
