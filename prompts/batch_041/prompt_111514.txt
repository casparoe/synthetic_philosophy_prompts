I run a monthly discussion night in the community room of our public library. We are fourteen or so adults — no researchers among us, though there is a retired surveyor, two nurses, a high-school history teacher who mostly does scheduling now, and a man who has read an implausible number of books about lost civilizations — and we meet for a hundred minutes. The group is used to doing things rather than being talked at, which is why I am asking for this rather than pulling an article for us to read. The reason the evening has to be a game is an argument I lost last month to a neighbour who had watched a documentary claiming that several centuries of the written record were fabricated by later chroniclers, and that the fabrication can be proved with arithmetic: tables of reign lengths, counts of surviving manuscripts, lists of names that line up. I did not believe him, but everything I reached for to explain why he was wrong turned out to be either an insult dressed up as a principle — that is the sort of thing only cranks say — or a rule that would also convict historians I respect, who revise the record all the time, who work from odd stray details, who argue that the consensus has been credulous. I do not know whether there is a name for the thing I could not do, but I want the group to try to do it and fail, together, in a way they can feel, before anyone explains it to them.

Here is the shape I have been imagining, and I would like you to take it as a starting point rather than a specification. For the evening we are the editorial board of an imaginary popular-history magazine with one meeting to decide which of three submissions to commission. The first thing we do, before anyone sees a single submission, is write down our standards — six rules at most, sealed in an envelope — because I want us committed to them before we know what we are judging. Then the dossiers come out. Each submission is a one-page pitch plus a short evidence table: what it claims, what it thinks the mainstream gets wrong, and the specific details it leans on. One of them is my neighbour's argument, with the arithmetic and the duplicated centuries; the others should be chosen so that the group's own sealed standards cut against at least one of them in a way that feels unfair once they see it. The twist I am most attached to is this: the same evidence table comes back a second time, identical in every detail, with only the line about who wrote it changed — one version by a professor at a famous university, the other by someone who self-publishes and believes one other strange thing. The tables should rate the evidence against their own standards before and after the byline changes, and I want the gap between the two ratings to be the thing they remember, because I suspect our standards live much closer to our sense of who is talking than any of us would like to admit. There is a second half I would like you to build if it fits in the time: the duplicated-centuries argument depends on a method for matching two lists against each other, so let the tables actually run that method by hand on a pair of chronicle lists, and then run it again on a pair they shuffle themselves — or on the same lists scored with a coding scheme the other table invented after seeing the data — and find that it matches just as well. Nothing in that half should be explained to them; they should simply get the same impressive number out of two lists that have nothing to do with each other.

What I need from you is a plan I can actually run: the materials and how to print or assemble them (I can set out three tables of four or five and would rather not use the projector at all), the exact wording of the submission pitches and the evidence details, the timing across our hundred minutes, what the facilitator says at each transition and, more importantly, what the facilitator must not say, and a debrief that maps each moment of the evening back onto the problem — the sealed standards, the byline swap, the matching method, the re-vote — and states plainly which parts of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself. I would also like an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time: where people will shortcut, where the exercise will make someone feel ambushed or accused, and where the group will slide into the conclusion that everyone has their own paradigm and truth is tribal. That last failure is the one I am most afraid of, since it is the opposite of what I am trying to get across, but I would rather hear it from you in advance than discover it at the end of the evening.
