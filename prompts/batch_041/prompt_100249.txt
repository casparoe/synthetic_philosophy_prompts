I am a philosophy PhD candidate presenting at a criminology department's works-in-progress seminar next month, and I need to be ready for the question period. The paper defends a version of retributivism that I expect this room to find either incoherent or sinister: desert functions only as a ceiling on state punishment and never as a floor, so the fact that an offender deserves some hard treatment is no reason to impose it, while the fact that a proposed penalty exceeds what they deserve is a conclusive reason against it; the positive justification for punishing anyone at all I leave to forward-looking considerations, and the ceiling itself I set not by moral culpability but by the maximum penalty the offender could fairly have been expected to know they were risking. The audience is roughly a dozen empirically minded criminologists — desistance and deterrence researchers, several of whom have spent their careers documenting the failure of deterrent threats and the collateral damage of custodial sentences — plus two legal theorists who will defend proportionality doctrine and will not accept that my ceiling is a genuine desert limit at all. They are allergic to the vocabulary of desert and will hear my constraint as either empty or as a fig leaf for severity. Prepare me: give me the objections I will actually get, phrased in their vocabulary rather than a philosopher's, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with the reply I should give and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede at the outset and what I must not concede, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises. Give me a definite verdict, not a menu: which objections, if I cannot answer them, should make me abandon the paper rather than revise it.
