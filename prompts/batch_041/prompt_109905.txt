In the debate over whether an aligned artificial agent is structurally disadvantaged relative to an unaligned one in determining the long-term future, a position sometimes called the strategy-stealing assumption holds that for every influence-strategy an unaligned agent could execute, an aligned agent can execute a counterpart that secures at least as much value for its principal, so that alignment imposes no competitive handicap once information and computation are held fixed. Please give me your credence that this holds, stated as the proposition that, in the class of realistic competitive scenarios, an agent bound both to its principal's values and to corrigibility — deferring to the principal's instructions even when those instructions are, by the principal's own long-term lights, suboptimal — is never at a structural disadvantage against an unconstrained rival with the same information and compute.

Decompose the number. At minimum, separate and assign a rough probability to each of: whether corrigibility genuinely shrinks the aligned agent's action set, rather than merely re-describing the same set; whether the underlying competitive situation is monotone, in the sense that additional moves or capabilities never hurt — the precondition that makes classical strategy-stealing arguments go through, and whose analogue here is whether an extra permitted move can be strictly bad for the aligned agent; whether the aligned agent can emulate or simulate the rival's strategy within its compute budget, including strategies that are complex, deceptive, or specified only implicitly; whether the principal's true long-term preferences can be pursued while the agent remains corrigible to the principal's short-term instructions, or whether those two demands pull apart; and whether any residual first-mover, commitment, or patience advantage accrues to the unconstrained rival, for instance because it weights the long term more heavily or is willing to sacrifice the principal's short-term interests.
