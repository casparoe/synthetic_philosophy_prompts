I'm running a session on Avicenna's flying man -- the guy conjured in midair with his senses sealed off, who's still supposed to be able to affirm that he exists -- and I want to close with a poem. Write me a villanelle in his voice. One refrain should be the bare certainty that survives having no sight, no sound, no limb: just that he is, with nothing added to it. The other should be the crack running through it, that he can't say *what* he is -- so the very words that are supposed to prove an immaterial soul might only prove that all he's holding is an empty pronoun. Let the refrains trade places the way the form makes them, and let them meet in the closing couplet. Commit in that last couplet: pick the reading you think is right (the argument works, or it only shows how little "I am" buys you) and let the final line land on it instead of hovering. Someone who knows the argument should be able to tell which refrain is the proof and which is the objection, and the poem should stay in loose pentameter.

Start with the poem itself, no preamble. After it, two or three lines on where you took liberties with the argument or leaned on a contested reading of it -- for instance whether your flying man has any memory, language, or sense of time, since the thought experiment is usually left vague there -- and be definite about that choice too: say which way you went and why, dont just lay out the options.
