I am a software engineer — fifteen years on backend systems, the last four on machine-learning infrastructure — with no formal training in philosophy. I have been awarded a six-month, part-time research fellowship that the foundation reserves for people from outside academic philosophy, and the application requires a two-page proposal. I have a half-formed idea and I would like you to write the proposal around it.

The idea. The systems I work on are routinely described as if their internal states were about things: a ranker "prefers" one document to another, a reward model "wants" the agent to do X, a classifier "mistakes" a husky for a wolf. I half-remember an example in which a coin detector in a vending machine is compared with its counterpart in a world where the coin that makes the machine vend is a different coin, and a long-running disagreement over whether such a device's states are about anything in their own right or only borrow their aboutness from the intentions of the people who design and use it. As I recall, one side of that disagreement denies that the line between our aboutness and the device's is as sharp as the other side claims. I would rather the proposal name the positions descriptively than attach them to authors, since I would get the attributions wrong.

Where I think both sides leave a hole. Each seems to treat the borrowing as a single relation with a single lender: the device's aboutness is on loan from a designer or a user, and that is the end of the story. My working life does not look like that. A reward model is trained on preference comparisons that another model generated; the policy is fine-tuned on trajectories that the reward model shaped; the reward model is then retrained on the policy's outputs. The chain of borrowing runs through artifacts that are themselves candidates for aboutness, and sometimes it runs in a circle. So the question I want to ask is this: when a state's aboutness is borrowed rather than original, what exactly is it borrowed from, and must the chain of borrowing terminate — and if it must, must it terminate in someone who meant something (a designer, a user, a community of speakers), or can it terminate in something that is not a meaner at all, such as a selection history, a training distribution, or a standing practice of use?

Turn this into the two-page proposal (roughly a thousand words, single-spaced). It should contain: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being an invitation to survey; an argument, not an assertion, that the existing positions leave it open; the method — what I would actually do with about ten hours a week for six months, month by month, concretely enough that a reviewer can tell I have a plan rather than a mood, and why this way of attacking it rather than the obvious alternatives, including the obvious alternative of simply taking the standard distinction for granted and applying it to my systems; what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and what would count as my discovering that the question is confused rather than open; and an honest scope, given that I have no research budget, no library, no German, and one colleague who thinks the whole thing is nonsense and will tell me so weekly.

Constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I will add references myself. Do not let the proposal depend on claims about what some particular author really meant, and do not presuppose a literature I have already mastered; the proposal may tell me what to read, but must not assume I have read it. Write for a panel of philosophers, while remembering that the applicant is not one. And make the case that the work is worth funding: they fund roughly one proposal in ten, and most of the others come from people who have already read everything.

Close by saying how confident you are in what you have claimed — in particular in the claim that the question is genuinely open rather than settled somewhere I have not looked — and single out the one point you are least sure of. End with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing a single sentence stating the research question.
