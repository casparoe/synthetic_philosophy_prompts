I am preparing a graduate seminar on hedonism about well-being, and the more I work through the material the more convinced I become that the heterogeneity problem is the whole game rather than a preliminary nuisance. The hedonist needs pleasure to name something capable of bearing the entire weight of welfare — something that can be summed, compared, and used to rank whole lives — and yet the states we group under the heading look almost absurdly various: the taste of something sweet, the warmth of a bath, the relief when a headache lifts, the absorption of a solved crossword, the enjoyment of a sad film, the glow at another's misfortune, the liking a masochist reports for what is also painful, the flat absence of any of this in anhedonia. A colleague keeps insisting the problem simply dissolves once we distinguish pleasure itself from the things we take pleasure in, and I want a list rigorous enough to test that claim rather than wave it through. So write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of pleasure itself — not of well-being, but of what pleasure is — would have to meet. I want the full specification: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the facts about how pleasure is felt and attributed that it must be consistent with, and, for each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it. Tell me which of these requirements are genuine constraints and which are merely the prejudices of one camp (say, the demand that pleasure be a single introspectible sensation, or the demand that it be a propositional attitude); flag any pair or subset that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list turns out to be internally inconsistent, that is the most interesting result rather than a defect; and say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise popular candidates.

I am especially interested in whether the desiderata should be indexed to a purpose, since adequacy is relative: a theory good enough to defend hedonism as a substantive thesis against desire-satisfaction and objective-list rivals may not be the theory needed to underwrite an interpersonal measure of pleasure for welfare policy, and I would rather you keep that distinction in view than pretend there is one neutral list. A brief closing scorecard — how a feeling-quality theory, an attitudinal or liking theory, and a sceptical no-common-property, no-natural-kind view fare against your list — is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring, since the scoring depends on knowing the contenders and the criteria are where the thinking actually lies. Please write in British English.
