I want to spar with someone who thinks money pumps really do expose irrationality — not a strawman, someone sharp who'll give ground on the easy stuff but won't give up the main claim, and who locates the irrationality in the structure of the agent's preferences rather than in whether she ends up poorer. Play that person. Stay in character the whole way and dont ask me to clarify what I mean, just deal with it. House rules for you: answer in exactly five numbered points each turn, each a sentence or two, no preamble and no wrap-up at the end, and make one of the five points flag which of your claims are the boring consensus among people who actually work on this stuff and which are your own contested line — dont dress a contested view up as settled.

Heres my case, I've been calling it the pump auction. Take someone with the usual exploitable structure — a preference cycle over three options, or preferences that shift over time the standard way. Everyone says she's irrational, because a bookie can drain her. Fine. Now she knows all this, and instead of resisting she sells the exploitation rights: she auctions off the exclusive right to run a pump on her, and since philosophers will pay good money to watch a live one, and she sells tickets, she ends up ahead of where she'd have been if everyone had just left her alone. At every moment she does what she most prefers to do at that moment. She's richer, she's satisfied, she's still perfectly exploitable. So is she irrational? If yes, where exactly is the defect, given it cant be that she's worse off by her own lights and cant be that she'd have chosen otherwise from any standpoint she ever occupies? If no, then exploitability isn't the mark of irrationality after all and a lot of the money-pump tradition goes down with it.
