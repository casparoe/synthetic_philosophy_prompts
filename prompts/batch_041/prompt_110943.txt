Two years into the research for a historical novel — a family chronicle set across a long stretch of the nineteenth century, with the usual problem that my characters don't know how it comes out — I have been quietly adopted by a seminar-sized circle of academic historians, and I have come to dread one argument in particular because it never moves. The charge is always Whig history: a narrative that arranges the past as a staircase up to the present, that treats earlier people as rough drafts of ourselves and grades them by how nearly they arrived at our conclusions, that reads intentions backwards out of outcomes and turns contingency into inevitability. I can state that indictment sympathetically, and I can state the reply just as sympathetically, because I have heard it a hundred times: that selection by present interest is not a vice but a condition of writing history at all; that the accusation can be levelled at any narrative whatsoever and therefore disqualifies none; and that the people wielding it hardest write teleologies of their own, merely with the polarity reversed — the forgotten, the defeated and the heretical installed as the true protagonists. Then both sides restate their positions, someone invokes the founding polemic, and we go to the pub. What I want from you is not another round but a diagnosis of the shape of this thing.

My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely and would like tested rather than confirmed, is that "Whig history" is at least three accusations wearing one coat: a metaphysical claim (that history has a direction, and the present is its point); a methodological claim about explanation and narration (that selecting facts by their relevance to us smuggles in anachronistic causes and flattens the actors' own horizon of expectation); and a political claim (that the present is an improvement and the past a preparation for it). If that is right, much of the impasse is people defending the accusation they care about while being refuted on one of the others — and I want to know whether anything survives the disambiguation as a genuine disagreement. A second, related suspicion: my friends use "presentism" in a technical sense that has nothing to do with the presentism complained of in the Whig charge, and I wonder how much of the heat is that single word doing double duty. And a third: I suspect that at least one live question here is not conceptual at all but about whether a narrator can possess a selection principle that is not, in the end, teleological — that is, whether the thing being demanded is even available to anyone who tells a story, historian or novelist.

Diagnose it using what each side actually says, concretely enough that the diagnosis engages their positions rather than a caricature of them. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, two different questions sharing a name, or a disagreement about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a criterion for when present-relevance selection tips over into teleology, a demonstration case, a formal result, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. This matters to me practically as well as theoretically: I am trying to decide whether my narrator may know how it all turns out, and I would rather not settle that question by accident. End your answer with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the diagnosis in a few words and states, in one sentence, whether progress is possible.
