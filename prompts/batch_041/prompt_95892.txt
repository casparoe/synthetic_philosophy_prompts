I keep noticing a move that I can't find a name for, and I'd like you to act like an expert in welfare economics with one foot in the philosophy of measurement and help me figure out whether there's an established concept for it. Please brainstorm a handful of candidate terms or literatures first, before you commit to one, and be explicit about which ones are close and exactly how they fall short. A bare name is not what I'm after — I can already tell that the interesting part is the boundaries.

Here's the pattern, as concretely as I can put it. Whenever somebody actually has to say who is worse off than whom, the comparison is almost never argued for; it gets performed by a technical choice that nobody treats as an ethical one. Some examples from my own life. My roommate sits on the housing committee of a refugee resettlement nonprofit, and the form they use ranks families by a "vulnerability score" with fixed weights for income, disability, family size and so on — the weights are where the comparison between one family and another actually happens, and they were set years ago by nobody in particular. At my old job, the university decided which departments to shrink by comparing student benefit per dollar, where the benefit numbers came from a consulting firm's benchmark; when a philosopher on the committee objected that you can't compare one student's benefit to another's, the answer was that it's just a technical input, not a moral claim. And in the health-policy seminar I sit in on, the whole fight over whether a year of life counts the same for a 30-year-old and an 80-year-old, or whether it should be weighted by health-related quality of life, is settled by which of the two metrics the model uses — and the metric usually gets picked for data-availability reasons, which is definately not an ethical argument. In every case the comparison is real and consequential, but it's never owned by anyone; it hides inside the unit, the weights, the benchmark, or the metric, and it's defended as merely technical.

So my question: does this have a name? I know people say interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless or unscientific, but that's the opposite of what I'm seeing — here everybody is comparing, they've just moved the comparison somewhere it can't be challenged. I also know the general line that measurement is never value-neutral, but that seems broader than what I'm pointing at; I want the specific shape of the thing, where a comparison gets smuggled into the choice of instrument and then insulated by calling the instrument technical. Is there an established term for this, or a tradition that discusses it — I'm offline, so just tell me what the idea is and roughly where it lives, no bibliography please, I can't use citations anyway. If there isn't a name, coin one and define it carefully, and then push on the edges for me: is the pattern still present when the weights are explicit and openly debated, or does it only arise when the instrument looks neutral? Does it survive if a single decision-maker sets the weights and publicly owns them as her own ethical judgment? Is it the same phenomenon when the unit is a physical quantity rather than a money-like one? And how does it relate to the standard worry that comparisons between people are arbitrary — is it a version of that worry, a response to it, or a third thing? Take your time; this has been bugging me for months.
