I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory, and I want to bring the group a question that our most recent session left hanging. We had been working through the arguments for the normative status of expected utility maximization, and a visitor pressed the familiar objection that the theory is a substantive and contestable claim about practical rationality rather than a neutral piece of bookkeeping — that an agent may have perfectly intelligible preferences, complete and transitive and stable, which nonetheless depart from expected utility maximization, so that the departure is a fact about the theory rather than a defect in the agent. One of my students closed the discussion with a line the rest of the room seemed happy to accept: anyone who violates the expected utility axioms is a money pump, since you can relieve such a person of their money for certain, and that is simply what it is to be irrational. The seminar moved on, but I would like to be able to slow that move down, because I suspect the slogan is doing several jobs at once and that no single reading of it can carry the weight the student placed on it.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that a rational agent cannot be a money pump — or the broader thesis that exploitability is irrationality — might be standing for, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, and give me a verdict on each: which are true but too weak to touch the visitor's objection, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which the student and the nodding room most plausibly meant? I would like the machinery each reading requires made explicit. The sure-loss reading, for instance, seems to need the agent to accept each step of the sequence in isolation rather than anticipating the whole, to need the sequence to be finite and the trades costless, to need the agent's preferences to be determinate over the relevant gambles, and to need a decision about whether the loss is synchronic or diachronic; I want to know which of those assumptions are doing the real work and whether the claim survives dropping them, and I want to hear the readings I have not thought of.

I am especially interested in two seams. The first is the gap between exploitability and irrationality, which I suspect the slogan assumes as an independent premise rather than derives from the axioms themselves. The second is the conflation of the sure-loss results that concern probability with the representation results that concern utility, since the slogan slides between them as though they were one theorem. Where it matters, tell me which reading would actually license the student's dismissal and which would leave the visitor's objection standing, so that I have something to hand out at the next meeting rather than an anecdote.
