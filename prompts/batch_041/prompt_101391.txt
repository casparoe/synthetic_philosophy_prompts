I'm a graduate student in political philosophy, and for a seminar on distributive justice we've spent several weeks on luck egalitarianism — the family of views on which inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck (circumstances no one chose) rather than to the choices people actually make. Half the room defends it, half finds it cruel, and in the last session neither side could say what the other was getting wrong; I came away convinced the position is best understood by asking what its administrative implementation would actually look like. So I'd like you to write a parody rather than an essay. Please approach this as someone who knows this literature cold and also has a satirist's ear, and begin by brainstorming on paper before you draft: think through which version of the view you intend to satirize (there are several, and they disagree precisely about the hardest cases), identify the four or five administrative questions on which that version's commitments become visible and uncomfortable, and decide which of the standard objections — the abandonment of the imprudent, the treatment of expensive tastes, the asymmetry between how the untalented and the reckless are handled, the difficulty of sorting a person's traits into chosen and unchosen — can be made to surface as bureaucratic detail rather than as commentary.

Your deliverable is a mock FAQ or citizen's handbook, roughly 700 to 800 words, for a fictional government agency that administers a luck-egalitarian redistribution scheme. Call it whatever you like — an Office of Unchosen Circumstance, a Bureau of Brute Luck, a Ministry of Option Luck Adjudication — choose one name and be consistent with it. Write the document entirely straight, in the deadpan register of a real bureaucratic publication: numbered questions and answers, cross-references to other sections, an occasional note about forms, processing times, or where to appeal. A reader should not be able to tell from the prose alone that the author is joking. The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right, not from wackiness. Let the fine print quietly commit to the substantive positions the view actually holds: an operational definition of brute luck that some citizens will dispute; a schedule for sorting outcomes into brute and option luck; an account of what happens to people whose misfortune flows from their own choices, phrased so that the harshness objection lands without ever being named; a policy on expensive tastes and voluntary risk-taking; a policy on the untalented and the disabled; and a decision about whether the scheme compensates for unchosen character traits and, if so, how it prices them. Invent administrative machinery wherever you need it — an appeals process, a hardship waiver, an actuarial table, a counterfactual-consent audit — but make each invented device do real philosophical work rather than merely sound absurd.

I also want the document to betray the view's internal tensions rather than smoothing them over: sections that seem to presuppose a choice/circumstance distinction the rest of the document cannot draw; a definition of the baseline that quietly shifts between two answers; a reassurance that turns out to underwrite a much stronger claim than the reassurance itself suggests. Do not editorialize, do not introduce a knowing narrator, and do not let any character announce the joke. Keep the register uniform and the tone uninflected from the first line to the last.
