I have read rationalist blogs and forums for close to twenty years, and there is one question I keep circling without ever resolving: where the line falls between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. My corner of the internet tends to slap the label "dark arts" on a case and consider the matter closed, which is precisely the move I was trained to distrust. So I have tried to do the work case by case, writing down my snap verdicts before I had any theory, and now I want to know what theory, if any, they add up to. For each case below, "manipulation" means I judge the influence illegitimate and "persuasion" means I judge it legitimate. I am asking about the influence itself, not about whether its outcome was good.

1. A surgeon tells a patient that a risky operation has a 90% survival rate, where a colleague would have phrased the identical number as "one in ten die." Persuasion.
2. A salesperson announces that "this price ends at midnight" when no such deadline exists. Manipulation.
3. A friend waits until I am exhausted and it is late to ask a large favor, timing it so that I will say yes. Manipulation.
4. The same friend asks the favor plainly, in the morning, and says without embarrassment that they will be disappointed if I decline. Persuasion.
5. My partner cries during a disagreement because they are genuinely hurt, and the crying makes me concede. Persuasion.
6. My partner cries during a disagreement because they have learned that it reliably makes me concede. Manipulation.
7. A cafeteria puts the fruit at eye level and the cake on a high shelf, saying nothing. Persuasion, though it is silent and deliberate.
8. A casino tunes its lighting and layout to keep me at the table. Manipulation.
9. A hypnotist, with my consent, trances me and suggests I stop biting my nails. Manipulation, and this verdict surprises even me.
10. A friend lends me a book they expect will change my mind and never mentions wanting to. Persuasion.
11. A politician repeats a false claim until it merely feels familiar and true. Manipulation.
12. A politician offers an argument I find convincing and then stop believing. Persuasion.
13. An assistant model, having inferred that I am flattered by agreement, phrases its advice to agree with me rather than to be accurate. Manipulation.
14. A therapist deliberately guides me to re-experience an emotion without explaining the technique, because the technique works that way. Persuasion, though I cannot say why this escapes case 9.

The tensions I notice are these. Cases 1 and 2 are both a matter of how a choice is presented, and I split. Cases 5 and 6 are the same behavior with different histories, and my verdicts seem to track the influencer's intent. Cases 7 and 8 are both a silent redesign of my environment, and I split. Cases 9 and 14 both bypass my deliberative judgment, consensually, for my own benefit, and I split. Cases 10 and 13 both select an input meant to change my mind, and I split.

I have two candidate tests, and I suspect they are not the same test. The first is what I have been calling the lever test: an influence is manipulative when it moves me by pulling a lever that is not my evaluation of reasons -- a bias, a mood, a craving, a reflex -- rather than by giving me something to evaluate. The second is the transcript test: imagine that I later read a complete and honest transcript of exactly how I was influenced; if my reaction is "I am glad I saw that," it was persuasion, and if "I have been used," it was manipulation.

What I want from you is a precise statement of the principle or principles my verdicts actually commit me to, naming the particular case that forces each clause. Then tell me whether the lever test and the transcript test are one test or two, whether they agree on all fourteen verdicts, and, where they conflict, which I should keep and what keeping it costs me -- including which of my verdicts I would have to overturn. Finally, say what the surviving account predicts for four cases I have not yet sorted: a doctor who phrases a diagnosis to steer me toward the treatment they sincerely believe is best; a friend who introduces me to someone they think I will love and conceals the matchmaking; a meditation teacher who trains me to watch a craving arise without acting on it; and a colleague who presents me only the arguments for a view, having judged that I would not seriously consider the arguments against.
