I have never studied philosophy formally, but I have been thinking on my own about Benjamin Franklin and his project of moral perfection. As I understand it, he drew up a list of virtues, put them in a deliberate order, and kept a small book with a grid: one virtue per row, the days of the week across the top. Each day he marked every failure to live up to the virtue in question. He tried to work on one virtue at a time, hoping that mastering one would make the next easier, and that in time he might arrive at a completely faultless character. He never fully succeeded, and the order and method were themselves part of what attracted him.

I would like you to act as an expert on moral psychology and the ethics of self-improvement, and to reconstruct the theory of moral perfection that this practice presupposes. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming a list of candidate commitments before you settle on your answer.

I am especially interested in the following. Does the practice presuppose that virtue is a set of separable habits that can be trained one at a time? Does it presuppose that moral failure is primarily a matter of inattention or weak habit, rather than bad character, bad luck, or social circumstance? Does it presuppose that a person can accurately observe and record their own moral conduct, and that such self-surveillance is good for them? Does it presuppose that moral perfection is a coherent goal at all, or only a regulative ideal? And does it presuppose a particular view of the relation between private self-improvement and public life -- that a better person makes a better citizen, or that the two are largely independent?

Please distinguish between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with. For example, if a Christian account of sin and grace would also be compatible with the practice, or if a purely secular habit-formation account would be, say so. Then say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of moral development could license it. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its implicit theory with a rival one -- for instance, with the view that moral behavior is driven much more by situations and social roles than by stable traits, or with the view that moral improvement is not cumulative but always starts from scratch. What would the chart look like then, and would it still make sense to keep it? As a further test, imagine a modern version of the same practice: a phone app that prompts you each evening to rate yourself on honesty, industry, moderation, and so on, and that keeps a streak. Would the app inherit the same philosophical commitments, or would the medium change the theory?

No citations or reading lists are needed; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
