I'm not a lawyer or a philosopher — I came to this from engineering and a long-running dinner argument — but I've spent a couple of years reading jurisprudence on my own, and the argument I keep losing is the old one between legal positivism and natural law theory. I've read the exchange between Hart and Fuller from the late 1950s, and Fuller's book on the inner morality of law, and what strikes me is how much of it is the two of them talking past each other: the positivist keeps insisting that we can identify what the law is without asking whether it is just, and the natural lawyer keeps replying that a rule nobody could be required to obey is not law in the sense that matters — and each of them treats the other's sentence as obviously question-begging. I don't want another summary of the debate; I want the document the two of them should have produced instead of the exchange they actually had.

Please draft that joint statement, the one a committed legal positivist and a committed natural lawyer could both put their names to after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want four things. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what a legal system is, how officials identify the rules they apply, what each theory is actually trying to do, and what neither side is committed to despite what the other says about it — including whether Hart's own account of the minimum content of natural law already concedes something to the natural lawyer, and whether Fuller's demand for fidelity to law is a moral requirement or only the standpoint of a participant committed to the enterprise. Second, the remaining disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair, and specifically on: whether Fuller's eight requirements — generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility, stability, and congruence between official action and the declared rules — are moral requirements or merely conditions of effective governance; whether a sufficiently unjust system fails to be law, and if so whether that failure is a moral fact or a conceptual one; whether officials are under any obligation to apply monstrous but formally valid rules; and what either theory can say about the postwar cases in which courts had to decide what to do with people who had informed on their neighbours under a monstrous regime, using that regime's own law. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side, each with both sides' predictions written down in advance. Fourth, for each crux, a case, argument, or piece of legal practice that both sides agree would bear on it, even though each expects it to come out their way.

Two things I want especially. First, tell me which parts of the dispute are merely verbal: whether the two sides are answering different questions with the same word law, and if so, whether anything real survives once that is cleared up. Second, give both sides a shared test case that neither has a stock answer to — I would propose this one, though you may amend it: a legal order that satisfies every one of Fuller's eight requirements, is administered with scrupulous legality by its officials, and is used to run a hereditary system of slavery, the enslaved being held under rules that are general, published, prospective, clear, consistent, feasible, stable, and faithfully applied. Before arguing, each side should say what it predicts this case shows — whether the rules governing the enslaved count as law, whether officials within the order are under any obligation to apply them, and what its own theory explains here that the other's cannot. Where the two sides cannot even agree on what would settle a crux, say so plainly rather than inventing a test. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table with columns for the question, the positivist's formulation, the natural lawyer's formulation, the crux, the shared test, and each side's prediction.
