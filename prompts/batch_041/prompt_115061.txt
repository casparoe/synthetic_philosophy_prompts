There's a blog post making the rounds on one of the mathy LessWrong-adjacent blogs that walks through Skolem's paradox, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. I know roughly what Löwenheim–Skolem says, but the post hangs a big moral on top of it and I'm not confident I can catch where the slide happens. Hoping you can audit it for me.

Here's the post's account, compressed as best I can:

Löwenheim–Skolem says any consistent first-order theory has a countable model. Set theory is a first-order theory, so ZFC has a countable model, call it M. But Cantor's theorem is provable inside ZFC, so M contains some set m that M itself proves is uncountable — no bijection to the naturals, M thinks. So we've got a countable model asserting the existence of an uncountable set. The paradox dissolves, the post says, once you notice that the bijection is out there all along; it just isn't in M. M's version of the powerset of ω is too small to hold it, so M is blind to the fact that m is really countable. The post's takeaway: 'uncountable' is not a fact about sets out in the world, it's a fact about what a given model can see and define, so Cantor's theorem is really telling us something about our language rather than about size. Skolem himself took it that way, which is why he's read as a relativist or skeptic about set theory. And the post ends by saying the whole thing is a first-order artifact: write set theory in second-order logic and the countable model vanishes, because second-order quantifiers range over all subsets, so uncountable means what it says. ZFC, on this view, is just a first-order approximation to the real, second-order thing.
