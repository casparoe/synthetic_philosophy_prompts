I'm a third-year student taking a course on Ethics and Global Development, and I also sit on the board of a small student-run nonprofit that ships medical supplies to a partner clinic in a country where alot of my classmates' families are from. At our next board meeting we have to vote on a messy case, and I have to write the memo we'll circulate beforehand. Our field coordinator, who is from the country and has worked with the clinic for years, took about $80 from petty cash and gave it to a customs official during a local holiday. The official had discretionary control over a pallet of antibiotics that was stuck in storage, and the pallet was released two days later. The coordinator logged the money as community relations, not as a payment, and says it was a customary gift she would have given anyway, not a quid pro quo; she also says that refusing would have insulted the official and made future shipments much harder. Our board policy bans payments to government officials to get them to do something they're already supposed to do, but it also has a clause allowing small customary gifts if they're not intended to influence an official act. Our bylaws give the board 30 days to ratify a field payment retroactively; if we don't, it automatically goes to our umbrella org as a reportable incident. The meeting is in 10 days. Half the board thinks this is a clear violation and wants to discipline her and maybe report it; the other half thinks the policy is a Western import that doesn't fit the local context, and that punishing a local employee for following local norms would be both unfair and practically self-defeating. I definately don't want a literature review or citations -- just a real recommendation we can act on.

Act as a political philosopher who works on corruption, clientelism, and cross-cultural ethics. Before you write, brainstorm the strongest considerations on both sides: the difference between a bribe, a gift, a facilitation payment, and a clientelist obligation; whether the relevant standard should be the law where we operate, our donors' rules, or a universal standard of impartiality; and what it means that the coordinator is a local employee while the board is mostly students from elsewhere. Then draft the memo to the board. It should recommend a concrete decision -- ratify the payment, reject it and discipline her, or approve a new policy going forward -- with a time frame for any action. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument for the side you come out against, not just dismiss it. It should also say what we should do about the broader policy, not just this one $80 payment. Keep it under 800 words, write it as a formal memo with a clear bottom line, and don't hedge at the end. After the memo, on the very last line, put a machine-readable tag in exactly this form: \answer{LABEL: short reason}, where LABEL is one of RATIFY, REJECT, or NEW-POLICY. Think carefully.
