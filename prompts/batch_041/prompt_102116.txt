I'm a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I'm grading the midterm for the unit on naturalistic theories of content. The course has covered Millikan and Dretske alongside the standard objections, and the students were told in advance that the exam would test their grasp of the teleosemantic program rather than their recall of any particular text. The question and one student's answer are below. I'd like a grade out of 20 together with the specific comments that justify it, so that I can hand the student a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

The exam question was: "State the teleosemantic strategy for naturalizing intentional content. Illustrate it with a biological example, explain how the account handles misrepresentation, and set out one serious objection together with how a defender might respond."

The student's answer reads:

"Teleosemantics, as developed by Ruth Millikan and Fred Dretske, is an attempt to naturalize intentionality by explaining mental content in terms of biological function. The guiding idea is that a state represents whatever it is the function of that state to indicate, and since functions are fixed by a history of natural selection, content turns out to be a matter of evolutionary history rather than of intrinsic resemblance, causal covariation, or convention. This is meant to answer both the representational-status question, of what makes a state representational at all, and the content-determination question, of what makes it represent this rather than that.

Millikan's treatment of the honeybee dance is the canonical illustration. When a forager returns from a nectar source it performs a waggle dance whose angle and duration covary with the direction and distance of the source, so the dance carries information about the source's location. But information is not yet content, since any state carries information about indefinitely many things at once. What makes the dance represent the location of nectar is the way it functions in the hive's economy: the other bees' response of flying out is the mechanism whose proper function the dance serves, and it is this consumer-side function that fixes the content. The dance means 'nectar at such-and-such a location' because that is the state of affairs in which the consumers' response was selected to succeed.

This apparatus is also supposed to solve the problem of misrepresentation, which is often treated as the acid test for any naturalistic theory of content. If content were simply information, a state could never misrepresent, because it would always carry information about whatever actually caused it. On the teleosemantic view, by contrast, a state's content is fixed by its function rather than by its actual cause, so a bee dancing in the presence of a deceptive experimenter can still produce a representation that means 'nectar at location L' even though no nectar is there. Misrepresentation is thus the production of a representation outside the conditions it was selected for.

The best-known objection is that the theory makes content hostage to actual evolutionary history. Dretske's Swampman case makes the point vivid: a being created by a lightning strike, molecule-for-molecule identical to you but with no evolutionary history, would have no states with content at all, which many find absurd. A teleosemanticist might reply that Swampman's states have the same content as yours because he has the same behavioral dispositions; but that reply simply abandons the theory's distinctive claim and collapses it into a crude dispositionalism. The theory therefore faces a dilemma between an implausibly externalist result and giving up its own explanatory core."
