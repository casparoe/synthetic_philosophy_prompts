I am a novelist at work on a book of ideas, and I have reached the chapter in which my protagonist, a philosopher of mathematics, delivers a job talk at a research institute devoted to formal verification. Her thesis is that the MIU string-rewriting puzzle—the one that starts from MI and asks whether MU can be derived using rules that append U to a string ending in I, double the part after M, replace III with U, and delete UU—is a minimal model of a general phenomenon: some goals are unreachable inside a system not for lack of ingenuity but because of the system’s structure, and the rational response is to jump out of the system, altering the rules, the representation, or the goal itself. She calls this JOOTSing, and she distinguishes representational jumps, which change the encoding or inference rules while keeping the goal fixed, from teleological jumps, which revise the goal. The chapter turns on the question period, and I need the Q&A prep sheet she would write for herself the night before.

The audience is precise. About twenty people will be there: mostly formal methods researchers and proof-assistant engineers who believe that any well-posed problem has an in-system solution if one is clever enough; two cognitive scientists who have read deeply in the literature on analogy and creativity, who are sympathetic to the idea but suspicious of its vagueness; one historian of logic who will care whether the MIU puzzle is being made to carry more weight than it can; and a department chair who controls the hiring decision, is not a specialist, and dislikes grand claims. She has twenty minutes to speak and perhaps fifteen for questions.

Please give me the eight or so objections she will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to her case. Phrase each in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it. For each, give a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection she is hoping nobody asks. Some of the pressure should come from the formal methods people, who will want to know how she distinguishes a failure to find a derivation from a proof that no derivation exists, and whether jumping out is ever more than changing the subject. Some should come from the cognitive scientists, who will ask whether JOOTSing is one capacity or a family of unrelated moves, and how one tells a disciplined jump from a rationalization. The historian of logic should press on whether the MIU puzzle is representative or a toy, and the chair should ask what any of this has to do with hiring a philosopher. I would also like one objection that is not really an objection but a request she cannot grant in fifteen minutes, with a note on how she might decline it without losing the room.

The deliverable should be a writer’s reference: a list, not an essay. For each objection, a one-line statement in the objector’s voice, a paragraph on why this audience finds it compelling, a two-minute reply, and a sentence on the weakest joint in the reply. Where she should concede, say so; where she should hold the line, say why; where the honest answer is that she does not know, mark it as such. The novel’s tension depends on her not being invulnerable, so do not smooth over the places where the position is genuinely exposed.
