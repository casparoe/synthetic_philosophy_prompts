You are a game theorist and decision theorist working on cooperative AI, at home with commitment devices, program equilibrium, and mechanism design under deliberately weak assumptions. Think carefully and at length before you answer: first brainstorm the full space of things one might demand of such a theory, then prune to what is genuinely required, then organize the result. I want the requirements list itself, not a literature review; do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading recommendations.

I need the desiderata that any adequate theory of safe Pareto improvements must satisfy. By a safe Pareto improvement I mean a modification of a strategic situation — either of the game the players face or of the instructions under which they are told to play it — that leaves every player weakly better off with certainty, that is, without any probabilistic assumptions about how the players, or the representatives they delegate to, actually play games. The purpose I have in mind is a theory good enough to guide the design of real commitment technologies: enforceable contracts, publicly posted programs that can read one another, promises whose violations are detectable after the fact.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Include at least three groups:

- The cases the theory must get right, with a note on what each one forces. I have in mind at minimum a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in which cooperation is achievable by symmetric posted programs; a bargaining or demand situation whose default leaves the worst outcome genuinely possible; and a case in which an apparently attractive improvement creates a fresh opportunity for one side to exploit the other.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: between improving a game and improving the way a game is played; between safety against best responses and safety against arbitrary programs; between the principals' payoffs and their representatives' payoffs; between what is guaranteed and what is merely likely; and between the existence of an improvement and its selection when several are available.
- The results or impossibility findings it must be consistent with.

Then treat these three cases as tests the theory has to pass, since I think the usual accounts handle them badly:

1. Asymmetric commitment. One party can bind itself — by contract, by posting a program, by making an ex post verifiable promise — and the other cannot. Is safe Pareto improvement still well defined? Should the definition be relativized to a commitment technology, and if so, does safety become a property of the pair (game, technology) rather than of the game?
2. The spoiler. A third party who is not a player, and who gains nothing from the improvement, can at some cost degrade the commitment technology itself: render the contract unenforceable, corrupt the verifier. Should safety be assessed against every agent who can act, or only against the players? What does each choice cost?
3. Introspective uncertainty. Each player is unsure, at the moment of agreeing, which payoff function is theirs. Is a modification that is Pareto for every possible type a stronger or a weaker requirement than one that is Pareto for ex ante expected payoffs? Which should the theory adopt, and why?

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and mark the requirement whose adoption excludes the most otherwise-attractive theories. Above all, flag any subset of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether demanding both a complete, decidable characterization of a game's safe Pareto improvements and safety against arbitrary programmatic deviations is consistent, or whether one of the two has to give. If the whole notion is degenerate, if the only modifications that survive a serious safety requirement are trivial ones, say so and argue it.

Be committed. Do not hand me a neutral menu of formulations with the trade-offs laid out; choose a formulation of safety, defend it, say which requirements you would drop, and state plainly where your choice is weakest.
