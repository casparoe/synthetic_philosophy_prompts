One of my grad students in the philosophy of history seminar handed me this argument last week and I still don't know whether to frame it or fail him. It's his own, and he's annoyingly pleased with it. Reconstructed:

(1) Every historical event is fully fixed by the conditions that preceded it.
(2) Among those conditions are some as small and dull as you like: a horse throws a shoe, a messenger stops for one drink too many, a nail is missing.
(3) A system in which huge outcomes turn on arbitrarily small antecedents is chaotic in the technical sense -- arbitrarily small differences in the initial state get stretched into arbitrarily large differences down the line.
(4) So whether the empire fell or staggered on may have come down to a nail.
(5) If it came down to a nail, then any explanation that cites the big structural causes -- economy, class, demography, climate -- is at best incomplete and at worst just false, since none of that fixed which way it went.
(6) So there are no true large-scale explanations in history at all. It's contingency all the way down.

That last line obviuosly can't be right; if it is, my whole field is a fraud and so is his. But I can't find the rotten step, and I'd rather not lose (2) and (3), which seem to me the genuinely interesting part. Where does it break, and what do I have to pay to break it there?
