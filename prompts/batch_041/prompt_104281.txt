You're a political philosopher who's read a lot of theory but also actually follows how governments justify themselves. Think carefully here, and maybe sketch some notes before you commit to an answer.

Here's what I keep puzzleing over. Take the way Beijing justifies one-party rule: not mainly through elections or consent, but through results — growth, stability, order, the claim that the people's real interests get served. Singapore's PAP runs a version of this too, a sort of results-first case for staying in power, though it also holds real elections and leans on winning them. Now the odd part: both also reach for procedural language when it suits them — people's congresses, consultative bodies, the idea of a whole-process democracy, the party as the genuine representative of the popular will. So one day legitimacy is output, the next it's representation.

What I want from you: reconstruct the theory of legitimacy this practice actually presupposes. Is it a pure performance/output theory, or does it quietly need a procedural element to do the work? What does it assume about the good, about whose interests count, and about whether a population can be wrong about its own interests? Which rival theories of legitimacy does the practice quietly rule out, and which could it happily live with? Also tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same behavior. And finally: if you swapped the presupposed theory for a consent-based or a purely procedural one, what concretely would have to change in how such a regime governs or defends itself? Be concrete, not abstract. No citations needed, just the argument.
