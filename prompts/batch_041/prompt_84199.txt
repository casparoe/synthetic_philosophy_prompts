I've been reading my way through the Dennett–Caruso exchange on free will and punishment, and I keep finding that my verdict on the whole debate hinges on something neither of them quite puts at the center, so I want an actual number from you rather than a survey of the positions. Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can get it: that no one ever deserves to be subjected to hard treatment simply in virtue of having done wrong — where "deserves" is read in the basic-desert sense, i.e. the suffering is intrinsically fitting as a response to the wrong, and not merely a means to some forward-looking good. I'll call that the abolitionist claim, and I want your credence that it is true.

Give me the number with its derivation rather than a bare figure. I expect the terms to include at least: the probability that some account of free will strong enough to ground basic desert is correct — and here say explicitly how you are treating the libertarian/compatibilist fork, since I suspect that single choice is doing most of the work; the probability that "basic desert" is a coherent notion at all rather than a reified projection of our reactive attitudes; the probability that, conditional on basic desert being real, our actual institutions track it well enough for it to make any practical difference; and the probability that the empirical case for desert-free deterrence, quarantine, and rehabilitation is as strong as Caruso needs it to be. Attach a rough number and a one-line justification to each, show how they combine into your final credence, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most. If you think one of my proposed terms is the wrong cut, replace it and say why.

There is one further thing, and it is really the reason I am asking. A friend of mine who works as a public defender has been pressing a reframing I cannot shake: she says Dennett and Caruso are both arguing about the wrong paradigm, because they treat state punishment as the central case of desert when the central case is resentment and blame within personal relationships — the demand that a friend who betrayed you answer for what they did. On her view, what we are tracking in desert is a person's standing as a participant in a relationship who can be called to account, not a metaphysical fact about the causal history of their will; and she thinks that once you start from there, basic desert looks considerably more defensible. So give me a second number: your credence that, if the debate were re-centered on interpersonal blame and answerability rather than on state punishment, the abolitionist claim would become substantially less plausible (I'll count a drop of 0.2 or more as substantial). Then tell me whether the reframing is a genuine insight or a bait-and-switch — specifically, whether the desert invoked by the relational reading is the same desert that a system of hard treatment would need to invoke, or whether the argument quietly changes the subject.
