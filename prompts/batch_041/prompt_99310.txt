(1) A maxim is morally ok only if you could will it as a universal law. (2) It fails that test if universalizing it contradicts the very thing the action presupposes -- the stock example being promising while intending to break it, since a world where everyone did that has no promises left to break. (3) Now take the maxim: "I'll skip uni and go straight into work, because my family needs the money." (4) Universalize it: everyone in my position skips uni. (5) But universities and the skilled trades only exist because enough people go through them; a world where everyone in my position skipped them wouldn't have the institutions my choice leans on. (6) So the universalized maxim can't be a law of nature. (7) So the test says it's wrong to skip uni to support your family. (8) That's absurd. (9) Try to save it by narrowing the maxim -- "I'll skip uni because I'm the one whose family needs the money" -- and the "I'm the one" clause makes the universalization vacous, so nothing ever fails. (10) So the test either condemns innocent choices or does nothing at all.
