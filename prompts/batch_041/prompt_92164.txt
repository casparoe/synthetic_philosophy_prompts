I do interpretation editing for a small museum partnership — curators and community groups send me draft labels and audio-guide scripts, I knock them into shape, then they go to the head of collections and the panel for sign-off. It's about forty bits of text a month, each one 30–60 words, and I get roughly twenty minutes a piece, so I can reframe a sentence or send it back, but I can't do new research or drag the curator off for a seminar.

The trouble is our funders' bid document has a line in it about every label "making the connection to today's visitor", which the curators definately read as: end on how far we've come. So I keep getting drafts like — and these are invented, but only just — "By 1900 the mill was finally forced to shorten children's hours: a small step on the road to the workplace protections we take for granted today." Or one about a local surgeon who pushed a treatment that was later abandoned: "a man ahead of his time, whose warnings went unheeded." Or a canal label that can't resist pointing out that the railway won and the canal men just couldn't see it coming.

I know this is the whig history thing. The line I half-remember from Butterfield is that it "studies the past with reference to the present", but every time I try to turn that into a rule for the curators I either end up banning any mention of the present at all — which the funders hate and which honestly feels wrong, because some of these stories really are about people who deliberately set out to change things and did — or I wave through stuff I know is sloppy. I've also been told the real complaint is about "abridgement", and about pretending outcomes were inevitable, and that Butterfield wasn't against historians caring about the present as such. I can't seperate those things, and I don't know what's left in practice.

So: you're a philosopher of history who actually thinks about how present-mindedness distorts what gets written, and I want a procedure, not an essay. Think it through carefully. Start by pulling apart, for yourself, the different things that go wrong when the past is told as a run-up to now — inevitabilism, picking only the winners, sitting in judgement on dead people by our standards, making them sound like us, and whatever else belongs on that list — because I suspect my rules keep misfiring because I'm lumping several distinct failings together under one word. Then give me a checklist I can run on a single draft in five minutes: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the tests I can actually apply to a sentence rather than to a whole philosophy, where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to the curator or the panel because it's a judgement call and not a rule, and which of the steps are really just my taste dressed up as method. Be honest about where it will misfire and what it can't do.

Then run it on two drafts. (a) The mill label above — my instinct is that the "road to today" line is fine here, because the factory acts were a causal chain the campaigners themselves intended and pursued, but tell me if I'm kidding myself. (b) The surgeon — I want "ahead of his time" struck, but I can't say why that's different from (a), and I suspect the procedure will end up flagging something I'm rather proud of: I've got a label about a local woman who campaigned for a change that only became law sixty years after she died.

No reading lists or citations, please — if a move has a name in the literature, name it in a clause, but I want the procedure, not the bibliography. Under 800 words, and lead with the procedure itself as a numbered list, with the two worked examples after it in a few lines each.
