I'm a backend engineer with no real philosophy background, and I keep getting sucked into arguments with coworkers about whether labs should be publishing frontier model weights. I want to actually work out where I stand instead of just picking a team, so write me a decision tree / taxonomy I can walk through.

Here's what I want. Start the tree from something I already accept: that a frontier model's weights, once released, can't be recalled, and that the interesting question isn't whether openness is nice in the abstract but what exactly is being opened. Build every branch node as a question I can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — so no node like "are you a safetyist or an accelerationist?". The nodes should be the real fault lines. A few I think belong on the tree, but you should add or cut as the logic demands:

- What's actually being released: weights, training data, the code, the eval harness, the system card, the scaffold around the model? (These come apart and I suspect most people collapse them.)
- What's the threat model — a copyable expert that makes bad actors stronger, a race dynamic that punishes whoever slows down, concentration of power in a few labs, or plain accidents?
- The marginal question: does my release change the global risk, given that someone else probably releases something similar anyway? And is the counterfactual "nobody releases" or "a worse actor releases first"?
- Does the many-eyes argument actually transfer from open-source code to open weights, or does it only work when the artifact is inspectable and modifiable by outsiders?
- Do you treat model weights as an information hazard (the knowledge itself is the harm) or as a dual-use tool (the harm is in use)?
- Is the goal to slow down the frontier, or to speed up the defense?

Then locate the named positions in the space your questions carve out. I want leaves like: release everything now; open weights but keep data and compute closed; structured / gated access; publish capabilities and evals but not weights; keep the small models open and the frontier closed; moratorium or delay; open by default but paired with a liability regime. Give each leaf the position name (or mark it unnamed if nobody really defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two things I specifically care about. First, note any combinations of answers that nobody occupies, and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, rather than pretending the questions distinguish them.

Keep it to maybe six or eight branch questions and six to nine leaves. No bibliographies, no reading list, no citations — I don't want famous names' positions, I want the shape of the disagreement. Also I'm a software person so don't dumb down the parts about weights vs data vs code, I get that.
