I'm a novelist, and the book I'm finishing turns on a woman who comes to awareness with no sight, no sound, no sense of a body — only the bare certainty that she is. A philosopher friend offered me the following argument to underwrite her state, but it seems to prove more than I can afford:

(1) Imagine a person made in an instant, adult and whole, held in empty air with his eyes covered and his limbs kept from touching anything, so that he receives no sensation at all.
(2) He would still affirm that he exists; he would not affirm that he has a body.
(3) So his assurance that he exists depends on no body and no bodily information.
(4) If a thing's assurance that it exists depends on no body, then the thing is not a body.
(5) Therefore the self that says "I" is immaterial.
(6) What is immaterial does not depend on the body for its existence.
(7) Therefore it is not destroyed when the body dies — and my protagonist outlives her accident.

I want to keep (2); without it the book has no floor. But (7) is a claim I can't put on the last page. Tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it costs me — what I lose, and what a defender of the argument could still say in its favor. And please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than handing me one camp's answer dressed up as the consensus.
