I retired from forty years of bench science and have spent the last two of them reading moral philosophy. The situationist critique of virtue ethics disturbs me more than anything else I have found there: experiments in which a small change of circumstance — being hurried, finding a dime in a coin-return slot, hearing a voice from another room — turns ordinarily decent people callous, alongside correlational evidence that honesty in one setting predicts little about honesty in another. The conclusion I keep meeting is that the stable character traits virtue ethics needs do not exist, and that we should redesign situations rather than cultivate, praise, or blame persons. Please describe the most important implications of that critique for how a scientist should think about scientific character: the traits we credit for good work, the praise and blame we assign in priority disputes and misconduct cases, and how we choose and train students. I have been asked to nominate an old colleague for a prize in scientific integrity, and I no longer know what such a nomination would be praising. Tell me also where the critique overreaches — which of its conclusions rest on experiments that do not generalize — and what a defender of virtue ethics could still keep.
