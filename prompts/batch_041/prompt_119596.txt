My friend Dan and I went at this for two hours last night and I'm still annoyed, mostly because I think hes wrong, but every time I try to say where, I end up halfway agreeing with him.

Some background: I'm a steward at my local, warehouse, about 400 members. We came within a day of walking out in March over scheduling, and I spent that week explaining to new hires why a strike is a serious thing and not just "everyone quits at once." So this isn't abstract for me.

Here's Dan's argument, which he wrote out on the back of an envelope like a lawyer:

1. You can quit your job whenever you want, for any reason or none. Nobody thinks you need a justification for it.
2. If you're allowed to do something, you're allowed to do it together with other people who want the same thing. Coordinating doesn't turn a permitted act into a forbidden one. If ten of us each have the right to walk off, then ten of us walking off is ten exercises of that right, not one new act with a new moral status.
3. A strike is exactly that: a lot of workers walking off at the same time.
4. So the "right to strike" is nothing over and above the right to quit, done in concert. There's no special right in there to discover.
5. But quitting ends the job. When you walk out, the employer can hire someone to do your work; you gave up the seat. If a strike is just mass quitting, the same follows for strikers. They have no claim on their jobs, and hiring permanent replacements isn't a dirty trick, it's just what happens when people quit.
6. Which means the "right to strike" as it's usually invoked — you walk out, you come back, the job is being held for you, the point is to change the terms rather than to leave — is asking for something that (1)-(4) can't pay for. That's a claim on the enterprise, not an exercise of a right to quit.
7. So pick. Either a strike is mass quitting, in which case there's no special right and replacement is fine, or it's more than mass quitting, in which case you owe me an account of what it is, because (1) and (2) are all you've got and they don't get you there.

I said that's like saying a boycott is just alot of people shopping somewhere else, and a picket line is just a lot of people standing on a sidewalk. He said yes. That's the point. That's all any of it ever was. He also said: by my own logic a lockout is just the boss firing everybody at once, and nobody thinks firing is illegitimate, so why does one of them get a right and the other one get a lawsuit.

My problem is I want to keep (1) and (2). They're the only thing that makes sense to me about why we have any standing at all: we're not asking for a favour, we're not a special interest with a licence, we're people who are allowed to walk and are walking together. And I can't accept the first horn of (7) — if replacement is legitimate then a strike is a slow resignation and the whole practice is a bluff.

So which step is doing work it shouldn't be? And be straight with me about the price. If something has to go, tell me what I'm buying and what I'm selling. I'd rather lose a premise than lose the strike.
