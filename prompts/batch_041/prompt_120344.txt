I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'm putting together a short after-dinner talk for a room of engineers and data scientists. I want to open with a joke, and I've decided it should be about the paradox of voting. I've seen that phrase used for two different puzzles — one about group preferences running in circles, and one about why anybody votes at all when a single ballot almost never decides anything. I mean the second one.

I want the joke to land for people who have actually thought about this, so the humor has to come out of the ideas rather than out of the word "vote." Here are the two ideas I'd like it to braid together.

The first is the jury theorem. If each voter is even slightly more likely than a coin flip to choose the better of two options, then the more voters you add, the closer the group's majority verdict gets to certainty. A big electorate is almost uncannily accurate, and it gets more accurate as it grows. (I know this assumes the voters are independent of one another, which real ones are not; you can acknowledge that if it helps, but I don't want the joke to be about that assumption.)

The second is the pivotality argument. The bigger the electorate, the less likely any one vote is to be the vote that decides the outcome. Past a certain size, the odds that your ballot matters are so small that they can't come close to justifying the trouble of casting it.

What keeps nagging at me is that both ideas are functions of the same variable, the size of the electorate, and they pull in opposite directions. The arrangement that makes the group most accurate is the arrangement that gives each member the least reason to take part.

Please write a joke that turns on exactly that collision. A one-liner, a short dialogue, or a two-paragraph story — whichever serves the punchline best. A few requirements:

(a) The punchline should be intelligible only to someone who understands both ideas. If the joke still works for a reader who has never heard of either, it's too generic and I don't want it.

(b) It should not be a joke about "your vote doesn't matter" in the flat sense; that version has been done to death. The twist has to come from the accuracy-versus-participation tension.

(c) No real politicians, parties, or elections. Keep it abstract, or invent the setting.

(d) American English, and keep any profanity mild.

If you think the two ideas can't honestly be braided the way I've described — if I've misread one of them — say so first, in a sentence or two, and then write the best joke you can with the correction.
