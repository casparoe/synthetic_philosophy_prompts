I'm a graduate student in a decision theory seminar and I've ended up defending a view that most of the room thinks is confused. The view: ambiguity aversion — the pattern Ellsberg's urn elicits, where people prefer the 1-in-3 bet on red to the bet on black, yet prefer the 2-in-3 bet on black-or-yellow to the bet on red-or-yellow — is not a bias to be trained out of people. It registers a real difference. In one case the odds are grounded in something, in the other they are not, and it is not irrational for that difference to make a difference to what one chooses. The price is the sure-thing principle, and I am prepared to pay it; the alternative, as I see it, is to tell people that the difference between knowing the odds and not knowing them can never on its own matter to a rational choice, which strikes me as a stipulation in favor of one formalism rather than a result.

I'd like you to act as a referee for this view — a decision theorist with deep knowledge of the foundations of expected utility and of the experimental literature on choice under uncertainty — and tell me what would count against it. Please don't write me a survey; I want a map of the view's exposure. Begin by reconstructing what the position is actually committed to: what it must deny, what it can remain neutral about, and where it risks being true by definition. Only then start hunting for defeaters, and think through the space of possible objections before you rank any of them. I want the formal ones handled carefully: what becomes of the view when the choice is sequential rather than one-shot; whether an ambiguity-averse chooser can be committed to declining information she would otherwise value, or to preferences that reverse themselves as she learns; and whether the whole pattern can be reconstructed as ordinary risk aversion applied to a second-order lottery — a distribution over candidate odds — in which case I need to know whether my view has quietly collapsed into a dressed-up version of the very framework I am trying to depart from, or whether it survives as a genuinely distinct claim. I also want the experimental ones, assessed honestly rather than merely listed: whether it matters that the attitude may appear only when subjects compare themselves with someone they take to be better informed; whether it can be elicited for urns whose composition was itself fixed by a known random process, so that nothing about the odds is ungrounded; and how much it should bother me if the attitude shifts with the mere description of the same choice. For each, say whether you think the claim is true, and whether its truth undercuts the thought that the aversion tracks something about the world rather than something about the chooser. Finally, the conceptual objections, including whether 'the odds are unknown' is a feature of the world or of me, what follows for a view that makes rationality turn on it, and whether the contrast the view leans on survives the observation that even the supposedly known urn is known only if one trusts whoever set it up.

Rank what you find from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about the difference between a defeater that should make me abandon the view, one that should force a qualification I can actually state, and one that is merely an embarrassment to a cousin of the view I don't hold. Tell me what the most sophisticated defender of my position would herself concede counts against it — not what her critics say she ought to concede. Then say whether the position, as usually stated, has been insulated against refutation by the way it defines its central term, and if so, whether that is a defect here or simply the price of a view at this level of abstraction. Finish by naming the single consideration you would want me to weigh most heavily if I had time to think about only one, and what the view would have to give up in order to survive it.
