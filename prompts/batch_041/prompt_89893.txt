I serve as parliamentarian for a membership organization of about forty thousand people, which meets every second year in a convention of some six hundred delegates. The convention can amend the articles, the bylaws, and the standing orders. I sit beside the chair during sessions, advise in real time on points of order, and write up the reasoning afterwards in a rulings memo. Most of what comes up is ordinary — a quorum count, whether a motion is in order under the current agenda, what two-thirds means — and the manuals cover it. What they do not cover is a recurring family of proposals whose effect is to change the rules by which rules are changed: a motion to lower the threshold for future amendments, to be decided under the present threshold; a motion to entrench a provision so that no future convention can touch it; a motion to let a standing committee amend the standing orders without coming to the floor; a motion making the convention's decisions final and unreviewable by any other body; and, once, a motion to change who is entitled to rule on the motion itself. The manuals will tell me what two-thirds means. They do not tell me how to think about a rule that applies to itself.

I want from you a decision procedure I can actually run, built on Hofstadter's contrast between a tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level — the picture in which a system can bend back on itself, as Escher's two hands each draw the other and a canon's variations comment on the theme that generates them, only because the bending happens against a level that is not itself bent: the surface the drawing is on, the rules that generate the variations, the artist who is nowhere inside the picture. What I want made operational is not "find the highest-ranking rule" but something narrower and stranger: for a given proposal, identify the level that must be held fixed for the proposal even to be a determinate act — for it to be true or false that the assembly did something, rather than there being no fact of the matter about what it did — and then ask whether the proposal is trying to move that level from inside the tangle.

Some things I need the procedure to confront rather than sidestep. First, our documents are silent on all of this and there is no court above us, so "inviolate" cannot mean "guaranteed by something outside the system." When the inviolate level is itself only a convention — nothing in the world forces our standing orders to be the surface rather than the thing drawn — its inviolate status is something we stipulate for the length of a single act, not something we discover. Second, I suspect we routinely conflate two things: a level that is inviolate for the purposes of this act, and a rule that is entrenched against the future. Third, the regress is real: if a proposal can move the level that constitutes the tangle, then there is presumably a level above that one, and I need to know when to stop climbing and why stopping there is not arbitrary.

Constraints. I have about five minutes while the chair waits, and whatever I say has to be sayable out loud without sounding like a seminar. The chair decides; I only advise, and the memo records my advice alongside the ruling. The delegates are not philosophers, but they will notice arbitrariness, and the point of the exercise is that rulings on self-amendment be followable by delegates who lost.

Please write the response in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

**The distinction, operationalized** (~200 words): the working difference between a tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level, stated so that a delegate could follow it, plus the single diagnostic question the rest of the procedure turns on. Include the one thing about this distinction that is most likely to be misunderstood by someone who has heard of it only in passing.

**The test, step by step** (~700 words): a numbered sequence I can run in five minutes. For each step give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, which answer terminates the procedure with a ruling that the motion is out of order, which answer terminates it with a ruling that the motion is in order, and which answer hands the matter to the chair as a judgment call that no procedure can settle. The steps should distinguish at least: proposals that change a rule, proposals that change the rule for changing rules, proposals that change the identity or authority of the body that decides, and proposals that purport to bind future conventions. State explicitly where in the sequence the climb up levels stops, and what makes stopping there principled rather than arbitrary.

**Worked cases** (~600 words): run the test on three motions. (a) A motion to reduce the threshold for future bylaw amendments from two-thirds to a simple majority, to be decided under the present two-thirds threshold. (b) A motion to declare a named provision unamendable by any future convention. (c) A motion to let the bylaws committee amend the standing orders on its own authority, subject to reversal at the next convention — this one I favor, and I have said so in a committee meeting, so I need the procedure to be run on it without regard to what I want. For each, give the ruling, the sentence I would say to the chair, and the sentence I would put in the memo. If the procedure tells me something I will not like about (c), say so plainly rather than softening it.

**The judgments the procedure cannot make for me** (~250 words): the points at which the honest answer is that the documents do not decide the question, the assembly decides by voting, and the record should note that the circularity was seen and accepted rather than papered over. Say when I should say that out loud, and what I should refuse to claim.

**Where it will misfire, and the warning signs** (~200 words): the situations in which the procedure will give a confident wrong answer or invite abuse, the kinds of proposals that will look tangled and are not (and the reverse), and the one piece of business I should insist on handling outside the procedure altogether.
