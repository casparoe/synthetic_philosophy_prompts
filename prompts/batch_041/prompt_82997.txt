Question 3 (20 points). In lecture we separated three things that a Dutch book argument may help itself to: the measurement premise (an agent's credence in A is the odds at which she is willing to trade on A), the combination premise (if each trade in a set is acceptable to her on its own, the set as a whole is acceptable to her), and the linearity assumption (her utility for money is linear over the relevant range, or else the stakes are small enough to be treated as if it were). (a) Reconstruct the synchronic Dutch book argument for the probability axioms and say which of the three the argument needs. (b) Explain how the argument is extended diachronically and what it is supposed to establish about conditionalization. (c) Assess: does the argument show that incoherent credences are irrational, or only that they are imprudent? Say what becomes of the argument if the agent is risk-averse, and what becomes of it if she declines to bet at all.

Answer: "A Dutch book is a set of bets such that, whatever happens, the person who accepts them is worse off than if she had accepted none of them. The Dutch book argument for the probability axioms is standardly credited to Ramsey and de Finetti. It needs the measurement premise and the combination premise; the linearity assumption is what licenses us to read a guaranteed monetary loss as a guaranteed loss in utility. Concretely, if an agent takes A and not-A each to be 0.6 likely, she will buy a bet on A at 6:4 and a bet on not-A at 6:4, and the two together leave her down whatever happens. The lesson is that anyone whose degrees of belief violate the axioms will lose money, so coherence is not an optional extra.

The diachronic version, usually credited to Lewis, extends the same idea to changes of belief over time. The bookie places bets with the agent before the evidence arrives, waits, and then trades with her again at her new odds; if her new credence in A is not her old conditional credence in A given E, the two rounds of betting constitute a Dutch book. Since an agent who updates by conditionalization is immune to this, conditionalization is the uniquely rational rule of updating.

I take the argument to be a good argument for something, but not for as much as it is usually taken to show. If an agent simply refuses to bet, or bets on grounds other than her credences, there are no odds to read off and the argument has nothing to work with; and it would be odd to conclude that such a person has no degrees of belief at all. The combination premise also fails for a risk-averse agent: a set of individually fair bets can be a package she has reason to refuse, because the package may carry risk she does not want to bear. That is a real problem when the stakes are large, and it goes away if we scale the stakes down, which I take to be the standard reply. So I would say the argument shows coherence to be a practically useful constraint rather than a requirement of rationality."
