I cover science and ideas for a metropolitan daily, and I am in the last stretch of a feature on a small research outfit — two people, one grant, a spare office — that has spent six years maintaining what it calls a trap register: a running list of situations classified either as a trap or as not a trap, each with a short published entry. The founder tells interviewers that she avoids the Moloch shorthand common in technology circles, on the grounds that it makes the phenomenon sound like fate. I am less interested in the vocabulary than in the doctrine underneath it, because the register has now been handed a case unlike anything in it, and the two researchers disagree about how to rule.

The register so far, as best I can reconstruct it from the published editions:

- Trap: routine antibiotic use in livestock, where each producer's best response is to use them and each knows that a shared restriction would leave every producer better off.
- Trap: the clickbait equilibrium among ad-funded news sites.
- Trap: the auction in which cities bid against one another for a large employer, each city preferring a world in which none of them bids.
- Trap: the wage-and-perks escalation between two firms recruiting from the same narrow talent pool.
- Not a trap: a retail chain whose owners deliberately ran the business down in order to sell the real estate.
- Not a trap: a costly sprinkler mandate that a city council imposed on all new construction.
- Not a trap: a regional price-fixing cartel that has held together for a decade, enforced by its own trade association — an entry that drew complaints from readers, since the cartel is obviously a bad thing.

Every entry so far involves parties who were, in some sense, in competition with one another. The case now pending is not like that. Sixty households in a dry valley draw from a single aquifer. Each household's cheapest option is to deepen its bore and pump harder; each would prefer a binding cap that all of them observed; none of them sells water or is in any kind of contest with any of the others; and none of them wants the aquifer drawn down. One of the two researchers wants to list it as a trap, the other does not, and neither has convinced the other.

What I need from you is the analysis rather than the headline. Give me the two or three rules that fit the register as it stands, say what each one predicts for the valley, tell me which of them you think the outfit is really running and why, and name a case that is not in the register yet but would separate whichever rules survive. If you conclude that the register actually supports only one rule and the rest are rationalizations, say so and show me where the others break. And whichever way they rule, tell me what principle the outfit would be adopting by ruling that way, in a sentence I can put in the piece.
