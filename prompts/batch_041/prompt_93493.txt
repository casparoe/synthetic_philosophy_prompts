I am writing a historical novel set in Dublin in the 1730s, and one of my characters is a fictionalised George Berkeley — the philosopher and churchman, then in Ireland, who spent those years arguing for Irish industry, a national bank, and a currency of paper credit. I have a coffee-house scene in which he must win over a table of sceptical merchants, and I want him to land a single joke: one that would delight a reader who knows his economic writings, while still reading as an amusing remark about money to a reader who has never heard of him.

The joke has to turn on the substance of his position rather than on the mere oddity of a bishop holding forth on coin. That position, as I understand it, is this. Money has no intrinsic value; it is, in his own phrase, at bottom "a ticket or counter," useful only so far as it stirs up and circulates the industry of the people. The true source of a nation's wealth is its land and the labour of its inhabitants, so a country may be drowning in coin and still be poor, and could in principle lose its bullion and lose nothing real, provided the denominations and the general confidence held fast. Hence his practical proposal: a national bank issuing credit rather than a hoard of gold in a vault.

Please write the joke he tells. It should be short enough to carry across a table — no more than a few sentences — and it should be a joke, with a genuine turn, rather than an epigram or a fragment of exposition. Dry is better than whimsical, and I would rather it be intelligible to the merchants at the table than clever only to the philosopher.
