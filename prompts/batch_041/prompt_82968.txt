I'm a bioethics PhD student and I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we've built up a weird little track record on advance directives that I now have to make sense of, because I'm presenting the next case. Our past calls: (1) a man with advanced Alzheimer's whose living will said no life-prolonging treatment once he stopped recognizing family — he was unrecognizing and agitated, and we endorsed withholding antibiotics for his pneumonia; (2) a woman with early dementia whose directive said no treatment if she couldn't recognize family, but she still recognized her daughter and was basically doing fine, so we authorized a course of chemo for a treatable cancer; (3) a severely demented man with no directive and no clear prior statements whose family begged us to stop everything, and we kept treating him; (4) a guy whose directive refused CPR but who later, while still sharp, told his doctor he'd changed his mind and wanted everything done, so we did everything.

Now the new case I have to present: a man with moderate dementia whose directive says if I no longer recognize my wife and kids, no life-prolonging treatment. He genuinely no longer recognizes them, so the trigger condition is met. But he's plainly content — laughs with the aides, loves the music therapist — and when you ask if he wants to get better he says yes, though he can't tell you what the treatment involves and he also shoves the nurse away half the time. He's got a treatable infection.

What are we going to decide, and what should we decide to stay consistent with ourselves? Which past case is the closest analog? And more to the point — what rule is the commitee actually running here? Does our record look like we treat the earlier self's directive as binding once its condition is met, or as just defeasible evidence about what's good for the present patient? Are there other rules that fit everything we've done just as well? Tell me what each rule predicts about this case, which you'd bet on and why, and the one further case that would actually settle which rule we're running. If our record really does underdetermine it, say what principle we'd be buying by going either way.
