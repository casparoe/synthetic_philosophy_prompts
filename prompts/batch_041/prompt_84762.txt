I am a psychometrician by trade: I build and validate instruments for a living, and my knowledge of the philosophy of mind is close to nil. My firm has been retained to say whether a conversational AI system should be described as having a mind, and my working suspicion is that the long-running philosophical dispute over behavioral criteria for mindedness is a dispute about test validity that has been conducted without the apparatus my field has spent seventy years developing. I would like the translation done properly, and then I would like to be told what the translation fails to carry.

Hold me to my own vocabulary, which is the target language for this exercise: operational definitions and test operations; construct validity in the sense that Cronbach and Meehl gave the term, where a construct is whatever occupies a place in a nomological network of lawlike relations rather than whatever a single test operation picks out; convergent and discriminant evidence; criterion contamination; the two canonical ways an instrument can be invalid, namely leaving part of the construct unmeasured and measuring something extraneous alongside it; the distinction between an instrument that predicts an outcome and an instrument that measures a construct; measurement invariance across populations; and the difference between inference about a distribution and inference about one case.

Restate each of the following positions in that vocabulary, one at a time and as faithfully as you can. (a) The claim that a system which cannot be distinguished from a human being in conversation thereby has a mind. (b) The stronger and more austere behaviorism according to which mental terms simply mean dispositions to behave, so that the question of an inner state is not merely unanswerable but empty. (c) The classic psychometric rejoinder that a construct is constituted by its position in a nomological network, so that behavioral criteria are evidence for a theoretical entity rather than a definition of it. (d) The objection that a system could pass every conversational test by shuffling symbols according to rules, with nothing we would call understanding occurring anywhere. (e) The intuition that a system could be behaviorally and functionally indistinguishable from a person and still have nothing going on inside it. (f) The deflationary reply that this last objection demands evidence which could not in principle be obtained, and that the demand is therefore not a requirement on a theory but a prejudice in favor of one.

For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Is the residue a defect of my framework, something about minds that psychometrics is structurally unable to represent, and if so what would have to be added to it? Or is it a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked? Three questions in particular. First, does the intuition that behavior could be perfect while nothing is going on inside have any counterpart in a framework that already distinguishes an instrument which predicts from an instrument which measures, or does that distinction turn out to be doing none of the work the philosophers want done? Second, is the question whether this one system has a mind even the kind of question my discipline is built to answer, given that its constructs are properties of populations and its evidence is aggregate? Third, does the nomological-network rejoinder actually dissolve the behavioral criterion, or does it merely postpone it by relocating the criterion to the whole network at once? Do not tell me which position is true. Tell me which of them my framework can state, which it can only approximate, and which it cannot hear at all.
