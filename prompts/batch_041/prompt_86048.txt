I'm a software engineer on the risk analytics team at a mid-size consumer lender, and I've been handed an odd assignment: build the fairness review that every credit model has to clear before it goes to production. My background is distributed systems and a little applied stats; I have no formal training in philosophy, and what I know about the fairness literature I've picked up from blog posts and a few papers I half-understood. I know enough to know that the usual definitions — demographic parity, error-rate balance, calibration, individual-similarity approaches — conflict, and that there are theorems showing some of them cannot all hold at once. What I want from you is not a summary of those theorems but the specification underneath them: the list of requirements that any fairness criterion has to meet to be usable in our setting, with the concrete case behind each requirement, so that when a vendor hands me a metric I can say what it does and doesn't deliver rather than just running it. Please don't send me citations or a reading list; I won't be able to chase papers down, and what I need is the criteria themselves, argued out in plain prose.

Our setting puts some pressure on the standard list, and I'd like you to treat these as part of the specification rather than as background. We only observe repayment for applicants we approved, and the missingness is driven by the decisions of the model we're replacing, so any criterion that needs to know what rejected applicants would have done is not something I can actually compute. Race and ethnicity are unobserved for most applicants — we have self-reports for a subset and a surname-plus-geography imputation for the rest, so a criterion has to be well-defined when the group variable is itself measured with error. Our scores are continuous and our cutoffs move for business reasons, and we retrain quarterly, so a verdict that flips when I change the threshold or rebin the score would be useless to me. The protected group we treat as the baseline is also a choice I make, and it shouldn't be the thing that determines the answer. Our legal reviewers, meanwhile, care about a distinction I only partly grasp — between a policy that treats people differently on purpose and a facially neutral one with an unjustified disparate effect, where a business-necessity justification is allowed — and I'd like the list to respect whatever that distinction actually imposes. And the committee that reviews these models wants a single verdict per model, decided in-house by the vendor's own team, so a criterion that can be satisfied by cosmetic post-hoc tweaking without changing anything real is worse than useless to me.

So: give me the desiderata. For each requirement, tell me what it demands and the scenario that motivates it — the case that would come out wrong if we violated it — and then tell me which of these are genuine requirements of any adequate criterion and which are really just the commitments of one camp dressed up as requirements. The part I care about most is the impossibility: if some subset of the list can't be jointly satisfied, say exactly which subset and what the impossibility turns on, because that tells me which dial I'm actually turning when I relax something. Then commit. Don't hand me the menu and leave the choice to me — tell me which single requirement you'd give up first in our situation, which criterion you'd actually put in the review, and what you'd be betting against by choosing it.
