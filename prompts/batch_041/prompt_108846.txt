My buddy won't shut up about coherent extrapolated volition. You know, the idea that what you "really" want is what you'd want if you knew everything, thought infinitely fast, were more coherent, etc. He's definately into it as the key to aligning AI. But he gave me this argument and now I think it's either empty or creepy. Here it is:

1. Your real preferences are the ones you'd have under idealization.
2. Idealization means being fully informed, perfectly coherent, and free of bias.
3. Under those conditions, you'd want whatever is actually best for you.
4. So your real preferences are whatever is actually best for you.
5. But "actually best for you" is just whatever an ideal advisor would want for you.
6. An ideal advisor is just you idealized.
7. So (4) collapses back into (1), and the theory is circular.
8. If instead idealization changes your values, then your "real" preferences might be ones you'd now reject.
9. If it doesn't change your values, then it's just your current preferences with more information.
10. So the theory is either circular, alienating, or trivial.

He says 8 and 9 are a false dilemma. I say he needs to pick one. Then he said something that really got me: if the idealized version of me would want to torture puppies, and I'd endorse that after idealization, then that's what I really want. I said thats insane — that's not me, that's a different person. He said I'm just clinging to my current self. Which step is wrong? What does it cost to reject it? I want to keep the idea that there's a difference between what I happen to want and what I really want — otherwise I don't know what all this idealization talk is for. But I don't want to end up saying some version of me that I'd hate is the real me. Is there a way between the horns? If not, which horn is less bad?
