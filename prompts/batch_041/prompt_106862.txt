I'm giveing a talk in two weeks to a game theory seminar -- econ department, mostly mechanism design and contract theory people, plus two algorithmic game theorists who build CFR bots for poker, and one philosopher of econ who's a card-carrying sceptic about idealizing agents as logically omniscient. I'm defending something like this: for one-shot interactions between AI systems that can read each others source code before they act, program equilibrium is the right solution concept and Nash is a modelling error.

The gist of my argument: take a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where each player's move is chosen by a program, and each program gets to look at the other's source before it outputs C or D. Then you can write a program that cooperates just in case it can verify that the other program, faced with this very program, outputs C -- and mutual cooperation becomes stable with no repetition, no reputation, no contracts, no cheap talk. I claim this generalizes, and that it should be the default story about why we'd expect AI systems to cooperate even when the underlying game is a one-shot PD. The objection I keep half-expecting is that this is just Kant's categorical imperative / superrationality with extra steps.

Give me the Q&A prep: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do to the thesis. For each one, a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what the single reply is that has to land for the thesis to survive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Don't bother with the obvious repeated-game / evolutionary stuff -- this crowd knows it cold and won't be impressed by it.
