Background: I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and two people in my cohort have been going at each other for months. One is a hardline Popperian — for her a theory is scientific just in case it forbids some observable states of affairs, and the people who hold it are willing to state in advance what would make them give it up. The other is a Bayesian — for him "scientific" is at best a cluster concept that tracks nothing deep, and what actually matters is whether a community's methods reliably raise the posterior probability of the hypotheses it accepts; the whole demarcation question, he thinks, is a leftover of a positivist project that quietly died. They've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration (my idea — I'm refereeing), and I want you to draft the joint statement they could both put their names to. Take the role of a philosopher of science who's spent a career on demarcation and confirmation theory, and think carefully before you write: start by brainstorming what each side is actually committed to, what's a matter of wording versus substance, and where the real live crux sits.

What I want in the document: (1) a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — about why the demarcation problem arose, about what any criterion would have to do, about which cases are genuinely uncontroversial, and about what their own preferred criterion conspicuously does not deliver. Make these substantive; "science is hard to define" is not a claim. (2) The precise points of disagreement, each phrased the way its own side would want it phrased — the Popperian's own formulation of her position and the Bayesian's own formulation of his, not a neutral mush that either would reject. (3) The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one is whether falsifiable is a property of the logical form of a theory or of the behavior of a community, and another is whether a novel prediction carries evidential weight that mere accommodation of already-known data lacks. (4) For each crux, name a concrete case or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and write down what each side predicts about that case, in advance.

Two things to avoid. Don't reach for the tired examples — no Freud-and-Einstein, no is-Marxism-a-science, no "is astrology falsifiable." Everyone's seen those and both of them will just recite their side's talking points. And don't just hand me string theory as the hard case either; I've heard that one at three conferences this year. Instead work with this pair of cases I've been using. Case A: a unifying framework with very few free parameters that retrodicts a large and diverse body of already-known anomalies, but makes no new predictions anyone can test soon. Case B: a program that makes one sharp, risky, novel prediction that would kill it if it failed, but which explains almost nothing else and has no unifying power. Which of those does each of them have to call scientific, and does either criterion actually sort them the way its own side would want? One more thing I've been mulling: a criterion that says the unit of demarcation is the research program rather than the individual theory — a program counts as scientific if it has a track record of generating hypotheses that get tested and sometimes die. Would either of them sign that, and what would each say it gets wrong?

Also: if you think part of their fight is just verbal — say one is answering a descriptive question about how the word gets used while the other is answering a normative question about what we ought to treat as knowledge-producing — say so explicitly and say which part. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the Popperian's prediction, the Bayesian's prediction, and what both agree would count as the outcome. Keep it under about 1200 words, and write the document itself, not advice to me about how to write it.
