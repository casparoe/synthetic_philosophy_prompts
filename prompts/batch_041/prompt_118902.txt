I'm a junior taking a course on social choice and mechanism design, and I also serve as parliamentarian of our student government's rules committee, which is midway through rewriting the bylaws for how we elect the council chair and how the council votes on budget amendments. Last term we replaced a plurality rule with instant-runoff voting, and a faction now wants to go further — either to score voting or to a Condorcet method — on the theory that our current rule is manipulable and that a sufficiently well-chosen alternative would not be. In lecture we covered the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, presented as the strategic-behavior counterpart to Arrow's impossibility theorem, and while I can follow the proof well enough, I have no clear picture of what it implies for someone actually drafting bylaws. A friend in the philosophy department keeps invoking Arrow to argue that any reform is pointless, and I suspect she is drawing the wrong lesson from the wrong theorem, but I want to be sure I understand why.

So please set out the most important implications of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — not a restatement of the result, but what actually follows from it. I would especially like the practical lessons separated from the overreach: what the theorem does and does not say about instant-runoff, Borda, approval, and score voting; why it is a claim about deterministic rules over an unrestricted domain of ordinal preferences, and what changes once you restrict the domain (say, to single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional budget), introduce randomization, shrink the choice set to two options, or weaken dominant-strategy incentive compatibility to a Bayesian notion of incentive compatibility; and whether those qualifications are genuine escapes from the result or merely relocations of the same problem. It would also help to have the standard misreadings identified, since I have heard classmates conclude that the theorem proves all voting is futile, and that strikes me as too quick. Finally, if any of the implications bear specifically on small bodies like ours — a dozen people who know one another's preferences and who vote together repeatedly on related questions, rather than a large anonymous electorate — I would like those flagged separately, since I imagine the theorem's assumptions look rather different at that scale.
