I retired from a chemistry department about four years ago and since then I've been basically giving myself a philosophy degree in my kitchen — ethics, phil of science, a bit of logic. A friend has now roped me into leading a session at our local adult-ed philosophy circle, and the topic I idiotically volunteered for is the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas, i.e. the Indian idea that there are only a handful of basic means of knowing. I have the rough shape of it — perception, inference, testimony, plus comparison, and then postulation and non-apprehension that some schools add on so you end up with six; I think Nyāya itself settles for four and the Buddhists cut it down to two, but don't trust my memory, tell me where I've got it wrong. What I don't have is any idea how to teach it, and I definately don't want to walk in and recite a list at people.

So don't explain the pramāṇas to me — plan how I should explain them.

The room: twelve retired scientists and engineers, no Sanskrit, no background in Indian philosophy, all fluent in statistics and allergic to the phrase valid cognition. 75 minutes, a whiteboard, and nobody will read anything beforehand, so please don't send me off to readings.

What I need:

- The order the ideas have to go in, and why each one has to land before the next can. My hunch is that the fatal mistake is opening with the list, so tell me what has to be in place before a list of anything is even intelligible, and what the one hinge idea is that the whole session turns on.
- The single example that should carry it, and what it has to have and not have. I've been toying with (i) a courtroom witness, (ii) me believing a number off a spectrometer, (iii) a nurse relaying what the doctor said. Pick one, or give me something better, and tell me why the obvious choice is probably a trap.
- The misconceptions they'll arrive with or pick up, where in the sequence each needs to be headed off, and roughly what to say. My guesses: pramāṇa gets heard as justified true belief; inference gets heard as deduction; testimony gets heard as hearsay and therefore obviously not knowledge; and the whole thing gets heard as religious apologetics. Add the ones I'm missing.
- The three contentious candidates. I gather comparison is the one modern Westerners choke on, and the stock illustration is being told some wild ox looks like a cow and then, on seeing one in the forest, knowing what it is. Postulation I've seen illustrated with a fat man who doesn't eat in the daytime, so he must eat at night. Non-apprehension with knowing there's no pot on the desk because you'd have seen it. Check those for me, tell me which will land and which will just derail the room, and whether the whole disagreement over how many pramāṇas there are should be mentioned at all or is just noise at this level.

Then the part I actually care about, because otherwise this is a museum tour. I want two twists built in, and I need you to work out where they go and what they presuppose — not to turn it into a lecture about AI:

(a) The instrument. In my old lab nobody ever saw a molecule. We saw a readout and we believed it, and we were right to. Is that perception, or is it testimony, as if the machine were a reliable speaker? Does the machine case slot neatly into the testimony category, or break it, or does the theory handle it better than I'd expect? Say what the room has to already accept for that question to even be askable, and where in the sequence it goes.

(b) The black box. Now suppose the reliable speaker is a trained model whose output is good but that nobody, its builders included, can explain. If the Nyāya line is that testimony counts as a basic source when it comes from someone reliable or trustworthy, does that give a usable test for when trusting such a model is knowing and when it's just deference — or does it show the old criterion quietly assumes something about the source that we've now lost? I don't need an answer, I need the plan: where it goes, what it hangs on, and what I should flatly refuse to open up in a 75-minute session.

Two last things: one question to put to the room at the very end that would actually tell me whether they've got the idea or just memorized some names; and what I should openly announce I'm skipping (I assume the fine-grained taxonomy of perception, the theory of error, and maybe the Buddhist reduction of testimony to inference — but tell me if I'm skipping the wrong things).
