Ok so I'm running a session on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the history one, and I want something better than a summary out of you — I want you to build me a case. Specs first.

One scenario, present day, a page at most, in which a single person has one decision in front of her, and all four of Nietzsche's attitudes to the past — monumental, antiquarian, critical, and the suprahistorical one he gestures at early and then drops, the art-and-religion stance that turns the gaze off the process and onto the eternal — land on different answers about what she should do. Thier answers have to fall out of the attitudes as he describes them, not be announced by me. If the case only works because I've stipulated "and the antiquarian would say don't touch it", its no good, I could've done that myself. Setting should be person-scale with real stakes — a lab, a family firm, a band, a farm — and please not a monument committee or a museum, and nothing with war or atrocity in it, those make the critical voice too easy.

Now the constraints that matter. The antiquarian has to be the one who saves her. I'm tired of the reading where antiquarian means nostalgia and critical means liberation; I want a case where reverencing and keeping the thing is the hard-headed live option and the critical voice is what would wreck her. Second, critical history can't be cheap: Nietzsche's point is that we're the continuation of what we criticise, she owes her standards and her habits to the very thing she'd be condemning, so whatever the critical attitude recommends has to be something she does to herself. Third, monumental has to be a mechanism rather than a motivational poster — show me how a past exemplar actually licenses her to act now, and how the same mechanism could lead her wrong. Fourth, the suprahistorical has to be genuinely available and genuinely tempting, and you have to say honestly why she can't take it, or what it costs if she does.

And make plastic power the pivot: her capacity to take the past into herself and grow is what should settle which attitude is right for her, so set the case up so that changing that one thing flips the verdict while everything else stays fixed, and show me the flip.

Then the walkthrough — for each of the four, the verdict and the reasoning. Tell me which feature of the case is actually doing the work, and whether removing it collapses things back into a plain keep-it-or-kill-it decision. And if someone objected that the whole thing cheats by building the right answer into her temperament, what exactly would that objection be and how would you answer it?

It's for a group that's read the essay twice and still treats the three modes as three moods, so the point is to force conflicting prescriptions out of one act.

Write it in continuous prose, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs like you're writing me an email. Start with the scenario itself and skip any throat-clearing about Nietzsche.
