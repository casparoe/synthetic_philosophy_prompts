I chair the exhibition content committee at our city's museum of world history. In eight weeks we open a permanent gallery whose working title is Collapse: When Civilizations Fall. A donor's gift names the space, and the gift agreement requires that the gallery be organized under a single organizing concept rather than as three loosely related rooms; the same gift pays for an interactive installation in which visitors choose policies for a fictional island and watch it fail, which our education team loves and our curators distrust. The three bays are the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui. Three things have now converged. The Rapa Nui descendant association has written to object to their island appearing under a collapse heading at all: they say the framing repeats an ecocide story their own advisers consider refuted by recent archaeological and genetic work, casts their ancestors as the authors of their own destruction, and turns a living people into an exhibit in someone else's morality tale; they have hinted that they may withdraw promised loans. Our education consultant reports that the word collapse maps onto our state's world-history standard in a way that transformation does not. And our archaeological consultant's report observes that at two of the three sites the occupational record continues across the supposed collapse horizon in the surrounding countryside even where the monumental cores were abandoned — what ended was a particular kind of thing: administrative and ceremonial centers, elite exchange networks, a monumental and scribal tradition. Not a landscape, and not a people. One committee member has already told me that a museum unwilling to say the word has no business warning anyone about anything, and I think he has a point that deserves an answer rather than a shrug.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our vote on the 14th. It should recommend a concrete course of action — keep the title and the organizing concept, rename, or restructure — together with an explicit definition of whatever concept we adopt and an explicit statement of what the gallery is and is not claiming. It should say whether the three cases can honestly sit under one heading and, if they can, what the heading must mean for that to be true; it should rule on the simulator; it should say what we owe the descendant association procedurally, whether co-authorship of the framing text, a right of reply, or a plain acknowledgement; and it should give a fallback if the loans are withdrawn. Distinguish the several things the word collapse is used to name — demographic decline, loss of political integration, the disappearance of a particular elite or institutional package, loss of overall social complexity — and say which of them our evidence supports at each site and which the gallery must not be read as implying. Include the four or five sentences of wall text that will state the gallery's framing, and a short timeline from the vote to the label-copy deadline. Be honest about which parts of the decision turn on evidence and which turn on values and whose values; state the strongest case for the position you reject, including my colleague's, and say what would change your mind. No citations and no scholarly names — the committee already has the site reports. I want to be able to read it aloud in ten minutes.
