I have no academic background in any of this. I have gambled on and off for years and worked the following out on my own, mostly on a spreadsheet, so take the framing as a layman's and correct it if it is wrong. What I keep circling is this: everyone tells you to bet in whatever way makes your average outcome largest, and I have never once met the average outcome. Take an even-money coin that lands my way six times in ten. Stake everything and my average wealth multiplies by 1.2 a toss, while the first toss that goes against me wipes me out. Stake half and the average still grows, by a tenth a toss, while the path I actually live goes down by about three percent a toss. Stake a fifth and the typical path gains about two percent a toss while the average gains four. Bigger stake, better average, worse life; and if I have the arithmetic right, anything above about two fifths goes down in the long run even though its average climbs the whole way. Someone told me this is what the Kelly criterion is about, and that the thing to maximize is a geometric mean rather than an arithmetic one, but I have only ever seen the difference written as an equation, and I think it is a thing you can feel.

What I want from you is a poem with that shape rather than that subject, and here is what I need from it.

Build it as a ladder of stakes: nothing, a fifth, half, everything, one stanza each, in that order, same coin, same six times in ten. Each of those four stanzas ends on a refrain, and the refrain is word-for-word identical every time. I want it to name a fraction of what is left rather than a sum of money, so that it can be the same sentence and never the same bet, since what is left is different every time it is said. The line I keep trying and failing to make work is a fifth of what is left; use it, replace it, or build your own, but the refrain has to be literally invariant while its meaning moves. I leave it to you whether the refrain names the stake the speaker keeps to or the stake he keeps failing to keep, but decide it on purpose.

Two voices, and neither one a fool. One counts every history at once and is right that the money grows; the other walks a single path. The counting voice must not be a straw man, because it is telling the truth, and the poem must not settle the argument between them. I want the last stanza to leave the arithmetic standing: to say plainly that across all the histories the money does grow, and then not take it back.

Give me numbers I can check. If a stanza's stake is half and the toss goes against the speaker, the next figure has to be the correct product, and the figures have to be the figures for that stake and that coin, not vague gestures at plenty and little. If a rhyme or a rhythm would cost me the arithmetic, lose the rhyme. Somewhere in it the poem should notice that a fifth of a small pot and a fifth of a large one are not the same bet even though they are the same fraction, and that what survives the sequence is the fraction and not the money.

No formulas on the page, no jargon like expected value, no logarithms, no names of anyone who wrote about any of this, and no citations or reading lists, since I would not know what to do with one. Twenty-five to thirty-five lines or so is fine; metre and rhyme are optional, accuracy and the two voices are not.

Finally, two things I want from you as a person rather than as a poet. If my premise is confused, say so plainly instead of writing me a poem anyway; if the typical path is not the right thing to point at, or if the average-versus-typical split cannot be carried by a poem at all and I should stop trying, tell me that. And if you do write it, end with a short note in your own voice about where the poem has to fudge or oversimplify: which lines lean on a contested way of talking about probability, where I am probably wrong about the mathematics, and what someone who knows this material properly would want to argue with. I would much rather be told my idea is muddled than be handed a poem that makes it sound settled.
