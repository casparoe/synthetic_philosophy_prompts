I'm writing a seminar paper on the School of Names, and I keep circling a comparison that I suspect is either genuinely illuminating or merely clever, and I would like you to help me decide which it is. My training is mostly in analytic philosophy of language, which is probably why the comparison tempts me. The text in question is the White Horse Dialogue in the Gongsun Longzi: one speaker defends the proposition that a white horse is not a horse, and he does it with two arguments that pull in different directions. The first concerns what names designate — horse is what designates shape, white is what designates colour, and since what designates colour is not what designates shape, the name white horse and the name horse do not designate the same thing. The second concerns seeking — if you are looking for a horse, a brown or black horse will satisfy you; if you are looking for a white horse, it will not; so the two requests are not answered by the same animals, and the two names therefore cannot be substituted for one another. The other speaker takes the claim in the ordinary way, that nothing which is a white horse fails to be a horse; and the usual modern diagnosis is that the dispute turns on the ambiguity of the negative copula, which can mean either is not identical with or is not a member of the class of. I should say that I have read the dialogue only in translation, and I am aware that classical Chinese has no articles, so I also want you to tell me whether the whole puzzle is best explained as an artefact of translating it into a language that has them, or whether that explanation is itself too quick.

What I want from you is a real comparison between this and the two Fregean instruments we would reach for in the West. The first is the distinction between sense and reference. The second is Frege's own difficulty about concepts: that as soon as one tries to say what a concept is, or to name it, one has made it into an object, so that by Frege's own lights the concept horse turns out not to be a concept — the awkwardness he conceded in the essay on concept and object. My working hunch, which I do not trust, is that the seeking argument is doing something close to the sense/reference work while the shape/colour argument is closer to the concept/object problem; I would like that mapping tested rather than confirmed. In particular: does either Fregean tool show the Chinese argument to be a verbal muddle that dissolves once the copula is disambiguated, or is the dialogue doing something the Fregean apparatus cannot state without distortion — something about names, ming, and the fixing of names to things, rather than about the truth of propositions? If the two Fregean tools return different verdicts on the same argument, I want to know that too, and I want to know which of them is doing real work and which is being flattered by the comparison. The same text has a companion dialogue on hardness and whiteness, where the hardness and the whiteness of a single stone are argued to be separate rather than one; bring it in only if it genuinely bears on the verdict.

Please commit to a view rather than surveying the interpretive options, and finish by telling me what would change your mind: which philological detail, counterexample, or case in which the Fregean tool yields an obviously wrong verdict on the Chinese argument would push you to the opposite conclusion, and where in your reconstruction you are most likely to be imposing a Western shape on the text.
