I'm a retired organic chemist — 34 years in industry, mostly catalysts — and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, mostly the older stuff. Pascal has me stuck. I think he's right that justice without force is helpless and force without justice is bullying, but I can't tell what principle he'd use to separate actual cases, and when I write down my own verdicts they don't line up either. Can you tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures the pattern below? State it precisely enough that I can apply it to a new case, say which case is doing the work in each clause, and be honest about which verdict I'd have to give up.

Here's what I think, going by gut:
- A rule that says drive on the right side of the road: just, even though it's arbitrary. If you drive on the left you should be forced to pay a fine.
- A law that says Black kids can't attend the same school as white kids: unjust, and using police to enforce it is not just force, it's tyranny. I don't care that it was established custom in some places.
- A tax that funds public schools: just, and I think the IRS can force me to pay it.
- A vigilante who shoots a known murderer who got off on a technicality: not just. He should be prosecuted, even if the murderer was guilty.
- A revolutionary group that overthrows a segregationist regime and jails its leaders: I think that can be just, even though it's force outside the law.
- teh World Court issuing a ruling against a country that ignores it: I think the ruling is just but powerless. I still call it law, sort of.
- A parent forcing a kid to take an antibiotic: legitimate force, I think, even if the kid refuses.
- A mob blocking a highway to demand a police shooting be investigated: I'm torn. Part of me thinks it's unjust force, part of me thinks it's the only force they have.

The pair that really gets me is the vigilante and the revolutionary. Both use force outside the legal system against people they think are guilty. I approve one and not the other. Maybe it's about whether you're acting as an individual or as part of a movement that could become a government? But that sounds like might makes right, which I don't like. Or maybe it's about due process: the revolutionary is trying to build a system, the vigilante is bypassing one. But the regime in the segregation case is itself the system, so what does due process even mean there?

What I want is a principle that gets as many of these as possible, with the cases I haven't listed filled in. What does it say about a foreign army invading a country to stop a genocide that the UN won't stop? Is that just force or tyranny or something else? And if the principle can't save all my verdicts, which one should I drop? I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a principle that's just gerrymandered to fit my prejudices.

I guess what I'm really asking is whether Pascal had a principled way to tell just force from mere force, or whether he thought it's all custom and power and we should stop pretending otherwise. If it's the latter, which of my verdicts is the pretense?
