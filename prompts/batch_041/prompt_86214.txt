I'm a novelist — two thirds through a book whose two leads are a married pair of decision theorists, and the whole saggy middle of it is them re-litigating the two-envelope paradox at other people's dinner parties, so before I write the last act I need to actually know what the argument is. Here's the setup I've given them: the host (a retired actuary, naturally) fills two envelopes, one holding twice the other, and hands you one at random. In the scene's first pass he's rolled a fair die for the exponent, so the amounts are 2 to the n and 2 to the n+1 with n equally likely to be 1 through 6, and you may open your envelope and then decide whether to swap. In the second pass he tells you only that every value of n was equally likely — no ceiling, no die, no process. My character Ines thinks the paradox is a bookkeeping error: it only ever shows up when the host's procedure is left vague or when you help yourself to a prior that doesn't add up to anything, and the moment you say what the process was, the always-swap reasoning dies of its own accord — so decision theory is innocent and the lesson is hygiene. Her husband thinks that's a dodge, and that the idealised no-ceiling version is a perfectly legitimate thing to reason about, and that what it exposes isn't a slip in the reasoning but the fact that the rational choice can be genuinely undefined — that there are setups where expected-value maximisation returns no verdict at all, and we should stop pretending the framework always has one.
