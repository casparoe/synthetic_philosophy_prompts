I am a novelist at work on a book about a sixteenth-century French expedition and its encounter with a people whose funeral rites involve eating a portion of their dead, and I have been reading Montaigne's essay on the cannibals alongside the early chapters of my draft — the passage in which he suggests that we cry barbarism at whatever is not our own custom, and seems to admire the very people his contemporaries would have despised. At dinner last week a friend of mine, a philosopher manqué who has read just enough to be dangerous, twice used the proverb 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do' to shut down an argument I was trying to have with myself. The first time, when I said I was unsure whether my narrator — a guest, and a foreigner — is entitled to call the rite degrading, he said: when in Rome, do as the Romans do. The second time, when I said I thought the narrator ought to keep his distance from the rite, he used the identical sentence to say the opposite, that the narrator should join in. I am fairly sure one proverb cannot be doing both jobs, and I would like to know what it is actually doing when it is doing anything at all.

Please lay out for me the distinct precise claims that 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do' could be making. I want a menu rather than a verdict, running from the nearly empty to the genuinely radical: the readings that are mere advice about manners or prudence, the readings that are claims about what it is permissible to criticise, the readings that are claims about the limits of a stranger's understanding, and the readings that are full-blooded theses about the truth of moral judgments, including any that quietly contradict one another. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a defensible and substantive position, or simply false, and tell me who, if anyone, would have reason to hold it. Then say which reading my friend most plausibly needed for each of his two moves, and whether the same reading can bear both — in particular, whether anything in the proverb licenses the slide from 'you are in no position to condemn this' to 'you are obliged to take part in it', or whether that slide is his own invention rather than the proverb's fault. If it helps, treat my narrator as a test case: he is a guest who finds the rite both repellent and, in a way he cannot articulate, admirable.

Begin your reply with your single-sentence verdict on which reading the proverb is most plausibly doing the work of in my friend's mouth, and then give the reasoning and the menu, without preamble and without restating my question.
