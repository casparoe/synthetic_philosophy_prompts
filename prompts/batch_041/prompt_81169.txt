I am a high-school student enrolled in an elective on cognition, and for the past two weeks our class has been living inside a puzzle world that our teacher calls a microdomain. The puzzle is the old one: abc becomes abd, so what does ijk become? Our class split over whether the answer is ijl, ijd, ijk itself, or something stranger, and the teacher refused to settle it, insisting that the whole interest of the exercise lies in the argument rather than the answer — that the relation between the two letter strings is not a fixed rule waiting to be read off, but something the solver constructs, and constructs more or less radically, so that treating the change as advancing the last letter and treating it as making the last letter a d are both answers, ranked by how much the original pair must be reinterpreted to accommodate them. She then told us that a computer program built on this idea, called Copycat, does not output a single answer at all: it keeps many competing interpretations alive at once and settles on one, with something like a temperature that rises when nothing fits and makes wilder reinterpretations more likely. I find this picture of concepts as fluid and slippable genuinely appealing, but it is also precisely the picture my logic class tells me to be suspicious of.

For in logic we learned the opposite lesson under the name of equivocation: when a word quietly shifts its meaning between the premises and the conclusion, the argument is invalid, and the shift is not deep or creative but simply a mistake — a failure to hold the term fixed. I would like you to compare these two attitudes toward conceptual slippage, slippage as the engine of analogy and concept formation versus slippage as a fallacy to be policed, and to tell me whether they are genuinely opposed or whether the second is really the first with a stopping rule attached.

In particular, I would like three things. First, on the fluid view, what licenses one reinterpretation over another? If the licence is just that the analogy eventually pays off, is that not circular, since one cannot know it pays off until one has already made the shift? Second, does the cost structure inside something like Copycat, where radical shifts are penalised by a rising temperature and by the destruction of earlier structures, amount to a principled rule for distinguishing productive slippage from equivocation, or is it merely a heuristic that happens to work in toy worlds? Third, and most importantly, which of the two attitudes better describes what people are actually doing when they reason well — for instance when a lawyer extends a statute to a case its drafters never imagined, or when a mathematician generalises a theorem by loosening a definition? Please use the letter-string puzzle together with at least one such real case to test whichever position you end up defending, and be explicit about where the comparison between the two attitudes breaks down.
