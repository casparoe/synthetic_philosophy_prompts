I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosphy of biology, and I've somehow been volunteered into organizing the fiction night for our reading group — last term we did Ted Chiang, someone keeps pushing for Lem, and this term the theme is "reasons without reasoners," which I'm apparently now in charge of making feel alive for people who mostly read journals. The idea I'm circling is that you can describe a behavior as being *for the sake of* something — a bee dancing in order to point its hive-mates at nectar, a bacterium tumbling in order to get to higher sugar — even when there's nobody who has that reason, considers it, or could be wrong about it. I think the phrase for this is Dennett's, "free-floating rationale," though I've read enough secondary stuff about it to be dangerous and not much of the primary stuff, so don't lean on the label. What I actually want is fiction that makes the weirdness palpable rather than fiction that illustrates a thesis.

So: write me a short story, maybe 1500-2500 words. I've got a setting in mind and you can take it or swap in something better — a field station on an invented island, where a grad student is studying an invented moth that lays its eggs on a particular glossy synthetic panel the station put up years ago for an unrelated project, because the panel happens to reflect UV the way the moth's host plant did before a blight wiped the plant out. So the moths are doing something that is reliably, catastrophically futile: the larvae hatch, find nothing, starve. The advisor wants to write in the discussion section that the moths are "making a mistake"; the student's co-author says a mistake requires something that takes the world to be a certain way, and a mechanism that just fires doesn't take anything to be anything. I want that tension to be the spine of the story without anyone ever delivering a lecture about it — let the student's own life (their thesis, their model organism, whatever you invent) echo it if that falls out naturally. Invent the characters, the island, the species, the weather. No citations, no footnotes, no named philosophers in the text, and please don't end with someone explaining the point at a whiteboard. I'd rather it be a little eerie than cute, and I want a reader who's never heard the term to come away feeling the problem in their gut.
