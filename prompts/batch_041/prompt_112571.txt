Two colleagues in my department argued at lunch about a case in the local paper. A man died after a heart attack because the town's only ambulance was out of service, and the ambulance was out of service because the mechanic whose job it was to service it that morning had gone home early. Neither colleague's position is mine, and I'd like a straight adjudication of the exchange rather than a verdict on the mechanic's character. It went like this.

(1) Rosa: the mechanic caused the man's death. Had the service been done, the ambulance would have run, and that is ordinary counterfactual causation.

(2) Dev: the mechanic caused nothing. He failed to prevent a death, and failing to prevent is a different relation from causing; moreover a non-event has no properties and so cannot stand in a causal relation at all.

(3) Rosa: by that standard I do not cause you to be late when I quietly unplug your alarm clock; I merely fail to leave it plugged in. Nobody thinks that description exonerates me, so the act/omission line cannot mark a difference in causal relation.

(4) Dev: if counterfactual dependence is the test, then the dispatcher who declined to call the neighbouring town also caused the death, and so did the council that never funded a second ambulance, and so did every resident who never volunteered to drive. He then offered a case of his own: two rangers each skip an assigned patrol, the hiker either would have found dies, and each can say his skipping made no difference, since the other's patrol would have found her. On Rosa's test, Dev said, neither omission caused the death, which is absurd, so the test cannot be doing the work she wants.

(5) Rosa: two fires, each of which would have burned the house down, are a familiar problem for counterfactual theories of any kind, so Dev's case is not about omissions; and what separates the mechanic from the council is that the mechanic's omission violated a definite expectation attached to his role — the same way we say the spark caused the fire and not the oxygen.

(6) Dev: that concedes the point. If singling out the cause requires a norm or an expectation, then the claim that the mechanic caused the death is a fact about us rather than about the world, and Rosa cannot have it both ways.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why, identify who is right about what, and finish by telling me what both are missing — in particular, whether the need for a norm to single out the mechanic shows that omissions do not cause, or that causal selection is interest-relative in general, or neither.
