I am a novelist at work on a book about the 2008 financial crisis, and I have reached the point where the intellectual drama matters more to me than the plot. I want to understand the crisis as a case study in the methodology of idealization, specifically through Milton Friedman's F-twist -- the thesis that a theory's assumptions need not be realistic, and that the only test of a theory is the accuracy of its predictions. My characters are quants and economists who believed they were doing exactly what Friedman licensed: using tractable, admittedly false assumptions (continuous trading, rational agents, stable correlations, normally distributed returns, a representative agent) to generate predictions that were good enough to trade on. The episode I want analyzed is not the whole crisis but the specific failure of the models that priced mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations: the assumption that national housing prices would not fall together, the treatment of default correlations as stable and inferable from history, the AAA ratings that followed, and the way the models' predictions failed precisely when the instruments became systemically important.

What I want from you is a disciplined mapping in both directions. First, take the F-twist seriously and say what it implies about this episode: if prediction is the sole criterion, which of these idealizations were vindicated before 2007, and does the crisis count as a decisive predictive failure or merely as an out-of-sample event that no theory could have been expected to capture? Then turn the lens around. Does the episode support Friedman, refute him, or show that the F-twist was never the right description of what the modelers were doing? I am particularly interested in a distinction I have been trying to make in my own notes: between what I call Galilean idealization -- a deliberate, controlled distortion that isolates a causal factor and is accompanied by some account of how the distortion matters -- and what I call caricature, where the distortion is load-bearing, unacknowledged, and does the explanatory work while being presented as mere simplification. Which of the assumptions above fall on which side, and does the F-twist have the resources to mark the difference? I also want the Lucas critique brought in, since it seems to say that assumptions about behavior must be invariant to the policy regime, which is a stronger demand than predictive accuracy; say whether the crisis is better understood as a failure of the F-twist or as a vindication of the Lucas critique.

Finally, tell me what the episode shows about idealization itself. Did the crisis reveal a limit to the F-twist, or only a limit to a particular set of models? Is there a principled way to decide when an unrealistic assumption is acceptable and when it is dangerous, or does the answer depend on the purposes -- prediction, explanation, policy, fiction -- for which the model is used? And because I am writing a novel, I would like you to end by saying what a novelist can legitimately take from this episode that an economist cannot: whether the failure was one of theory, of institutions, of incentives, or of a certain kind of imagination, and which of those the F-twist is blind to.
