I'm in a reading group that's grinding through Popper on historicism, and last week I tried to write out the argument for "there are no laws of history" in my own words, tightly enough that my flatmate (an economist, obviously) couldn't wriggle out of it. This is what I came up with:

1. A law is a universal generalisation: whenever conditions of kind C obtain, an outcome of kind E follows. No exceptions, and no restriction to a particular time or place.
2. History is the study of a single sequence of events — what actually happened, once, in this world.
3. A generalisation that holds only of a single sequence can't be tested against anything else, so at best it summarises that sequence; it can't tell us what would have happened had things gone differently.
4. A generalisation that does cover other cases — other societies, other famines, other inflations — is a law of economics or demography or psychology applied to history, not a law of history.
5. So every generalisation a historian actually uses is either borrowed from another discipline or a mere summary of the one sequence. Nothing is a law of history.
6. So there is no science of the historical process as such. You can explain bits of the past using laws from elsewhere, but the course of history isn't law-governed.

My flatmate says its a machine for proving too much. He calls sciences whose subject matter has exactly one instance "singleton sciences", and says (3) would show that none of them has laws: there's one universe, so no laws of cosmology; one origin of life, so nothing lawful to say about how it actually got going; one Earth, so geology's out. He also says (4) is just bookkeeping — laws about societies and famines are laws about historical events, so calling them "not laws of history" is a filing decision rather than a finding. I half think hes right, and half think he's only noticed that the argument is valid.

What I actually want to keep is the prediction claim: you can't forecast the overall course of history, partly because forecasts about society feed back into what happens, and partly because you can't predict what we'll end up knowing. So — which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost? If I ditch (3) or (4), can I still get the prediction claim, or does the replacement cost more than the original did? And if I keep the whole thing and just shrug — "no laws of history, but plenty of laws in history" — what have I actually conceded?

Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and which bit of it you're least sure of. I'd rather have an honest wobble than a tidy answer.
