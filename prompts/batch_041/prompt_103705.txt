I have been trying to separate two questions that discussions of Hiroshima and Nagasaki tend to run together, and I would like you to help me pull them apart. The first is historical: would Japan have surrendered without the bombings? The second is normative: granted whatever the facts were, was the decision justified? My suspicion is that much of the disagreement is really about the second question — about the correct standard for justifying an act done under uncertainty — and that people litigate the first because the second is harder. I want you to analyze the decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki through a framework I will specify, rather than through the standard just-war categories of discrimination and proportionality.

The framework is the ethics of choice under deep uncertainty. Treat the decision as made under uncertainty in the strict sense: not risk, where probabilities are known or estimable, but a situation in which the relevant probabilities — that Japan would have surrendered without the bombings, without the Soviet declaration of war, after a demonstration on an uninhabited area, or after a guarantee for the emperor — were not merely unknown but perhaps not well-defined. Consider four decision rules: expected-value maximization using the best available estimates; maximin, which selects the option whose worst outcome is least bad; maximax, or best-case reasoning; and a lexical or precautionary rule that forbids certain acts regardless of their expected consequences. Throughout, distinguish the ex ante perspective, what the decision looked like to the actors, from the ex post perspective, what we now know about how it turned out. Add one criterion of my own, which I will call counterfactual robustness: the claim that an act is permissible only if it remains justified across a reasonable range of the plausible counterfactual scenarios, not merely under the single scenario that in fact obtained.

Then work through the following, in order. First, map the decision as the actors faced it: who the effective decision-maker was, what the live options were — invasion, blockade, continued conventional bombing, a demonstration, a negotiated surrender, the two-bomb sequence, and whatever else belongs on the list — what the payoffs were taken to be, and which quantities were genuinely unknown rather than merely disputed. Second, say what each decision rule prescribes and whether the actual decision survives under any of them. Third, tell me what the framework explains about the episode and what it misdescribes: in particular, whether it can represent the difference between intending to kill civilians and merely foreseeing their deaths, and whether it can represent the difference between the first bomb and the second, which many people find the harder case because the demonstration had, in a sense, already occurred. Fourth, apply counterfactual robustness and say whether the bombings pass it; this is where I expect the analysis to bite, since the decision looks far better under the scenario in which Japan would not have surrendered and far worse under the scenario in which it would have. Fifth, and explicitly, say where the framework is silent — what about the episode it cannot address at all, and whether that silence is a defect of the framework or evidence that the question was never purely a matter of decision theory.

Finally, take a definite position on the question I began with: is the disagreement between defenders and critics of the bombings substantially a disagreement about the counterfactual facts or about the correct decision rule? And, on the rule you think is right, were the bombings justified? Give me a verdict rather than a survey; I would rather you commit and be wrong than lay out the options and leave me to choose. Do not lean on contested or obscure historical details — where a fact matters, state the version you are relying on and why.
