I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about a claim most conservationists would call monstrous: that destroying habitat is, on balance, good for wild animals. What I want from you is a dialogue between two people who both know the ecology and both take animal welfare seriously — not a conservationist arguing with an abolitionist, but two welfare-oriented thinkers who disagree about whether the net welfare of an ecosystem even has a sign, and about what follows if it does.

The first holds that for r-selected species — the insects, the fish that release millions of eggs, the songbirds whose broods are mostly eaten before they fledge — the arithmetic of lives lived and deaths suffered comes out negative, so that replacing a rainforest with pasture, and a vast multitude of brief and violent lives with a smaller number of longer ones, may be a welfare improvement even while it is an ecological catastrophe. The second holds that this rests on a comparison no one can make: that we cannot put a mayfly's life and an elephant's on the same scale, that many wild animals plausibly live lives worth living, and that the argument proves too much, since the same arithmetic would license killing the survivors and then the last animal.

Have them press each other on the hard cases: species that go extinct and cannot return, animals whose habitat is destroyed but who die slowly, whether what remains of a habitat can support more individuals rather than fewer, and whether comparing lives that were lived with lives that were never lived is even coherent. Both should be equally competent, each should answer the other's strongest point rather than a caricature of it, and neither should win by outrage. Close by naming what each has had to concede.
