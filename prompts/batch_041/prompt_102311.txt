I run trust and safety for a mid-sized online community — thirty-odd flagged cases a week, I'm the one who signs off whenever a case ends in a ban or a removal, and the moderators who do the first pass are volunteers with no training in any of this. My problem is the rulebook. Half of it is arbitrary (we have a 'no links to competitor sites' rule that exists because of one argument three years ago and nobody can actually justify it), and I've got two seperate failure modes that both look bad. If I apply a rule literally to someone the community likes, I look like a tyrant. If I wave it through because 'it's only a convention anyway', the rule quietly dies and the next moderator inherits the mess. I've been reading the bits in Pascal's Pensées about justice and force — justice without force being powerless, force without justice being tyranny, and the darker point underneath, that justice is the thing everyone argues about while force is the thing everyone can see, so we end up treating whatever is established as just, and the authority of the rule comes from its being established rather than from any reason we could give for it. I want to turn that into something my moderators can actually run on a case in ten minutes.

Please write me a decision procedure — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with 'enforce' or 'let it go', and where it hands the decision back to me because it's a values question dressed up as a factual one. Two things I care about. First, don't let it collapse into a bland 'weigh the rule against the harm' test; the whole point of the view is that the rule's authority is its establishment and not its justifiability, so the procedure has to say something about when it is and isn't appropriate to litigate the rule's rationale at all — and it has to be honest that Pascal isn't handing us a way to make arbitrary rules just, only a way of living with them. Second, keep what's genuinely Pascal in it and drop what won't survive the trip to a volunteer moderator.

Then run it on three cases: (a) the long-standing arbitrary rule, applied to a first-time offender who is otherwise the most helpful person in the community; (b) something genuinely awful that no rule covers, where everyone is demanding we do something; (c) a rule that's plainly just, that we have no power to enforce against a user who will simply make a new account.

Give it to me as structured data. Table one, the procedure, with columns: step number | the question | what counts as a sufficient answer | what to do if the answer is no | who decides (moderator or me). Table two, the three cases, with columns: case | verdict the procedure gives | which step is doing the work | where it nearly misfires | what it leaves unsettled. Table three, the limits, with columns: limitation | why it can't be fixed | what to do instead. Keep the prose to a couple of sentences above each table and nothing else.
