Experimental philosophy settles questions about concepts by putting short vignettes to laypeople and asking whether the agent in the story did something intentionally, knew something, caused something, or acted freely, and then treating the majority verdict as evidence about the content of the concept. Its best-known result is an asymmetry in side-effect cases: a chairman who is indifferent to the environment and orders a program he knows will pollute is judged to have harmed the environment intentionally, while a chairman described in exactly the same terms except that the program will instead improve the environment is not judged to have helped it intentionally -- even though both men foresaw the side effect, caused it, and cared about it not at all. The same body of work that reports this asymmetry as a discovery about the concept of intentional action also debunks intuition elsewhere, showing that verdicts flip with the order in which cases are presented or with incidental details of wording, and it treats ordinary verdicts as the standard that philosophical theories of intentional action must answer to while treating the verdicts of philosophers as merely more data of the same kind.

I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes: what view of concepts, of the evidential status of intuition, and of the authority of ordinary usage it only makes sense under, and which rival views it quietly rules out. Answer as exactly five numbered claims, with no introduction and no closing summary, each one or two sentences, each naming one commitment the practice presupposes and saying whether the practice strictly requires it or would survive its denial; order them by how much of the practice would have to change if the commitment turned out to be false.
