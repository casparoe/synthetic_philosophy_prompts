I'm presenting a paper at our lab's alignment reading group in a fortnight, and the working title is deliberately combative: Debate Does Not Scale, It Only Moves the Hard Part Around. The thesis is that debate-style oversight — two models argue, a weaker judge rules — is not limited primarily by whether the judge can follow an argument, but by an asymmetry in who has to pay for verification. The honest debater must produce a case whose checking cost the judge can afford; the dishonest debater needs only a case whose refutation cost exceeds the judge's budget. I call this the verification asymmetry, and I distinguish three species of it. Computational obfuscation: the argument is in principle checkable, but only by redoing the original computation, so the judge's budget is the binding constraint. Definitional obfuscation: the dishonest debater keeps a load-bearing term under-specified, and the judge's ruling silently settles a question neither side argued. And what I'm calling rhetorical obfuscation, where an argument's persuasive force runs inversely to its auditability, so the honest debater is punished for being clear and the judge's confidence tracks the wrong signal. My claim is that only the first is really a compute-scaling story, and that the second and third are the ones that actually bite — which means the usual "wait for a stronger judge" response misses the point. I've been arguing about this with a colleague for months and I think the framing is right, but I haven't stress-tested it in front of people who don't already share my priors.

The audience is an awkward mix and I'd like the prep sheet calibrated to them specifically. Roughly half are empirical: two who have run preference-modeling pipelines and are allergic to any claim that doesn't cash out in a measurable failure mode; one who works on chain-of-thought faithfulness and will want to know exactly what the judge is stipulated to read and whether my "verification" is over the transcript or over the model's internals; and one who is fairly sure debate is just adversarial training with extra steps and that my three-way distinction collapses into a single robustness question. The other half: a Bayesian epistemologist who will ask what prior the judge holds over debaters, and whether my asymmetry is anything more than the familiar point that verification is hard under an adversarial model; a philosopher of language with strong views about under-specification, who will say that "definitional obfuscation" is either ordinary vagueness or plain equivocation and that I've smuggled a theory of meaning into what I'm presenting as a technical result; and one interpretability person who will insist that if we could read the judge's internal state directly, the whole problem evaporates. There are also two or three people who are simply there for the cookies and may ask whether any of this matters for systems that exist now.
