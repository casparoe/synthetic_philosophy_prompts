So I write about science and ideas for a living, which mostly means I stand around at parties while people who know more than me argue, and then I try to make it sound like I understood. Last week two friends of mine — both philosophy grad students, one working on truth, the other on knowledge — went at each other for about forty minutes and I couldnt follow half of it. I want you to settle it.

Roughly what happened.

Marcus started by laying out a proof he said kills a whole position. The position: for every truth there is, it's possible in principle for someone to know it — that's what makes something a truth, on this view, rather than something floating out there regardless of us. His proof: take any truth that nobody actually knows. "There are truths nobody knows" is surely true. Now consider the compound fact — [that truth], and nobody knows it. If everything is knowable, this compound is knowable; someone could know it. But knowing "P and nobody knows P" would require knowing P and knowing that nobody knows P — and if you know P, then somebody does know P, so the second half is false. So the compound can't be known. Contradiction. Therefore if every truth is knowable, there are no unknown truths — everything is already known. Which is ridiculous. So the "everything is knowable" thesis is false, and with it the whole idea that truth is tied to what we could know.

Ines said the proof is valid, she wasn't disputing the logic, but she said it proves almost nothing. What it shows is that one specific kind of proposition — the "P but nobody knows P" kind — can't be known. And that's a proposition that can't be known by its own nature: if you ever knew it, it would stop being true. Nobody ever thought the "everything is knowable" thesis was a promise that you could know self-undermining things. The thesis was about ordinary truths. So the paradox just tells you not to include the self-undermining propositions in the scope of the thesis, and once you don't, there's no paradox left.

Marcus said that's the oldest dodge there is. You can save any universal claim by carving out the instances that refute it. The thesis said every truth. The compound is a truth. If the thesis needs a footnote that says "except the truths that break it," it isn't a thesis, it's damage control. And he said it gets worse: once you only apply "everything is knowable" to the truths that can already be known, the claim is empty — "everything that can be known can be known." You've conceded the point, because the whole idea was that being knowable is what makes something true in the first place; if some truths are just structurally out of reach of knowledge, then truth is independent of us after all, which is exactly what the position denied.

Ines said that's not fair, because the excluded class has an independent description. There's a recognizable category of propositions that can't be truly believed or known because believing them makes them false — not just the "P and nobody knows P" ones, but things like "nothing has ever been believed," or "every belief I have is false" (when I'm having it). You can define that category without mentioning the paradox at all. So the restriction is principled, not gerymandered: every truth is knowable except the ones that are logically self-defeating to know. And she said the restriction still does work — it still rules out truths that are in principle beyond any possible knower for reasons that have nothing to do with self-defeat, like facts about a reality we could never in principle come into contact with. So it's not the empty slogan Marcus says it is.

Marcus came back with a case he said tests her. "There is at least one truth that no one will ever know." Is that a self-defeating thing to know? No — if some being knew it, it wouldn't turn false; knowing that there are unknown truths is perfectly coherent. So by Ines's own rule it's a legitimate candidate: it should be knowable. But is it? To know it, you'd have to be certain that no possible knower, ever, comes to know the truth in question. And that looks like something you could never be in a position to know — you'd have to survey every knower in all of history and all the future. So here's a truth that isn't self-defeating and yet seems in principle unknowable. Which sinks her restriction, not just the broad thesis.

Ines said that last step is doing something sneaky with "knowable." If "knowable" means "knowable by some actual finite person with finite information," then loads of things fail and the thesis was never about that. The point is more like "not excluded from knowledge in principle" — there's no obstacle of principle to a suitably placed knower. A knower with enough information could know the fact about unknown truths; there's nothing self-defeating about it. So Marcus's case isn't a counterexample, it's just the difference between "no finite being happens to be able to know it" and "no one could know it even in principle."

Marcus said fine, but now watch the principle dissolve into "every truth is such that there's no obstacle of principle to knowing it," and the interesting question is what counts as an obstacle of principle — and the only clear cases are the self-defeating ones, which lands her right back where she started, excluding only the paradox's own cases.

And that's roughly where they left it, both convinced. I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it.

Not just who wins overall. And tell me what both of them are missing; I suspect there's something neither of them has said, but I don't know what it is.
