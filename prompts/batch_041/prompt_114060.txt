I'm opening a seminar session on the sunk cost fallacy with a poem, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. Write a sestina in the voice of someone who has spent years on a project they can no longer defend to their friends. The six end-words are spent, still, keep, enough, worth, and because; you may choose the order in which they first appear, but from then on they must rotate by the sestina's rule, and the envoi must use all six. The rule that makes the last end-word of one stanza the first end-word of the next should be doing real work here: the thing you end on is the thing you begin with.

The speaker has to be able to state the standard view at full strength, not as a straw man. What is already spent is gone; the only question a decision can ask is what the next increment costs and what it is expected to return, including the value of keeping options open; the past bears on that question only as evidence about the future, or as a device for binding yourself. A decision theorist should read the poem and find nothing in that statement to correct.

But the poem also has to show the two things that view has trouble with. First, the years already spent are often the best evidence the speaker has, about whether they are any good at this and about what they have become able to do, so treating the past as irrelevant is itself a way of deciding badly. Second, for some goods the history is not a cost already paid but part of what the good is: nobody would say the twenty years in a marriage, a farm, a garden, or a body of work were merely upstream of its value. Let the word sunk carry both of its senses at least once, gone beyond recovery and laid down below grade so that something else can be built on it. And please don't let the poem turn into a moral about not throwing good money after bad.

Do not resolve any of this. The envoi should leave both positions standing, and the last line should be one that can be read either way. Give me only the poem: no title, no epigraph, no notes, nothing in prose. Thirty-nine lines exactly.
