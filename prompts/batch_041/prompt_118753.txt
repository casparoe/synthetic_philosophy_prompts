I've been reading Hume's essays on money and trade with a reading group (I'm the only one in it who isn't an economist, which is probably why nobody else finds this strange) and there's a move in them that I keep running into everywhere else too, and I dont know if it has a name.

Here's the first bit. Money gets treated as basically a token, a thing you measure trade with rather than a part of the wealth being traded, and from that it follows that doubling how much of it there is changes nothing real — prices adjust and nobody is actually richer. Fine. But then the same essays say a country is lucky to be getting more of it, because while the new money is working its way around, before prices catch up, people are busier, trade picks up, industry does better. And then a third bit: a country losing its gold is supposed to be fine, because the gold leaving makes its prices fall and its exports cheap and then the gold comes back by itself.

So money doesn't matter, except that it matters while it's arriving, and a country can't be hurt by losing it. All three are supposed to be true at once, and the thing that's supposed to hold them together is always a time horizon — the long run. But nobody ever says when it starts, or what you'd have to see to find out you were wrong about it coming.

I run into the same shape in arguments about central banks now. Somebody says a general rise in prices is harmless in real terms, and the same person is very worried about who gets the new money first, and about what happens to people on fixed incomes in the meantime. Those cant be the same claim.

So: is there a name for the second thing, the while its arriving doctrine, as opposed to the it cancels out eventually doctrine? And is it one idea or three stapled together, because I suspect a claim about who gains and loses, a claim about prices lagging, and a claim about idle factories getting going are seperate claims that people bundle. If the answer is that I'm really looking at three existing ideas wearing one coat, say which ones and where the seams are. And is there a name for the whole syndrome — a doctrine that only comes true at a limit nobody reaches, but that gets used to settle arguments about what's happening right now? If there isn't one, coin one, but tell me where it stops applying: does the arriving story need wages to lag behind prices, or spare capacity, or people not noticing, or does it survive even if everybody sees it coming?

And what has to be true for the automatic gold thing to actually work? Every time I hear it the person seems to have assumed away exactly what the argument is about.
