I am preparing a handout for a decision theory reading group. The handout should be a decision tree that takes a reader from their answers to a set of diagnostic questions to a named position on the family of problems that includes counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. The tree should be built from the logical space of the problems rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed.

Here are the problems the tree must cover. Counterfactual mugging: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If the coin comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000 if and only if it predicted that you would have paid had the coin come up tails. You know all this. The coin has come up tails, and Omega asks for $100. XOR blackmail: a blackmailer flips a fair coin and sends a threat letter to exactly one of two people, Alice and Bob. The letter demands $100 and threatens a $10,000 loss if not paid. The blackmailer is known to carry out the threat. You are Alice, and you have received the letter. You know the coin came up heads, so Bob did not receive a letter. In a variant, the blackmailer is a perfect predictor of each agent's decision policy; in another variant, the blackmailer has no predictive ability. In a further variant, you can precommit before the coin flip to a policy that will govern both Alice and Bob, or just yourself.

The diagnostic questions should be concrete cases, not abstract labels. Each branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. For example, the tree should ask whether the reader pays in the one-shot tails case, whether their answer changes if they can precommit before the coin flip, whether it changes if the problem is repeated many times, whether it changes if the blackmailer is a perfect predictor, whether it changes if the other agent shares their decision policy, and whether it changes if they do not know which agent they are. Use these and any other questions you think are necessary to separate the positions. Do not ask questions like whether the reader accepts causal decision theory, because that would sort by labels rather than by beliefs.

The leaves should carry the names of positions. At minimum, the tree must be able to locate causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, updateless decision theory or timeless decision theory, functional decision theory, at least one view that says you should pay only if you can precommit before the coin flip, and at least one view that says the problem is ill-posed or that the predictor assumption is incoherent. Add any other positions you think are needed to cover the space, such as a view that pays in the one-shot case but not in the repeated case, or a view that treats the decision as a choice of policy rather than an act. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The tree should have roughly 10 to 14 branch nodes and 7 to 10 leaves. It should be usable as a scoring key: given a reader's answers, the tree should lead to exactly one leaf, or to a flagged pair of leaves if the tree cannot separate them. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag that pair explicitly and explain why the diagnostic questions fail to distinguish them. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no named position, note it as unoccupied and say whether it is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please write the tree in a clear outline or flowchart format, with each branch node labeled by its question and each leaf labeled by its position and its awkward commitment. Do not include citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; the handout should be self-contained.
