Ok so I need help with a speech and I keep putting it off. I'm the outgoing chair of our county historical society, and in two weeks we've got the centenery dinner — the mayor's coming, maybe two hundred people. The society was founded a hundred years ago basically to keep the town's founding story alive, and the after-dinner speech is mine to give. The trouble is the room splits right down the middle. Half of them are the local-history crowd who've believed since they were kids that the founder really existed and did the things in the story; the other half are the academics we've started inviting — a couple of archaelogists, one who works on Roman Britain — who mostly think the founding story is a Victorian invention and are too polite to say so over dinner. I want a speech that honours the evening and doesn't pick a fight, but that actually says something honest about the question that's the title of my talk: when does a legend become history?

What I want it to do is not just trot out the three cases everyone reaches for — Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus — but use them to say something about the logic of the thing. My own half-formed view, which I'd like the speech to argue for without sounding like a seminar, is that a tradition crosses over into history not when we find evidence that confirms it, but when we stop needing it to be true — when the community can afford to be wrong about its founder. I think that's why Troy feels different from the other two: nobody's identity is riding on whether a Greek coalition ever sacked a city on that coast, so the Hittite tablets that seem to name a place that might be Wilusa, and the prince whose name looks like the second name of Paris, can be weighed as evidence rather than defended as heritage. Arthur sits in the middle — the Welsh annals give him two dates and a final battle, and the earliest writer who could have named him doesn't, which is a fact the room will find either thrilling or offensive depending which half they're sitting in. And the Exodus is the hard case, because for a lot of the room it was never an academic question in the first place.

Can you write it? Roughly seven minutes spoken. It has to be warm, and it has to land for both the grandmothers and the archaeologists, and it can never sound like it's telling the believers they're naive or the academics that they're vandals. And please don't put any quotes from ancient sources in it — I'll get them wrong and someone will definately notice.
