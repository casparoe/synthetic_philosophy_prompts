I am a high-school student preparing a short talk for my school's philosophy club about how a group can arrive at a verdict that most of its own members voted against. I would like you to compare two ways a committee can aggregate its members' votes on a decision that has several parts: (a) voting separately on each premise and then deriving the conclusion from whichever premises win, and (b) voting directly on the final conclusion. To make this concrete, suppose a five-member disciplinary board must decide whether a student committed a violation, and a violation occurs only if the student acted knowingly and the action caused harm. Assume every member votes sincerely and consistently, meaning each votes yes on a premise only if they believe it, and votes for the conclusion that follows from their own two premise-votes. The votes are: Member 1 — knowingly: yes, harm: yes, violation: yes; Member 2 — knowingly: yes, harm: yes, violation: yes; Member 3 — knowingly: yes, harm: no, violation: no; Member 4 — knowingly: no, harm: yes, violation: no; Member 5 — knowingly: no, harm: no, violation: no. Please work out what each method yields here, explain why the two methods diverge even though every member is individually consistent, and then give your verdict on which method better captures what the group as a whole "thinks." Finally, state what would change your mind — which consideration or counterexample would lead you to favor the other method. (If it sharpens the contrast, you may briefly note whether the divergence could still arise if a violation required only one of the two conditions rather than both.)
