Something I want us to pick apart in seminar next week. You know the ritual in any hospital: nurse holds up the card, 0 to 10, patient picks a number, number goes in the chart, and from then on that number is treated as the thing we're treating — the actual pain, not a hint about it. Same in psychophysics and consciousness research: verbal report is the gold standard, and everything else (wincing, grip strength, BOLD signal) is a proxy, a stand-in. And yet the same fields get visibly excited when a patient who looks vegetative follows a command inside the scanner, or when we infer pain in a rat from the way it guards a paw — non-report evidence that the experience is presumably there anyway.

So: what theory of mind does that whole apparatus presupose? Put the epiphenomenalist on the stand for a second. If experiences cause nothing, then my 7 on the scale is caused by whatever causes the pain and not by the pain itself, and the practice looks like it's leaning on a correlation nobody can cash out — which is roughly the old paradox of phenomenal judgment, or the meta-problem if you prefer. Work out whether the practice strictly requires experience to be causally efficacious, or whether some common-cause story buys it off, and what that story costs whoever tells it. Does the practice presuppose that the patient can refer to her own experience, or only that she can produce something reliably tied to it? And is reportability being treated as evidence of consciousness, or quietly as constitutive of it? Then the fun part: if we rebuilt clinical and lab practice on epiphenalist lines, what would actually have to change — the numbers, or just our commentary on them? Keep the two questions seperate in your answer.
