I run a philosophy reading group — about 14 people, mixed undergrads and a few grad students — and last month we did Strawson on the reactive attitudes and moral responsibility. Next session I have the room for 90 minutes and I want to open with something they *do* rather than another round of discussion. No lecture up front; the point should show up in what happens to them and only get named at the end. Activity plus debrief has to fit in the 90.

The case I want it built on (keep these specifics, they're doing work):

Five people share a house. Over about four months one of them — call her N. — has been taking money out of the house kitty in small amounts and lying about it, including lying to the face of the housemate who caught her, M. M.'s own deposit money is in that kitty, so she's out real cash. Halfway through the session the group gets handed a document (handed, not announced — I don't want it arriving as a twist from me) with what's known about N.'s history: a chaotic upbringing, a parent who took her money and blamed her for things, and a real pattern of impulsive spending when she's under stress. It's not a clean excuse — she's an adult who lied to a friend's face — and it's not a clean exemption, because she isn't ill in any way that settles it. She's a full member of the house and also someone with a pattern she can't control.

Mechanics I want:

Two groups. Group A is the house, M. included. Group B is a ring of friends who know the house and N. but don't live there — their job is to help produce a plan. Same case, same documents, different distance from the wrong.

Round 1, before the document: each group decides what to do and writes down what they want from N. Round 2, after: each group revises and produces two things — a plan, written in the third person in the register of a case file, and a demand, written as something they'll have to say to her out loud. Then I (or a volunteer) play N., and each group has to actually deliver both, as written. No paraphrasing, no softening on the fly; the rule is they read the plan and the demand as written and then deal with what happens. After that, everyone privately writes one line on whether they could have said the plan to her face, and what happened to their sense of the relationship while they did.

What I want them to come away with: a felt sense that (a) what they're demanding of N. isn't just better behaviour but a certain attitude, and that a technically correct plan doesn't discharge it; (b) the clinical stance is cheap from where Group B is standing and nearly unavailable from where M. is standing, and M.'s refusal to move isn't a failure of reasoning; (c) the backstory doesn't leave them with a stable attitude at all — they'll oscillate, and that oscillation is the thing, not a sign the exercise is broken.

In the debrief I want you to point at specific moments from the run and hook them to specific bits of the picture: the participant attitude vs the objective attitude; the difference between excusing her (which stays inside the participant attitude) and dropping the demand altogether; the personal/vicarious asymmetry between the two groups; the half-way cases where neither stance is stable; and the fact that nobody in the room can answer the question whether it was rational of M. to resent her without either doing it or stepping outside the practice entirely.

Format, in this order: Setup (case, cast, materials, what the room looks like — ~150 words); The rounds (numbered, with timings, rules and any written prompts in full — ~600 words); Debrief (~400 words); What will go wrong the first time (~150 words, including how to handle it); What this activity can't reproduce (~100 words — I suspect it can't get at the first-person side of the picture, guilt and self-directed resentment, and I want you to say so plainly if that's right, and tell me what I have to supply in the debrief instead).

Two things to avoid. Don't hand me a reading list or cite anything — I don't need it and neither will the group. And don't let the rules smuggle in a diagnosis that makes N. a clean case for management; the whole thing depends on her being neither, so the ambiguity has to survive contact with the activity. Also don't worry about covering every part of the picture — I'd rather it be tight and land one or two things properly.
