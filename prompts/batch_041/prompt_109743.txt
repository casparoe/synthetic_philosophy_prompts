I chair the grantmaking committee of a small foundation that funds long-horizon projects, and roughly once a quarter a proposal arrives whose stated justification is acausal: the applicant argues that by acting a certain way now — funding a line of research, making a public commitment, or declining to do something — they raise the probability that agents with whom we can neither communicate nor interact causally (a future superintelligence, a distant civilization, a counterpart elsewhere running the same reasoning) will act in kind. I am drawn to this way of thinking and want a procedure I can defend to a skeptical board rather than one that merely ratifies my sympathies. Write it: the questions to run in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps rest on facts and which are irreducibly evaluative, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the matter to the committee because the question is one of values rather than evidence. The committee has five members, meets quarterly, and must publish a short written rationale for every decision. Then run the procedure on three cases: a proposal whose entire claimed value depends on such reciprocity; an ordinary grant where acausal considerations are said to be no more than a tiebreaker; and a proposal that argues we should fund it because a counterpart we cannot reach would penalize us for refusing. Tell me where the procedure cannot help, and whether the reasoning, taken at face value, licenses so much that it stops discriminating among proposals at all.
