I host a monthly discussion group — about twelve adults, mostly engineers and scientists, with no philosophy training — and we meet for a single 75-minute session. I would like to run an activity rather than a lecture, one that lets the group feel its way into a cluster of ideas about money and exchange that I keep circling back to but cannot get my colleagues to treat as ideas rather than as economics. Here is the cluster, stated as plainly as I can manage:

- Every possession seems to have two uses. You can use the thing itself, and you can trade it away. A shoe can be worn or sold, and the second use is not the same as the first and does not follow from it.
- Exchange between unlike things — a house against a pile of beds, say — appears to require that the things be commensurable, that there be some single scale on which each has a magnitude. But nothing about the things themselves supplies that scale.
- What actually does the measuring, on this view, is need or demand: the fact that each party wants what the other holds. Money then enters as a stand-in for need, and it is a stand-in by agreement or convention rather than by nature. The coin and the law are, in the original vocabulary, the same word at root — the thing is a nomisma, and it is so by nomos.
- This yields a split between two ways of getting wealth: acquiring what a household actually needs and will use, versus acquiring money for its own sake by buying and selling. Only the first is bounded; the second has no natural stopping point.
- The extreme case is lending at interest, which looks like a category error on this picture. Money was invented to serve as a medium of exchange, so making it breed from itself is asking a measure to be the thing measured.

I am after the activity and the reasoning behind it, not an economics seminar and not a lecture on Aristotle. Please design a runnable session in which the group runs into these problems on its own, before anyone names them.

The activity should satisfy the following. The group should begin by trading heterogeneous goods with no obvious exchange ratios and no common currency, so that the commensurability problem shows up as friction they feel rather than as a claim they are told. Some device should then let a currency emerge or be introduced, so that they can experience both what the currency solves and what it quietly imposes — the artificiality of the measure, the way a convention starts to look natural once it is in use. If a lending phase can be added without overloading the session, include it, so that the interest question arises from the participants' own incentives. The payoff or valuation scheme should make the stakes real to them without any actual money changing hands.
