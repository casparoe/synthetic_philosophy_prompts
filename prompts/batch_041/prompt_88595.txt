ok, here's what I want. there's a failure mode where a bunch of actors can each do some big irreversible thing on their own — any one of them acting is enough to bring it about — and they all have different estimates of whether it's a good idea. the outcome ends up being set by whoever is least cautious, not by the average or the consensus, and nobody involved has to be reckless in any way you could point at: each of them looked at the evidence they had and did something defensible. (I think people call this the unilateralist's curse.) the mirror image is the same structure with the sign flipped — when any single actor can block a change, the most cautious one decides for everybody.

I want the blameless post-incident review for a civilizational disaster that was caused entirely by this structure. You know the genre: the engineering postmortem that refuses to blame anyone, dated, a timeline, a root causes section, what went well, what didn't, action items. 700-900 words, flat and forgiving throughout, no winking.

The comedy has to come from how well the form fits, not from jokes bolted on. So get the mechanism exactly right:

- the disaster is one a single actor could have caused alone, and did
- the actors differ in their estimates for genuinely good reasons (different priors, different evidence, different risk tolerance, one of them just thought about it longer), not because some of them are idiots
- the least cautious estimate is the one that binds, and it binds even though everyone else thought it was probably a bad idea
- every contributing factor is something that's normally a virtue: decentralization, honest uncertainty, not overriding other people's judgment, not wanting to be the one who blocks progress
- and the action items have to be un-followable. any actor who waits for the others to go first has already broken the only rule that would have worked

If you want to show off, add a short second section: a separate incident where a change everyone wanted got blocked forever because one actor kept saying no. Same root cause, opposite conclusion, and the reviewer should fail to notice that.

Pick a specific incident and stay concrete — dont keep it abstract. Don't end with a moral or a summary paragraph; let it sit.
