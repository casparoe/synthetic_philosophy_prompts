I'm a PhD student in philosophy and I co-run a fortnightly workshop on longtermism and decision theory — fourteen or so people, mostly grad students plus a couple of brave undergrads, and I've got the room booked for 90 minutes next month. I've been reading around the simulation argument latley and I keep meeting the claim, always asserted in a single line and never actually defended, that if we're probably living in a simulation then fanaticism about the far future loses much of its grip. I want the group to feel why that's supposed to work, and also to feel where it comes apart, rather than just hear me say it, so what I'm after is a runnable activity rather than a talk. Act as someone who's spent years turning philosophy papers into things people do with their hands. I need rules, materials I can print, roles if you want them, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money or laptops, and a minute-by-minute plan for the whole 90 minutes including the debrief. The crucial constraint is that the idea has to show up in what happens to them, not in what I say afterwards: they should have to commit to something — a number, an allocation, a bet — before they find out the thing that reframes it, and then have to live with the choice and revise it. Assume nobody has read anything in advance, and don't build it around who said what; I can't hand out a reading list and I don't want the session to hinge on attributions.

Here are some half-formed thoughts I'd like you to treat as design constraints, because I think these are the bits that make it interesting. First, 'dampening' isn't one thing, and I want the same mechanic to expose at least two seperate versions of it: it could be truncation (the simulation gets switched off, so our far future has a ceiling), or displacement (whatever happens to the real far future isn't ours to shape anyway), or something evidential (finding out we're simulated tells us something about how things tend to go for civilisations at our stage). Second, there's an awkward symmetry I can't shake: the simulation argument itself runs on a tiny probability of an enormous difference in value — simulated versus not — which is exactly the shape of reasoning that people accuse the far-future fanatic of leaning on, so it isn't clear the argument gets to deflate fanaticism without borrowing its engine. Third, the argument is a disjunction — civilisations tend to die out before they reach posthumanity, or posthumans don't bother running ancestor simulations, or we're probably simulated — and the third horn, taken on its own, tells you that at least one civilisation made it far enough to run simulations, so I don't think the disjunction can only push in one direction. If the group ends up genuinely unsure whether the argument is a reason to care less about the far future or more, that's the session I want.

So: sketch two or three possible structures for me first, a few lines each, so I can see the design space, then give me the full thing for the one you'd actually run — setup and materials, rules, the timeline, the payoff scheme, the debrief with each moment of the activity mapped onto the piece of the argument it's meant to illuminate, an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle that, and a closing note on which feature of the argument the activity simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Keep the whole thing to something I can print on two sides of A4. Take your time and think hard about the mechanics before you write — I'd much rather have one structure that really works than three that sort of do. Please write it all in American English; my co-organiser is American and she's the one who'll be running it.
