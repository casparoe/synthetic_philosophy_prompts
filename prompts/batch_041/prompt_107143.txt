I am grading the midterm for an upper-level undergraduate course on twentieth-century analytic philosophy, and I would like a second opinion on one student's answer. The exam question was: Explain Ayer's treatment, in Language, Truth and Logic, of the claim that a person survives the death of his body. On what grounds did he deny that this is a genuine factual question, and what exactly does his verdict amount to? Then say whether his own near-death experience, which he described publicly near the end of his life, ought to have led him to revise that treatment. The student wrote the following.

"Ayer's verification principle holds that a statement is literally meaningful only if it is either analytic (true or false by definition) or capable of being verified by observation. On this basis he held that the claim that a person survives the death of his body is not a genuine factual claim at all. Since the only way we could check whether someone has survived death would be to observe him after he has died, and death is precisely the end of the sort of thing that can be observed, there is no possible observation that could confirm or disconfirm the claim. It therefore fails the test. Ayer is careful to say that this does not make the claim false; it makes it meaningless, in the sense of being neither true nor false.

His position is sometimes confused with the view that survival is impossible. But Ayer's point is different: he is not saying the soul cannot exist, only that the sentence asserting a person's survival has no empirical content, because whatever content we try to give it — for example, reports of apparitions or mediumistic communications — turns out to be about something happening in the world of the living, not about the dead person continuing to experience anything.

Ayer's own near-death experience, in which he seemed to see a red light and other figures, is often cited as an embarrassment for his view. In my view it does weaken his position, since it is a case of experience continuing while the body was, by the doctors' account, not functioning. If a person can have experiences when clinically dead, then survival is at least empirically investigable, and the verificationist's dismissal of it as meaningless is too quick. Ayer himself seems to have recognized this, and it is reasonable to read his later account as an admission that he had been wrong about the meaningfulness of survival claims."
