I teach a seminar on the moral emotions and I'm tired of the standard guilt-is-about-what-you-did, shame-is-about-who-you-are framing everyone already knows. So: build me a thought experiment where shame comes out morally better than guilt. Same act, same harm, two agents, one moved by guilt and one by shame, and the ashamed one ends up doing the better thing. Constraints: realistic (a clinic, an office, a family — no fables), one paragraph, and dont rig it by making the guilty agent a hypocrite, or the ashamed one a doormat, or by having shame work only because the audience is watching and might punish them. The shame response itself has to do the moral work.

Then the analysis: say what each emotion is tracking in the case, why shame wins, and which single feature is carrying the result. Then tell me which repair each agent is actually attempting — the one aimed at the person they hurt, or the one aimed at their own standing — and whether swapping that target flips the verdict. Finally: could a culture deliberately cultivate the winning emotion, or does the win depend on something a culture cant manufacture? Cap the whole answer at 400 words.
