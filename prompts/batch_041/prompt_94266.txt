At a departmental workshop last term, two colleagues — one working in philosophy of medicine, the other a social epistemologist — spent most of an evening arguing over a case I had put to them, and I have not been able to settle it since. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move rather than pronounce a winner in the abstract.

The case. A regional health authority pilots a "care navigation score" that ranks patients for a scarce resource: the top fifteen percent receive a care coordinator. The score has three inputs — diagnostic codes, laboratory values, and "narrative flags," which are phrases the intake clinician selects from a fixed list of forty maintained by the authority (items like "reports fatigue," "anxious presentation," "pain out of proportion"). The list is published; the weights are not. "Pain out of proportion" was placed on the list as a marker of possible somatization and carries a substantial negative weight, which the intake clinician does not know. A patient with a poorly characterized illness describes her symptoms at length. The clinician believes her completely, and her note, read as a whole, records the account faithfully; but the closest item on the list is "pain out of proportion," so she enters it. The patient's score falls below the cutoff. She never sees the score or the phrase, and no one reviews the entry.

Their exchange ran as follows.

(1) The philosopher of medicine: This is testimonial injustice. The patient's word is deflated by a prejudice about who exaggerates pain; that the deflation is performed by a form rather than by a person is irrelevant, since the form was designed by people and the design carries the prejudice. The wrong is to her credibility.

(2) The social epistemologist: No one disbelieves her. The clinician believes her fully and records her account accurately. What fails is the vocabulary: the only items available to the clinician distort what the patient says, so she must choose between being unintelligible to the institution and being misdescribed by it. That is the paradigm of hermeneutical injustice — a gap in the interpretive resources available to her as a patient. Note, too, that the clinician is herself kept in the dark about the weights; the failure lies in the resource, not in any hearer's attitude.

(3) The philosopher of medicine: Hermeneutical injustice requires that the gap lie in the shared resources, arising from the unequal power of groups to shape the common vocabulary. Here the vocabulary is adequate — the patient's own words exist and the clinician understands them. What is missing is uptake, not resources. That is a distinct phenomenon and deserves its own name: receptive injustice, meaning a systematic distortion in the reception of testimony that is fully believed, where no hearer's credence is misallocated but the record that results misrepresents the speaker.

(4) The social epistemologist: That is a distinction without a difference, and it trivializes the concept — on that standard every bureaucratic paraphrase becomes an injustice. A case counts as epistemic injustice only if the harm falls on the person's capacity as a knower, on her standing as a participant in the exchange of reasons, rather than merely on the accuracy of some representation of her.

(5) The philosopher of medicine: But a misrepresentation you cannot see, appeal, or even learn about damages you precisely as a participant: you are shut out of the process that decides what is known about you. And your own criterion is unavailable to you — if structural cases count as epistemic injustice, as you have argued, then in your view too it is the wronged knower's standing, not a wronging hearer, that does the work. So the requirement you are imposing on my proposal is one your own position cannot meet.

(6) The social epistemologist: Even granting that, "receptive injustice" earns nothing. Everything you want to say can be said with the two familiar categories plus an account of institutional design. A new category should be introduced when it explains something the old ones cannot, and you have not produced a case in which the old vocabulary fails.

What I want from you is a point-by-point adjudication, not a survey of the literature. First, for each of moves (1) through (6), say whether it is sound, sound but overstated, or mistaken, and give your reasons in a sentence or two — including, where a move is overstated, what the defensible core of it is. Second, answer the substantive questions the exchange turns on: (a) which of the two initial diagnoses, (1) or (2), better locates the wrong in this case; (b) whether receptive injustice earns its keep as a third kind, and if it does, what a case would have to look like to distinguish it from both familiar kinds, and if it does not, which familiar kind absorbs it; (c) whether the "capacity as a knower" criterion in (4) is defensible as a necessary condition for epistemic injustice, and whether move (5) succeeds in turning that criterion against its author; and (d) whether the fact that the clinician is also kept ignorant of the weights changes the diagnosis or merely adds a second complaint. Third, say what each of the two is missing — the consideration that, once noticed, would force each to revise a claim they actually made. Finally, give an overall verdict on who has the better of the argument, and state plainly what would have to be true of the case for the opposing side to be right. Do not resolve the dispute by awarding each party half of it; if a move fails, say so.
