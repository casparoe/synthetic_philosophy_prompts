For a graduate seminar on dynamic choice I want a worked example that is not the standard money pump — the one in which an agent with a preference cycle is walked around the cycle by a trader who charges a fee at every step, which by now every student has seen and which half of them suspect of being a trick. What I want instead is a diachronic money pump, built to a specification, and I want the case to earn its conclusions rather than stipulate them; the point of the exercise is to let us ask whether exploitability really is a mark of irrationality, so the victim's exploitability has to fall out of the structure of the case and not out of a fiat that they are silly.

The specification. First, the setting is ordinary: a household, a workplace, a subscription, a course of treatment, with ordinary money and ordinary goods, and no brokers, no auctions, no exotic instruments. Second, at every moment the victim's preferences are transitive and complete — there is no synchronic cycle anywhere in the case, and whatever is wrong with the victim, it is not that they rank three options in a loop. Third, nothing turns on deception, on the trader's reading the victim's mind, or on the trader's predicting the victim better than an attentive observer could; the offers are made openly and the trades are made in full awareness of what they are. Fourth, every trade is one the victim strictly prefers, by the preferences they hold at that moment, to not trading. Fifth, the victim ends up strictly worse off than they began when measured by the preferences they held at the outset, and strictly better off when measured by the preferences they hold at the end, so that there is no time-neutral answer to whether the victim lost; the case has to make that plain rather than fudge it. Sixth, the victim must not be a mere dupe: if the case works only because the victim is ignorant of how their own preferences will change, you must say precisely what that ignorance consists in, and then say whether a victim who knows only that their preferences will change — but not in which direction — could still be pumped, and whether one who knows the direction as well could. If these conditions cannot all be met at once, do not quietly drop one: identify which condition is doing the damage, explain why, and build the closest case that satisfies the rest.

Then the demonstration, which matters as much as the case. For each of the following, give the verdict it delivers on your victim — irrational or not, and the exact charge — together with the step in the case that generates that verdict: the time-slice view, on which rationality attaches to a person at a time and there is no requirement that a person's choices cohere across times; resolute choice, on which the victim should have settled on a plan and treated the plan rather than the current preference as authoritative; sophisticated choice, on which the victim should have reasoned backwards from the last trade and declined the first; and the classical money-pump argument, which treats exploitability as itself the mark of irrationality and which was designed for the synchronic case. For each verdict, say which single feature of the case it depends on and what becomes of the verdict when that feature is deleted. Close by telling me which feature is doing the real work, which of the four views comes out of the example under the most pressure, and how someone who holds that view would go after the case.
