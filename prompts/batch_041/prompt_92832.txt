I have drafted the essay below and would like your comments on it. It is an attempt to stake out a position rather than to survey a literature, so please assess it on its own terms; I am not looking for reading suggestions, and I would rather have the argument pressed than annotated.

Draft essay: *Two Clocks, One Body: Against Biological Monism About Death*

The dispute over the definition of death is usually presented as a choice between two biological criteria. On the circulatory criterion, a person is dead when circulation and respiration have ceased; on the neurological criterion, when the brain — whole or brainstem — has ceased to function. Both criteria are stated in the language of irreversibility. My claim is that this framing is unstable, and that once the instability is exposed the dispute turns out not to be a dispute about biology at all, but about which losses of integration we are prepared to treat as final. I do not take this to be a debunking result. I take it to be the correct description, and I think the honest response is to stop dressing an institutional decision as a biological discovery.

The first crack is the gap between irreversibility and permanence. Irreversibility is a claim about what cannot be undone by any means we could in principle deploy; permanence is a claim about what will not be undone, because no attempt will be made or because attempts have been abandoned. The definitions require irreversibility. The tests establish, at best, permanence. When circulation ceases and a procurement protocol waits several minutes before declaring death, what has been established is that resuscitation has been forgone, not that it was impossible — and there are reports of circulation returning spontaneously after it had been taken to have ceased for several minutes, which makes the point concrete rather than pedantic. If we insist on the literal definition, nobody is dead at the moment we say they are, since the future is open and techniques change. If we accept permanence as sufficient, the criterion includes an irreducibly social element, namely a decision not to intervene, and the biological vocabulary is doing rhetorical work rather than descriptive work.

The second crack is integration. The neurological criterion is commonly defended on the ground that the brain is what makes a body an organism rather than a collection of organs: it is the integrator, and with it gone the body is a mere aggregate. But it is by now a familiar point that bodies meeting the criteria can, with mechanical support, do a good deal of what organisms do — gestate a fetus, heal wounds, mount immune responses, and in some cases retain a degree of hormonal and thermal regulation. If those count as integration, the criterion is not tracking integration. If they do not count, we owe an account of why the brain's integrating is the kind that matters and the rest is not.

So I propose to replace the question "when does the organism die?" with "which losses of integration are we prepared to treat as final, and on what grounds?" Answering that requires separating three things that are normally run together. First, *integrative death*: the point at which the body no longer coordinates its own subsystems and instead has its unity supplied from outside, by ventilator, pressors, dialysis, warming. Second, *relational death*: the point at which the capacity for any conscious or agentive relation to the world is gone. Third, *institutional death*: the point at which our practices authorize treating someone as dead — when the funeral happens, the will is read, the organs are taken. My claim is that the neurological criterion is a proxy for the second presented as a criterion for the first, and that the circulatory criterion is a proxy for the first implemented by way of the third. The proxies coincide often enough that we stop noticing the joints.

To make the first notion usable I offer what I call the substitution test. For any function the body is said still to perform, ask whether the body is performing it or whether it is being performed *for* the body. If the latter — if the unity is now supplied from outside — the function does not count toward the body's self-integration. Applied naively the test has the absurd consequence that anyone on a ventilator is dead, so it must be applied not to this or that function but to the set of functions that constitute the body's self-maintenance as a unit. The criterion then reads: death occurs when the body's remaining activity is fully accounted for by the intrinsic capacities of its parts plus the support we supply, with no residual capacity for coordinated, adaptive response to perturbation.

I am aware that this last formulation may simply be the brainstem criterion in other words, and I want to know whether it is. If it is, the whole essay may be a relabelling exercise, and I would rather be told so than flattered. If it is not, I want to know which cases separate them.

The conclusion I am drawn to is uncomfortable. If the criterion is really about which losses we treat as final, then the dead donor rule — the requirement that donors be dead before organs are removed — is being asked to do work that the definition cannot support. Either we should say plainly that we are taking organs from living patients who have irreversibly lost the capacity for consciousness, and rest the practice on consent and on the absence of any remaining interests, or we should say plainly that death is an institutional status and stop pretending the institutions are discovering a fact. I do not know which I prefer, and the essay is meant to force the choice rather than to settle it.

What I would like from you:

1. Where does the argument do the least work? I suspect the integration section is doing less than the permanence section, but tell me which premise you would attack first.
2. Is the irreversibility/permanence distinction load-bearing, or can a defender of the neurological criterion simply grant it and say that permanence was always the intended notion? If so, what is left of my first crack?
3. Does the substitution test collapse into the brainstem criterion, or into the whole-brain criterion, or into neither? If it collapses, is there anything in the essay that survives the collapse?
4. Does the three-part taxonomy of integrative, relational, and institutional death generate any verdict or prediction that the standard two-criterion view cannot produce? If it does not, is it idle?
5. Which cases does my account get wrong? I am thinking especially of cases where the three deaths come apart: the brain-dead body maintained on support, the permanently unconscious patient with an intact brainstem, the donor whose circulation might have returned, the anencephalic infant, and the conscious patient who can never breathe unaided.
6. Is the closing claim about the dead donor rule a consequence I should accept, or a reductio of my own argument? If it is a reductio, where exactly does it bite?
7. Finally, setting aside whether it is correct: is this interesting? Or is the terrain so thoroughly worked over that my reframing is a distinction without a difference, and if so, what would have to change for it to stop being one?
