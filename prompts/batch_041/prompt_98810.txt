I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy and the more mathematical corners of the social sciences, and I have been trying to work through the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — specifically the family of demonstrations that, whenever base rates differ across groups, no predictor can be simultaneously well calibrated within each group and satisfy a balance condition such as equalized odds. I would like you to describe the most important implications of these results for a design problem that departs from the standard setup in one respect. Suppose the relevant group distinction is not a pair of discrete categories but a continuous, noisy, and partly heritable biological quantity — for instance a polygenic score — that itself contributes to the outcome the predictor is meant to forecast. I am interested in whether the impossibility survives in any recognizable form when the protected attribute is graded rather than binary and is entangled with the target, and in what the answer implies for how a regulator ought to specify fairness requirements under such conditions. Please reason from the structure of the underlying arguments rather than rehearsing a catalogue of named fairness criteria, and be explicit about which implications you take to be robust features of the mathematics and which are artifacts of the idealized assumptions.
