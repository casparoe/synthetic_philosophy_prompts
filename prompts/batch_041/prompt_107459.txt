I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in England in the 1880s and 1890s. My protagonist is a Cambridge moral philosopher closely modeled on Henry Sidgwick, the utilitarian who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research and served as its first president. What draws him into the investigation of mediums, apparitions, and thought-transference is not curiosity but need: he has lost his religious faith and hopes that disciplined empirical inquiry might yield evidence bearing on whether anything survives death — the question he cares about most and trusts least. The historical Sidgwick was convinced by the evidence for telepathy yet remained cautious about treating mediumistic phenomena as proof of survival, and he died without resolving the matter; I want my character to arrive at that same caution honestly, through his own reasoning, rather than simply announcing it as a mood.

The difficulty is that my plot turns on his repeated decisions — séance after séance, claim after claim — about what to make of a report, what to write up for the Society's Proceedings, and what to tell the people who press him for a verdict. Around him I have placed a wealthy spiritualist patron who funds his research, a hard-nosed physicist colleague who regards the whole enterprise as a scandal, a recently bereaved friend who needs him to confirm that the dead survive, and his own wish to believe. He is not a statistician and has no laboratory; he works from testimony, notes, and his own observation, and he must often decide within a week whether to stake his name on a case. The Society's council has to sign off on anything published, and its credibility — which is also his — is the thing most at risk.

What I need is a decision procedure he can actually run under those pressures: the questions he should ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a firm no, and the point at which it must hand the decision back to him because the matter is one of hope or of values rather than of evidence. Keep whatever distinctions the historical Sidgwick's approach depends on — above all, between the bare occurrence and its interpretation, between the question of what caused a result and the question of what that result justifies believing, and between evidence for thought-transference and evidence for survival — and tell me which of his scruples can be dropped for the sake of usability and which must survive the simplification. Be candid about where the procedure will misfire or merely dress up a judgment call as a step.

Then run the procedure on three cases: first, a private séance, held with no meaningful controls, at which a medium produces a particular detail she could plausibly have learned elsewhere; second, a series of controlled card-guessing trials in which a sender in a separate room appears to transmit an image at a rate above chance; third, a sitting at which he suspects fraud but cannot prove it. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not want — I suspect it will refuse my protagonist the verdict my ending depends on — and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: whether it can ever license belief rather than merely suspend it, how it should treat his own hope for survival, and where he, and not the procedure, must decide.

Please write the answer in American English.
