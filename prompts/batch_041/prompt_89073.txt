I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar on labour, markets and justice: eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, and most of them arrive already convinced that the right to organise is either self-evidently just or self-evidently overrated, without having thought hard about what makes it either. What I want them to feel, rather than be told, is the structural bind at the centre of that right. A right of association that amounts only to a liberty to join cannot, by itself, generate any bargaining power against an employer whose interests are concentrated, and closing that gap appears to require binding the people who decline to join — through dues, agency fees, or exclusion from the gains the union wins — which is itself an interference with association. I want them to collide with the free-rider problem and the coercion problem as a single problem, and to see why the familiar individualist objection, that nobody should be compelled to fund a body they did not choose, is not merely confused, even though honouring it also ratifies a baseline of unequal bargaining power in which a formally free refusal is not an effective one.

Please design a runnable classroom game for this, with rules, roles, materials, and a payoff scheme built from points or tokens rather than money, arranged so that participants encounter both horns themselves before either is named. I am after something in the spirit of a wage-negotiation simulation that can be replayed under changed association rules: let them bargain, let them see what a merely voluntary association achieves against a concentrated counterparty, then let them see what happens when the rules permit the association to bind non-joiners, and then when the rules protect the opt-out — and let the numbers do the arguing. Give me the setup, the round-by-round timeline, how the employer side should be played and by whom, how you would keep the stakes real enough to bite without money, and the specific rule changes that produce the shifts in outcome. The debrief should map particular moments in the game onto the underlying ideas: the difference between a liberty to associate and an effective capacity to bargain, the way a dispersed many faces a concentrated few, and the thought — worth taking seriously rather than dismissing — that a right to combine is best understood as a corrective to an inequality that already exists rather than as a privilege invented by statute. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle each failure, and be frank about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief. No reading lists, no citations, and no quotations: I want the activity and the debrief, not a bibliography.
