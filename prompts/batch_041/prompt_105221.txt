You are a political philosopher and legal theorist who works on the grounds and limits of free expression. I want to work out, from the ground up, what a theory of the legitimate limits of state restriction of speech must accomplish before it can even be assessed, and I would like you to think carefully rather than recite familiar positions. Begin by brainstorming the considerations in play — the value of expression, the kinds of harm speech can do, the ways law can operate on it — and then produce the specification.

The target is any theory that, taking something in the neighborhood of Mill's harm principle as its starting point, tries to say when the state may restrict expression: incitement, threats, fraud, defamation, obscenity, disclosure, and the harder cases below. Give me the desiderata such a theory must meet: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it.

Then do three things with the list. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. Second, flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say exactly where the tension lies. Third, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories, and explain why.

Make your requirements answerable to at least these constructed cases, which I want handled explicitly rather than waved at:

- The focal-point broadcast: an anonymous post names no one and threatens no one, is harmless read on its own, but serves as a coordination point for many dispersed actors who each take a small, individually lawful step that aggregates into serious harm.
- The retroactive harm: a true disclosure whose damaging effect is realized only because of later events, so that at the moment of speaking no harm is yet determinable and no particular consequence is foreseeable.
- The constitutive harm: an utterance that does not cause a harm downstream but simply is the harm — a contract, a conspiracy, a betrayal, an agreement to exclude.
- The epistemic-commons case: speech that injures no identifiable person but degrades a shared resource, such as the reliability of a public record, on which everyone depends.
- The asymmetric-uptake case: the same utterance is harmless among one audience and catastrophic among another, and the speaker cannot tell which audience she is addressing.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or bibliographies; argue from the cases and considerations themselves. Where a requirement is doing real work, show me the case that would be mishandled without it, and where a requirement is merely a prejudice of one camp, say so. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you score existing theories against the list, do that briefly at the end.
