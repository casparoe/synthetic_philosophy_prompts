I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or mathematical logic; everything I know about Skolem's paradox I've assembled on my own, and I want a careful assessment rather than a summary. Let me set out the setup as I understand it, since my question only makes sense against a precise version of it.

Assume ZFC is consistent. Then by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem (equivalently, via the completeness theorem) ZFC has a model M whose domain is countable; we may take that domain to be a set of natural numbers. Since M satisfies every axiom of ZFC, it satisfies Cantor's theorem, and so M holds that the set it calls the set of real numbers is uncountable: there is no bijection in M between that set and the set M calls ω. From outside M, matters look different. The domain is countable; M's version of the power set of ω has only countably many members, even though M holds that it contains every subset of its ω; and there is a bijection between M's reals and the natural numbers. That bijection is simply not an element of M. So whether a collection is uncountable appears to depend on which model you occupy, even though the uncountability of the reals is supposed to be a fact about the reals themselves, independent of any model.

Here are the responses I keep running into. I want them ranked and scored.

1. The non-absoluteness response: there is no contradiction, only an equivocation. Countability is not absolute. M's claim that its reals are uncountable means only that no bijection to its ω lies inside M, and the bijection that exists outside M is not in M, so M never asserts anything false. The appearance of conflict comes from treating a metatheoretic fact about M's domain and an internal cardinality claim as claims about one and the same object.

2. The wrong-model response: the real lesson is that a first-order theory with infinite models never pins down its interpretation. The countable model is one interpretation among many rather than the universe of sets, and the paradox is a fact about the limits of first-order axiomatization rather than about sets. It is therefore no threat to the existence of an intended, genuinely uncountable universe.

3. The relativist or Skolemite response (the reading usually attributed to Skolem himself): there is no intended interpretation to fall back on. Set, countable, and the reals are relative to a background model, and the thought that uncountability is an absolute feature of a collection is an illusion produced by treating a formal and relative notion as though it had a unique referent. Set theory becomes the study of what follows from the axioms, and nothing more.

4. The upgrade-the-logic response: the culprit is first-order logic. Formalize set theory with second-order axioms under their standard (full) semantics and countable models vanish, since every model of second-order ZFC is isomorphic to the cumulative hierarchy up to some inaccessible cardinal, and any two models agree up to isomorphism or initial segment. The cost is that the semantics presupposes a determinate notion of all subsets of a domain, which is exactly the notion under dispute, and that there is no complete proof procedure to match.

5. The universe-is-not-a-model response: the paradox concerns set-sized models, whereas the intended universe V is not a set and so is not a model at all; model-theoretic relativity never gets a grip on it. Pushing the argument into the metatheory only pushes the relativity up one level, and one never arrives at a standpoint from which the Skolemite conclusion could itself be asserted absolutely.

Keep the scores comparable to one another and consistent with the ranking.

And is the right conclusion that the original formalization was defective, or that the notion of absolute uncountability was never coherent in the first place? If you think that dichotomy is false, say so and set out the actual options.
