A colleague in my department presses the following argument on me, and I cannot decide whether to concede or to find the loose joint. (1) Every historical outcome is the product of prior conditions. (2) Non-human conditions — a cooling climate, a failed harvest, an epidemic, a volcanic veil over the sun — are among those conditions. (3) Human choices are themselves conditioned by prior conditions and so enjoy no exemption from the causal order. (4) Hence every outcome has non-human factors among its causes. (5) If a factor is a cause of an outcome, then citing that factor explains the outcome. (6) Therefore every historical outcome admits a non-human explanation, and any account that foregrounds human decision is at best partial and at worst misleading. (7) So the serious question about any upheaval is always its environmental background, never what people chose to do.

I find (7) absurd — it would make the fall of a republic and the outcome of a war "really about" the weather — and I would give up a great deal to avoid it. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked harmless, and what rejecting it costs me elsewhere; I would rather keep (1) through (4), which seem to me plainly true, than keep (7). If the argument is in fact valid and the only honest move is to swallow (7) and revise my sense of what historical explanation is, say so rather than rescuing me. And if the very idea that this comes apart into discrete steps is the mistake — if "a cause" and "the explanation" are not two things that can be prised apart in this way — then say that plainly instead of manufacturing a culprit. I want a diagnosis I can defend, not a comfortable one.
