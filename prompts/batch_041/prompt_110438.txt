Bit of an odd request, but here's the situation. I'm stuck in a three-way argument that's been going for months and I want someone to lay out the actual terrain instead of taking a side.

Setting: my roommate is a med student, my brother works in insurance. The roommate says race shouldn't be a variable in clinical algorithms at all, because race is a social construct — full stop, nothing more to say, the variable goes. My brother says fine, but so is money, and money runs the world, so "social construct" obviously doesn't mean "not real," and the algorithm can keep using it as a rough proxy for whatever it's actually tracking (ancestry, zip code, access to care, who knows). Then a third friend jumps in with the opposite move: if race is constructed, then we built it, so we can unbuild it or rebuild it differently, which he thinks is the exciting part. All three of them are using the same four words to mean completely different things, and I'm fairly sure at least two of the readings are just false and one is a truism dressed up as a bombshell.

What I want is a menu. Take "race is a social construct" — and say where it comes apart from "gender is a social construct" or "money is a social construct," if it does — and seperate out the precise claims it could be making. I'd guess it ranges from stuff nobody would deny ("the boundaries are fuzzy and the categories vary across times and places") through to much stronger things, and I want the whole spread: the trivial ones, the substantive ones that are probably true, the contentious but defensible ones, and the ones that are false or incoherent. For each reading, tell me what it commits you to and what it leaves open.

Then the part I actually care about. For each of my three friends, say which reading of the slogan they'd need in order for their conclusion to follow. Does any reading get the roommate all the way to "so drop the variable"? Does the brother's money point land as a reply, or is it quietly changing the subject? Is there a reading under which the third friend's "so we can rebuild it" is right, and does that reading clash with the one the roommate needs? And if there's a standard name for the move where someone asserts a construction claim and treats a specific practical conclusion as already bundled into it, tell me; otherwise just say why it's a non sequitur in the cases where it is one.

No citations needed, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the map.
