I am a high school senior and the moderator of my school’s philosophy club. Last week, during a discussion of bounded rationality, two of our most careful members, Nadia and Eli, got into an argument that the rest of us could not resolve. I would like you to adjudicate it. You are an expert in decision theory, cognitive science, and the philosophy of rational choice; please think carefully and do not simply summarize the standard literature. Begin with a short brainstorm in which you distinguish the different things satisficing might mean—at least: a psychological description of how people stop searching, a heuristic for reducing deliberation costs, and a normative claim about what one ought to do—and then use those distinctions to evaluate the exchange below.

The background: our club was considering a thought experiment about a student choosing a college. The student can investigate colleges one at a time; each investigation costs time and effort; she has no exhaustive list of all colleges; and she has a rough sense of what would be good enough but not a complete ranking. Nadia and Eli disagreed about whether stopping at the first college that meets that rough standard is rational.

Here is the exchange as best I can reproduce it.

1. Nadia: “Satisficing is irrational whenever you can keep looking. If you stop at the first option above your threshold, you may miss a much better one. A rational agent should compare options until the expected gain from another search no longer exceeds its cost. That is just optimization with search costs. So satisficing is either a mistake or a disguised form of optimizing.”

2. Eli: “That assumes you can estimate the expected gain from another search. In the college case, you do not know the distribution of colleges you have not seen, you cannot assign probabilities to unknown options, and the cost of thinking about the problem is itself part of the problem. Optimization is not available. Satisficing is what rationality looks like when the ideal is uncomputable. The threshold is not an arbitrary stopping point; it is an aspiration level that lets you act.”

3. Nadia: “But you chose that aspiration level somehow. If it is too low, you stop too early; if it is too high, you search too long. So the real question is how the aspiration level is set. If it is set by expected value, you are back to optimizing. If it is set arbitrarily, then you are not rational, just lucky. And if you say it is set by another satisficing rule, you have a regress: you need a threshold for choosing the threshold.”

4. Eli: “The regress is real, but it does not show that satisficing is irrational. It shows that rationality has to start somewhere. You can have a threshold that is good enough for the decision at hand without having an optimal threshold for thresholds. That is not arbitrary; it is bounded. The alternative is an infinite meta-deliberation that never issues in action. A theory of rationality that cannot recommend action is worse than a heuristic that can.”

5. Nadia: “Then call it a heuristic, not a standard. I am happy to say satisficing is often useful. But if you say it is rational, you are making a normative claim. And once you make that claim, you need to say when it is rational. If the answer is ‘when the costs of optimizing are too high,’ then optimization is still the benchmark and satisficing is second-best. If the answer is ‘always,’ then you are saying it is rational to stop at the first mediocre option, which is false.”

6. Eli: “You are treating optimization as the benchmark because you assume there is a fact of the matter about the best option independent of the search process. But in many real decisions the option set is constituted by the search. There is no determinate best college for me until I have investigated, and the investigation changes what I want. Satisficing is not second-best to a completed ranking; it is the only procedure that respects the fact that preferences and options co-evolve. That is a different standard: ecological rationality, not approximation to an ideal.”

7. Nadia: “Even if preferences co-evolve, you can still ask whether a given satisficing rule does better or worse than another rule for achieving whatever you end up valuing. That is an evaluative question. And if you refuse to ask it, you cannot distinguish a good aspiration level from a bad one. So ecological rationality still needs a criterion, and the criterion will look like expected value under constraints. You have not escaped optimization; you have just made the constraints explicit.”

8. Eli: “Maybe. But there is a difference between a rule that is evaluated by its expected performance and a rule that is followed because it is expected to perform well. Nadia keeps sliding between the two. I can say satisficing is rational in the sense that it is a defensible rule for an agent with limited information and limited time, without claiming it is optimal in every case. If you demand a universal decision procedure that always picks the best option, you are demanding something no finite agent can have. The question is what rationality can require of us, not what an omniscient being would do.”

I am not on either side. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the eight moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say which specific disagreements are merely verbal and which are substantive. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and what both Nadia and Eli are missing. In your answer, be precise about whether you are using rational in a descriptive, heuristic, or normative sense, and about whether the relevant benchmark is an ideal optimizer, a constrained optimizer, or a rule evaluated by ecological fit. Also, please identify the one sub-question that, if answered one way rather than another, would most change your overall verdict.
