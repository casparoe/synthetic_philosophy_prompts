I'm in a reading group working through Mill's political economy, and we have stalled on the question of his late socialism in a way that has begun to feel structural rather than merely unresolved. The continuity readers point out that the Principles already treats distribution as a matter of social choice rather than natural law, already condemns the existing division of the product as unjust, already endorses worker cooperatives and profit-sharing, already looks ahead to a stationary state in which growth is not the goal, and already contains the chapter on the probable future of the working classes; on this reading, the posthumously published Chapters on Socialism and the Autobiography's remark that he and Harriet Taylor were commonly classed as socialists are the same position in a different key, and nothing turns on the lateness. The conversion readers point to the retraction of the wages-fund doctrine in his 1869 review of Thornton's On Labour, to the more sympathetic hearing given to socialist criticism in the late writings, to the influence first of Harriet Taylor and then of Helen Taylor, and to the fact that the Chapters are unfinished and were edited posthumously; on this reading, reading the late Mill through the Principles flattens a real shift. A third group holds that the question is malformed, because "socialism" in the 1860s and 1870s covered Owenism, Fourierism, Louis Blanc, Lassalle, Marx, Christian socialism, and cooperative experiments, and Mill endorsed some items on that menu while rejecting others, so that asking whether he was a socialist is like asking whether he was a liberal.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on whether Mill was a socialist. Is this a verbal dispute about the word, a substantive dispute about which texts and which periods should carry the weight, a dispute about what counts as evidence (finished works versus drafts versus letters versus the posthumous editorial situation), or is there a hidden question about whether the predicate "was a socialist" can be applied at all to a thinker writing before the term's later consolidation? My working hypothesis is that it is mostly a dispute about which Mill is canonical, with the verbal and evidentiary questions riding along, but I would like that hypothesis tested rather than confirmed. Use what each side actually says and what Mill actually wrote, and tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a reframing, a decision about which texts are authoritative, or an argument that the question should be abandoned. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
