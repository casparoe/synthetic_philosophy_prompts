Next month I am presenting a paper to a small reading group in philosophy of language, and what I need from you is not advice about the paper but a Q and A prep sheet for the hour after it.

The thesis I will defend: the triviality results for indicative conditionals should be read not as one more constraint that a truth-conditional semantics has to accommodate, but as a proof that the equation linking the probability of 'if A, then B' to the conditional probability of B given A is a category mistake. Indicative conditionals do not have truth conditions at all; the suppositional treatment is the only stable resting place, not a fallback for people who have already given up on truth conditions.

The argument I will actually give has three parts. First, the triviality proof runs on assumptions so weak that the standard escape routes — restrict the equation to a privileged class of 'simple' conditionals, deny that the language is closed under embedding, allow the equation to hold only approximately — leave no stable position: any restriction strong enough to block the proof must itself be stated in the language it is restricting, and I claim such a restriction either collapses back into the unrestricted equation or leaves the semantics with no account of compounds. Second, the impossibility result connecting the Ramsey test to the standard AGM postulates for belief revision (Gärdenfors's theorem) is not an artefact of AGM's crudeness but the qualitative shadow of the same phenomenon, and it shows that the acceptance of a conditional is not a propositional attitude with a content. Third, the alternative of keeping truth conditions and dropping the equation is less attractive than it looks, since it makes the near-universal practice of using conditional probability to govern conditional assertion a vast coincidence rather than a constraint on meaning. My positive proposal is that 'if' contributes not a content but a suppositional mode of assertion, and that the hard cases — negated conditionals, disjunctions of conditionals, conditionals with conditional consequents — are to be treated as asserted or embedded suppositions rather than as embedded propositions.

The room: eight regulars, and you should treat their commitments as fixed. Two are formal semanticists working in the Stalnaker/Lewis possible-worlds tradition; they regard the truth-conditional option as obviously live and will want to know exactly which premise of the proof I need and why I cannot have it. One is a probabilist who will press on closure and measure-theoretic detail — what the language is, whether the equation is assumed for every antecedent or only some, what happens as the antecedent probability goes to zero — and who has no patience for arguments from intuition. One is an epistemologist who knows the belief-revision literature well, will ask why the impossibility theorem is anything more than a proof that AGM is too crude, and will want to know what my alternative says about iterated revision. One works on natural-language semantics and will ask what my story predicts about the embedding data that motivated truth-conditional accounts in the first place. Two are graduate students who will go straight for compounds, because that is where the no-truth-conditions line looks worst. Nobody in the room works on expressivism, so I cannot assume the speech-act machinery will be granted to me.

What I want from you is the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do — roughly, what arrives in the first ten minutes versus the one that actually hurts — each phrased in the voice of the person who will raise it, with a note on who that is. For each objection give me a two-minute reply in my own voice, and then a candid note on where the reply is soft: the premise it silently relies on, or the follow-up I have no answer to. After the list, give me the three things I should concede outright rather than fight, the one objection I should expect to lose on if it comes and how to make losing it cheap, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. End with the single weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Write the sheet I will have in front of me during the question period, not another defence of the thesis; assume I know the literature and do not send me to anything.
