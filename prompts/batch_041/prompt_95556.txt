I'm a novelist, third book just went into copyedits, and the new one has a character who's a decision theorist, so I've somehow agreed to give a forty-minute talk at my local bookshop's Sunday salon explaining the one idea her whole subplot turns on, to about twenty people who read novels and not much else — no econ, no stats, nothing past arithmetic. The idea is the one usually called Good's theorem, about the value of information, and the version I want to get across is roughly this: if you're a decent Bayesian and someone hands you free information before you have to commit to a choice, taking it can't leave you worse off on average than not taking it — you can always just ignore it and do what you would've done anyway — and it only actually helps you when it changes what you'd do. Sounds flat until you notice how many people are dead sure the opposite is true.

Dont explain the theorem to me, I've got the theorem. What I want is a plan for explaining it. So: whats the order the pieces have to come in, and why that order and not another — what has to land before the next bit even makes sense. Then the one example that carries the whole talk, and what it needs to have in it and what it needs to leave out. My instinct keeps being the weather-and-umbrella thing and I have a feeling thats wrong for a reason I can't quite put my finger on, so tell me if it is and what to use instead. Then the misconceptions this room will walk in with or build on the spot — I'm guessing 'so more information is always better' and 'so you should never stick your head in the sand' are the big two, but I bet there are worse ones underneath, like people thinking it's about getting good news rather than about the choice, or thinking it says looking is *required* — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, not just that it'll happen. I also want the part where it breaks: I know it stops working once the information itself is something you care about (the surprise is ruined, the test result changes what you want), or once you're not the kind of agent the proof quietly assumes, and I want to say that out loud rather than pretend it's a law of nature. Give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they actually got it or just nodded along nicely. And tell me what to cut — I'll want to do the math and I definately shouldn't; say what to leave out and say it out loud so nobody feels cheated. And dont send me a reading list, I won't do it.
