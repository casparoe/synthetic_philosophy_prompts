In a seminar on the epistemology of logic we have spent several weeks on Carroll's tortoise, and a classmate of mine has worked up what he takes to be the real moral of the story: that the regress does not stop with the tortoise's peculiar obstinacy but infects every inference any of us ever makes. I have written his reconstruction out below and would like you to find the place where it should be stopped, if it should be.

His governing thesis, which he calls "premise parity," is that any entitlement licensing the move from premises to a conclusion must itself be something the reasoner is entitled to, on pain of the move being a bare fiat. He then argues:
(1) To be justified in believing Q on the strength of P and if-P-then-Q, one must be justified in the transition from those premises to Q, not merely in the premises themselves.
(2) As the tortoise shows, accepting P, accepting if-P-then-Q, and accepting the further conditional that if both of those are true then Q is true still does not amount to being justified in inferring Q; the further conditional is just one more premise.
(3) By premise parity, the entitlement to make the transition must itself be something one is entitled to.
(4) Any justification of a rule of inference must proceed by way of some inference, since there is no other way to justify anything.
(5) If the inference that justifies a rule is that very rule, the justification is circular; if it is a different rule, that rule stands in need of justification in turn, and so on.
(6) A circular chain justifies nothing, and an infinite chain is never completed and so justifies nothing either.
(7) Hence no rule of inference is justified; hence no inferential transition is justified; hence no belief reached by inference is justified.
(8) Since nearly everything any of us believes is reached by inference, nearly nothing we believe is justified.

He is not naive about the obvious replies. He says it is no help to insist that the entitlement to infer is non-inferential, because an entitlement one has is still something one holds, and anything held is either a premise or a fiat; nor is it any help to say that some rules are meaning-constitutive, because that would put the meanings of the connectives beyond criticism, which he regards as worse than the regress. What I would most like to keep, if I can, is (1), which seems to me the whole substance of an epistemology of inference, together with the plain conviction that we are sometimes justified in believing what we deduce. Tell me which numbered step to give up, why it looked so plausible, and what giving it up costs. Does rejecting it commit me to foundationalism about basic rules, to a non-inferential entitlement I cannot describe, to a regress I am forced to call benign, or to something else again? And if the trouble is a word quietly doing two jobs, say which word and where the two jobs come apart.
