I sit on the committee of a student society that is midway through an unusually heated argument about how we elect our officers, and two members of the committee have spent a fortnight going back and forth about what Arrow's impossibility theorem does and does not show. I have read the statement of the theorem and can follow the shape of the proof, but I cannot tell which of them is overclaiming, and I would rather have the exchange adjudicated as it actually happened than be given a general essay on social choice. For what it is worth, the two of them are, respectively, a mathematician who has just discovered the result and a third-year economist, and neither is a fool.

The exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Ravi: "Arrow proved that no voting system can satisfy a short list of obviously reasonable requirements all at once — no dictatorship, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and free rein over the preferences people may hold. So there is no fair way to run an election with three or more candidates. Whatever method we adopt, we are simply choosing which defects to live with, and the mathematics settles the matter." (2) Imogen: "It settles nothing of the kind. It is a theorem about four conditions, not about fairness, and the sensible response is to identify the weakest condition and drop it. Independence of irrelevant alternatives is the obvious candidate: it says the social ranking of two candidates may depend only on how individuals rank those two, which forbids the method from using any information about how strongly people prefer one option to another. Adopt approval voting, or scoring, or a Borda count, and you get a method that is well defined, unanimous and not dictatorial; the theorem simply does not bite. So the lesson is not that fairness is impossible but that we must decide, knowingly, which value to sacrifice — and that is a political question, not a mathematical one." (3) Ravi: "Every one of those escape routes has a matching pathology, and I don't think that is a coincidence. Gibbard and Satterthwaite showed that any non-dictatorial method choosing among three or more options can be gamed by someone who misreports their preferences, so Borda and scoring do not escape; they relocate the impossibility from honesty to strategy. And Borda's breach of independence is precisely what makes it vulnerable to nomination games: if a faction puts up a near-duplicate of its rival's candidate, the ranking of the original two can reverse. Trading a theorem for a manipulable fudge is not obviously progress." (4) Imogen: "Manipulability is a fact about incentives under fixed rules, not a fact about fairness, and a method that can be gamed may still be the fairest one available. More to the point, the theorem's assumptions are doing all the work: it needs three or more alternatives, an unrestricted domain of preference profiles, and purely ordinal inputs. Relax any one of those and the impossibility dissolves — with only two candidates majority rule satisfies all four conditions at once, and if preferences are single-peaked, as they often are for a single left-to-right question, majority rule behaves perfectly well. So 'democracy is incoherent' is a wild overreach from a result that really only says that ordinal aggregation over an unrestricted domain cannot be flawless."

Please go through their exchange move by move, rating each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating briefly. I would like you to be precise about which claims are matters of mathematics and which are further normative or interpretive claims smuggled in alongside them; to say explicitly what the theorem does and does not establish about the fairness of concrete methods such as plurality, Borda, approval and scoring; to identify what each of them is missing that the other half-sees; and finally to say who has the better overall position, and why. Please write your answer in British English.
