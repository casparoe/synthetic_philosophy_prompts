I've been going to a reading group on decision theory and we keep circling back to the Ellsberg case, and at this point I think we might be arguing about something other than what we think we're arguing about. The setup, for context: an urn with 90 balls, 30 of them red, the other 60 some unknown mix of black and yellow. Most of us would rather bet on red than on black, since red is a known 1 in 3 and black is anywhere from 0 to 2 in 3. And most of us would rather bet on black-or-yellow than on red-or-yellow, for the mirror-image reason. But you can't get both of those preferences out of a single probability for each color plus maximizing expected value, because the first says you think red is likelier than black and the second says the opposite.

The expected-utility people in the group say fine, so much the worse for those preferences; they're incoherent and you should straighten them out. The other half say no, so much the worse for the theory; a theory that can't even represent a sensible attitude -- that when the odds are unknown you'd just rather not bet on them -- is too narrow. We've been going around this for months and nobody budges, and it doesnt feel like either side is being dumb.

What I want is not a verdict on who's right but a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. My hunch, and please treat it as a hunch, is that the two sides are quietly answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking what it takes for a set of preferences to be written down as expected-value maximization and answering that, correctly, while the other is asking what you should actually do when you don't have a single number to plug in, and the two answers just don't touch. If that's right, then alot of the heat is verbal -- about whether 'rational' means internally consistent or actually correct -- and the real question underneath is whether your probabilities have to be one number or whether a set of numbers, or a probability over numbers, can be a legitimate state to be in.

But I'm not sure, and here's a wrinkle that makes me doubt my own hunch. Suppose you're indifferent between betting on red and betting on black in an urn whose mix you don't know. That indifference, if you're an expected-value person, forces you to think red and black are each 50-50, which means you should be just as happy betting on a fair coin as on either color. But most people still prefer the coin. If the whole thing were about missing numbers, why would the preference survive once the numbers match? That smells less like a verbal dispute and more like the two sides having genuinely different starting points about what a probability is even supposed to be -- a summary of your actual information, or a state of your head that can be more or less justified.

So diagnose it properly. Tell me whether this is a verbal fight, a clash of bedrock intuitions, a hidden empirical question about whether people's avoidance of the ambiguous bet is a stable and coherent attitude or a mistake that would evaporate on reflection, or some combination -- and back the diagnosis with what each side actually commits itself to, not a tour of decision theories. Then tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the verbal part, an argument that pins one side to something it can't defend, a case where the two attitudes come apart, or a straight admission that it's bedrock and no argument will move it. Write it in plain paragraphs, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables -- just prose like you'd write in an email.
