I am the TA for a third-year metaphysics course and I am marking the time-travel question from the take-home midterm. The question as it was set and one student's answer are below. Please grade the answer out of 10 — telling me briefly how you weight the three parts — and give me the comments that justify the grade, point by point: for each of (a), (b) and (c), what the student gets right, what is wrong or unsupported, and whether the problem is a substantive error or only a matter of exposition. Where a claim is contestable rather than plainly wrong, say so, and say whether the argument as written establishes the conclusion drawn from it. Please also flag any tension within the answer itself, and let credit turn on the arguments rather than on fluency of presentation.

Exam question. Consider a world W in which the laws of physics permit closed timelike curves, so that a person can travel into her own past and interact with it. The physicists of W also accept a further principle, which we will call the Closure Condition (CC): whatever happens in W, the resulting history is never self-contradictory — no process that forms part of a closed causal loop yields an outcome that conflicts with the rest of the history. W's physicists summarise CC by saying that history protects itself.

Suppose a traveller in W, Anna, goes back in time intending to kill her grandfather before he has any children. (a) Does CC make it true that Anna is unable to kill her grandfather? (b) Can Anna rationally deliberate about whether to make the attempt? (c) Is CC a substantive law of W or an empty truism, and what does either answer imply about whether the grandfather paradox is a problem for time travel?

Student's answer. (a) Nothing here is a formal contradiction; the appearance of paradox comes from wanting to keep all three of time travel, Anna's competence with a gun, and the fact that her grandfather survived long enough to have descendants. CC does not remove Anna's abilities; it removes the possibility that her abilities are exercised successfully. She has the capacity and the means to make the attempt, and nothing in the laws of W singles out her trigger finger. What CC rules out is the combination of Anna's firing and her grandfather's death, because a history containing both is not a history of W at all: Anna exists, so her grandfather survived. So 'Anna can kill her grandfather' is true if 'can' means that she has the capacity and the means to attempt it, and false if 'can' means that her success is a way W's history could have gone. When the failure comes it will have an ordinary explanation — a misfire, a slip, a change of heart — but CC does not predict which.

(b) Anna cannot rationally deliberate about the attempt. Deliberation makes sense only where the outcome is open, and CC settles the outcome in advance: the attempt fails. Anna may not know this, but her ignorance is beside the point, since what matters is whether the matter is genuinely undecided, and it is not. She can at most try to work out how W will stop her.

(c) CC is not a further law of W but a truism. A history in which Anna kills her grandfather is not a history at all; it contradicts itself, since a dead grandfather cannot have grandchildren. Since no consistent history can contradict itself, CC cannot be falsified by any experiment. It follows that the grandfather paradox was never a real threat to time travel in W: the only histories CC permits are consistent ones, so Anna is safe from paradox whatever she does.
