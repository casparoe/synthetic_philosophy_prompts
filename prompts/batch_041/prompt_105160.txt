ok this is a slightly weird request. I'm in a small reading group on bargaining / fair-division theory and next week is our last session, on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. I want to close by reading a poem out loud, and I want the poem itself to basically *be* the solution rather than just chat about it.

Quick version of KS in case you dont have it loaded: two parties, a disagreement point (what each of them gets if talks break down), and an ideal point (the most each could ever hope for). The feasible set is everything they could jointly agree to. KS says: draw a straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point, and take the point where that line first hits the edge of the feasible set (the Pareto frontier). That point has a nice property — each side's gain over the disagreement point is in the same proportion as their maximum possible gain. Its the "proportional" fair split.

So: write me a two-voice poem, speakers A and B, splitting some concrete thing (pick anything — a flat, a weekend, a dying parent's care, the bands royalties, whatever gives you real stakes). Constraints I actually care about:

- start them apart, at the disagreement point — give each voice a line or two of what they'd get / be if they walk away
- let them alternate, and let the poems *shape* be the argument: the two voices' shares should come out proportional to their ideals measured from the disagreement point — so its the gains that are in proportion, not the raw totals. If A stands to gain twice as much as B at best, A's gain in lines over their opening should be twice B's. Dont announce this, just do it, and make the counts legible to someone who bothers to count.
- the last lines have to land on the frontier — the point where neither can take more without the other losing, and where they both stop
- somewhere in the middle let one side's ideal grow, and let the other side's share grow along with it, so the proportionality survives the change
- it should still be a real poem, not a diagram. Meter and rhyme whatever you like, but the two voices should sound like different people with different things at stake, and someone whos never heard of KS should still find it a good poem

bonus if a reader who knows the theory can look at the final line-counts and go "yep, thats the KS point."
