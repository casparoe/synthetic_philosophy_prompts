I'm a graduate student sitting in on a seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences, and last week we covered information cascades — the model, developed by economists in the early 1990s, in which agents act in sequence, each seeing what those before them did but not why, and each deciding whether to follow his own private signal or to go along with the observed choices. A fellow student who has read more of this literature than I have offered the group the following summary, and since it sounded authoritative I have been repeating it in my own work without checking it. His account runs roughly as follows: an information cascade is the situation in which people abandon their own private information and simply copy the people ahead of them; the lesson of the theory is that crowds are irrational, since once a handful of people happen to make the same choice, everyone else falls into line, and the result is a contagion of imitation with no informational content; this is why markets are bubble-prone and why the supposed wisdom of crowds so often fails; and the model's central result is that a cascade, once it begins, is essentially irreversible, because by then everyone has committed to following the herd. He added, as a gloss, that herding and cascades are basically the same phenomenon, and that the theory explains fads and fashions precisely because in a cascade nobody is using his own information.

I suspect this is mostly right but subtly off in ways that matter, and I would like an audit rather than a correction in general terms. Please go claim by claim and tell me what a careful economist working on this would accept as stated, what is a standard popularization that loses something important, and what is simply wrong. I am especially unsure about two things: whether the irrationality framing is fair to a model whose whole point is supposed to be that following the crowd can be perfectly rational, and whether the irreversibility claim is right or is the opposite of what the theory actually says. It would also help to know how the cascade concept is supposed to relate to the other things economists call herding — the reputational and payoff-externality stories, say — since my colleague's gloss that they are "basically the same" seems too quick. Finally, tell me whether his concluding claims — that this explains bubbles, and that it discredits the wisdom of crowds — actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive, or whether they are doing work the model does not support.
