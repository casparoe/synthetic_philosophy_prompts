I'm an undergrad and somehow ended up as the student rep on my university's animal care committee (long story), and I have to vote Thursday on a protocol, so I need to write the one-page opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. I'm also taking a consciousness course this term, which is probably why this is suddenly eating my brain.

Here's the case. A lab wants to run avoidance-learning trials on 60 shore crabs: brief electric shocks to a leg, repeated over a few days, and they're asking us to waive the anesthetic requirement on the grounds that crabs don't have the kind of nervous system that supports experience. Our policy says invertebrates only need the full treatment if there's positive reason to think they can suffer, and if there is, we have to require anesthesia or a humane endpoint. What's making it hard is that one of the lab's people built a little portable rig that reads what she calls a "global ignition index" off the crab's central ganglia — basically how widely and how long a stimulus signal gets broadcast through the animal's nervous system — and in the pilot, the shocks produced almost no ignition. Their argument: no broadcast, no global workspace, no experience, so no anesthetic, and anyway anesthesia would wreck the learning data they're actually after. One of our members says that's just global workspace theory being used as a consciousness detector, and that GWT was built to explain access — what gets broadcast and made available to report and to control behaviour — not phenomenal consciousness, i.e. what it's like for the animal. Her view is that we should require anesthesia or a humane endpoint regardless, since nothing in the pilot speaks to whether the shocks hurt. The lab's reply is that if we won't act on the only relevant evidence they can produce, the policy is decoration.

Draft the opinion for me: recommend approve, approve with conditions, or reject, with the reasoning I can actually defend in the room, and be honest about the strongest thing on the losing side rather than just landing on the precautionary answer because it feels safe. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete enough that the committee can vote on them as written. I'd also like you to separate the questions that more data could settle from the ones that are conceptual and won't be settled by any experiment, and to say what single piece of evidence or argument would actually flip the recommendation. Max 600 words, one page, and don't bother with references — nobody in the room wants a reading list.
