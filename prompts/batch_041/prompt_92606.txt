I teach a second-year epistemology section, and I want to open the unit on the value of knowledge with something better than a lecture. The question I want my students to sort themselves by is the one the Meno leaves us with: what, if anything, makes knowledge worth more than a true belief that happens to be right. Most of them arrive holding what they take to be the obvious answer — knowledge is justified true belief, and the justification is the part that is worth having — and I would like a quiz that shows them they have in fact been choosing among a family of answers. Please write it: ten questions, each a concrete case or forced choice with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. Two constraints on the questions themselves. None of them may ask outright which theory the respondent holds (nothing in the shape of 'do you think knowledge is more than true belief?'); each has to be a case or a choice on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a respondent can answer it without seeing where it leads. And please invent the cases rather than recycling the ones my students have already met — the road to Larissa and the statues of Daedalus — so that a belief-implanting device, a person who could not revise her mind, two people who would act identically, and whatever else you devise do the diagnostic work instead.

The distinctions I want the scoring key to turn on are these. First, whether there is any difference in worth at all, or whether the supposed difference is a practical or institutional artifact of the way we treat beliefs we are entitled to rely on. Second, if there is a difference, whether it is instrumental — knowledge is likelier to stay true, likelier to survive challenge, likelier to be usable — or final. Third, if it is not merely instrumental, whether the extra worth attaches to the belief-state itself, to the knower's agency (the truth is to her credit, an achievement of hers), or to the standing that knowledge confers within a practice: the licence to assert, to rely, to teach, to stop inquiring. Fourth, and I suspect this one cuts across the rest, whether the difference is one of degree or of kind. The key should be able to place a respondent in at least the following positions: there is no difference; knowledge is better only because it is more stable or more likely to remain true; knowledge is true belief that is not a matter of luck; knowledge is a cognitive achievement, valuable as an exercise of agency; knowledge is valuable because of what it entitles the knower to do; knowledge's worth is inherited from understanding, which is the thing worth having for its own sake; and one unnamed leaf for the view that the extra worth is final and intrinsic to the belief-state, unconnected to the subject's agency or to any practice — the view that, if truth is the only thing that matters epistemically, makes the extra ingredient look like an ornament that adds nothing. Mark that leaf unnamed if nobody would defend it.

Give me the scoring key as a tree rather than a table of correlations: the branch nodes should be the questions, or the distinctions above, and the leaves the positions, so that I can see which answer patterns lead where. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, tell me whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. Flag honestly any two positions the quiz cannot separate, and name the further question that would separate them, rather than pretending the instrument is finer than it is. I would also like a short note on where the quiz will mislead — whether a respondent who thinks the whole value question is confused gets sorted into the no-difference leaf by default, for instance, or whether a respondent with no settled view is quietly assigned one. I am not asking for a survey of the literature and do not want a reading list; my students have no library to hand, and what I need is an instrument built out of cases and distinctions they can weigh sitting at a table.
