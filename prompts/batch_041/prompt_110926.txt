I am assembling a proposal for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in moral and political philosophy, and I would like you to draft it out of a half-formed idea of mine. The fellowship funds conceptual work, not fieldwork, and the review panel is philosophically literate but not specialized in this area: the document has to be legible to a generalist while being specific enough to show that I know where the real difficulty lies. Aim for roughly 1,500 words of polished prose, with a short month-by-month work plan.

The half-formed idea. Discussions of compromise between people who hold different value systems tend to run together three things that I think need separating. (i) Splitting the difference: the parties converge on some point between their starting positions, measured along a single shared scale, so that each simply surrenders ground. (ii) Exchange: each party concedes something it ranks comparatively low in order to secure something it ranks comparatively high, so that each ends up better off by its own lights than it would have been without agreement. (iii) Bundling: the agreement ranges over several issues at once, and the surplus comes from the parties' intensities differing across issues rather than from any concession on a single issue. Only (ii) and (iii) look like genuine gains rather than mutual losses that both sides merely tolerate, and both appear to require a condition with no analogue in (i): the parties' rankings must be misaligned enough that each has something the other wants more than it does — a kind of moral comparative advantage.

The hypothesis I want the proposal to develop and test. Exchange and bundling are available only within a band of divergence. Where the parties' values nearly coincide there is nothing to trade; where they are diametrically opposed there is no common currency in which the trade can be struck; the interesting cases lie between. Call this the bandwidth conjecture: there is a characteristic degree and structure of value divergence within which mutually improving compromise exists, and it fails at both extremes. Part of the appeal is that it would make both the optimism of the compromise literature and the pessimism of the incommensurability literature wrong in the same way.

The proposal should contain all of the following.

1. A statement of the research question precise enough to have a determinate answer, with the bandwidth conjecture as the thing to be established, refuted, or replaced.
2. Why the positions already on the table leave it open, set out as arguments rather than as a survey: the view that compromise is demanded by respect for persons, the view that incommensurable values make rational compromise impossible, and the view that compromise is merely a second-best accommodation to non-ideal circumstances. Say what each of them settles and what it leaves untouched.
3. The approach, and why it rather than the obvious alternatives. I expect the core to be the smallest possible formal model — two or a few agents with rankings over a finite set of options or issue-bundles — in which exchange compromises can be defined, existence conditions stated, and the boundaries of the band located. Explain why formalizing pays here rather than remaining at the level of cases, and why I am not simply borrowing a bargaining or social-choice framework wholesale.
4. What a result would look like, and what would count as failure. Be explicit about the failure mode I am most worried about: if any exchange compromise turns out to be re-describable as splitting the difference over a suitably enriched space of options, then the distinction I am trading on collapses and the project has no subject matter. Say how I would detect that and what I would do if I found it.
5. A realistic scope for twelve months: what gets done, what gets deliberately left out, and where the project could balloon past the time available.

Two constraints on the draft. First, no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; I will supply those myself. Where you need to refer to a recognizable position, describe it by its content rather than by the name of a proponent, unless the name is one you are confident about. Second, present the three-way distinction and the bandwidth conjecture as the working hypothesis of the project, not as an established view, and do not attribute them to anyone.

Finally, if on reflection you think the three-way distinction is unstable, or that the bandwidth conjecture is either trivially true or trivially false once the model is written down, say so in the proposal and give me the repair you would make. I would rather have that than have it papered over.
