I am assembling a small anthology of philosophical verse for a reading group that has spent the term on the metaphysics of mind, and I want one poem that takes the epiphenomenalist's side seriously rather than as a straw man. Please compose a pantoum: sixteen lines in four quatrains, observing the form's rule of repetition, so that the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and the poem's opening line returns as its closing line.

The speaker is a convinced epiphenomenalist about phenomenal properties: someone who holds that experiences exist, that they have their characteristic felt character, and that they nonetheless do no causal work. On this view experiences are caused by, or at least reliably correlated with, physical goings-on in the brain, but they cause nothing themselves, including the speaker's own reports about them. The poem must also be a phenomenal judgment: the speaker is, within the poem, accurately saying what their experience is like, and is aware that this very act of saying is one of the things their position says the experience cannot have brought about.

Two of the repeated lines should carry the load. One should be a first-person report of a felt quality, concrete and specific rather than a gesture at the word qualia. The other should state the epiphenomenalist thesis about causation. Because the form forces these lines to change places, the poem should end by arriving back at its own first line: the judgment recurring after the thesis that it was not caused by what it judges.

The closing movement should not resolve the tension but should register its exact shape. The epiphenomenalist may deny that the experience caused the report and locate the cause in the physical substrate instead, but then the reliability of the correlation between them, the fact that the report is so consistently about the experience that it seems to be about it, is left either brute or miraculous. A reader who knows the debate should recognize which horn is being grasped; a reader who does not should still find the poem intelligible and a little sad.

Please avoid treating epiphenomenalism as the denial that experience exists, confusing it with the problem of other minds or with the claim that we cannot know what our own experience is like, and letting epiphenomenal serve as a mere synonym for ineffable. The poem should be accurate enough that an epiphenomenalist would sign it.
