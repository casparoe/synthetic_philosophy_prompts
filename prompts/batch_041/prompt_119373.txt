I write about ideas for a living, and after a year of reporting on the decision sciences I have started keeping a list of the gambles I would and would not take, on the assumption that my gut is at least internally consistent. A guaranteed £900 beats a 90 per cent chance of £1,000, though both are worth £900 on average. A £1 ticket in a one-in-twenty-million draw for £10m is worth buying, though it returns 50p on average. I will pay £120 to be rid of a 1 per cent chance of a £10,000 loss, though the loss I am insuring against is £100 on average. Offered a certain £100 loss or a 30 per cent chance of a £400 loss, I take the gamble. And offered a 25 per cent chance of £1,000 or a 50 per cent chance of a ticket that is itself a 50 per cent chance of £1,000, I take the single-stage 25 per cent chance — even though the two are the same gamble, a quarter-chance of £1,000 either way.

The only principle I can put on paper is that what I mind is the shape of the chances rather than their average: I act as though a small chance of a bad outcome were larger than it is, as though a small chance of a good outcome were larger too, and as though a 90 per cent chance of anything were less certain than it is. That feels to me like one idea, but I cannot get it to yield the fifth verdict, and I suspect it is quietly smuggling in a second one.

Tell me what the best version of the principle is. State it precisely, name the verdict that forces each clause, say what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a coin-flip whose odds are my own estimate rather than a coin's, or a gamble whose worst outcome I could buy back later — and say plainly which of my five verdicts it cannot keep, and whether that verdict or the principle is the thing to give up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question, and please answer in British English.
