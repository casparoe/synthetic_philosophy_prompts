Every review site I use does the same thing: before I can rate a restaurant or a product or a landlord, it shows me the running star average and how many people have rated it already, and then it turns around and treats that same average as hard evidence — it sorts its search results by it, pins the number and the review count to the top of the page like that settles whether the thing is any good. But those two moves can't both be innocent. If I can see everyone else's scores before I give mine, my score isn't independant of theirs; I'm partly just falling in line, so the number that comes out the other end isn't 2,300 separate opinions, it's closer to one opinion echoed 2,300 times. So what does the practice actually presuppose? That each rater is judging the thing itself on their own? That averaging a pile of scores is a legitimate way to learn a fact about the world? Or maybe that there's no fact of the matter and the average just *is* the quality? Reconstruct whatever view of evidence, belief, and value the practice seems to need. Tell me which parts it genuinely can't do without versus which are just nice to have, and where it's underdetermined so that several theories would license it. Then: if I swap the assumption of independent raters for the crowd-copying-crowd story, what actually has to change — the math, the interface, the way we talk about the number, all of it? And is the same problem lurking when the site shows me what other people bought after viewing this thing? This has been bugging me, thanks.
