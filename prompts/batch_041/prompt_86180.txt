I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of early modern moral theology and contemporary decision theory, and next month I have to present a short paper to a reading group that mixes historians of philosophy with people who work on formal ethics. My working thesis is that the present literature on moral uncertainty — the question of how one ought to act when one is unsure which moral theory is correct, and in particular the proposal that one should distribute credences over the candidate theories and then choose the act with the highest expected choiceworthiness, weighting each theory's verdict by how much credence it enjoys — has a far closer ancestor in the seventeenth-century controversy over probabilism than anyone in the room appears to realise. I have been arguing the point with a friend who insists that Pascal, who died in 1662, would simply have dismissed the entire enterprise as a new laxism dressed up in mathematics, and that the comparison therefore illuminates nothing. I am not persuaded, but I want a properly worked-out answer rather than a debating point, so please give me one. Assume no web access: do not attempt to supply citations, and do not lean on particular pieces of scholarship you cannot be sure of; where you rely on a specific claim about Pascal's texts, mark it as such.

Act as a historian of early modern philosophy and moral theology who is equally at home in contemporary normative decision theory, and give me your best-supported prediction of what Pascal would have made of this literature. You may take the following as given. In the Provincial Letters, written under a pseudonym in 1656–57, Pascal attacked the casuistry of the Jesuits and defended Antoine Arnauld against censure by the Sorbonne; a central target was the doctrine of probabilism, on which a person may, in difficult matters of conscience, safely follow a merely probable opinion even where the contrary opinion is more probable — the rival positions being probabiliorism, which obliges one to follow the more probable opinion, and tutiorism, which obliges one to take the morally safer course. Pascal was also the author of the wager argument in the Pensées, published after his death, which is itself a piece of decision theory under uncertainty with infinite stakes; my friend thinks this is a curiosity, I think it is the crux. Your prediction should address, at minimum: whether Pascal would accept the framing of the problem at all, or would treat the very idea of weighing moral theories by credence as a continuation of the disease he diagnosed in the casuists; what he would make of the fact that different moral theories may not share a common cardinal scale, so that credence-weighting seems to presuppose comparisons the theories themselves do not license; what he would say about the suspicion that one can gerrymander a view into many near-variants in order to inflate its weight, and whether that worry is the modern descendant of the casuists' counting of authorities and opinions; whether the wager supplies a precedent that legitimises expected-value reasoning about what one ought to do, or whether Pascal drew a principled line between betting on God and calculating one's way through a moral dilemma, with infinite or lexically prior stakes on one side of that line; and whether he would see hedging — doing a little of what each theory demands — as prudence or as a failure of the singleness of heart he prized.

Begin with a brief brainstorm of the three or four features of Pascal's moral and theological outlook that any responsible prediction has to respect, and then build the answer on them rather than assembling a list of scattered observations. Reason carefully about what follows from those commitments, give a determinate verdict on my friend's claim rather than hedging it into nothing, and end with a short section listing the points at which the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by what survives of his writing. Please write in British English, at around 1,000 to 1,400 words, and do not include a bibliography or any citation apparatus.
