I have no academic training in linguistics or philosophy of mind; I taught myself enough machine learning to build and fine-tune small language models, and for the past couple of years I have been working through, on my own, the argument that a child could not acquire grammar from the speech it hears, because the evidence available to the child is too thin to single out the grammar it ends up with.

Restate that argument in the vocabulary I actually think in: training data and its distribution, hypothesis spaces, inductive bias, hard constraints versus soft regularizers, generalization and sample complexity, memorization versus generalization, priors and priors over priors, distribution shift, and the gap between what a model can represent and what training will actually find. Treat the child as a learner and the ambient speech as a corpus, and note that, as I understand it, the corpus arrives unlabelled, with nothing marking which of the utterances are well formed. Be as faithful as you can, and do not quietly upgrade or downgrade the original claim in the course of translating it.

Then give me the residue report. First, what survives intact — and in particular, tell me whether the central claim collapses into the observation that many hypotheses fit any finite corpus, which I take to be uncontroversial and to prove nothing by itself. Second, what survives only in a distorted form, and in which direction the distortion pulls: does the translation make the argument look stronger than it is, by turning a claim about human children into a theorem about learners in general, or weaker, by making it sound like a complaint about dataset size? Third, what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I suspect three things, and I would like to know whether I am right: the difference between an inductive bias that merely makes some hypotheses cheaper to reach and one that makes the wrong hypothesis unrepresentable; the fact that grammaticality is a matter of correctness rather than frequency, so that no corpus, however large, tells a learner which of two internally consistent conventions is the right one; and the possibility that the argument requires a prior that cannot itself be learned from anything, on pain of an infinite regress.

Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original argument claims less than it appears to, and what I would have to add to my vocabulary in order to see what it is really claiming — along with whatever that addition would commit me to that I might not want to accept.
