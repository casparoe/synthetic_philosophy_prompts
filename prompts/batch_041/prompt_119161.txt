I'm a philosophy student taking a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and last week the instructor devoted a whole session to the Condorcet jury theorem. He is a political scientist by training and presented the result as much as a moral as a piece of mathematics, so I wrote down his central claims almost as he said them: 'Condorcet's theorem is the mathematical vindication of majority rule. If each voter is more likely than not to get a yes-or-no question right, and their judgments are independent of one another, then the majority is more likely to be right than any single voter, and the larger the group the closer the probability of a correct majority comes to certainty — which is why the result is sometimes called the miracle of aggregation. What this means for democracy is that a large electorate of only moderately informed citizens will almost certainly get the answer right, and that individual competence hardly matters: ten thousand voters each only 51 per cent likely to be right will be right with probability above 0.9999. The theorem also rescues majority rule from Condorcet's other famous discovery, the cyclical majority — A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A — since with enough voters the majority is right anyway. And of course the converse holds too, so that if the voters are worse than a coin flip the majority is almost certainly wrong and the minority should be followed. As for the assumptions: independence is really just a way of saying that voters must not collude or coordinate before voting, which the secret ballot more or less guarantees; and since the theorem is about majority voting as such, it tells us something about any majority vote — a jury deciding guilt, a referendum on a tax schedule, a parliamentary division.'

I came away with the strong sense that the theorem is being asked to underwrite conclusions it cannot underwrite, but I cannot say where the trouble starts; my training is in philosophy, not in probability, and every step of his argument sounded plausible as he made it. Could you audit the presentation claim by claim, sorting what a specialist would accept as stated, what is a standard simplification of a more modest result (and what the accurate version of that claim would be), and what is simply wrong or unsupported? Where a claim is an overreach, I'd like to know what the theorem actually assumes and what it actually delivers — what has to be true of the question being decided, and of the voters, for the result to apply — and where those requirements bite in the political applications he has in mind. It would also help to know whether the vocabulary he attaches to the result, such as calling it the miracle of aggregation, is apt.

Finally, please say which of his practical conclusions survive correction: that a large electorate of moderately informed citizens is epistemically trustworthy, that individual competence hardly matters, that the secret ballot secures the independence the theorem needs, and that the result bears on majority voting of every kind. If some of these fall, I'd like to know whether they fall because the mathematics was misreported or because the theorem's conditions are simply not met by real electorates — and what a defender of the theorem would say in the instructor's place.
