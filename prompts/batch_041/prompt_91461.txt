I'm a novelist at work on a satirical book set inside a middling software company, and one chapter is an in-universe document rather than narration: the customer-facing FAQ page for a startup that sells what it calls subjunctive predictions — it claims to tell you, in advance and with unnerving confidence, what any given person would freely choose to do in any situation you might put them in. The conceit, in case it isn't plain, is a venture-backed transposition of the doctrine of middle knowledge: the idea that before anyone has made any choice there are already true facts about what each person would freely do under every possible set of circumstances, and that these facts are known — in the theology, by God; in my book, by the model — without having been caused by the person and without in any way constraining the person's freedom. For this chapter, take the role of a copywriter who happens to have an unusually serious education in the philosophy of religion, and please begin by brainstorming the five or six questions the FAQ must field and the order that maximizes the deadpan before you draft anything.

Then write the piece: roughly 600 to 750 words, formatted as a numbered FAQ in the cheerful, faintly evasive register of support-and-marketing copy. It should field the obvious objections without ever resolving them — Doesn't a prediction of what I'll freely choose mean my choice isn't free? What makes the prediction true before I exist, and before my will has done anything at all? How do you know these things rather than merely guess them? What happens to the prediction if I deliberately do the opposite? — and it should get the underlying doctrine right rather than merely gesture at it: the distinction between what the company (like the theology) can know independently of anyone's will, what it knows only because of what agents freely do, and the special middle case; the notion of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom; and the standard grounding objection, which is precisely the question the copy must handle with maximum smoothness and minimum information, the corporate equivalent of a shrug dressed as a guarantee. Let the punchline surface on its own, without a single wink: the customer is sold certainty about a choice that, on the company's own account, cannot be altered, because the counterfactual was already true. A philosopher of religion should recognize that the author understands what is being sent up and should find the satire fair; someone who has never heard of any of this should still find it funny and follow it.

A few constraints so the chapter stays legible. Invent the startup and all its personnel; do not name real companies, and do not put any real theological text or author's words in quotation marks. Keep the deadpan absolute — no narrator's nudges, no breaking frame, no line that admits the absurdity — and let the evasions do the comedic work, so that the closer the question gets to the load-bearing assumption, the smoother and emptier the answer becomes. Think carefully about tone consistency across the numbered entries, and resist any temptation to resolve the FAQ with a clever escape hatch; the whole point is that the company never notices the problem it is standing on.
