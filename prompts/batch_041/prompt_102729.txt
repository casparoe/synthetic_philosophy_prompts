I retired last year after thirty-five years as a chemical engineer, and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy-of-math reading group at the local university — mostly to keep the brain moving, though I'm clearly the slowest person in the room. Last week one of the regulars, a retired math teacher who's read alot of pop-sci, gave us his summary of Gödel, and its been rattling around my head ever since. Roughly what he said: Gödel proved that in any consistent system rich enough to do arithmetic there are true statements the system can never prove, so mathematics can never be complete; and since the system can't prove its own consistency either, you have to step up to a stronger system to see the truth of the unprovable statement — but then that stronger system has its own blind spot, so there's no bottom to it. He then took a further step and said this is why physics can never have a final theory of everything, and also why no computer can match a human mind, because a person can look at the Gödel sentence and just see that it's true, whereas the machine is stuck inside the system. People nodded along and so did I, but a couple of the things he said felt like they were doing more work than they'd been given permission to do.

Could you go through it piece by piece and tell me, the way a logician would, what's fine, what's a fair simplification that's harmless over beers but not in a seminar, and what's actually wrong? I have a vague sense that "the system can't prove it but a stronger system can" is not the same thing as "we can never know it," and that the jump from a result about formal proofs to a claim about minds and about physics is smuggling something in, but I can't put my finger on the seam. I'd also like to know which of the conditions he rattled past — "consistent," "rich enough for arithmetic," and whatever else belongs on that list — are actually load-bearing, because I suspect some of the half-remembered counterexamples I've heard about (some arithmetic too weak to pull the trick off, some geometries that turn out to be perfectly decidable) are exactly where his phrasing goes slack.

And at the end, tell me whether the mind argument and the theory-of-everything argument follow from any version of the theorem at all, or whether they each need an extra premise he never stated — and if so, what that premise is and how controversial it is. Don't worry about giving me chapter and verse or who said what when; I just want the ideas sorted out, since I can't look any of this up and wouldn't trust myself to anyway.
