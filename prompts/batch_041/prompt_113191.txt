I am a novelist, and I am stuck on a scene that I cannot get right. My protagonist is a woman who has come to a small harbor town for a single winter to settle her late brother's affairs. On the morning of a funeral she stands in line at the town's only bakery, and the townspeople observe an unwritten rule that no one has ever explained to her: anyone carrying a covered dish, as several of the mourners are, goes to the front of the line, ahead of customers who arrived much earlier. She does not move aside, and the room goes quiet. Writing the awkwardness of that moment is easy; what troubles me is whether I have made her a villain without meaning to. One reader tells me she is simply rude, that when you are a guest in a place you follow its customs, full stop. Another insists the townspeople are the ones at fault for treating an arbitrary habit as though it were a law of nature and then punishing a stranger for failing to know it. I suspect both are missing something, but I cannot articulate what.

What I want from you is not a verdict about my invented town but a way of thinking about whether she has done something wrong — not whether she has been impolite, but whether she has violated anything that actually binds her. I am interested in the anatomy of the case: what makes a local practice the sort of thing a visitor is obligated to honor, and what makes it the sort of thing she is entitled to ignore? If the answer turns on whether the practice is just, or on whether the townspeople would extend the same deference to a stranger that they demand of her, or on whether she could reasonably have been expected to know the rule, I would like you to say which of those considerations does the real work and why the others are tempting but secondary. And I would like you to tell me plainly where the line falls between a custom that merely marks who belongs and one that creates a genuine claim on someone who does not.
