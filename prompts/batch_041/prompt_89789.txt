OK so I've been going around in circles on this and I want an outside read. I'm trying to pin down what I actually believe about the old complaint that utilitarianism treats a society like one giant person, adding up gains and losses across different people the way you'd add them up inside one person's life. The complaint says thats a mistake: seperate people aren't a single person, so you can't trade one person's agony for another person's bliss the way you can trade a bad year for a good one within a single life. Fine. But I can't get my own verdicts to line up, and I'd rather find out what principle is actually generating them than keep guessing.

Here are my gut verdicts.

1. Forcing someone to spend a year in pain so that they themselves get twenty healthy years after: fine. Nobody blinks. Intrapersonal tradeoffs are just what prudence is.

2. The same trade across two people -- one person's year of pain, a different person's twenty good years, neither consenting: not fine, even though the total is identical. This is the case that makes me think the separateness thing is real.

3. Diverting a runaway trolley so it kills one instead of five: fine.

4. Pushing a bystander in front of it to stop it: not fine. And I don't think that's just about pushing vs. diverting -- if I could stop it by remote control, and the only way was for that one person's body to absorb the impact, I still wouldn't.

5. Letting a factory emit pollution that will predictably kill three people, in exchange for saving a hundred lives elsewhere: fine, or at least I'd do it and sleep OK.

6. A million people each losing a dollar so one stranger gets a life-saving operation: fine.

7. A million people each losing a dollar so one stranger gets a slightly nicer car: not fine, wierdly, even though it's the same structure and the same total.

That's where I'm stuck. (4) and (5) both involve knowing some number of people will die because of what I do, and one feels like murder and the other feels like arithmetic I'm allowed to do. (3) and (4) differ only in the causal path. And (6) vs (7) says the size of the stakes flips something, which sounds suspiciously like the utilitarian calculus I thought I was rejecting.

I have a half-baked principle I'd like you to test. Call it the boundary reading: what separateness forbids isn't adding up costs and benefits across people, it's using one person's body or life as the material of someone else's benefit. So aggregating is fine as long as no single person is the instrument. Under that, (4) is forbidden because he's the instrument, (5) is fine because the three are collateral, (3) and (6) are fine. But it says nothing about (7), which is not instrumental at all and still feels wrong to me. So either I need a second clause, or (7) has to go.

What I want: state the best version of a principle that captures as many of (1) through (7) as possible, tell me which verdict it forces me to drop and whether dropping it is defensible, and then say what your principle predicts about two cases I genuinely haven't decided. (a) A lottery that kills one random person a year but saves fifty, where everyone including the eventual victim is told the odds in advance and takes their chances. (b) A case where the person bearing the cost is also the person getting the benefit, but the cost is huge and the benefit is small -- conscripting someone for a war that will mostly benefit them, at a personal price they'd never accept. Also, if the separateness-of-persons thing turns out to be nothing but the doing/allowing distinction wearing a fancier hat, just say so -- I'd rather know than keep a distinction that's doing no work.
