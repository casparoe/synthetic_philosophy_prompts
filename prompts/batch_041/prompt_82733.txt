I am an analyst on a long-horizon risk team, and I keep running into a difficulty that I would like explained as a conceptual matter rather than settled with more data. My colleagues are comfortable with probability and regression and much less comfortable with the philosophy of statistics, so I want an explanation of the joints of the problem: argumentative rather than a survey, and not a reading list — no citations, no names of researchers, no bibliography, and no appeal to what some named authority has said.

The setting. Suppose a register of polity-episodes, with the following rule of entry and exit: an episode opens when a single sovereign authority is established over a named core territory, and it closes when that authority is replaced — by conquest, partition, dynastic extinction, or internal reconstitution — by a different one. Treat the rule as given for now; part of what I want explained is how much it is quietly assuming. Take the following figures as stipulated rather than as claims about the actual historical record: 240 closed episodes and 31 open ones; the closed episodes have a median duration of 140 years; roughly one closed episode in nine reaches 300 years; among those that reach 300, the median total duration is 620 years; the 31 open episodes have a median current age of 210 years; and the probability of closure, estimated from the closed episodes, declines rather than rises with age.

Two analysts look at this table. The first says: our subject is a 300-year-old order, and among episodes that reached 300 half were over by 620, so its median remaining life is around 320 years — but the figure that ought to discipline our expectations is the unconditional one, since half of all episodes are over by 140 years, and a median computed over the long-lived tells us about the long-lived rather than about our subject. The second says: read the hazard off the register; it declines with age; therefore the longer an order has already lasted, the longer it will last in total, and a median drawn from the whole register describes a different population from the one we are asking about. They are working from the same table.

What I want explained:

1. What the two analysts are actually disagreeing about, ordered from the superficial to the deep. I do not want the answer to be that they use different base rates; I want the locus of the disagreement named precisely, including where each of them is relying on something the register cannot supply.

2. Whether the disagreement is empirical or conceptual: is there a body of data that would settle it, or is there a reason why data of this kind cannot settle it? Be specific about which of the difficulties are difficulties about the world and which are difficulties about the partition of the world.

3. The reference class problem as it bites here, and in particular how the entry-and-exit rule individuates episodes. What must the rule assume about when a political order is the same order over time, and why does that assumption affect the base rate rather than merely the bookkeeping?

4. What follows from the open episodes being open. Explain what the 31 unfinished episodes can and cannot contribute — including any selection or sampling effect that makes the set of orders standing today unrepresentative of the orders in the register, and any assumption of exchangeability between finished and unfinished episodes that the first analyst's conditional median requires.

5. A minimal constructed example, three or four lines, in which one body of duration data yields opposite outside-view forecasts under two defensible readings — something I can put on a slide to show my colleagues that the difficulty is not a failure of care on anyone's part.

6. What would have to be true for an outside-view forecast about a still-standing order to be well-posed at all; and if those conditions are not met, what the honest residue of the method is — what it can still tell me, what it cannot, and what I should say out loud when I use it anyway.

7. Where I am most likely to be fooling myself in wanting the outside view to work here: the version of the mistake that would be most tempting to someone in my position.

Treat the stipulated figures as fixed, and do not substitute figures from the real historical record for them. If you think the stipulation is internally incoherent — for instance, if the conditional median and the shape of the hazard cannot both hold — say so and show me exactly where the tension lies, since that would also be useful. If you need concrete historical cases to make a point about contested individuation, choose your own and keep them uncontroversial; I care about the structure of the problem, not about which empires illustrate it.
