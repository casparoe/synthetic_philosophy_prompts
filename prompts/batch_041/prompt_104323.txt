I am a retired natural scientist — four decades in experimental physics, mostly instrument design and the metrology of things that are hard to measure — who has spent the past few years reading philosophy without much of a guide. I have arrived at a puzzle that I suspect is philosophical rather than empirical, and I would like you to work through it with me in a disciplined way.

I would like you to take the role of an expert in the philosophy of the social sciences, with a secondary competence in the metaphysics and ethics of harm. Please think carefully and reason explicitly rather than producing a tidy summary. Begin by brainstorming: set out the candidate theories, note where they diverge, and say which parts of the analysis are genuinely underdetermined before you commit to a reading. I would rather see your working than your verdict.

Here is the practice I want you to examine. A large quantitative literature on conflict and crime codes events for a database according to written rules. A killing is entered only if it is a direct, intentional act of physical force by an identifiable perpetrator against an identifiable victim; deaths that follow from the disruption a conflict causes — famine, epidemic, exposure, the collapse of hospitals — are recorded, if at all, in a separate column or not at all. A conflict is promoted to the category of a full war only once it crosses a stipulated body count within a stipulated period. Homicide is tallied by a different agency on a different basis, and the two series are then combined when someone wants to say something about the level of violence in a society. Some of the summary claims are stated as absolute counts and some as rates per hundred thousand; the choice is rarely argued for and sometimes seems to track which one makes the trend look steeper. And out of this machinery, together with a great deal of historical scholarship, a well-known body of work draws a large conclusion: that violence has declined over the long run.

My question is not whether that conclusion is true. It is what theory of violence this practice presupposes. When the coding rules count a bombing but not the famine that follows it, and count a homicide but not a death from an untreated disease, what view of what violence is are they assuming? Does the practice require the view that violence is essentially direct intentional force, or only that such force is the tractable proxy — and are those two positions even distinguishable from the inside? Does it presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about how violent a period was, or is the number a stipulative operationalization that has been quietly promoted to a description? I spent my career distinguishing the measurand from the measurement procedure, and I want to know whether this literature conflates them.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories of violence would license the very same counting rules. Then say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival: one on which violence includes the foreseeable harms of institutional arrangements, or one on which what matters is not the act but the suffering produced, whatever its cause. Finally, tell me whether the per-capita-versus-absolute choice is itself philosophically loaded, or merely a presentational matter. Where the practice forces no answer, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.
