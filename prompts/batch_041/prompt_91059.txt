The question:

Q4 (20 marks, 5 each). (a) Distinguish two things one might mean by calling oneself a reductionist about testimony: justification-reductionism, the thesis that a hearer's testimonial belief is justified only if she has grounds for taking the speaker to be reliable, and genesis-reductionism, the thesis that our tendency to believe what we are told is acquired from experience rather than being innate. (b) Within justification-reductionism, distinguish a global version from a local version, and say what each demands of a hearer in a particular case. (c) Hume and Reid are conventionally placed on opposite sides of the reductionism/anti-reductionism dispute. Say which is which and why, and say whether either of them is thereby committed to the matching genesis thesis. (d) A child cannot learn a language without trusting what she is told, well before she is in a position to check it. Say how far this tells against justification-reductionism, distinguishing the global and the local versions.

The student's answer:

Hume is the reductionist and Reid the anti-reductionist, and the disagreement is about whether testimony can justify belief on its own. Hume's argument is that there is no a priori connection between a person's saying that p and p's being the case, so whatever confidence we place in testimony has to be derived from experience: we observe that people mostly report things as they are, and this observed conformity is what makes testimony credible. So a hearer needs grounds for trusting a speaker, and those grounds have to come from outside testimony, otherwise the reasoning would be circular. Reid takes the opposite view. Credulity, for him, is an innate principle of our constitution, on a par with the principles that make us believe in the external world and in other minds, and we are entitled to trust what we are told unless we have some positive reason not to. Testimony is therefore a basic source of justification rather than something we have to earn a licence for, witness by witness. Since Reid thinks the entitlement is basic, he does not think it can be defeated by contrary evidence: if a stranger tells me the water in the next town is poisoned and everything else I believe tells me that it is not, I am still justified in believing him, because the principle of credulity is part of my constitution and does not answer to the rest of my beliefs. As for the child, the argument in (d) bites hardest against the global reductionist, who wants a general justification for treating testimony as reliable in the first place, because the child has nothing non-testimonial to go on before she understands what is being said. But I would go further and say it refutes justification-reductionism as such, since the same point applies in every case. Whatever grounds I have for trusting this speaker on this occasion, I would have had to get them by trusting someone. And since Reid is right that we do not learn credulity from experience but are born with it, the genesis thesis is false, which is a further reason to reject the reductionist's demand for non-testimonial grounds.
