The question was: State the knowability principle, show how Fitch's argument gets to the conclusion that every truth is known, say which assumptions the derivation relies on, and explain what this means for someone who thinks all truths are knowable.

Here's what they wrote:

"The knowability principle says every truth is knowable: if p is true, then it's possible that someone knows that p. This is what anti-realists like Dummett need, because if truth isn't some mind-independent property but is tied to what could in principle be verified, then there can't be a truth that's in principle beyond all possible knowledge.

Fitch's argument: let p be a truth that nobody actually knows, so p and not-Kp. Consider the conjunction 'p and nobody knows that p'. That conjunction is itself a truth, since p is true and by assumption it isn't known. Now apply the knowability principle to it: it's possible to know the conjunction. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction — if you know A and B then you know A and you know B — it's possible to know p and possible to know that nobody knows p. Now, knowledge is infallible: you can't know something that isn't the case, and in particular you can't know that you don't know p. So the second conjunct can't hold together with the first, and we get a contradiction, namely that it's possible for p to be both known and not known. So the assumption that there's a truth nobody knows has to be false, and every truth is known.

The argument leans on three things: the knowability principle itself, closure of knowledge under conjunction, and the fact that knowledge entails truth (that's the infallibility step above). I think it's way more fragile than it looks though, because of the nested modalities — the derivation only goes through in a strong modal system like S5, and in something weaker like T you can't get from 'possibly known' to the contradiction. So it's really the modal logic doing the work.

What it means: the anti-realist can't have both the knowability principle and the everyday fact that there are truths nobody knows. Since that everyday fact is obviously true, the knowability principle has to go, and with it the anti-realist account of truth."
