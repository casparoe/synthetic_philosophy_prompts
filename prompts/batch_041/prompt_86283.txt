I'm a novelist and I've been losing an argument to my brother-in-law, who teaches philosophy of science, for about two years now. His line: to explain an event is just to show it had to happen, given a general law plus the particular facts on the ground. The pole's height explains the length of its shadow and the shadow explains the pole — same little deduction running either way — and the only reason one direction feels like an explanation and the other doesn't is a fact about us, not about explaining. Mine: what explains a historical event is a story, and a story deduces nothing. It makes an outcome feel reasonable, or possible, or inevitable in hindsight, and it does that partly by describing earlier events in terms of later ones. You can say a man was packing up to leave, but only after hes left. He says thats an explanation sketch, come back when I have laws.

I want to end this with a poem instead of a rebuttal. Give me a sestina — six end words, all six in every stanza, and the closing three lines have to cram them all in — where the form is his thesis: every stanza forced into the same six slots whether the material wants to go or not, and the envoi is where the story gets squeezed through the law's mold and either fits or splits. Pick the six end words yourself; at least one should be carrying the weight of "had to." It should be accurate enough that he can't wince — the deduction that runs both ways, the explanation that only runs one, and what the story is supposed to add — but it shouldn't just hand me the win. If the last lines can hold both without cheating, better. American English. No names, no citations, just get the ideas right.
