In a history unit on reparations, I noticed that the cases we studied are all called by the same name even though the arrangements differ in ways that seem to matter. Under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, the United States paid a fixed sum to each surviving person who had been interned during the war on the basis of Japanese ancestry; the children and grandchildren of internees received nothing in their own right, and Japanese Latin Americans who were taken from their homes and held in the same camps were excluded because they had not been citizens or permanent residents, although the wrong done to them was identical in kind. In the agreement West Germany signed in 1952, part of the money went to the State of Israel as a contribution toward the cost of resettling refugees — a state that did not exist when the crimes were committed — and part went to a conference of Jewish organizations that then distributed it to individual survivors, so that the recipients and the claimants were often not the same people. Proposals for slavery reparations, finally, would pay people who were never enslaved, selected by ancestry rather than by any injury they could describe.

I would like to understand what each arrangement presupposes. Does restricting payment to surviving victims assume that a wrong is owed only to those who experienced it, so that the claim lapses when the last of them dies? If so, on what view of harm or of group identity can a later generation hold a claim at all, and does that view require treating groups as things that can themselves be wronged and owed, rather than as collections of individuals? What is presupposed by the fact that the money is handed to individuals in equal shares, with no attempt to measure what any one person lost — is the payment compensation in the ordinary sense, or is it doing something else, such as transferring a status or recording a recognition? Ordinary tort law requires a plaintiff to prove a loss and forbids suing on someone else's injury; reparations practice waives both requirements. Tell me which of these commitments the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits well with, and what would have to be given up if it were rebuilt on a different account of what the original wrong was.
