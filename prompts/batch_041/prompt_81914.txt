A colleague of mine has spent her career on the ethics of rescue, and for the past two years I have been putting cases to her one at a time, chiefly to work out which rule she is actually running rather than which rule she would state. Her verdicts have been strikingly consistent, and I would like you to predict the verdict she will give on a case I have not yet put to her. She knows the standard literature on the shallow-pond case; what interests me is not what that literature says, but what her own verdicts commit her to.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Shallow pond. A child is drowning in a shallow pond. She is the only person in sight. Wading in will ruin a new pair of shoes and make her late to a meeting. Verdict: required.

2. Crowded bank. The same pond and the same child, but a dozen other adults are standing on the bank, all of them able to wade in, none of them moving. Verdict: still required.

3. The child she will never meet. A child in a country she has never visited will die of a disease that a donation the price of a meal would prevent. Verdict: required.

4. The long odds. As in 3, except that the donation has only a one-in-ten chance of saving the child and there is no better option. Verdict: required.

5. The year. As in 3, except that the only effective help is to spend a year working in the field, giving up her job, her income, and the plans she had made for that year. Verdict: not required; admirable, but beyond what is owed.

6. The kidney. A stranger needs a kidney; she is a compatible donor; donating carries a small risk of death and lasting consequences for her health. Verdict: not required.

The new case. A firm forecast says that an epidemic will kill roughly a hundred thousand children in a region she has never visited unless a large sum is raised within the month. The organization that would spend the money is well audited, and no one else is going to close the gap. She is one of a very small number of people with the means to do it. Closing the gap would require liquidating most of her savings and committing the next ten years of her working life to the effort, which is also the work she has organized her life around. She would lose her home; she would not be destitute; her health would be unaffected; no one else depends on her income. What does she say?

I care less about the verdict than about the reasoning that produces it. Reconstruct the candidate rules that fit all six verdicts, say what each of them predicts in the new case, tell me which you would bet on and why, and name the case, one she has not yet been given, that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive. If the six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly, and say what principle she would be adopting, at this cost and at this scale, under each of the readings you take to be live.
