I convene a fortnightly reading group for graduate students in philosophy of science and metaphysics — a dozen people, ninety minutes, one seminar room, a whiteboard, no budget, and no set reading for the session — and I want a single activity that makes them feel the problem of identifying what a system really contains when the information that would settle the question is inaccessible in principle rather than merely out of reach. Our discussions keep collapsing into two lines that both feel too cheap to have earned: someone says the hidden facts are just unobservable and we should be instrumentalists about them, someone else says that of course there is a fact of the matter even if we can never get at it, and nobody is made to pay a price for either move. What I want the session to produce is the felt difference between two situations that talk tends to run together — a box we cannot open yet, where a better instrument would settle everything, and a case in which no further observation exists to be had, because the complete record of what any possible observer could ever register has already been handed to us and the only remaining question is what is left to disagree about. Design it so that this distinction arrives through what the participants do, not through anything I announce before or during the activity.

Concretely, I would like the group to end up having committed, in writing, to an inventory of what a hidden system contains, and then to have that commitment embarrassed by a second system that differs from the first in what it contains while agreeing with it on every entry of a record I have stipulated to be exhaustive; and I want at least one candidate inventory on the table whose extra element is stipulated to be causally inert with respect to everything in that record, so that the group is forced to say aloud whether the element exists and on what grounds. I care less about a puzzle they can crack by cleverness than about one that forces a decision about what could count as evidence at all, and about whether a difference that makes no difference to any possible observation is a difference. Give me the whole thing: the setup and materials (assume only paper, pens, a whiteboard, and whatever I can carry in), the rules, any roles, the sequence with timings that fits ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the part of the problem it is meant to make vivid — the underdetermination of what exists by what is observable, the temptation to treat a relabeling of one structure as a rival ontology, and the several responses available when the record runs out (deny that the residual question means anything, insist it has an answer that no evidence could reach, or redescribe both inventories as one structure under two descriptions). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where the group will game the rules, where the stipulation that the record is complete will be quietly ignored, which participant will derail it — and be candid about the feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce, given that a real investigator never gets to know the record is complete. Close by naming the design choice you are least confident in, saying where your reasoning about how the group will actually behave is most likely to fail, and telling me what you would have to see in the room, or what argument I could give you, to conclude that the activity's central mapping is wrong.
