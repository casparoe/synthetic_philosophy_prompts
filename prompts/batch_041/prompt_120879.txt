I'm a backend engineer — no philosophy degree, just a couple of MOOCs, a friend who did a PhD in it, and way too many arguments in the kitchen — and I'm trying to write down where I actually stand on the "where does the mind stop" question so I can stop flip-flopping every time I read something new. Here's the pile. (1) What makes something a mental state is the job it does in a system, not the stuff it's made of or where the stuff sits — if a process is doing the remembering or the pattern-matching inside the same loop my brain is running, it's part of my cognitive system, period. (2) I have a test for that which I've never seen written down anywhere: the seam test. A tool is part of me when the interface disappears — my keyboard, my muscle memory for vim, the autocomplete I stop reading and just hit tab on. If I have to consciously operate the UI, it's outside me; once the UI dissolves, it's inside. (3) I have a second test that I'm starting to suspect pulls the other way: the power-outage test. If the wifi drops and I suddenly can't do the thing, I don't say I knew it, I say I had access to it. Knowing has to survive the tool going away. (4) I'm a physicalist, and my experience is happening in my head and doesn't leak into my laptop — reaching for a tool doesn't change what it's like to be me. But I also think (1) is just flatly true, and I half-remember reading that the extended-mind people wave off consciousness and say they're only ever talking about the boring functional part, which would be super convenient for me but I can't tell if that's actually allowed given how hard I'm pushing (1). (5) I don't believe in group minds — a team with a shared repo and a shared Slack channel is not a mind, only the individual people are. My PhD friend says you can't get constitution out of coupling, meaning something causally hooked into my loop isn't thereby a part of me, and he calls the alternative "bloat," but honestly I can't tell if that's an argument or just a taste.

So here's what I want: which of these can't live together, ranked by how bad the damage actually is, and for each pair that's in real trouble, what's the cheapest thing to give up and what do I loose by giving it up? Please don't take the lazy way out in either direction — don't tell me it's all fine and compatible with some clever gloss, and don't tell me they're all in conflict either, because I'm fairly sure at least one pair only looks like a fight and would evaporate once you say what each one is actually claiming. If there's one single edit that cleans up the most mess at once, say what it is and be honest about the price.

One more thing that I think matters and I want you to actually take a position on: does your answer change depending on the tool? A paper notebook, an autocomplete that finishes my sentences, and a search engine feel like three genuinely different cases to me, and if my two tests are handing me different verdicts on the most integrated tool of the three, I want to know whether the tests are tracking the same thing or whether one of them is just a vibe I picked up from a blog post.
