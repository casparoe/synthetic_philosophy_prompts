I keep an ant farm on my desk, and about a month ago my seven-year-old asked me whether the colony is one animal or a lot of animals, and whether it minds being in the glass. I gave a bad answer — something about emergent behaviour and how the queen isn't in charge — and I've been gnawing on it since. It turns out I've been volunteered to run next month's session of a small philosophy-and-science meetup I go to, and I've claimed this topic for myself: can an ant colony have a mind? The group is about a dozen people, non-specialists but sharp: a beekeeper who will insist a hive is plainly a single organism, an engineer who writes ant-colony-optimisation code for a living and I suspect thinks the algorithms have already settled the question, a retired vet who will want to know whether the ants themselves suffer, and a couple of people who have read a little philosophy of mind and will reach for the word "functionalism" before they've earned it. I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no slides. Assume nobody has read Hofstadter and nobody will read anything for this.

I don't want you to explain the topic to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it. The crux I want to install — and I want it to arrive as a discovery they make, not as a definition I hand out at the top — is the gap between there being a colony-level point of view and there being a colony-level subject: between a system whose behaviour is best predicted and explained by treating it as a single agent with interests, and a system in which someone is home. Everything else in the session has to be sequenced so that this distinction lands late and lands hard, which means the earlier moves have to be chosen for what they make available later. So: what has to come first, and what depends on what; where the word "mind" has to be pinned down before anything else can be asked; when the ants' own status has to be settled, if it has to be settled at all, and what happens if I let the audience decide it in the wrong order. I'm also undecided about the carrying case, and I'd like you to rule on it rather than offer me options. My instinct is to invent a case rather than lean on the Hofstadter dialogue, partly because the dialogue's form may smuggle in exactly what's at issue — a colony that speaks in one voice has already been given a unified speaker, which is the thing I want them to notice as a temptation rather than accept as a premise. My two candidates: the merger, where two colonies are induced to exchange trail pheromones and start foraging as one, and I ask whether the number of minds went from two to one and whether there was a moment when it was one and a half; and the staggered replacement, where I swap the ants out one at a time for micro-robots obeying the same local rules, and ask what, if anything, is lost and when. Tell me whether either can bear the weight, what a case would need in order to bear it, and what my candidates lack. If you think the Hofstadter material should be the spine rather than a garnish, say so and say why — but don't build the plan around my recollection of the dialogue's details, because I may have garbled them and I'd rather the session not depend on that.

Please give me the plan in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths. First, The spine: the order the ideas must go in and the dependency relations between them, with a note on what breaks if I run two of them out of order (400–500 words). Second, The carrying case: your verdict on my candidates and a specification of what the case must have and must lack, including what it must not be allowed to do (200–300 words). Third, The misconception map: the misconceptions they will bring and the ones they will form during the session, in the order they will surface, each with the point in the spine where I head it off and the one move I use, and with the beekeeper, the optimisation engineer, and the vet each placed explicitly (400–500 words). Fourth, The turn: exactly where the point-of-view-versus-subject distinction gets earned rather than announced, what question I put to the room at that moment and how long I let them sit with it before I say anything (150–200 words). Fifth, The check: one thing I ask them to do at the end that shows the idea landed rather than that they've learned the vocabulary, plus what a confident wrong answer would look like and what it would tell me (100–150 words). Sixth, Deliberate omissions: what I should refuse to settle in seventy-five minutes, and the words I use to refuse it so that it sounds like a decision rather than a gap (100–150 words). Seventh, Where I will go wrong: the one place in this plan where I personally am most likely to start lecturing instead of teaching, and the tell that it's happening (100 words). No readings, no citations, and if you find yourself writing my speech for me, stop and write the plan instead.
