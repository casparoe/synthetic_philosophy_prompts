A mate of mine and I had a proper two-hour row in the pub last night about Wittgenstein's On Certainty — he's doing a course on it and I've been dipping in and out of it for years — and it got under my skin enough that I've banged out a draft of my side of it, mainly to see whether it holds together or whether I was just being loud. I'd really like comments on it. His line was that when one of these basic certainties gives out, the person was just being sloppy and should have been more careful, since you can always in principle say what your reasons were. I think that's wrong, but I need the distinction below to do the work of showing why.

Here's the draft.

Hinges, seepage and rupture (working title)

The thing I take from the book is that doubting needs somewhere to stand. Asking "how do you know?" has a point when there's a procedure to point at, and no point at all for the things that make pointing possible — that I have two hands, that the world was here long before I was born, that the words I'm using mean what they mean. These aren't our best-evidenced beliefs; they're the ones that hold still so that anything else can be tested. He compares it to a river: propositions are the water, but the bank is made of the same stuff and moves too. Some of the bank is rock that doesn't budge, some is silt that gets carried off and dropped somewhere else.

My gripe is that "the same proposition" makes hinges sound like beliefs whose evidential status drifts up and down. But what's at the bottom usually isn't a belief we hold, it's a way of going on — a practice. You only ever catch sight of a hinge when the practice jams and someone asks what you were assuming. Nobody believes that a signature means money will change hands; that's built into what signing is. So the bank is better described as the standing shape of a practice than as a set of propositions. And when it shifts, it usually isn't because the evidence finally stacked up. It's because a way of doing things stopped working.

The bit I actually want to argue is that hinges go two seperate ways. Seepage: worn down over years by small frictions, so the ex-hinge leaves a rational trace. You can tell the story — this happened, then that, and gradually I couldn't go on as before — and the story isn't a proof but it is an answer to "why". Rupture: a single event takes the hinge out without arguing with it. Afterwards you can't reconstruct the case against the old hinge, because the case would have to be made in a vocabulary you no longer have. It isn't that you were persuaded; the old thing now reads as theatre. Think of someone whose partner of twenty years turns out to have had a second family, or who finds out the official account of the war they fought in was invented from the start. Ask them for the evidence that convinced them to stop trusting and there isn't any — not because they're being coy.

Why I think it matters: our ways of holding each other to account — blame, argument, "why on earth did you think that?" — assume seepage. They assume the person can walk back along the trace. Where it was rupture, that assumption is empty, and pressing them for reasons isn't just harsh, it's a category error. It also explains the double bind: they get called irrational for having no reasons, when no reasons were involved in getting them there, and whatever reasons they offer afterwards are reconstructions. And it explains why what actually helps is other people doing the practice with you rather than better arguments.

Last piece: a test for telling a hinge from a merely deeply held belief. A deeply held belief can be attacked from inside your own practice — you can find it, and see what would count against it. A hinge can't be located from the inside at all until it's gone, and when it goes you don't feel you've changed your mind. You feel the world has changed.

What I want comments on:

1. Accuracy. Have I got the riverbank image right, or is that image really about how we're taught things rather than about practices? Please don't hand me a reading list — I won't get to a library, so if there's something I ought to read, just tell me the idea.
2. Is seepage versus rupture a real distinction, or is it slow belief change versus fast belief change with extra steps? Give me the strongest version of the objection that it's a fancy redescription of something much more boring.
3. Where does it overreach? I'm least sure about the accountability claim. People do sometimes give reasons after a rupture, even bad ones, and sometimes the rupture is something they brought on themselves.
4. Which single claim, if false, sinks the whole thing? And is there a case that definately breaks my hinge-versus-deeply-held-belief test — something that looks like a hinge but can be located from the inside?
