Two colleagues and I are drafting a joint seminar paper on the epistemology of testimony, and the part that keeps defeating us is a comparison we assumed would be routine. The two items to be compared are the standard pair: reductionism, on which a hearer's warrant for believing what she is told must be inherited from evidence she already possesses — about this speaker's competence and sincerity, or about the reliability of the practice of telling in general — so that testimony is at best a derivative or borrowed source of warrant; and anti-reductionism, on which a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told unless and until something specific counts against this speaker or this occasion, so that testimony stands alongside perception and memory as a basic source of warrant rather than one that must be earned case by case. What I want is not a general verdict about which of the two is correct, but a comparison of how they fare on one structural test case that neither of us can get past, namely chains. Practically everything I take myself to know at second, third, or hundredth hand — that a particular bridge is closed, that a treaty was signed, that a substance is toxic at a given dose — reaches me through informants who were themselves relying on informants, and I have no evidence at all about most of the links. If justification is inherited link by link, it looks as though it should attenuate with chain length, each link's reliability multiplying the next, which would make long chains nearly worthless and much of our cognitive life unjustified; if it is not inherited link by link, it is not obvious what stops the entitlement from extending to a rumour passed along by people who are all merely repeating it.

I would like the comparison run on that case specifically, with three questions answered in order. First, what exactly does each position predict about a chain of a given length: does reductionism really entail attenuation, or can it treat the chain as a single composite source whose reliability is assessed as a whole, or as a token of a type about which the hearer does have evidence, and does anti-reductionism really extend to arbitrarily long chains for free, or must it say something special about relays and about the difference between transmitting and originating a claim? Second, I have been experimenting with a distinction that I suspect is doing quiet work in the dispute, between a warrant that makes the belief likely to be true and properly held, and an entitlement to rely on others that is grounded not in evidence about them but in the fact that inquiry is unavoidably a division of labour; I want to know whether the reductionist and the anti-reductionist are best read as theories of the first thing, in which case the second is a separate matter that neither of them addresses, or whether one of them can absorb the second without changing what it claims. Third, and this is the comparison proper: in what ways is anti-reductionism special as an account of these cases — does it earn its keep against a reductionism made sophisticated enough to handle chains, or does it collapse into a claim about the reliability of a social practice, which is a claim a reductionist could equally make? Keep the two positions distinct throughout rather than blending them into a middle view, and end by pricing each side's treatment of chains: what each has to give up, and what would have to be true of long chains for that price to look acceptable.
