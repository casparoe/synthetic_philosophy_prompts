I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and I'm working up a piece on chance, luck, and the trouble you get into the moment you start saying that two possibilities are "equally likely." I want to hang it on the opening of Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead — the two of them sitting there flipping a coin that comes down heads again and again, like ninety-odd times in a row (I forget the exact count, doesn't matter), while Guildenstern works himself into a state about the law of probability, monkeys and typewriters, all that. What I'd like from you is a reading of that opening, and of the play as a whole if the rest of it will carry one, through Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. Please don't quote the play at me — I can't verify quotes right now and neither can you without the text, so paraphrase — and stick to bits that anyone who's seen it or read it once would know: the coin run, the two of them losing track of which of them is which and which way they came in, the players arriving, the ending on the boat.

Here's the angle I actually care about, and it's the reason I want this rather than a general "probability in Stoppard" thing:

1. Is the coin run the friendly, discrete case where indifference is safe, and Bertrand's chord the continuous case where it collapses — or is that distinction not really doing the work people think it is? If the run of heads is only surprising against a prior over the coin's bias, tell me what prior the scene is implicitly assuming and whether it's entitled to it.

2. The parametrization thing. Being indifferent over outcomes and being indifferent over the coin's bias (and then over the bias on a log-odds scale, say) give you different answers, and a run of heads looks very different depending on which partition you picked. Bertrand's three chord recipes are the same disease. Which partition is the play secretly leaning on, and is there a reading of the scene where the joke is precisely that there's no fact about which partition is "the" one?

3. I half-remember a physicist arguing that the paradox dissolves if you demand your indifference be invariant under the transformations that actually matter for the problem, instead of picking a coordinate at random. I may be mangling it. Does that move fix the coin? Does it fix the chord? And what does it cost — doesn't it smuggle in a symmetry you haven't earned?

4. The play is a play and the two of them are characters in Hamlet. If the "random" process is authored, is there even a chance setup for indifference to be about — is the question ill-posed rather than merely hard? And does that make the scene an argument that indifference needs a real chance mechanism, or just an elaborate way of saying they're doomed?

5. Does the play dramatize the Bayesian shrug (the evidence just drowns the prior, nothing to see here), or something darker — that the space of possibilities was never well defined in the first place? And does it add anything to the debate that the paradox literature doesn't, e.g. the felt difference between "unlikely" and "rigged"?

What I do need: keep what's actually settled seperate from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — the arithmetic and the fact that different parametrizations disagree are settled; whether indifference can be rescued at all, and whether Bertrand shows something about credence or only about continuous sample spaces, are not — and don't hand me a contested view as if it were the consensus. Give it back to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
