Ok so I'm putting together a case study for a grad seminar on Marx and technology, and I'd rather hand the actual analysis to you than do it myself. Here's the episode: the 2023 Hollywood strikes. The writers were out from May 2 to September 27, the actors from July 14 to November 9, and AI was one of the two headling issues in both (streaming residuals being the other). What I find interesting is that neither settlement banned AI. What the writers got was roughly rules against using their material to train models or to make digital recreations of them, and against using AI to cut writers or their pay, plus gains on the size and duration of writing teams and on streaming residuals; the actors got consent and compensation requirements around digital replicas. So the tool stays, and the contract grows around it.

Now run that through Marx on machinery and automation, and please use the parts of the account that usually get flattened. Specifically: the machinery chapter of Capital, where the claim is not that machines replace workers but that the same machine, as capital, reorganizes the labour process, lengthens and intensifies the working day, and cheapens labour-power by cheapening the goods workers have to buy -- so you can get rising real wages alongside deepening exploitation. The reserve army of labour, where the credible threat of replacement disciplines the people still employed, whether or not anyone is actually replaced. And the older formal/real subsumption distinction: capital first takes over a labour process it finds lying around and just squeezes more time out of it, then later rebuilds the process around itself so that the worker's skill stops being the thing that matters. That last step is where I'd expect the general-intellect idea to bite -- once social knowledge is sunk into fixed capital, the worker is no longer the measure of production.

What I actually want from you: (1) Is this episode better read as real subsumption or as reserve-army discipline? I.e. is the fight about the labour process being rebuilt around the tool, or about the threat of replacement giving capital leverage over people who are still working? (2) Marx keeps insisting the machine isn't the enemy, the capitalist employment of it is. Does the settlement vindicate that, or is it a case where workers won a voice in the employment relation without getting anywhere near ownership of the means of production -- and if so is that a confirmation of his point or an embarrassment for it? (3) Where is the account just silent or wrong here? If automation cheapens wage goods and raises productivity, why is the fight about residuals, credits and staffing floors rather than hours and wages? And (4) the reverse direction -- what does this episode show that the machinery chapter can't really handle? The people at risk here are the ones with the most bargaining power and the most legible individual authorship, which is nothing like the textile operative he had in mind. The tool is also general-purpose and its output substitutes for the finished product rather than being a component of it, and I'm not sure his schema cares about that difference.

Two things to avoid. Don't let it collapse into either doom or apologetics -- if part of this is better explained by plain bargaining power, or by ordinary labour economics (the hold-up problem, the fact that the studios were the side with a depleting content pipeline), just say so. And don't pad it with Marx 101; if a concept from the machinery chapter doesn't earn its keep, drop it and tell me why.
