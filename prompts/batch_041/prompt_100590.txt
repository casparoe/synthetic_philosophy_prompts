I write about science and ideas for a small quaterly, and I'm working on a piece about a weird corner of pain research. Here's the phenomenon: people with damage to certain parts of the brain — I want to say the insula is involved, don't quote me on that — will report that a pinprick hurts, they can point to it and rate how sharp it is, but it doesn't bother them at all. They say it hurts and then kind of shrug. Some of them won't even pull their arm away. I believe the clinical name is pain asymbolia, though you won't be able to check that.

I got a pain neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind on the phone about it and they argued for an hour. Roughly:

1. Neuroscientist: Here's a sensation that is pain but isn't bad for the person having it. So the unpleasantness has to be a separate component, added by a different bit of the brain. Badness is the gift wrap, not the gift — contingent, not part of what pain is.

2. Philosopher: If he doesn't mind it, avoid it, or suffer it, then I don't care what he calls it — it isn't pain in the sense that matters. Maybe it's a funny tingle. You've shown a damaged brain can produce a pain-report; you haven't shown pain can be neutral.

3. Neuroscientist: But he says it hurts! He's not confused or in denial, and his ability to detect and localize the stimulus is intact. You're just insisting that nothing counts as pain unless it's bad, which is circular — you've defined your conclusion into the word.

4. Philosopher: And you're assuming a clean seam between a sensation and its badness, when the badness might just be what that sensation is like. What would a pain that didn't hurt be? Aches ache.

5. Neuroscientist: Then explain where the badness comes from. If it's intrinsic, no creature could have pain without being harmed by it — which is exactly what we seem to be looking at. And it matters clinically: if suffering is a separate system, we can treat it without killing the signal.

6. Philosopher: Even if I grant it's pain, you're sliding from "doesn't mind it" to "isn't harmed by it." His tissue is still being destroyed. We don't say a man under anesthesia isn't harmed by the surgery.

Can you referee this point by point — who's right about what, where each of them overreaches, and what both are missing? You can say who's more right overall at the end, but I mainly want the move-by-move. American English, please — the magazine is US-based.
