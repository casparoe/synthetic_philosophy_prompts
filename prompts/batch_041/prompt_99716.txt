In my reporting on elections I keep running into a mismatch that neither camp seems eager to resolve. Civics instruction, campaign rhetoric, and the ritual of the concession speech all rest on the premise that each citizen's ballot is consequential — that staying home is a kind of abdication, and that an election could hinge on any one vote. The political scientists I interview will concede, if pressed, that the chance a single ballot decides a national election is vanishingly small; small enough that a citizen reasoning in a narrowly cost-benefit way would stay home. Both claims are asserted in earnest, often by the same people, and I have never received a straight answer about whether they are inconsistent.

I would like you to treat the civic ritual — the whole apparatus that presents each vote as decisive — as a text in need of interpretation, and tell me what it presupposes. What theory of rational action does it commit us to? Does it require that voting be instrumentally rational and that citizens are simply mistaken about the odds? Does it trade on a distinction between the causal question (did my ballot cause the outcome?) and an evidential one (does my voting raise the probability that others like me will vote?), so that the paradox dissolves once we see which question is meant? Does it presuppose that the act carries value independent of its effect, or a conception of duty that does not reduce to consequences at all — a minimax-regret logic, say, or an obligation owed to fellow citizens rather than to the tally? Set out the rival theories, say which ones the practice rules out and which it merely accommodates, and tell me where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several incompatible views would license the same ritual. I am especially interested in whether the practice is internally consistent: can a society coherently moralize turnout while its own political science relies on the fact that most individual votes change nothing?

Then pressure the reconstruction with a concrete reform. Suppose a city were to replace its elections with a civic lottery: each cycle, a random sample of residents is drawn to serve as the legislature for a term, while the rest of the public may bind their delegates with instructions. Assume the reform is implemented competently and honestly. Which of the theories you identified would survive the change, which would be refuted by it, and which would turn out to have been doing no work at all? I want to know what the reform reveals about what the old practice was really committed to — and, in the end, whether the paradox of voting is a genuine problem about practical rationality or merely an artifact of one particular way of valuing participation.
