Our son was born in March, and I have spent the months since rereading Fear and Trembling, which I had assumed I was finished with at twenty-two; it turns out that becoming a father makes Abraham's position much harder to treat as an academic puzzle, and I have been circling the book ever since. I am to give five minutes at my reading group's year-end dinner, and I would like to open with a joke about it. I want one joke — the one you judge best, told whole, not a menu of candidates and not a preview of why it is supposed to be funny. The laugh, if there is one, has to come out of the substance rather than the atmosphere: the suspension of the ethical for the sake of something higher, the fact that the knight of faith cannot explain himself and remain what he is, the contrast with the tragic hero — Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus — whose sacrifice his community can at least understand, and the concealment that the third problem makes Abraham's real difficulty. Keep it clear of the cheap shots: not "God told me to" as a punchline about what children cost, and not the general observation that faith makes people do appalling things. It should be short enough that I can tell it from memory and pitched at people who have read the book, not at people who have read about it. Finally, tell me plainly what I am committing to when I tell it: where the joke takes a liberty with the text or leans on a contested reading — whether the suspension is Kierkegaard's own view or only Johannes de Silentio's, and whether the story leaves Abraham a father of faith or a would-be murderer — and give me your own judgment about which reading the joke bets on and whether that bet is defensible, since I would rather know now than learn it from a colleague across the table.
