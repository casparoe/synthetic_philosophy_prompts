The following pattern is sometimes called the gender-equality paradox: the more economically developed and legally egalitarian a country is, the larger the average differences between men's and women's educational and occupational choices tend to be. Women in the most egalitarian societies, for example, are relatively less likely to enter engineering or physics and relatively more likely to enter nursing or primary education than women in poorer and less egalitarian ones.

Suppose an analyst offers the following explanation of the pattern and asks you to judge it. Call it the friction hypothesis. The pattern, on this view, reveals nothing about innate psychological differences between the sexes. It reflects the lifting of a constraint. In poor and insecure economies, both men and women must pursue whatever work pays, and that shared necessity flattens the observable differences between them. Only in wealthy, secure, and legally egalitarian societies can people afford to follow inherited cultural tastes rather than economic need, and so only there do those tastes show up in the statistics. On this reading, greater freedom yields greater apparent divergence, and the divergence is the shadow of culture rather than of nature.

Note that defenders of an innate-difference reading use the very same pattern to argue the opposite: that once legal and economic constraints are removed, natural differences between the sexes are free to express themselves. Your essay should ask what, if anything, distinguishes these two readings of one and the same set of facts.

Write an essay on what this pattern, assuming it is genuine, actually shows about nature, about culture, and about justice. Address at least the following. Is the friction hypothesis a genuine explanation of the data, or a redescription of it dressed in causal language? What further evidence would be needed to decide between the cultural and the biological reading of the same divergence? How much weight should be given to the possibility that the pattern is an artifact of how gender equality and career choice are measured, rather than a fact about preferences? If the pattern is real and culturally driven, does it weaken the case for policies aimed at parity in fields such as engineering, or does it strengthen that case by showing that current choices are shaped by inherited expectations rather than by free preference? And does the fact that a divergence emerges under conditions of formal freedom bear at all on whether the resulting inequality is unjust? Weigh the main considerations on each side, and do not end on a non-committal note: state what the paradox does and does not license us to conclude, and defend your verdict against the strongest objection you can raise to it. You need not cite any literature; argue from the considerations themselves.
