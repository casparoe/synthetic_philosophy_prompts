I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I read a fair amount of it on the side, and I've been trying to think through the claim that insects might matter morally on a scale that dwarfs everything else we care about. I work in reliability engineering, so expected-value calculations are more or less my day job, and the shape of the argument people make about farmed insects has been nagging at me. The move runs roughly like this: the probability that a cricket or a mealworm has experiences in any morally relevant sense is very low — one in a thousand, one in ten thousand, the figure is never pinned down — but the number of insects reared and killed every year is enormous (I've seen estimates on the order of a trillion, though the exact figure doesn't matter for my question), so multiplying the two yields an expected quantity of suffering that swamps the suffering of every animal we already take seriously. The same arithmetic is then turned around by critics: if you truly accepted it, you would have to abandon your job and your family in service of the least likely beneficiaries in the world, which is supposed to show that something has gone wrong with the calculation rather than with the person running it.

Two features of this bother me, and I can't tell whether either has a name. First, the population size is doing all the work while the per-individual probability is left deliberately vague, since any positive value can be drowned out by a sufficiently large number. Second — and this is the part I'd most like clarified — multiplying a probability by a population assumes that the uncertainty is a lottery run independently for each individual, but whether crickets can suffer doesn't look like that to me; it looks like a single fact about the kind, so that if it is true of one it is presumably true of all of them, and I don't see why the small probability and the huge population are entitled to be multiplied at all. The whole pattern also feels adjacent to Pascal's wager, except that in the wager the improbable payoff is one unbounded prize rather than an enormous number of small independent chances, and I suspect that difference is doing real work.

So what I want is not merely a label. Does this pattern — a minuscule probability of moral status, multiplied by an astronomical population, invoked to make the resulting expectation decisive — have an established name, and how does it relate to the concepts it most resembles? Is the nearest existing idea about expected value under uncertainty, about arguments from absurdity, about aggregation across individuals, or about something else entirely? Does the counter-move, the one insisting that the reasoning would oblige you to sacrifice everything for the least probable beneficiaries, have a name and a standard reply, or is it just a reductio that each person reinvents? Please draw the boundaries rather than stopping at a term: tell me what exactly the phenomenon is, which existing concept fits it best, precisely where the analogy to that concept breaks down, and if nothing fits, coin a name and give me a definition sharp enough that I can tell which cases fall on either side of it.
