I am a senior engineer on the platform team at a mid-sized software company, and I have no formal training in philosophy. Over the past two years our systems have begun to describe and modify themselves, and every design review turns into the same argument. Our configuration service can deploy changes to the code that reads the configuration. Our permission model lets a sufficiently privileged administrator edit the rules that define what privilege means. Our build pipeline compiles the compiler that builds the pipeline. One of our training jobs now ingests outputs produced by earlier versions of the same job. Whenever one of these comes up, someone invokes a half-remembered idea from Gödel, Escher, Bach about tangled hierarchies and an inviolate level — the level that stands outside the loop, the way the hand in Escher's Drawing Hands stands outside the drawing it is drawing and the two drawn hands cannot reach up and alter it — and the discussion stalls, because none of us can say what the inviolate level of a running system actually is, or how we would recognize that a design has none.

I would like you to turn that idea into a procedure my team can actually run during a design review, and then apply it.

The procedure should be a short, ordered set of questions, phrased so that an engineer who has never read the book can ask them out loud. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a red flag, and whether the question is genuinely about the structure of the system or is a disguised value judgment. Say where the procedure reaches a definite no — do not build it this way — and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person, because what remains is a question of acceptable risk or of who should hold power rather than a question about the structure of the code. For each hand-off, tell the engineer how to frame the question for the person receiving it, and who that person should be (a security lead, an architect, a lawyer, an executive). Where a step is only a heuristic that will misfire, label it as such rather than dressing it up as procedure. And be explicit about the cases where adding a level is the wrong fix, because sometimes the loop is harmless or even the point.

Then run the procedure on all four systems I described. I want to see where it settles the question cleanly and where it cannot, and I want at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my team will not like — where it says the guarantee we thought we had is not one we can actually make, or that the clean design we are proud of is the one that has to change. If the honest answer for some system is that the only inviolate level is a human process rather than a technical one, say so and say what follows for us.

Assume the review lasts forty-five minutes, that no philosopher is in the room, and that whatever we produce has to be written down in a form an auditor can follow later. Any recommendation to add a layer or move a decision out of the system has to be costed in engineering time, because that is the objection it will meet.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do — the questions it cannot answer, the systems it will misclassify — and which of my four cases it handles well and which it merely waves at.
