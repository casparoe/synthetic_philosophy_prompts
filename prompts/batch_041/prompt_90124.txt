I keep running into a line that gets used to shut down any discussion of economic inequality: that it is poverty, not inequality, that should concern us, and that fretting over the gap between rich and poor is just envy dressed up as politics. The last time was an argument with a relative who works in finance. He used it to say that so long as the poorest are getting richer, the fact that the richest are pulling away is not something anyone should legislate about, and that Europeans who talk about "the gap" are simply resentful. I have no training in economics or philosophy, but I have been chewing on this on my own, and I suspect the line is doing several different jobs at once, some of them legitimate and some not.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a verdict on inequality in general. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "it is poverty, not inequality, that should concern us" could actually be making, from the almost empty to the ambitious, and give me a verdict on each: which are truisms nobody would dispute, which are serious empirical claims that could turn out true or false, which are normative claims that stand in need of an argument, and which are false or confused. I would especially like the readings separated along axes I suspect are being blurred together, such as: whether inequality is being denied intrinsic importance, instrumental importance, or both; whether the claim is that only absolute consumption matters for how well someone's life goes, or merely that relative position matters less; which inequality is at issue — of income, wealth, opportunity, or something else; whether we are comparing a snapshot or whole lifetimes, and whether the relevant comparison group is one's neighbors or the whole society; whether the "envy" charge is a claim about the motives of the people who worry about inequality, about the welfare of the people who are unequal, or neither; and whether the line is really the claim that reducing inequality would cost the poor something, which is a different and much more concrete assertion.

Then tell me which of these readings the line actually needs in order to do the work my relative was making it do — dismissing the case for redistribution outright — and whether any reading both does that work and survives scrutiny.

The other half of this is the contrast he drew with Europe, where the attitude seems to be that the gap itself is an offense regardless of whether the floor is rising. I would like the same treatment for that side: what would someone have to be claiming to hold that the gap is intrinsically objectionable, and which version of that claim is actually defensible — is it about the fairness of the process that produced the gap, about relative position mattering to people's welfare, about the wealthy converting money into political power, about solidarity and the kind of society one wants to live in, or something else entirely? I want the menu, not a single answer, and I want to know which items on it are doing real work and which are decoration.

One more thing. If the way I have framed this — as two national attitudes squaring off — is itself a mistake, and the real disagreements cut across the American/European line or dissolve once the claims are made precise, then say so plainly rather than humoring my framing. I would rather be told that my question is confused than be handed a tidy answer to a badly posed one. And please do not send me to books or papers; I am not going to track them down, and a list of names would not help me.
