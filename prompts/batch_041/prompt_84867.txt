I am giving the launch-night speech for our town's new local currency, a scrip that member shops and residents will use alongside the national currency. The room will be mostly volunteers who believe they are building something with real value behind it, a few shopkeepers who have agreed to accept the scrip and are privately afraid of being left with worthless paper, and a retired economics teacher who will notice any overstatement. My plan was to tell the room that our scrip is backed by real need, unlike ordinary money, which is only a convention; but I have been reading Aristotle on exchange, and I no longer think I can say that. On his account money exists by agreement rather than by nature, it stands in for need so that exchange need not wait for two people who happen to want each other's goods, and the pursuit of money for its own sake is the unnatural case, as against exchange that serves real need. Write the speech so that it stays faithful to that and still moves the room. It must not promise that the scrip will gain value, it must not trade on a real-money-versus-fake-money contrast, and it must answer the shopkeepers' actual question -- what makes the scrip worth anything -- without inventing a backing behind it. Refer to Aristotle if it helps, but paraphrase him rather than quoting, and do not cite chapter and verse at a launch party. Keep it to about five minutes, take a definite line on whether a currency can rest on anything beyond agreement, and make the case inside the speech rather than hedging. Begin with the speech itself, with no preface; afterwards add one sentence naming which reading of Aristotle you committed to.
