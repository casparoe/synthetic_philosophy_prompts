You're a political anthropologist who also knows the analytic literature on corruption and on gift exchange — so you'll have instincts about both the ethnography and the concepts. Think carefully before answering, and start by brainstorming the candidate terms you'd consider, because I don't want the first label that comes to mind.

Here's a pattern I keep running into and I don't know if it has a name. In a provincial office I've been reading about, the man in charge hands the road contracts to his cousins and to the families that backed him. Nobody in the office thinks this is stealing. They think it's what you owe the people who made you. Outside auditors come in, call it corruption, file a report. Fine — that half is just the usual relativism about corruption and I already know that argument.

The strange half is this. The same office workers are genuinely furious about a different set of contracts going to a rival network, and they denounce that as corruption, in exactly the moralising tone the auditors used. And in my own country it's the same: we shrug at the envelopes our side takes and are scandalised by the other side's. So the judgement doesn't seem to track the act, or the culture — it tracks whose network is doing it, and everyone seems to be running two standards at once without noticing. The size matters too: a small envelope is a gift, a big one is a bribe, and the line moves depending on who's counting.

Is there an established concept for this? My guesses are the gift/commodity distinction, patron-client relations, and something like a moral economy of corruption, but none of them quite gets the bit I care about — the double standard that both sides share, and the way the vocabulary of corruption becomes a weapon in the hands of people doing the same thing. If nothing fits, coin me a term and define it. What I really want is the carving: how is this different from plain hypocrisy, from ordinary in-group favouritism, and from the flat relativist claim that corruption is just a Western category? Give me one case that clearly falls inside your definition and one that clearly falls outside, and say what turns on getting it right — say, for someone designing an anti-corruption programme. Skip the reading list, I only want the distinction.
