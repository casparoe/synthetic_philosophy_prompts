I hold a view I will call evidence-constrained narrativism about history. It grants the central point of the sceptical tradition associated with Carr and Hayden White: the archive does not by itself dictate a single narrative, and the historian's selection, emplotment, and causal emphasis are shaped by present concerns, rhetorical conventions, and the kind of story the historian wants to tell. But it denies that this makes historical narratives merely literary constructions. On this view, a narrative is objectively assessable to the extent that the causal claims it encodes can be tested against surviving evidence, and to the extent that rival emplotments of the same episode make different predictions about what further evidence would look like. Ranke's aspiration to reconstruct the past as it actually was is not a naive copy theory of representation; it is a regulative ideal that disciplines the selection of evidence even though it can never be fully achieved. What would count against this position?

I want the defeaters, not a reading list. Sort them by how decisive they would be. Which arguments, cases, or results would a committed defender of this view have to concede as genuine problems, and which would they be entitled to dismiss? I am especially interested in the following pressure points: first, whether the distinction between narrative form and causal content can be made precise enough to do the work I am asking of it, or whether the two inevitably contaminate each other; second, whether the view collapses into a covert form of positivism by treating causal claims as the only truth-apt component of history; third, whether the same episode can sustain two equally evidence-supported but incompatible narratives, and if so, whether that is a fatal underdetermination or merely an ordinary case of theory choice underdetermined by evidence; fourth, whether the view can account for the way historical narratives themselves function as evidence for later historians, so that the object of assessment is not a static archive but a changing historiographical tradition; and fifth, whether the regulative ideal does any real work if no historical narrative can ever be shown to be uniquely correct. I would also like an account of what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and an honest assessment of whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications.

Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on whether evidence-constrained narrativism is tenable, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I am not asking for a verdict on whether history is a science; I am asking whether this specific attempt to combine narrative construction with objective constraint survives the objections it invites.
