I run a joint physics-and-philosophy reading group, and next month I have a ninety-minute session on the hole argument for spacetime. The room is split in a way I want you to exploit: the physicists know the Einstein field equations and have been taught, correctly but shallowly, that diffeomorphism invariance is just the freedom to choose coordinates, while two philosophers who have read the surrounding literature will be sitting at the back waiting to object. I do not need the argument explained to me; what I need is a plan for the explanation. What has to be in place before what, and why — my own guess at the dependency order is that active versus passive diffeomorphisms comes first, then the setup of a hole to the future of a Cauchy surface, then the invariance of the field equations under dragging the fields along, then Leibniz equivalence posed as a fork, and only then determinism as the casualty. Tell me where that order is wrong, what it will lead them to believe if I use it anyway, and whether the distinction between this argument and the older global shift argument — the whole world translated rather than stirred in one region — has to be drawn at this level at all, or whether drawing it costs me the session.

I have two candidates for the case that carries the whole thing, and I want a verdict on each rather than a menu. The first is the gauge analogy with electromagnetism, potentials differing by a gradient yet yielding the same field, which I like because it is already in the physicists' heads and distrust because it may teach the wrong moral: that the metric is a potential with a field-strength-like thing underneath it that is the real content. The second is a concrete general-relativistic scenario: a compact pulse of curvature in otherwise flat spacetime, together with a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside a region containing the pulse and a smooth non-identity rearrangement inside it, so that the two solutions agree on every surface to the past and diverge only in the hole. Say which of these actually carries the idea, what a carrying case for this argument must have and must not have, and repair whichever one fails. Then map the misconceptions this particular room will bring. I predict "it is just a relabelling", "the hole has to be empty of matter or nothing follows" — I have never been sure whether that is a real constraint or an artefact of how the argument is usually written, so rule on it — "so general relativity is indeterministic and therefore false", and "the diffeomorphism moves the matter too, so nothing has really changed". Add the ones I have missed, including those I will manufacture myself through my choice of order, and give me the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off.

Finally, the worry I most want to handle well is the circularity: the indeterminism only follows if the two models are distinct situations, and whether they are distinct is precisely what is in dispute, so an audience that spots this early may decide the whole thing is a quarrel about words. Decide where in the sequence it should surface and how to keep it from collapsing the session. Close by giving me the one question I should put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the argument or merely learned its conclusion, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to announce out loud that I am leaving it out. Throughout, separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where you think one of my assumptions above is simply wrong, say so plainly.
