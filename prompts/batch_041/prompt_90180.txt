I'm a philosophy professor (mind & language, mostly) and I keep butting heads with a colleague from the cog-sci side over a move he makes, and I want to figure out how much weight it can actually bear. His line goes roughly like this: Gödel showed that any formal system rich enough to talk about itself can cough up a sentence that's true but unprovable from inside the system -- the system, so to speak, gesturing at a truth it can't reach. He says that's exactly the shape of a mind becoming aware of itself: a self-aware mind is a system that has stepped outside itself, and so no formal system -- no program, no brain described as a mechanism -- could ever capture it. On his reading that's the whole moral of that big book that braids Gödel with Escher and Bach, that self-reference is the engine of selfhood. And he uses it to license a pretty strong conclusion: consciousness isn't computational, and the "just build a bigger formal system" program in AI is doomed in principle.

I want you to act as a hybrid philosopher of mind and mathematical logician and do a genuine audit of this analogy, not a summary of it or a book report. After that, tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in two things: whether the "self-awareness" on the mind side has anything like the structure of a provability predicate, and whether the mind is supposed to be the *same* system doing the stepping-out or a different one that observes it -- because those pull in very different directions. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for his conclusion and the ones that are just noise, and tell me straight whether the conclusion (minds aren't formal systems) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole thing all along. Take your time with this one and don't pull punches -- I'd much rather have the map with the holes marked than a tidy story.
