I am teaching Kafka's In the Penal Colony in a seminar on punishment, alongside the standard retributivist and deterrence arguments, and I do not want to use it as a mere illustration. I want the penal theory it dramatises reconstructed and judged on its merits. The material, as I read it: the colony executes a man who does not even know he has been sentenced; the accused is assumed guilty and never heard in his own defence; the apparatus does not simply kill him but carves the law he has broken into his skin over twelve hours, so that he is meant to come to know his offence through his own wounds and, in his last hours, to undergo something the Officer presents as a kind of illumination. The Officer is the last believer in the method, nostalgic for the crowds that once attended, and when the Traveller whose endorsement he begs refuses it, he frees the condemned man, takes his place, and is butchered by the machine when it fails.

So: what theory of punishment does that practice presuppose? Not consequentialism in any ordinary sense — the procedure is slow, ruinously unreliable, produces nothing but corpses, and its last defender destroys himself with it. But not straightforwardly retributivism either: retribution answers a known offence with a proportioned response, whereas here the offender is kept in ignorance of his sentence, is denied a hearing, and is governed by an authority whose justice the Officer never really argues for, appealing instead to the old Commandant, to the blueprints, to tradition. Consider then a third possibility and test it hard — an expressive or communicative account on which punishment's point is to make the law legible, to write the norm on the body, so that the sentence is never announced and then executed but is executed in order to be announced, and suffering is the medium in which the offender is finally brought to know what he did. If that is what the story dramatises, does it expose something the respectable theories share and conceal: a wish that the wrongdoer be made to know his wrong, and a suspicion that only suffering can guarantee the knowledge?

Give me a reading faithful in both directions. Where does the expressive reading illuminate the story — the Officer's devotion to the blueprints, the machine's promise of illumination, the nostalgia for spectators, the ending in which the machine denies the Officer even the death it promised? Where does the story resist it, and does the self-sacrifice tell for my reading or against it? What does the disappearance of the audience do to a theory of punishment that appears to need one? And if, as I suspect, the story dramatises a theory no living philosopher would defend — no retributivist today would accept a practice that dispenses with announcing the sentence, with the offender's capacity to understand it, and with any distinction between the sentence and its execution — then say so plainly, and say what it nonetheless reveals about the theories that are defended. I am not after a summary of the criticism; I want the implicit penal theory reconstructed and assessed.
