In the literature on aligning artificial agents, one repeatedly encounters the claim that a well-designed system should be uncertain about its own values: rather than maximizing a fixed objective, it should treat its present objective as a fallible guess and defer to better-informed judgments about what it ought to want. I have never seen the conceptual issue here stated to my satisfaction, and I would like you to explain it. The difficulty is this. On the standard decision-theoretic picture, a preference ordering is not a belief about anything; it simply is what the agent wants, and there is no gap between the agent's preferences and the facts that could open up room for mistake. An agent can be wrong about the world, and it can be wrong about which actions will satisfy its preferences, but it cannot be wrong about its preferences in the way it can be wrong about the capital of Australia. If that is right, then uncertainty about one's own values is either incoherent or a label for something else, and I want to know which.

Please do three things. First, distinguish the several things that might be meant by value uncertainty — uncertainty about the world that bears on how to act, uncertainty about one's own preferences as a matter of self-knowledge, and uncertainty about whether one's preferences are the ones one should have — and say, for each, whether it can be rendered coherent on the standard picture or whether it requires giving up something in that picture.

Second, take the crux case, which I have been calling value humility: the disposition to treat one's present preferences as a noisy estimate of the preferences one would arrive at on fuller reflection. Explain what has to be true of an agent for this attitude to be well defined. In particular, if the agent's deference to its idealized self is itself one of its preferences, then its actual preference is simply to want whatever the idealized self would want, which is a perfectly determinate preference, and the uncertainty looks relocated rather than accommodated. If instead the deference is not among its preferences, say where it comes from. Say whether the regress of second-order and higher-order preferences terminates in anything, or whether it is a sign that the notion was doing illicit work all along.

Third, tell me what turns on the answer. If value uncertainty cannot be made coherent, it looks as though the alignment problem has to be solved at the point of specification — the objective must be got right before training, since the agent cannot be relied on to correct it. If it can be made coherent, part of the problem can be outsourced to the agent, which will want to find out what it should want. Explain which of these the conceptual analysis actually supports, and where the analysis runs out and empirical questions about training take over.

I want continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just an essay in paragraphs, the way you would write it to a colleague.
