I'm putting together a 90-minute session for about 15 grad students in an interdiscplinary seminar -- half philosophy of science, half ML -- on what happens to a measure once people start optimizing it. I've done the lecture version of this before and it always lands as a slogan; this time I want them to actually feel the pull and catch themselves doing it mid-game. Act as someone who designs classroom simulations for a living and has run this one twenty times. Before you commit to a mechanic, brainstorm two or three candidate setups in your head, pick the one with the least fiddle and the most bite, and think carefully about whether the incentives in it will actually bite the way you expect.

Constraints on whatever you design:

- The number everyone is scored on has to be public to all teams. The thing they're actually supposed to be achieving stays with me, the facilitator, and only shows up as points I award privately afterwards. The asymmetry has to be real, not narrated.
- The game has to make four different ways the number can come apart from the goal separately visible, roughly one per round, and in every round the move that maximizes the published number has to be the sane-looking move, so nobody feels like they're cheating when they do it. The four: (i) the number is estimated from a sample, so chasing the top of the leaderboard is partly chasing luck; (ii) past some range -- the extremes, the cases the scale was never calibrated on -- the number and the goal stop tracking at all; (iii) teams can move the number without touching the underlying thing (reclassify a case, change who gets counted, relabel a failure as a different kind of failure); (iv) another team is optimizing the same number in its own favor, so every move you make changes what they'll do next.
- Include one round where the number is a genuinely good stand-in for the goal, so they can't walk out with "measures always rot."
- No real money, no props beyond index cards, runnable in the room in 90 minutes with me as the only facilitator.
- Don't say the name of the phenomenon or the standard term for the failure until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto each of the four pressures above.

Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no closing paragraph:

1. the setup -- room, teams, roles, materials, the clock
2. the rounds -- what each one is and what changes between them
3. the payoff scheme, and how I keep score without a spreadsheet getting in the way
4. the debrief
5. what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what the activity structurally can't reproduce (including, I suspect, the fact that my private scoring is itself just a stand-in -- the game pretends there's a measurable ground truth, and the real world doesn't hand you one)
