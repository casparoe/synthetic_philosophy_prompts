Here's the answer:

"The endurantist holds that a material object is wholly present at every time at which it exists. When I exist now and existed ten years ago, it's the same object, entire, at both times. The perdurantist denies this: an object persists by having temporal parts, one for each time it exists, and the whole object is a four-dimensional worm spread out across those parts. At any single moment only a proper part of the worm — a time-slice — is present. So endurantism says the whole thing is at each time, while perdurantism says only a piece of it is.

The interesting test case is change. Suppose I'm straight at t1 and bent at t2. Taken at face value this says one thing is both straight and bent, which is a contradiction. This is the problem of temporary intrinsics, and I think it presses hardest on the perdurantist, because a worm built out of a straight slice and a bent slice is itself neither straightforwardly straight nor bent, and if it's both then the contradiction has just been relocated rather than solved. The endurantist escapes the problem by relativising: I'm not straight and bent simpliciter, I'm straight-at-t1 and bent-at-t2, and there's no incompatibility in that.

On presentism, endurantism is the natural fit, because if only the present exists then the object had better be wholly present now, and perdurantism goes with eternalism. Some endurantists try to hang on to eternalism anyway, but I don't think that combination is stable.

There's a further view, stage theory, on which the things we name and quantify over are the momentary stages rather than the worms. Since on this view the object we're talking about is itself located at a single time, it's really a species of endurantism, just one that keeps the perdurantist's temporal parts in the background.

Finally, I don't think the disagreement is merely verbal. Both sides agree that I persist and change, but they disagree about whether there are temporal parts, and that's a real disagreement about what exists, so it's substantive."
