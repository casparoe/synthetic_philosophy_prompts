I was recently appointed to the grantmaking committee of a small foundation, and several of the proposals now before us rest on the claim that because the future may contain infinitely many people, certain interventions carry infinite expected value and therefore dominate every alternative. I am not yet persuaded either way, but I have realized that I cannot responsibly vote on any such framework until I know what a theory of value would have to deliver in order to be adequate to worlds of infinite population at all. Before we adopt one, I want the specification rather than a defense of any particular view. Please write the desiderata.

Structure the response in exactly the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated.

**The purpose (about 100 words).** State what the theory is being asked to be good for — in my case, ranking and then choosing between policies whose consequences include populations that may be infinite or unbounded — and tell me if you think I have misdescribed the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. You may narrow or widen it, but say so.

**The desiderata (700–900 words).** This is the substance. Give a numbered list. For each requirement, name it, state it as a requirement, and give the case or consideration that motivates it. Do not pad the list; include a requirement only if you can supply the case behind it. At minimum, the theory must get the following right, and you should say for each what verdict you think it should deliver and why that is a constraint rather than a prejudice:

- Two worlds that are identical except that one contains one additional person living a life barely worth living, where both worlds already contain infinitely many people.
- A world of infinitely many flourishing lives against a world of infinitely many miserable lives.
- A world that is infinite against a world that is finite but enormous and uniformly excellent.
- Two worlds that can be put in one-to-one correspondence with each other, so that they are "the same size" in the set-theoretic sense, yet one is plainly better on any intuitive reading.
- A world containing, for each natural number, infinitely many people whose lives sit at that level — so that the population is infinite at every level of well-being.
- An infinite sequence of worlds, each better than the last, with no best member, where a choice must still be made between two of them.
- A world whose population grows without bound over time.

The list should also register the distinctions the theory must not collapse and the results it must not contradict. Among the distinctions: the value of a world versus the value of a life; *at least as good as* versus *better than* versus *incomparable*, and whether the theory is permitted to return *incomparable* or is required to be total; finite versus infinite, and countable versus uncountable; the difference between a ranking of worlds and a usable decision rule, since an ordering may exist while no maximum does; and whether the theory must be operable by an ordinary agent who cannot evaluate infinite sums. Among the constraints: it must not simply reverse the verdicts we accept in the finite case without saying which finite verdicts it is prepared to abandon; it must not let the addition of a single person flip the ranking of two worlds already fixed; and it must not be defeatable by a mere relabeling or pairing argument that converts any ranking into an equality.

**What is non-negotiable and what is a camp preference (about 200 words).** Sort your own list: which requirements any adequate theory must meet, and which are commitments of a particular camp that a rival could reasonably reject.

**Where the list cannot be jointly satisfied (300–400 words).** This is the part I most want. Identify any subset of the requirements that cannot all hold at once, exhibit the conflict concretely rather than gesturing at it, and say what the conflict shows — whether the desiderata are jointly inconsistent, or whether some apparently obvious requirement was never really a requirement. If you believe the whole list is jointly satisfiable, say which requirement you had to weaken to achieve that, and defend the weakening.

**Which requirement does the most excluding (about 200 words).** Name the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise serious candidates, and explain the mechanism.

**The usual contenders against the list (about 250 words).** Briefly, how totalism, averageism, person-affecting views, and prioritarianism fare against the criteria — a short paragraph each, no more. Keep this section subordinate to the criteria; I care about the list, not the scorecard.

Argue from the cases. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or references to the literature, and do not assume I can look anything up.
