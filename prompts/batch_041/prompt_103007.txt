Two Ledgers, One Audit: Bootstrapping Without Circularity

The bootstrapping problem is usually introduced with a perceptual example. A subject believes that a wall is red. She also believes, on the basis of a series of such perceptual beliefs, that her color vision is reliable. She then uses that second-order belief to certify the first-order belief. The conclusion is that reliabilism makes knowledge too easy. I want to argue that this diagnosis is half right and half wrong. The problem is real, but it is not a problem about circularity, and it is not the same problem as the problem of easy knowledge. The two are independent, and running them together has obscured the structure of the difficulty.

Call the first phenomenon closure-based easy knowledge. A subject knows that the table is red. She knows that if the table is red, it is not a white table illuminated by red light. By closure, she knows that it is not a white table illuminated by red light. This seems too easy because she has not done anything to rule out the skeptical hypothesis; she has simply deduced its negation from a perceptual belief. The issue here is the closure principle and the transmission of warrant through known entailment.

Call the second phenomenon track-record bootstrapping. A subject forms a series of perceptual beliefs: this is red, that is red, and so on. She notices that her color judgments have been correct, or at least that they have been produced, and concludes that her color vision is reliable. She then uses that conclusion to defend her color judgments against the charge that they might be unreliable. The issue here is not closure but induction: she is using the outputs of a faculty to establish a positive claim about the faculty's reliability.

These are not the same. Closure-based easy knowledge can be generated by a single perceptual belief and a known conditional; it does not require a track record. Track-record bootstrapping requires multiple first-order beliefs and an inductive inference; it does not require closure. One could accept closure and still find track-record bootstrapping objectionable; one could reject closure and still find track-record bootstrapping objectionable. The standard reliabilist reply to easy knowledge—that the subject does know the negation of the skeptical hypothesis, because her perceptual process is in fact reliable—does not by itself address the track-record case, because the track-record case is not about the first-order belief's reliability but about the subject's basis for believing in that reliability.

So what is wrong with track-record bootstrapping? I suggest it is not premise-circularity. The subject does not assume the conclusion as a premise. Nor is it merely rule-circularity, if that means using a rule to establish its own reliability, because every vindication of a basic rule will be rule-circular in that thin sense, and that cannot by itself be an objection. The defect is rather that the subject treats the first-order beliefs as evidence for the reliability of the faculty that produced them, when their status as evidence depends on the very reliability in question. I call this evidential self-dependence. The first-order beliefs are not independent data points; they are the faculty's own outputs. To count them as confirming the faculty is like a bank auditing its own ledger by counting the entries in the ledger.

Imagine a bank that wants to assure depositors that its ledgers are accurate. It produces a second ledger, which records that the first ledger has been checked and found accurate. But the second ledger is written by the same clerk, using the same entries, and its accuracy depends on the first ledger being accurate. Depositors are not unreasonable to say that the audit is not independent. The problem is not that the second ledger contradicts the first or that it is formally circular; it is that the second ledger cannot provide the kind of assurance it claims to provide because it is not independent of the thing it is supposed to assure.

I propose the following principle: a subject is not justified in believing that a belief-forming process is reliable on the basis of beliefs that were produced by that process, unless the subject has independent entitlement to rely on the process. Call this the independence of vindication principle. The principle is meant to capture the intuition that a faculty cannot be its own independent witness. It is not a general prohibition on self-knowledge or on using a faculty to learn about itself; a subject can use her eyes to learn that she has eyes, and she can use her memory to learn that she has remembered correctly in the past. The principle applies only when the conclusion is that the process is reliable and the premises are the process's own outputs.

This raises the question of what independent entitlement amounts to. I want to distinguish entitlement from justification. A subject may be entitled to rely on her perceptual faculties without having a justification for a positive belief that they are reliable. Entitlement is a permission to rely; justification is a warrant for a belief. Track-record bootstrapping tries to convert entitlement into justification. It tries to turn the fact that the subject may rely on her color vision into a reason for thinking that color vision is reliable. If the independence principle is correct, that conversion fails. The subject may continue to rely on her color vision, and she may know many color facts, but she cannot bootstrap her way to a justified second-order belief about color vision's reliability.

The view has consequences that may seem unwelcome. It suggests that a subject cannot have a justified belief that her memory is reliable, if the only evidence she has is what she remembers. But that may be the right result: she is entitled to trust her memory, and she can know things by remembering, but she cannot justify a general claim about memory's reliability from within memory. This is not skepticism about memory; it is a distinction between first-order knowledge and second-order vindication. The same goes for perception, induction, and testimony. If the distinction is stable, the bootstrapping problem is not a problem for reliabilism specifically but for any attempt to vindicate a basic faculty from within its own resources.

The upshot is that the bootstrapping problem should be separated from the easy knowledge problem. The latter is about closure and the transmission of warrant; the former is about evidential self-dependence and the limits of vindication. Reliabilism may still have a problem, but it is not the problem of circularity, and it is not solved by denying closure. It is solved, if at all, by recognizing that entitlement is not the same as justification and that some commitments cannot be vindicated from within.

I would particularly value your thoughts on four points. First, does the distinction between closure-based easy knowledge and track-record bootstrapping hold, or do the cases ultimately collapse into one another? Second, is the independence of vindication principle independently motivated, or is it an ad hoc restriction on reliabilism? Third, does the principle have unacceptable consequences for memory, testimony, or induction—for example, does it imply that a subject can never be justified in believing that her memory is reliable? Fourth, is the two-ledger analogy doing real work, or is it just a vivid restatement of the circularity charge? Finally, I would like an honest judgment about whether the essay says anything that someone familiar with the debate would not already have thought, and if not, what the most promising way to sharpen it would be.
