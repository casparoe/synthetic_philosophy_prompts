A working group I am part of is designing a proof-based agent. The agent begins from a fixed, finite set of axioms, derives further sentences by explicit formal rules, and treats the sentences it derives as beliefs on which it may act. Its language is rich enough to encode arithmetic and to express statements about proofs themselves, so the agent can, in principle, reason about its own derivations and arrive at conclusions about its own reliability. The design goal is that the agent be able to trust itself: to treat its own reasoning as a guide it need not double-check from the outside.

Throughout our discussions, one colleague keeps closing off objections with a single sentence: "If it can be proved, then it's true." He deploys it to mean that once the agent has derived some sentence, the agent may simply take that sentence on board as a belief — including sentences concerning the agent's own trustworthiness — and that nothing further needs to be said about why the agent should rely on what it derives. When I press him, he treats the point as obvious and says I am overcomplicating it.

I suspect the one-liner is doing several quite different jobs at once, and that some of the readings are innocuous while others are precisely what makes a self-trusting agent difficult to build. Please lay the readings out for me as a numbered menu, from the most trivial to the most radical. For each reading, state the precise claim it amounts to, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and make clear who is supposed to be asserting it: is it a fact about the system stated from the outside, a sentence the system can itself derive, a principle we choose to impose as an extra axiom, or a norm telling the agent what it ought to believe? Where a reading generates trouble, explain the trouble in full rather than merely naming it, and say what kind of system would and would not fall foul of it. Finally, tell me which of the readings my colleague must be relying on for his move to work, and whether that reading is genuinely available to an agent powerful enough to reason about its own proofs.

Write your answer in British English, in continuous prose under numbered headings rather than as bullet fragments.
