Exam question: Explain the utility monster thought experiment and assess what it shows about utilitarianism. In your answer, (a) state the thought experiment clearly; (b) distinguish at least two forms of utilitarianism and explain how the monster bears on each; (c) evaluate one standard reply; and (d) conclude whether the thought experiment refutes utilitarianism. The course has emphasized the distinction between act and rule utilitarianism and between total and average utilitarianism, and has discussed the separateness of persons and the repugnant conclusion.

Student's answer: Robert Nozick introduced the utility monster as a criticism of utilitarianism. The monster is a being who gets enormously more utility from any given resource than an ordinary person does: an apple might give you one unit of pleasure and the monster a hundred. Since total utilitarianism tells us to maximize total utility, it tells us to give the monster all the apples, and then all the resources, even if everyone else suffers. Total utilitarianism would therefore require that everyone else be sacrificed for the monster's pleasure.

This is a problem for act utilitarianism, because act utilitarianism evaluates each act by its total utility and so would have to sacrifice the many for the monster. It is less of a problem for rule utilitarianism, because rule utilitarianism follows rules rather than calculating each act. A rule utilitarian could adopt the rule that we never sacrifice innocent people, and that rule would block the monster. It is also less of a problem for average utilitarianism, because average utilitarianism looks at average utility rather than total utility, so it would not sacrifice everyone merely to increase the total. So the monster mainly refutes total act utilitarianism, not utilitarianism as such.

A common reply is that the utility monster is empirically impossible: we have no reason to think such a being exists. Another reply is that utilitarianism is not hedonism, since utility can be preference satisfaction rather than pleasure. But the monster could have stronger preferences too, so this reply does not fully answer the objection.

I conclude that the utility monster is a powerful objection to utilitarianism. It shows that utilitarianism is not egalitarian, because it lets one being count for more than everyone else. But because rule utilitarianism and average utilitarianism can avoid the conclusion, the thought experiment does not refute utilitarianism in every form.
