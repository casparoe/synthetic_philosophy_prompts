I chair the state historical marker commission for our state historical society. We review roughly thirty applications a year and vote at monthly hearings; our plaques stay up for decades, so a bad one is at once a permanent embarrassment and a public insult. Our enabling language says markers must be "historically accurate," a phrase no one has ever defined. In the past two years we approved a marker that a descendant community says erases them, and rejected one that a neighborhood group says we dismissed only because its claim rested on oral tradition rather than documents. I want a decision procedure my staff historian and review subcommittee can actually run before each hearing — twenty minutes at triage, an hour for a contested application — working from what applicants submit plus digitized records and local historical society files, since we have no research budget and no power to compel anything.

The procedure has to settle what our arguments keep circling: when a community's memory and the documentary record conflict, it is not obvious which should give way, because the two answer to different things — the record to evidence, the memory to obligation, identity, and the present needs of those who hold it. So do not hand me a rule that simply ranks the two. Build the procedure out of the distinctions that do the work: between a claim about an event and a claim about what a community is; between what a culture keeps in active circulation and what it merely stores, and which of the two a plaque is doing; between the three things a marker text can do — assert, commemorate, and attribute ("the X community holds that...") — and the trap by which attribution becomes a laundered assertion. It must treat the absence of documents as evidence about who had the power to write things down rather than as automatic refutation, without letting that license invention, and it must keep "we found evidence against this" separate from "we found nothing either way." It should also say who has standing to speak for a community, and what to do when two factions of the same community both claim to.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it hands the decision to the commission because the question is about whose story deserves scarce public attention rather than about what happened. Then run it on three applications: (1) a descendant group seeking a marker asserting that their settlement predates the first land deed by thirty years, on the strength of an unbroken oral account and one ambiguous church record; (2) a town's application commemorating a founder whose story, as our staff historian reads the 1880s promotional history it rests on, appears to have been manufactured to justify taking land from the community that now objects; (3) a marker for an unmarked burial ground known only from oral tradition, where the official record is silent and a county history calls the site a battle. I am the commissioner who usually votes against tradition-based claims, so at least one of these should come out against my instincts; say so plainly. Finish with what the procedure cannot do, including the cases where the honest answer is an ugly compromise or a refusal to mark at all.
