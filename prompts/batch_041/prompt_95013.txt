I just got back from the longest dinner of my life and I need someone to settle it for me, because I couldn't. My mate Yusuf and my friend Ruth spent two hours going at each other over a documentary about the collapse of the Soviet Union, and by the end they were both appealing to me like I was the referee, which was awful because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about each step? Not just who wins overall — I want the individual moves rated and the reasoning.

(1) Ruth said the collapse was overdetermined. The economy was bankrupt, the republics were pulling away, the whole thing was coming down anyway — so singling out Gorbachev's decisions (or Yeltsin's, or the coup) as 'the cause' is just telling the story with a hero in it. Yusuf said thats cheating: a pile of things going badly doesn't prove the thing was going to happen. Plenty of rotten states stumble on for decades. If you want to say it was bound to fall you owe me an argument, not a vibe.

(2) Ruth said fine — imagine a roof held up by two pillars. Take one away and the roof stays up, so neither pillar was necessary for the roof. But obviously both were holding it up. She said that's her whole point: necessity is the wrong test, and once you drop it, hunting for 'the' cause is hopeless. Yusuf said she'd just handed him his own argument — if necessity isn't required, then Gorbachev's decision counts as a cause too, and she can't wave it off as storytelling. She said the pillars are both doing continuous work, whereas his 'cause' is a one-off nudge, and a nudge only causes anything if the stuff was already primed to go up. She said it's the gas, not the match.

(3) Yusuf tried to give an actual test: a cause is something where, if you'd changed just that one thing and left everything else the same, things would have gone differently. Ruth said that test is useless in history because it makes everything a cause — the weather in Moscow, the price of oil, whether some guy in a breadline went home in a mood that day. If your test can't tell a cause from the wallpaper then the test is broken, and she'd rather keep the necessity test and admit it has exceptions.

(4) Yusuf said the reason we don't call the weather a cause isn't that it wasn't one, it's that nobody's asking about it. The question 'why did it fall?' always comes with a hidden 'rather than what?' — 'why 1991 and not 1985?' has one answer, 'why fall at all?' has another, and the fight about the real cause is really a fight about which question is being asked. Ruth said that's just relabelling: if the answer depends on the question then it's a fact about us, not about the past, and historians are supposed to be telling us about the past.

Yusuf ended up calling her a relativist who smuggled in her conclusion, and Ruth ended up saying he can't tell a cause from a coincidence. I've been chewing on it alot since and I think they're both onto something and both full of it, but I couldn't say where. Rate each of the four moves from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who has the better overall position, and tell me what they're both missing.
