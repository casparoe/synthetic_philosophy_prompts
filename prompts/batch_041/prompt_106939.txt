I chair the disciplinary panel of a state professional licensing board. We hear twenty-five to forty cases a month involving nurses, pharmacists, contractors, financial advisers, and similar licensees. Our statute tells us to protect the public and to impose sanctions proportionate to the seriousness of the conduct and the harm caused, but it does not tell us how to treat the fact that two licensees can engage in equally poor conduct and only one of them happens to cause a catastrophe. Complainants, the public, and sometimes our own members treat the outcome as the measure of the wrongdoing. I want a decision procedure the panel can actually run, grounded in the moral luck literature, that preserves the real distinctions while being usable by seven board members who are not philosophers and who have about thirty minutes per case plus a written-reasons requirement. Our sanction schedule has five bands, from reprimand to revocation. Legal counsel signs off on the final order.

We have adopted an internal principle, which I will call the conduct-first rule: before the panel considers the actual outcome, it must assess the quality of the licensee’s conduct and decision-making using only what the licensee knew or should have known at the time. The actual outcome may then adjust the sanction, but only through a procedure that says when and how much. Your task is to turn that principle into an operational procedure.

Please give me the following.

1. A set of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops and produces a sanction band or a recommendation.

2. A way to separate at least four kinds of luck that can affect a licensing case: outcome luck, where the same conduct causes different harm; circumstantial luck, where the licensee happened to face a situation others did not; constitutive luck, where the licensee’s temperament, training, or background is at issue; and epistemic luck, concerning what was knowable at the time. Say which of these the panel can legitimately adjust for, which it should record but not adjust for, and why.

3. A treatment of tracing: when a later bad outcome can be traced back to an earlier culpable decision, and when the chain is too loose for the board to treat the outcome as part of the licensee’s fault.

4. A rule for hindsight bias: how the panel should keep the fact that harm occurred from inflating its judgment of how risky the conduct was ex ante. Include a concrete debiasing step, not just a warning.

5. A small number of tests the panel can run in order, with a scoring or ranking rule if you can make one that is honest, and a clear statement of the points where the procedure cannot decide and the panel must vote on values. Mark those points explicitly.

6. Worked applications to three cases: two pharmacists make the same dispensing error, one is caught by a nurse and one kills a patient; two commercial drivers drive the same number of hours fatigued, one hits a child and one arrives without incident; and a surgeon who follows the standard of care but has a rare-anatomy catastrophe, compared with a surgeon who cuts a corner and whose patient recovers. For each, show the procedure’s recommended adjustment to the sanction and the written rationale.

7. At least one case where the procedure gives an answer the public or the complainant will not like, and say how the panel should explain that answer without sounding indifferent to harm.

8. A short section on what the procedure cannot do. In particular, say where the board is really deciding a policy question about how much outcome should matter, not applying a philosophical result, and say what would need to change in the statute or the evidence for the procedure to give a different answer.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or a survey of the moral luck debate. I need the procedure, the hard cases it must handle, and the places where judgment cannot be eliminated. You may invent intermediate concepts if they help, but label them as your inventions and keep them simple enough for a board member to apply under time pressure.
