ok here's one i've been stuck on for a week. wrote it up for a seminar, can't decide if it's a real arguement or a trick with a step i keep sliding past.

The setup. Some authority is chosing between two long-run policies, P and Q. P is resource-hungry: it keeps a lot of stuff going now that makes life in, say, 2200 harder — worse health, worse options, hotter, you get the picture. Q is leaner and the people in 2200 are noticeably better off. Only catch, and my friend insists this is just true rather than a philosopher's stipulation: P and Q move upstream conditions enough (timing, migration, who meets who, when people can afford kids) that they don't produce the same people in 2200. Different conceptions. Nobody is swapping souls around; it's just that which sperm meets which egg depends on a thousand upstream things. Assume the people under P have lives worth living, just worse than the ones under Q.

The argument:

(1) For a policy to be wrong it has to wrong somebody.
(2) A policy wrongs somebody only if it leaves that person worse off than they would have been otherwise.
(3) Take any person alive under P. Under Q, that person is never born. And there's no alternative history in which they exist and are better off, since the alternative history is the one where different people get conceived.
(4) So the alternative to their actual, worse life isn't a better life. It's no life.
(5) No life isn't worse for anyone — there's nobody there to be worse off.
(6) So P leaves nobody worse off than they would have been; by (2) it wrongs nobody; by (1) it isn't wrong.
(7) Nothing in that turned on the policy being about resources. Any choice that moves the upstream conditions moves who gets conceived.
(8) So a big swathe of what we do to the future — what we build, where we build it, what we leave in the water — is off the hook for the people it lands on, as long as it changes who those people are. A theory that can't call that wrong isn't a theory of our obligations to the future at all.

I'd really like to keep (5) — you can't be worse off for never having existed, that seems unarguable to me — and I'd like to keep (1) if I possibly can. I half-suspect the trouble is in how (3) is worded, but I can't make that do any work. If it comes to it I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (7) and (8), but I want to know what I'm paying.

Two things that might be clues and might be noise. First: change the case so the same child exists either way (mum's already pregnant when the policy flips, P exposes her to something, Q doesn't). Now (3) fails, the child is just worse off, and we all say the policy wronged her. So the whole escape hatch is bought by an upstream detail about which sperm met which egg, and it feels like the argument turns a difference in how a harm came about into a difference in whether there was a harm. Second: a friend keeps saying you can wrong someone without making them worse off at all — using them, treating them as a means, that sort of thing — but I can't tell whether that helps here or just changes the subject, because if nobody is worse off, who's the one being used?

So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me? In particular if I drop (1) or (2), what comes in the door with them? And if there's a version of the argument that survives with one change, what is it? Don't worry about who said what or what any of this is called, I'll fill that in myself.
