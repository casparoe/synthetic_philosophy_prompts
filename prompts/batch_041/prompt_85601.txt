I am an undergraduate in a moral philosophy course, currently writing a paper on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and our seminar has been going in circles. The aggregative consequentialist says that the objection either collapses into a bare assertion that we may not aggregate or else begs the question against consequentialism; the restrictionist says that the consequentialist is treating persons as mere receptacles for value, and that this is not a side constraint but a condition on what counts as a moral reason at all. I want something more constructive than another round of that exchange, so please draft the joint statement that a committed aggregative consequentialist and a committed separateness-of-persons restrictionist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they agree on -- about the structure of the moral problem, about what the objection is not, about the distinction between persons as separate loci of experience and persons as separate loci of claims, and about which cases both agree are not decisive. I also want the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction.

In particular, I want you to use a distinction from our handout that I had not seen before: between separateness as a metaphysical fact (persons are distinct subjects of experience) and separateness as a normative constraint (that distinctness forbids certain kinds of aggregation). The question I care most about is whether the second follows from the first, and if so under what description of the trade-off. Please also separate two further questions: whether interpersonal aggregation can be part of a decision procedure, and whether it can be part of the criterion of rightness. And please consider a case we invented in class, 'the Divided Ventilator': one ventilator can either be used to save one patient for a full recovery, or split in a way that saves two patients but leaves both with a serious impairment; no one is used as a means, the patients are strangers, and the total welfare is greater under the split. I want to know whether the collaboration can agree on what the case shows, and whether the restrictionist can reject the split without appealing to a rule that the aggregative consequentialist would reject. If the dispute turns out to be partly verbal, say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the aggregative consequentialist's formulation, the restrictionist's formulation, the shared test or case, and each side's prediction. At the end, say how confident you are in the joint statement you have drafted, and single out the point you are least sure about -- whether it is a claim of agreement or a formulation of disagreement. Please write in British English, and end with a final line in the form \answer{...} enclosing a short phrase naming the crux you think is most likely to be settled by the shared test you have proposed.
