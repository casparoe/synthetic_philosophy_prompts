Twenty-odd years ago I left the river town I grew up in and I've been back maybe five times since, but they've asked me to give the speech at the unveiling of the memorial to the 1934 fire at the chair factory -- 41 dead, almost all of them women who worked on the third floor -- and I said yes before I thought about it.

The town has told one story about that fire for ninety years: a drifter who'd been hired that week knocked over a lamp and ran, and everyone else stayed. My great-grandmothers brother was the drifter. A retired schoolteacher has spent six years in the county archives and found the insurance adjusters report, which puts the fire in the drying room above a boiler the foreman had been running hotter than the policy allowed, and puts the drifter across the river asleep in a boarding house. She's giving a talk at the library two nights before the unveiling. The committee knows all of this; last month they quietly cut the line about the cause being undetermined off the plaque. The first three rows at the ceremony will be descendants of the women who died, a few of whom have asked the committee to leave the old story alone, since its what got the town to pay for the memorial in the first place. Two reporters will be there.

Write the speech, five minutes, ending the way the committee wants with the names read. It has to honor them first of all, that part isn't optional. The real work is that I have to take a position on which one gives way when a community's memory and the record disagree -- the memory that made the memorial possible, or the archive that just landed on top of it -- and I have to take it in the speech itself rather than by announcing it, without humiliating the families in the front row and without pretending the report doesn't exist. Then one line for me alone, not for the room, saying which one you just let win.
