I'm putting together a class on social choice and I want to build the whole session around Twelve Angry Men -- the 1957 Lumet film, Fonda as juror 8 -- but not in the usual "reasonable doubt is noble" way. Read the deliberation through Arrow's impossibility theorem. Start with the obvious mismatch: the theorem is about aggregating rankings over at least three alternatives, and a verdict is just guilty / not guilty, so I want you to be honest about where the analogy actually bites and where I'd be forcing it. What I'm really after is the rule. The jury's rule is unanimity, and the film treats that as its moral high point, but in Arrow's terms it looks less like a safeguard and more like handing every single juror a veto -- a dictatorship smeared across twelve people -- and paying for it by giving up on the rule always producing an outcome at all, since a hung jury is no collective ordering whatsoever. Does the movie know it's making that trade, or does it just luck into a dissenter who happens to be right? Second thing: the deliberation is basically a run of majority votes on separate premises -- the knife, the old man's testimony, the el train timing, the woman's eyesight -- and I want to know whether the film ever stages the case where the jurors' majorities on each premise and their majority verdict point in opposite directions. If it does, thats a lovely dramatization of a group being consistent on every part and incoherent as a whole; some political theorists call that the discursive dilemma, but explain it in your own words rather than name-dropping it at me. Third, Arrow takes everyone's preferences as fixed, while the entire film is about preferences being changed by argument, so is it a counterexample to the framework or just a profile that happens to move? Also note the foreman has no power except choosing what gets discussed in what order, and that turns out to be a lot of power -- say whether that's the agenda-setter problem in miniature. No quoting, I just want the reading, plus which scenes the class should fight about and where the film says something the theorem can't.
