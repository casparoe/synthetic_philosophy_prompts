I am preparing a graduate seminar on the politics of technology, and a colleague in political science has offered the following précis of Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" for us to use as a discussion text. I am uneasy about it, because it seems to me to smooth over a distinction Winner is at pains to draw, but I want to be certain before I correct it in front of students. Here is the précis:

"Winner answers his own question with a qualified yes. He identifies two ways artifacts can have politics. First, technical arrangements can settle social questions: the design of a machine or system can advantage some groups and disadvantage others, often without anyone intending it. Second, some artifacts are inherently political: they either require or are strongly compatible with a particular social order. His central example of the first is Robert Moses's parkways, whose low overpasses were designed to keep buses off the roads to Jones Beach, thereby excluding poor and Black beachgoers. His central example of the second is nuclear power, which requires a centralized, technocratic state. The essay's upshot is that technology is not neutral, and that because certain technologies carry a politics with them, the choice of technology is a choice of social order. Winner therefore holds a version of technological determinism: the politics of a society is fixed by the technologies it adopts, and the only way to change the politics is to change the technology."

Please audit this claim by claim. For each claim, say whether a philosopher of technology working on this topic would accept it, regard it as a defensible simplification, or reject it; where the précis is wrong, say what Winner's actual position is and why the difference matters. I am especially interested in four things. First, does the précis's treatment of the Moses example capture Winner's first sense of "politics," or does it import an assumption about intentional design that Winner explicitly sets aside? Second, the précis moves from "some artifacts require or are compatible with a social order" to "the politics of a society is fixed by the technologies it adopts." Does that move preserve the distinction between requiring and being compatible with a social order, and does it attribute to Winner a determinism he rejects? Third, the précis omits Winner's further distinction between conditions internal to a technical system and conditions external to it. Does that omission matter for the accuracy of the précis's two-sense taxonomy? Fourth, suppose the empirical details of the Moses story are contested — suppose the clearance standards were not unusual and the exclusionary intent is not documented. Does Winner's thesis, or the précis's version of it, depend on the story being true? Finally, tell me whether the précis's concluding recommendation — that we must choose the right technologies because the politics follows automatically — follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is the opposite of Winner's point. Be explicit about which parts are contestable among specialists rather than settled. No bibliography or reading list; I want the audit itself. Maximum 650 words.
