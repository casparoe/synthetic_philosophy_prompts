I report on science and ideas for a national daily, and I sit as a lay member on the editorial standards committee of our public broadcaster — the panel that adjudicates complaints under the broadcaster's code. I've been assigned to draft the committee's written opinion in a case we sit on Thursday, and I want you to draft it for me.

The case. Our flagship ideas-and-science programme ran a segment on a contested clinical claim. The presenter named a researcher and, without engaging her published work, described it as discredited and described her as a campaigner rather than a scientist. She complained. The broadcaster's lawyers then settled a threatened defamation action; the settlement is confidential, and on counsel's advice the on-air statement was drafted so that it concedes neither falsity nor fault. What aired, scripted, was this: "There have been claims that our reporting on this was wrong. Mistakes were made, and I'm sorry for the upset this has caused. We'll do better." The complainant says this is not an apology and asks us to order a further on-air statement. The broadcaster says it has already apologised and that more would breach the settlement and force it to utter words it does not mean.

The gap in our code. The code requires that where a complaint is upheld the broadcaster "publish an apology" — and we have never defined what that is. We have held that a correction counts only if it identifies the specific statement corrected, states the accurate position, and reaches the original audience; and in an unrelated ruling we said an apology must be directed to the person wronged and be capable of being received by her. That is all we have.

What I need. A short written opinion that recommends a concrete disposition — uphold in full, uphold in part, or dismiss — together with the precise remedy and a deadline, and a workable test for when a published statement counts as an apology under our code. Apply your test to the statement above. Our chair has proposed that the code requires only a remedial apology (one that repairs the record and restores the complainant's standing) and not an expressive one (one that conveys the speaker's actual feelings), and she wants the opinion to say whether that distinction can do the work she thinks it can; the committee is split on it, so treat it seriously rather than dismissing it. The opinion should also confront the strongest objection to whatever you recommend — including the objection that a compelled apology cannot be sincere, and the question of what it means for an apology to need the wronged party's uptake when she has already refused it.

Write it as a document the committee can vote on, in formal register, and do not spend space on scholarly citations or outside reading; reason from the case file and the code as I've described them.
