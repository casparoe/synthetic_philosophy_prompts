I'm putting together a discussion piece for a graduate seminar on material constitution, and I want to avoid the well-worn statue-and-clay setup that everyone has already seen. So I've invented a case: a fourteenth-century bronze bell that is also a protected historic monument, and that is now being restored. The conservators replace the clapper, then—because of corrosion—gradually replace the entire bronze shell with a new alloy, preserving the shape, the inscription, and the acoustic profile. The city's heritage office insists the statue survives; the music foundation insists the bell survives; and a legal dispute over ownership turns on whether there is one object or two. Write a dialogue between two philosophers who have been asked to advise: a constitutionalist, who holds that the statue and the lump of bronze are numerically distinct but coincident entities with different persistence conditions and different modal properties, and a sortal relativist, who holds that there is only one material object and that 'statue' and 'lump' (or 'bell') are different descriptions that answer different identity questions.

They should use the restoration case to press each other. The constitutionalist should argue that the statue can survive the replacement of the clapper and perhaps even the gradual replacement of the shell, while the lump cannot; that the lump could survive being melted down and recast, while the statue cannot; and that these differences show we are dealing with two things, not one. The sortal relativist should argue that this multiplication of entities is unmotivated, that the apparent modal differences are artifacts of shifting between sortals, and that the legal dispute is better understood as a conflict between two ways of tracing continuity rather than a conflict between two objects. Make both characters equally competent—no strawmen. Let each respond to the other's actual arguments, concede where the other has a real point, and press where the other is weak. End not with a tidy resolution but with a sharp statement of exactly what remains in dispute and what would have to be true for either side to win. No citations or references; I want the dialogue itself to do the philosophical work.
