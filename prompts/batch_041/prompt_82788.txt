I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in my fifth book where I need to know what I have already committed my characters to. The book follows the deliberations of a fictional literary award across a decade. I can of course make the jury do anything I like; what I cannot do is make it act arbitrarily and have the book survive it. I have written five of its rulings. The sixth is the one I am stuck on, and I would rather it be forced than chosen.

The five, in the language of the minutes as I have drafted them:

The first: a formally superb novel, narrated by a man who savours his contempt for the tenants he is ruining. Shortlisted, not awarded. "There is no way to call this book good without saying something false."

The second: a novel by a writer whose private cruelty had just become public, with no trace of it anywhere in the book. Awarded. "We are asked to judge the book, and the book asks nothing of us that we should refuse."

The third: a memoir, awarded in an earlier chapter, stripped of the prize when its central atrocity turned out to be invented. "The fabrication did not merely mislead us; it took the ground out from under the book."

The fourth: a very funny comic novel whose best jokes are at the expense of a named living woman and her documented misfortunes. Not shortlisted. "The laughter is at her expense, and she was not asked."

The fifth: a bleak novel about the perpetrators of a massacre, rendered from the inside, unflinching, without a flicker of relish. Awarded. "The horror is rendered, not savoured."

Now the sixth. It is a first-person account by a colonial official complicit in a massacre. The prose is extraordinary; the sympathy with which the narrator is rendered is never withdrawn and never framed as a failing; the book contains no endorsement of him. The readers' reports I have written say that they found themselves unable to condemn him, and two of them say the book made the atrocity beautiful. The author, in interviews, has said that she shares her narrator's politics.

Tell me which way this jury goes, and which way it ought to go if it is to be consistent with itself. Set out the two or three rules that fit the five earlier rulings equally well, say what each predicts here, and give me your own best guess with your reasons. Tell me which of the five is the closest analog, and why it beats the others. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the sixth, say so, and then tell me which way the tie breaks and why. Tell me, honestly, what principle the jury adopts in either direction, because I need the book to know what it is arguing. Finally, name the further case I should write in a later chapter that would tell the candidate rules apart. I have room for one more ruling, and I want it to be the one that separates a jury that judges books from a jury that judges authors.
