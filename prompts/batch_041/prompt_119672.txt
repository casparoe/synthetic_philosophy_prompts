I am convening the second meeting of a small reading group on the KK principle and the anti-luminosity argument, and I want to open with something that forces us to see how much of the argument's force comes from its structure and how much from the examples it is usually run on. One member of the group keeps insisting that the whole thing is an artefact of the example, and I would like a ranking that settles whether that is right. So instead of another round of the standard cold-morning case, please take the list of conditions below and rank them strictly, from the one most resistant to the anti-luminosity argument to the one least resistant. Call a condition resistant to the extent that it is hard to construct a sorites-style series of cases, differing only by increments too small for the subject to discriminate, in which one is in the condition at one end and not in a position to know that one is in it. I take the argument to turn on three moving parts — a luminosity claim, a margin-for-error or safety condition on knowledge, and the tolerance of the series — and I want the ranking to expose which part is doing the work for each item.

The list, in no particular order: feeling cold; being in pain; having a visual experience as of something red; being mildly hungry; being bored; intending to do something tomorrow; believing some arbitrary proposition p; understanding a sentence one has just heard; being in a position to know that p; knowing that p; being conscious; and, as a deliberate oddity, being in some mood or other, which I include because I suspect determinables behave differently from determinates.

For each item give me three scores out of ten — first, how smoothly the condition admits of a graded series of cases; second, how much knowing that one is in the condition requires a margin for error, that is, requires that nearby cases also be cases of it; third, its overall vulnerability to the argument — together with one sentence naming the premise that does the work for it. Then give the final strict ordering, and flag any item whose placement is sensitive to whether knowledge requires safety or only reliability, saying briefly how the ordering shifts under the reliabilist reading. I am particularly interested in two possible escape routes: whether determinables such as being in some mood or other, or having some experience or other, can be luminous because the relevant series cannot be built at the level of the determinable at all, or whether it is simply rebuilt one level up; and whether a condition that is thresholded on a discrete, non-graded fact rather than on a continuum escapes the argument. Finally, tell me whether any item on the list is luminous in the full sense — if one is in the condition, one knows that one is in it — and if so, whether that would refute the argument or only mark its scope.
