I'm in a small reading group that's been working through AI-alignment-adjacent philosophy — we did Superintelligence last term, now we're on preference-based accounts of value — and I volunteered to bring a short piece next week on coherent extrapolated volition. It's a discussion draft, not a paper, and this isn't really my area, so I'd rather find out now then in the room. Here's the draft, more or less as it stands:

"CEV tells us to hand the world to whatever we would want if we 'knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together.' My worry is that this one move is being asked to do two different jobs, and it can only do one at a time. Job one is measurement: idealization strips out ignorance, bias and social pressure so that we see what people really want, the way you clean a lens. Job two is construction: idealization produces the preferences people ought to have, which they dont yet have in any form, so it doesn't reveal a fact, it makes the standard. Under job one the criterion is too thin to authorize anything — if my idealized self is just me minus some factual mistakes, nearly everything I actually want survives, and 'extrapolation' is a small correction rather than a source of authority. Under job two it can authorize, but then you owe two answers: why does what a merely possible agent wants give me a reason, and which idealization? Idealize away my prejudice but not my taste for sad songs? My ignorance but not my discount rate? There's no principled menu, so 'coherent' quietly does the selecting, and coherence is cheap. And the cases where idealization feels most necessary are the ones that hurt job one worst: take someone whose wants have been flattened by deprivation — the fox deciding the grapes are sour. No amount of extra information recovers a want that was never there; you have to change what they want, which is job two, which is where the laundering worry bites. Two smaller things. The phrase assumes the extrapolation converges; if it doesn't, CEV either names nothing or names whatever the procedure spits out, and then we're just describing a decision procedure. And it's 'our' volition, which is trouble at the base before it's trouble anywhere else — does the extrapolation include the severely demented, the comatose, the unborn? Extrapolate us separately and the results conflict, so you still need an aggregation theory, which was the real work all along; extrapolate us into one shared standpoint and you've built the answer into the setup."

What I want is to know whether that holds up. Is the measurement/construction split a real distinction, or have I manufactured a dilemma out of two things that aren't actually in tension? Of the three objections, which one is carrying the argument and which should I cut? And if the framing itself is wrong — say, if CEV was never meant to be an authority criterion and I've been attacking a target it doesn't claim to be — tell me plainly instead of being polite about it, I'd rather hear it now. Where you're genuinely unsure, hedge rather than sounding confident, and if you think the essay is worse than I think it is, that's fine, I'd rather know that too.
