Jeremy Bentham died in 1832, and the following three features of punishment all postdate him. First, sentencing in which a statistical instrument — often proprietary, its weights undisclosed — assigns each convicted offender a probability of reoffending, with the length of imprisonment varying accordingly. Second, preventive detention, in which a person who has completed his sentence remains confined because he is predicted to be dangerous. Third, restorative justice, in which victim and offender meet and agree on a reparation, the state's sentence being reduced or waived to match. Predict the verdicts Bentham would have reached on each, setting out what his utilitarianism implies, where that implication collides with his other commitments — to legality and publicity in the criminal law, to parsimony in the infliction of punishment, to the equal weight of every person's interest — and how he would answer a retributivist critic. Then say which of the three he would have judged most and least defensible, and where your reconstruction is least secure.
