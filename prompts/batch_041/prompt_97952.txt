I retired a few years ago from a career in atmospheric chemistry — most of it at a government lab in the States, with a long stretch seconded to an agency in Oslo — and I've been spending my new free time reading philosophy, mostly epistemology and political philosophy, which I'm finding harder than the chemistry ever was. Here's what I keep circling. I've lived under two very different defaults about government. In Norway, everyone I worked with seemed to start from the assumption that the agency was basically trying to do the right thing and would tell you the truth; back home, the default among my neighbors was closer to the opposite — assume they're lying or incompetent until proven otherwise. My gut says both defaults are lazy, but I can't get a principled handle on which is closer to right, or whether the honest answer is just "it depends on the domain." A colleague of mine made it worse by suggesting that trust should be indexed to what it costs you when you're wrong — being wrong about the weather service costs you a wet walk, being wrong about a drug regulator costs you your kid — which sounds right to me but I don't know how to turn it into a rule.

So here's what I'd like from you. Take a list of the sorts of things governments claim or do — public health agencies telling you what's safe to eat or breathe, statistical agencies reporting unemployment and inflation, central banks, the courts, the administration of elections, the police, drug regulators, the military's public statements, and why not, politicians' campaign promises — and sort them into tiers by how much trust they actually deserve from an ordinary citizen, where trust means the combination of three things: that they're reporting honestly, that they know what they're talking about, and that they're acting for you rather than for themselves or some third party. I want the ranking to say something about which of the two baselines, the Scandinavian default of trust or the American default of suspicion, is the more reasonable starting point, and whether the ranking itself should come out differently depending on which baseline you begin from — my suspicion is that the high-trust default is self-reinforcing and the low-trust one is too, so the same institution might genuinely deserve different treatment in the two places, but tell me if that's confused. Say in a sentence why each thing lands where it does, then finish with the bottom line: if you had to hand a young scientist one rule of thumb for calibrating trust in a government institution, what would it be, and would it be closer to the Norwegian attitude or the American one?
