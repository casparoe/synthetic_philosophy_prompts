I teach a third-year seminar in population ethics, and I am drafting the take-home essay for the final. My worry is that every strong student can already recite the standard battery of nonidentity cases and will simply reproduce one of them, so I want a question that cannot be answered that way, and that is self-contained enough that no one needs to look anything up or cite anyone. To that end I have built the question around a distinction from my own handout, which the students will have to apply rather than recognise. On the handout I separate three accounts of what it is for an act to harm someone: the ledger view, on which an act harms a person only if it leaves that person worse off than they would have been under some feasible alternative in which that same person exists; the floor view, on which an act harms a person if it leaves them below a threshold of a minimally decent life, whether or not any better alternative for that person was available; and the warrant view, on which an act harms a person if it breaches a duty owed to them, whether or not it leaves them worse off or below any floor. I want the question to make students see that the nonidentity problem is fundamentally a claim about which comparisons are available — that it is generated by the ledger view's demand for a same-person alternative — and that whether the problem survives therefore depends on a modal premise about personal identity which they must state explicitly rather than assume.

The case I want them to work with is invented, so that it cannot be recalled. A coastal town's water authority must choose between two plans. Plan A treats the water now by a cheap method that leaves a residue; children conceived after the switch are exposed in utero and will have, on average, a mild but lifelong cognitive deficit, though their lives will still be well worth living by any ordinary standard. Plan B defers treatment for five years while a superior method is built; during the deferral, families in the town postpone childbearing, so that the children eventually born under Plan B are not the same individuals as those who would have been born under Plan A. A minority of children, however, are conceived before the switch and gestate across it, and so are the same individuals under either plan. The authority's lawyer argues that Plan A wrongs no one, since every child born under it owes its very existence to it. I would like the question itself, aimed at about ninety minutes of writing, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words that reaches a defensible verdict on the lawyer's argument, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth four marks each, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically miss it. Neither the question nor the model answer should depend on naming any philosopher or work; the marks should attach to the moves, not the names. Please end with a single final line in the form Crux: <one sentence>, stating the one move that most cleanly separates an A from a B.
