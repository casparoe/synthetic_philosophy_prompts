I've spent the last few weeks doing interviews for a piece about thought experiments -- how it is that just imagining something can tell you anything about the world -- and I've come out of it with a pile of gut verdicts about which ones actually delivered knowledge and which ones didn't. I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them, and which verdicts I have to give up to keep it.

Here's the pile. Tying a light stone to a heavy one: if heavy things fall faster, the pair should fall slower than the heavy stone alone, because the light one drags, and faster than the heavy stone alone, because the pair is heavier. The assumption eats itself. I say that gave us knowledge, with no lab and no new fact about the world going in. Same family: a loop of balls draped over a double incline, where the argument is that if it slid around it would keep sliding forever, so you'd have a perpetual motion machine, therefore it doesn't slide, and out drops the law of the inclined plane. I also say knowledge -- and this one bugs me, because it gets a quantitative law out of a symmetry and a no-free-lunch hunch.

Then the demon that sorts fast molecules from slow ones: knowledge, but only in hindsight, once it got cashed out as a theorem about information and entropy. As a bare picture it told us nothing. Schrodinger's cat: no knowledge, and I'd say worse than nothing -- it dramatizes a discomfort, it doesn't decide anything, and I think it entrenched a bad picture of measurement. Einstein's elevator: knowledge, obviously. The twin paradox: no knowledge. It's a puzzle whose answer is already sitting inside the theory, and there's no way the thought experiment comes out the other way. The old EPR setup, two particles flying apart with perfectly correlated properties, so that measuring one seems to tell you what the other one is doing unless you say the outcomes were fixed all along: knowledge, but of a strange kind -- the conclusion is one everyone now rejects, and its value was that it forced a real experiment into existence decades later. Productive failure. The dark night sky, where an infinite eternal starry universe predicts a bright sky: knowledge, it narrows the field. And the where-is-everybody argument, that if life is common and the galaxy is old, somebody should have shown up by now, so one of my premises is false: no knowledge. It tells you something in the picture is broken and refuses to say which. That's a mood, not a result.

My tentative principle: a thought experiment earns its keep only when it could have come out otherwise -- when it rules out a way the world could be that we could in principle have found it to be. When it just unfolds what the theory already implies, or when it only tells you that something has to give without telling you what, it's a mood.

Test that against the pile. The inclined plane looks like a counterexample, since nothing about it could have failed. The demon only qualifies retroactively. And the dark sky and the where-is-everybody cases have the same logical shape -- a conjunction of plausible premises with a false member -- and I've given them opposite verdicts. So: is there a principle that keeps all of these, or close, and what does it cost me? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? The one I keep going back and forth on is Newton's bucket, so say what your principle does with that, give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed, and add a line on my editor's favorite question -- whether a machine that can grind out thought experiments by the million is generating knowledge this way or just generating pictures. American English, please, and skip the bibliography; I'm filing from a hotel with terrible wifi. End with a final line that is exactly \answer{...}, and inside the braces put the single verdict of mine you'd overturn, or none if your principle saves them all.
