I chair the drafting group for a national scientific advisory body. Each year we publish an assessment consisting of roughly forty separately evidenced findings, each defensible on its own, followed by an executive summary that asserts them together; ministers, regulators and the press read the summary as a single commitment, and I and our chief executive both sign the cover. Our meetings now cycle through the same two arguments — that each finding is well supported so the summary is well supported, and that if we cannot assert all forty together we should not assert any — and I need a procedure that settles what we may actually sign.

Write me a decision procedure the drafting group can run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to the chair because the question concerns our risk appetite rather than the evidence. Ground it in what the lottery and preface paradoxes really establish — that a high threshold for accepting each of many claims does not license accepting their conjunction, and that it can be rational to believe each of a set of claims while believing that at least one is false — together with whatever work on acceptance versus graded credence, and on dependence among evidence claims, is usable by people who are not philosophers.

It has to run inside a two-hour meeting, and it must say what to do when we have no joint probability and cannot assume the findings are independent, which is our normal situation. Apply it to three cases: forty findings each supported at 95%, all drawn from one dataset; a report whose findings all rest on the same three studies; and a case where the defensible summary is a hedged one the communications office will reject. State plainly what the procedure cannot do, including how it behaves as the number of findings grows.
