My sister-in-law has turned our family WhatsApp into a war zone. Anything I send, she says she "saw it with her own eyes," and if I send a link, that's apparently just somebody's opinion, and my dad's whole position is that if enough people say a thing then it's basically true. I got annoyed enough that I actually wrote up house rules for the group. A friend who did a couple of years of Sanskrit read them and said, congrats, you've reinvented Nyāya — the four pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, testimony) plus the thing where testimony only counts if it comes from a reliable speaker. Now I've gone down the rabbit hole and honestly it's made me worse, not better.

So write me the document. A welcome packet / house-rules handbook for anyone new joining the family chat: numbered clauses, flat condo-board or HR-onboarding register, deadpan, ~700 words. The comedy is that the rules take all this seriously as *policy* and never explain it — they just presuppose it. Someone who knows the tradition should laugh and also not wince; my mum, who doesn't, should still be able to follow it as a comedy about a bossy family. And please don't let it turn into a lecture or a list of doctrines — every rule should be doing work in the actual family drama.

Bits I want in there, because they're the ones that are genuinely funny:

- A screenshot is not perception (there's no sense organ in contact with anything), so the document has to decide what a forwarded photo actually is. Testimony from whoever took it? Memory? Both?
- Inference has to be licensed by a relation that holds *always*, not usually. So the rules demand that people produce the license, and my cousin's "he's always late anyway" fails.
- Comparison — the one everyone forgets. It's how you know what a thing is when all you've been told is that it resembles something else (you've never seen a wild ox, you're told it's like a cow, then you see one). In the group chat this is every "it's like X but for Y" analogy, and the rules should have a whole clause on it.
- Testimony needs a reliable speaker, and the document has to say how reliability gets checked. That walks straight into the regress: who certifies the certifier, and does a claim certify itself or does it need another claim to certify it — the tradition splits on exactly that, so let the house rule pick a side and suffer for it.
- The Absence fight. Whether "there's no evidence of X" is its own way of knowing something, or just inference/perception in disguise — one school says it's its own pramāṇa (anupalabdhi), Nyāya says no. So somebody in the family keeps filing tickets for an Absence tab and keeps getting denied. That should be a running bit.
- Double counting: does it count for more if I both saw it and inferred it? One school says two pramāṇas can converge on the same fact; another says each has its own exclusive turf and they never overlap. I think the words are samplava and vyavasthā, don't ask me which is which. Either way the house rule needs to settle it and someone's uncle should be furious.
