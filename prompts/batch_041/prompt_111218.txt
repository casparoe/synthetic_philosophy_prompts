I'm in a pragmatism seminar this term and I'm trying to get a head start on my final paper, and I keep running into the same wall. Every time we argue about Peirce versus James on truth, everyone just quietly assumes their own list of what a theory of truth is supposed to do and then uses it to declare the other guy's view a failure. Peirce's story, roughly, is that a true belief is the one inquiry would settle on at the limit, and James's is that a belief is true when holding it works, when it pays off for us in practice. Those are obviously different, but I can't even tell which one is worse until I know what the job description is. So before I pick a side I want the requirements written out: what does any theory of truth, pragmatist or otherwise, actually have to deliver to be worth taking seriously? And I mean the specific things it has to get right, each one tied to the case or consideration that forces it, not just a wish list. For instance, I think it has to accommodate the fact that calling a belief true seems to just be endorsing what the belief says (the disquotational thing, whatever we're calling it this week); it has to explain why truth is something we're supposed to care about and aim at, since we treat it as a norm of belief and assertion; it has to keep truth from sliding into justification or warranted assertability, otherwise there's no sense in the thought that an entire community could be as warranted as you like and still just wrong; and it has to say something about truths that nobody will ever investigate. My professor loves a case like this -- some totally inert fact about the remote past, say exactly how many people happened to be standing in a particular marketplace on a given afternoon three thousand years ago, is either true or false but no one will ever know and it makes no practical difference to anyone, and I genuinely don't know what a pragmatist is allowed to say about it.

There's a second case I want you to deal with too, because it's the one that actually worries me: two communities of inquirers, each perfectly rational, each using good methods, each internally coherent, and each converging over time on a verdict that contradicts the other's. If you want convergence to be the criterion of truth, that seems to break you, but if you don't, I'm not sure what's left of the pragmatist idea. So here's what I want. Give me the list of requirements, with the reasoning behind each -- why that case or consideration actually forces the theory to do something, not merely that we'd find it nice -- and tell me which ones are non-negotiable and which are really just parochial demands of one camp that we could drop without losing anything. The part I most want is the conflict: which of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once, and what has to give. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so outright and say which one loses; that's the actual result I want to build the paper around, and I'd rather have a sharp trade-off than a tidy list. Please don't smuggle in a correspondence theory of truth as if it were a neutral requirement -- if it is one, show me why. Once the spec is on the table, give me a short, honest read on Peirce and James against it: which requirements each clearly meets, which they fudge, and which one they flat-out fail. Not a history lesson, just the spec and the scorecard. I don't need citations or a reading list, I need the criteria. American English please, and honestly a table for the requirements would help me a lot when I'm studying.
