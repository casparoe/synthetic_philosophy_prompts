I would like one specific episode analysed through the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and through global workspace theory: Libet's timing experiments on conscious intention, and the long argument about free will that they set off. The episode as I understand it runs like this. Subjects were asked to move a finger or a wrist whenever they felt like it, while watching a fast-moving clock, and to note where the clock hand stood at the moment they first felt the urge to move. A brain signal known as the readiness potential began building up a few hundred milliseconds before the movement itself, and the clock time the subjects reported for their urge fell after that signal was already under way. This is very often reported as showing that the brain decides before we do.

What I want is for you to pull apart three things that the popular discussion runs together: first, the phenomenal feeling of wanting to move, whatever that is like from the inside; second, the access-conscious and reportable judgment "I am deciding to move now", which is the only thing the clock reading can actually capture; and third, the underlying motor preparation that the readiness potential is a sign of. Then tell me, for each of two views — the view that access and phenomenal consciousness can come apart, and the global workspace view that treats a state as conscious just when it is broadcast widely enough to be available for report and control — which of the three things Libet's data really bear on, and whether the free-will reading survives once the three have been separated.
