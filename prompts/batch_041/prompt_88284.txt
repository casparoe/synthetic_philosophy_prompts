A colleague in my ethics reading group keeps modeling consent on contract. Her claim is that the three standard ways consent goes wrong — deception, coercion, and incompetence — are just the consent-theoretic analogues of the three standard ways a contract goes wrong: misrepresentation or fraud, duress, and incapacity. She draws the moral that a deception-tainted consent is void in the way a fraudulently procured contract is void, that the disclosure owed before consent is the disclosure owed before signing, and that the whole apparatus of contract doctrine — the void/voidable distinction, the materiality and reliance requirements, the objective reasonable-person standard — can be transplanted wholesale to tell us when consent is valid. She also likes to put it in a Hohfeldian register: consent is the exercise of a power to waive a claim-right, and a contract is the exercise of a power to create one, so the two are the same kind of legal-moral move and should share validity conditions.

I am not convinced, but I do not want to dismiss it either. Audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of offer and acceptance, of consideration, of the meeting of the minds, of the signature, of breach, and of remedy — and say which of those roles has no occupant on the consent side, and whether each empty slot is a genuine gap or a sign that the mapping was wrong from the start. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that matter for her conclusion and the ones that do not. I am especially interested in four points: (i) whether consent is bilateral at all, given that a proposal need not be made and, once consent is given, need not be accepted to be effective; (ii) whether the revocability of consent has any counterpart in contract, and what its absence does to the analogy; (iii) whether the void/voidable distinction carries over, and what the difference between invalid consent and merely vitiated consent actually amounts to; and (iv) whether importing the objective reasonable-person standard changes the subject, since a great deal of consent theory is about what this particular person actually understood or willed. Finally, tell me whether the transplanted conclusion survives — that deception vitiates consent exactly as fraud voids a contract, with the same materiality and reliance thresholds — or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. And if there is a better analogy for the validity conditions of consent, name it and say what it gets right that the contract analogy gets wrong.
