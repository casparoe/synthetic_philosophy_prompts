I teach a second-year decision theory seminar — 24 undergrads, one 50-minute session. I'm intrested in organising a game that makes them feel the absent-minded driver problem from the inside, not just hear about it. You know the standard setup: two seperate identical junctions, exit at the first is worth 0 points, exit at the second is worth 4, and if you carry on past both you get 1; the driver can't tell which junction they're at when they choose. I want to inject a new twist so they can't just recall the puzzle: there's a passenger who can see which junction they're at, but the passenger is bound by a contract written before the trip and can only communicate by coughing once (or not coughing) before the driver chooses. The group has to write the contract, then run trials. I want them to first play without the passenger, then with the passenger, so they feel why the problem is a problem and then see what it would take to escape it. I don't want to lecture first; they should hit the conflict themselves and only hear the name afterwards.

Design the activity: setting, roles, materials, exact payoffs in points (no real money), how many rounds, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between choosing at a node and choosing a policy, and why act-by-act expected utility maximisation looks right but leads you astray. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the absent-minded driver problem the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in the debrief. Keep it under 800 words, and start with a one-sentence summary of the game before the rules.
