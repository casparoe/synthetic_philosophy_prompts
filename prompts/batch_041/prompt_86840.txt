I'm a novelist, and I've been noticing that I have strong instincts about when a person is actually in a position to say something — not whether it's rude, but whether they're entitled to put it forward as true at all — and I've started suspecting my instincts don't add up to any single rule. I'd like you to tell me what rule, or rules, my gut is running, whether it's coherent, and what I'd have to give up to make it coherent.

The thing is, I decide this stuff for a living, because every line of dialogue is me deciding whether a character is allowed to say what they say. So I have opinions. When a character tells someone the library closes at six because she's pretty sure she saw the sign, and she's right — I don't blink, that's fine. When a character looks at a raffle ticket and says "well, this won't win," and it's true she won't, one in a million — that one bothers me, I wouldn't let her say it, it feels like she's overstepping somehow even though what she said is true. When a character says "I'll have the draft to you by spring," meaning it, hoping it, having no real idea — totally fine, people say that sort of thing out loud all the time and I'd never call it a lie or even a stretch. But when a character is dead certain the pub is on Oak Street and he's wrong, it's actually on Elm — that feels like a transgression, even though he believed it completely, and honestly it feels like a worse one than the raffle ticket, which was at least true.

And then there's the case I really can't place. A character tells a friend their manuscript is good when she privately thinks it's a mess. I want to say that's worse than the wrong-pub thing, but I couldn't tell you why — the friend might even come to believe it. And off to the side there's the one where a character states something flatly false on purpose as a joke and nobody's fooled, and my instinct is that this isn't even in the same category, it's not an assertion at all, so the whole question doesn't apply to it.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these? I've heard it said the rule is that you should only say what you know, and that sounds clean until the spring-deadline case, where nobody knows anything and yet it's definately fine. I've also heard it's just that you should believe what you say, but that can't be it either, or the raffle ticket would be fine and it isn't. So which is it, or is it alot of different rules for different kinds of speech? Tell me the strongest single principle that saves the most of my verdicts, tell me which verdict I should abandon to get a cleaner one, and tell me what your favored principle predicts about a case I haven't mentioned — someone who knows a thing perfectly well but is lying to herself about whether she knows it. I'm also curious specifically whether the spring-deadline case and the raffle case are really the same kind of thing to you, because to me they feel miles apart and I can't say why, and I suspect the whole answer lives in that gap.
