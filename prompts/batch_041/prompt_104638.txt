I got into a long argument at a dinner party a few weeks back about whether a country can be guilty for things done before anyone currently alive was born, and the specific fight was about reparations. My friend kept falling back on one move: a nation is like a corporation. The corporation is a legal person that outlives every shareholder, it can be sued and still owe money decades after the people who signed the contracts are dead, the debts don't evaporate just because the original owners are gone, and the thing keeps its identity through a total turnover of members. So, he said, a country is the same kind of entity, the guilt attaches to it and not to the individuals passing through it, and the conclusion is that the debt is still owed and the guilt still stands. I'm supposed to write a seminar paper on Jaspers's book about German guilt, the one where he splits guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical kinds, and I want to use this dinner-party fight as my case study.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the corporation, the charter, the shareholders, the board, the creditors, the courts, the limited liability — and then the places where the mapping breaks. The ones I already suspect: membership in a nation isn't voluntary the way buying stock is, there's no exit option, there's no explicit charter, and corporate liability is mostly financial rather than the kind of guilt that can be felt or repented. But I want you to tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion my friend was trying to license and which are just noise. The thing I most want to see is whether the corporation picture can carry all four of Jaspers's kinds of guilt or only some of them — my hunch is it does okay for the political and maybe the criminal kind and falls apart on the moral and metaphysical ones, but I want to know why and whether that hunch is right. Then say whether the reparations conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work the whole time. If there's a better comparison for what's actually going on across generations, tell me what it is and what it gets right that the corporation doesn't.

Please write it in American English, and don't worry about giving me citations or reading lists — I just want the argument laid out. A table for the correspondences is fine, and feel free to be blunt about where my friend's move is smuggling something in.
