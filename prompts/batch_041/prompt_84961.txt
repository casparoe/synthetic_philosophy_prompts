I am an undergraduate in a jurisprudence seminar whose final unit has been on constitutions that amend themselves. To make the problem concrete, the seminar played a round of Nomic: players begin with a fixed set of rules, some marked off as entrenched and hard to change and the rest ordinary, and on each turn a player proposes an amendment that the table then votes on. The whole point of the game is that the rules governing amendment are themselves amendable, so the game can rewrite its constitution while it is being played, and our round produced exactly the tangles we had been reading about. One player proposed a rule lowering the threshold required to amend itself; another proposed a rule that would have made itself permanently unamendable; and by the end of the evening we could not agree whether the last successful vote had been valid at all, since the procedure it was taken under was the very procedure it changed. No one could point to a rule outside the game that authorized the game's rules.

For the final meeting each of us has to bring something to read aloud, and I would like mine to be a poem. Please write it.

Take the role of a poet who is also a philosopher of law: someone who can write a genuine poem and who understands why the bootstrapping problem here is not a mere curiosity. Think carefully before you write; I would rather have one poem that is exactly right than three that gesture at the idea. Start by sketching two or three candidate architectures for the poem in a few lines of prose, say briefly why you chose the one you did, and then write the poem.

The form must do the work of the paradox, so here is the constraint I want the poem to obey. Open in quatrains, each stanza closing on the same one-line refrain, repeated verbatim. Then, at a marked point, a numbered rule inside the poem amends the poem's own form, and from that point the poem must actually obey the amendment: whatever the rule changes must really change on the page, not merely be announced. The amendment is authorized by nothing except the rule it alters; there is no rule above it. You may choose which formal feature it changes, but choose one for which an overlap between the old and new orders is genuinely possible, and then make the final stanza satisfy both orders at once, so that a reader who has followed closely cannot say whether the amendment took effect or quietly failed. The ambiguity has to be carried by the lines themselves, without adding or subtracting words to force it.

Further requirements:

- Number the stanzas as a rulebook numbers its rules, and let those rules sound like rules — imperative, flat, faintly bureaucratic — while the verse around them stays alive. Do not let the bureaucratic register swallow the poem.
- The poem should register the mirror-image danger as well: a rule that entrenches itself and can never be repealed makes a mockery of the game's openness, while a rule that lowers the bar for its own amendment leaves nothing final. Both should be present as drama, not explained in a footnote.
- Do not name any philosopher, cite any text, or append notes. The poem has to carry the idea by itself, since I will be reading it to people who have not done the reading.
- Twenty-four to thirty-six lines. No fixed metre is required, but the line must be a real line, with attention to rhythm and, wherever the form still permits it, to rhyme.
- A specialist in self-reference should recognize the problem in it and not wince; someone who has never heard of Nomic should still hear a good poem.

After the poem, add three or four sentences, clearly separate from it, explaining where the reader can see that the amendment is self-authorized and why the final stanza can be read under both orders. If you conclude that both readings of the last stanza cannot be sustained honestly, say so, and tell me which one you sacrificed and why.
