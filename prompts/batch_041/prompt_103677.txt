I convene a final-year seminar in Indian epistemology, cross-listed with the philosophy of knowledge, and I am drafting the take-home section of the final examination. The section is meant to test whether students can reason about what it takes for a knowledge-source to count as a distinct pramāṇa, rather than recite the received list. I want a single essay question on the status of upamāna, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question may assume the following background, which students will have had and which therefore need not be re-explained inside the question itself: the Nyāya school admits four pramāṇas — pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (knowledge of something through its resemblance to something already known, as when one is told that a gavaya is like a cow and later recognises a gavaya), and śabda (testimony). The perennial challenge, pressed by rival schools, is that upamāna is not a genuine fourth source: its deliverances are alleged to be recoverable either by inference from the remembered instruction or by testimony taken together with perception.

The question should do three things. First, it should ask the student to adjudicate the dispute using an independence criterion that I shall stipulate within the question itself, so that the exercise does not turn on whether the candidate has read any particular commentary. Second, it should introduce a constructed case that the stock examples do not cover. Third, it should ask whether the criterion, as stipulated, cuts against the reductionist, against the Nyāya defender, or against both.

Please supply the essay question (roughly 150 words, self-contained and answerable in about sixty minutes), a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks, organised around five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the mistake that most often costs candidates that point. No bibliographic references are required of the candidates, and the question should not ask for any.

For the stipulated independence criterion, use this: a candidate source P counts as independent only if (i) there is a class of veridical cognitions that P reliably produces; (ii) no other admitted source reliably produces cognitions of that class on its own; and (iii) P has a distinctive instrument, a karaṇa, that is, a distinctive causal route from world to cognition, and not merely a distinctive occasion on which some other instrument operates.

For the constructed case, use this: a traveller has never encountered a gavaya. A trustworthy guide, who is unable to point to one, tells her only that gavaya is the word for animals that look like cows but are not cows. She later sees an unfamiliar animal, and the thought occurs to her that this is the sort of thing the guide meant. The student should say which source, on the Nyāya inventory, produces her cognition, and should use the case to say whether the reductionist or the Nyāya defender gains more from it.

Two further constraints on the model answer. It must not simply declare that Nyāya wins; it should identify what the reductionist gets right and should say which of the three conditions is doing the real work. And it should close by saying whether any of the other three pramāṇas would fare better or worse under the same criterion, so that the student is forced to treat the criterion as a general test rather than a device aimed at upamāna alone.
