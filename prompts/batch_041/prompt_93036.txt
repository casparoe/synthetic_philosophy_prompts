I am a program analyst at a mid-sized private foundation that makes roughly forty grants a year, and my job is to produce the ranked shortlist that our trustees vote on at the end of each funding round. A new strategic plan commits us to a single cross-cutting metric: every proposal is scored on one wellbeing scale, and we fund down the list until the money runs out. That means I now have to do something my graduate training taught me was either impossible or vaguely disreputable, namely compare improvements in the lives of different people, in different countries, living with different conditions, against one another. The trustees will not accept these are incommensurable as an answer, and I do not need a lecture on why the exercise is fraught. What I need is a procedure I can actually run on forty proposals in about a week, mostly on my own with one part-time colleague, using the program data and published cost-effectiveness estimates we already have and no budget for new research, and that I can defend in writing when a trustee asks why a deworming proposal outranked a proposal to treat severe depression in adolescents. I have roughly $12 million to allocate this cycle and the decisions are annual, so the procedure has to be stable enough to reuse next year.

Please turn whatever the philosophical and economic work on interpersonal comparison actually establishes into something operational. I want the steps in the order I should run them, and for each step what counts as a sufficient answer rather than a gesture; I want to be told which steps can be settled with ordinal information alone and which genuinely require cardinal information, and within the cardinal cases which require only that I can say how much better off one person is than another and which require that I can say whether one person is better off than another at all. I keep half-remembering some distinction along those lines and I suspect a good deal of our internal disagreement is really about which of the two we are quietly assuming, so the procedure should make the assumption explicit at each step rather than bury it. Where a dominance test or a Pareto-style shortcut can let me avoid a hard comparison altogether, I want that shortcut early and clearly marked, since I would rather skip the hardest comparisons than resolve them. I want the points where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to the trustees, stated as rules rather than as advice to use judgment, and I want a separate list of the substantive comparisons the procedure presupposes, so the board can see exactly what it is buying when it adopts the score. Flag plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic. The procedure should be immune to two obvious embarrassments: a proposal that makes everyone worse off but more equal must not rank well, and a proposal that raises the total by bringing many new people into existence at a level barely worth living must not be able to win on volume alone. If the honest answer is that some of these cannot be handled by a score at all, say so and tell me what to do instead.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: a large deworming program whose benefits are real but mostly modest, spread over hundreds of thousands of children; a small intensive psychotherapy program for a few hundred adolescents with severe treatment-resistant depression and elevated suicide risk; and a cash-transfer program that also appears to increase births in the recipient community, so that part of its measured benefit comes from lives that would not otherwise have existed. Show me where the procedure runs cleanly, where it stalls, and where it forces a comparison I would rather not make, and for at least one of the three give me the ranking I will not like, with the reasoning intact so I can pass it upward. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of proposal it will systematically mishandle, what it will do to grants whose benefits fall on future generations or on non-human animals, and the point at which I should stop trusting the score and put the question to the trustees as a question of values.
