I am a high-school senior and the student representative on our district's Instructional Technology Review Committee, which approves or rejects pilot programs involving AI systems that make or recommend decisions with ethical weight. Because I am the youngest member and have no technical training, I have been trying to infer the committee's implicit policy from its past rulings, and next week we hear a new proposal that I expect to be contentious. I would like your help predicting what the committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, rather than with any abstract theory of machine ethics.

Our recent decisions are these. We approved an automated essay-screening tool that flags possible plagiarism; it never assigns a penalty, a teacher reviews every flag and speaks with the student before any consequence, and the tool's internal model is proprietary and cannot be inspected. We rejected a hallway-camera system that would automatically issue detention for fights; in pilot tests it was more accurate than the assistant principals and produced a confidence score, but students received no explanation of the specific evidence and no chance to contest the detention before it was recorded. We approved a course-placement recommender built on an opaque neural network; a counselor makes the final placement, meets with the student, and can request the system's input data, and students and parents may seek a written rationale and a review. We rejected a system that allocated tutoring slots by optimizing a district-wide fairness metric; human oversight occurred only through aggregate monthly reports, so no individual student could learn why they were excluded or challenge the allocation before the term began. And we rejected an AI tutor that inferred students' emotional states and deliberately withheld praise to increase persistence; it raised test scores and parents could opt out, but its model of student affect and its motivational strategy were hidden from students and teachers.

The new proposal comes from a community nonprofit. It would deploy an AI system that screens student essays and attendance records for signs of abuse or neglect and recommends whether a school counselor should file a report with child protective services. The system is opaque, trained on decades of case outcomes, and in retrospective tests it identified more true cases and fewer false alarms than the counselors did. Its technical lead describes it as a hybrid moral reasoner: a deontological subroutine flags any disclosure of harm, a consequentialist subroutine estimates the effects of reporting on the family, and a third module trained on this committee's own past written decisions adjudicates when the first two conflict. A counselor reviews every positive recommendation before any report is filed and has the authority to disagree, but the system's output is a single risk score with no case-specific explanation, students and parents are not told the system was used, and there is no mechanism to contest the recommendation before the report is made. The nonprofit argues that the counselor's review satisfies our past insistence on human involvement, while the system's accuracy addresses our concern about error. I am uneasy, but I cannot tell whether our precedents actually forbid it. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own record? Which past case is the closest analog, and which implicit rule does it support? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, what principle would we be adopting by approving, and what principle by rejecting? Please give me your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, say what each candidate rule would predict about this proposal, and name the further case or variation that would best distinguish them, so that I can propose it at the meeting.
