Write a satirical product catalogue, closing with a Risk Factors section, for a fictional consultancy that sells historical analogies to governments, foundations, and multinational firms as a decision-support product. The register should be corporate and deadpan throughout; there should be no narrator, no winking asides, and no signal that the document knows it is funny. Aim for roughly 700 to 900 words, organised under headings such as About Us, Our Method, Catalogue, Testimonial, and Risk Factors.

The catalogue should sell recognisable goods: a Munich product for pre-emption against a revisionist power that has so far made only limited demands; a Vietnam product for restraint, warning about gradual escalation, quagmires, and the domestic cost of casualties; and a power-transition product built on the idea, now usually called the Thucydides trap, that a rising power and a ruling power tend toward war. Invent the firm's metrics, tiers, and internal vocabulary — a Munich multiplier, a quagmire discount, a transition-trap index, a premium second-order lesson tier for clients who have learned the wrong lesson from the last lesson — and keep them mutually consistent. The scoring apparatus should resemble something a policy shop would actually build, only more candid about what it is doing.

The humour has to come from the substance, so the document should incidentally get the historiography right. It should trade in the fact that the Munich lesson was largely assembled after the fact, out of a policy that was popular when it was made; that the imperative drawn from Munich and the imperative drawn from Vietnam pull in opposite directions and are invoked by the same institutions within the same decade; that whether a power transition is a structural cause of war or a story elites tell afterwards is a genuinely open question, which the product page should settle with complete confidence; and that analogy selection is conclusion-driven, which the Method section should demonstrate in the act of denying it. The Risk Factors section should disclose the real objections in flawless corporate euphemism: hindsight in the case selection, the absence of any out-of-sample test, the client's preference for the analogy that licenses a decision already taken, and the possibility that the product is best understood as a rhetoric-delivery mechanism. The testimonial should corroborate all of this while praising the firm.

Use no quotations from real documents, no real people, no citations, and no meta-commentary. A reader who knows the policy-analogy literature should find the satire fair; a reader who does not should still be able to tell what is being mocked.
