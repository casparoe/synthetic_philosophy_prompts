I am trying to state precisely what I mean when I call something democratic backsliding, and my verdicts on the cases below do not fit comfortably under any single principle I have tried. I would like you to find the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures them; to say what it implies for cases I have not listed; and to tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. The cases are loosely modeled on recent events in Central Europe, Turkey, and the United States, but I have altered the details so that the names of countries do not do the work for you.

1. A party wins a genuine parliamentary majority on ordinary turnout and uses that majority to rewrite the constitution: the constitutional court is enlarged, its members are appointed by the executive for twelve-year terms, and its jurisdiction over electoral law is stripped. Every step is legal and was announced in the manifesto. I call this backsliding.

2. In the same country, the captured court outlasts the government that built it. Six years later a different party wins and governs under judges it did not appoint and cannot replace. I still call the whole episode backsliding, and I call it backsliding in the year when the second party holds office.

3. A president postpones a scheduled parliamentary election for eight months, citing a security emergency, then holds it and is defeated. I call this backsliding, and I rank it as more serious than the constitutional rewrite in case 1, even though the incumbent lost.

4. A long-established democracy bans a party that was polling at a quarter of the vote and had campaigned on dismantling judicial review. I do not call this backsliding, though I find it troubling and possibly wrong.

5. A country has been governed by a single party for thirty years, with clientelist networks, a concentrated press, and weak local government, but nothing about that arrangement has changed recently. I do not call this backsliding; I would call it a defective or low-quality democracy.

6. A state legislature redraws districts so that the governing party can win a majority of seats with forty-five percent of the vote, and a referendum that would have reversed the map fails after a campaign dominated by misleading advertising. I call this backsliding.

7. A defeated incumbent pressures election officials and state legislatures to overturn certified results, and fails. I call this backsliding, and I do not think the failure reduces it to a mere attempt.

My best attempt at a principle is this: backsliding consists in a deliberate use of state power that makes the incumbent's continuance in office less dependent on winning the next election. When I test it, it seems to miss case 2 entirely, since no one is currently wielding state power there; it seems to count case 4, since banning a strong rival plainly reduces the incumbent's dependence on elections; and it seems not to count case 7, since the attempt failed and nothing changed. I am least willing to give up case 7, and least sure about case 4.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it, and whether the clause is doing independent work or merely restating another. Give me its verdicts on at least two situations I have not mentioned: a caretaker government of unelected technocrats that indefinitely postpones elections and is genuinely popular, and a private donor with no state office who buys most of a small country's newspapers and television and uses them to favor one party. Say honestly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the loss is worth the gain in simplicity. Finally, address two worries about the concept itself. First, if the principle needs a substantive standard of democracy and not merely a standard of removability, does applying it require me to take sides in a contested theory of what democracy is, and does that make the label an outside observer's judgment rather than a fact about the country? Second, since every one of my verdicts compares a country to its own earlier condition, is the concept essentially comparative, so that the very same institution can be backsliding in one country and merely unusual in another?
