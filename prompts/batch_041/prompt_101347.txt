I teach high school bio and I keep catching myself doing purpose-talk in class -- the plover fakes a broken wing to lead the fox off the nest, the aphid drops off the leaf to get away from the ladybird -- as if the animal is following a reason it has in mind. A friend told me not to sweat it, that this is what people call a free-floating rationale: there can be a reason for a trait or a behavior even when nothing anywhere is actually doing the reasoning, the reason just floats in the selection history. I half get it but I dont see how far it goes. What are the most important implications of taking free-floating rationales seriously -- for how I should talk about function and purpose in class, and for the weird end of the spectrum, like whether I now have to say a liver has a reason to store glycogen after a meal or that an ecosystem has reasons of its own? And where does the idea stop earning its keep and just turn into a fancy way of saying something is adaptive?
