I'm in the second term of a philosophy of science course, and we've just finished a unit on induction that culminated in Goodman's grue puzzle. The course has no assigned textbook; instead the department circulates a study guide written some years ago by a doctoral student, which is usually dependable but occasionally loose. I'm drafting an essay on whether the "new riddle" of induction is a genuinely new problem or merely Hume's problem in new clothing, and before I commit to an interpretation I'd like to check the guide's account against someone who actually knows the material. My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that the puzzle is more than a restatement of Hume, but I can't yet articulate why, and the guide has me second-guessing that instinct. The relevant passage, as I've transcribed it, runs roughly as follows.

"Goodman's grue paradox is his version of Hume's problem of induction. Hume showed that we can never justify our expectation that the future will resemble the past, since any argument for that expectation would have to presuppose it; Goodman sharpens the same difficulty into a puzzle about which regularities our evidence actually supports. Call an object grue if it is first examined before the year 2100 and is green, or is first examined after that year and is blue. Every emerald that has been examined so far has been both green and grue, so our evidence confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' to exactly the same degree, even though the two generalizations make opposite predictions about the first emerald examined after 2100. Since nothing in the evidence can prefer one to the other, Goodman concludes that induction cannot be rationally justified and that the honest position is skepticism. His proposed way out is to restrict projection to predicates that mark genuine joints in nature: green is projectible because it corresponds to a natural kind, whereas grue is not, being a contrived predicate that has to be defined in terms of green, blue and a date. The technical notion that does the work here is entrenchment: a predicate is entrenched to the degree that it is simple and natural for us to use, which is why green is entrenched and grue is not. The upshot is essentially a no-free-lunch theorem for learning: no inductive method can be shown to be superior to any other without first assuming something about which hypotheses are the right ones."

I'd be grateful for a claim-by-claim audit rather than a general verdict. Specifically: which statements are accurate; which are the sort of licensed simplification a popularizer is entitled to make; and which are actually mistaken, or would embarrass me if I reproduced them in the essay. Where the guide attributes a particular position or solution to Goodman, I'd like to know whether it is really his or belongs to someone else, and whether the skeptical conclusion it draws follows from anything Goodman actually establishes. Finally, since the passage seems to be doing several things at once, I'd like to know whether its parts are even mutually consistent -- whether the solution it offers can coexist with the diagnosis it gives.
