So I've spent the last few months reporting on the self-deception fight — philosophers and psychologists who've been sniping at each other for years over whether self-deception is even possible. The core of it: deceiving someone normally means getting them to believe something you know isn't true, and here the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, which sounds like a contradiction. I've got two of them on the phone next week and I want them to produce the document their grad students all wish existed. Camp one: self-deception is real and does involve something functionally like an intention to keep a truth out of view — a divided or partitioned mind, one part working against another. Camp two: that's incoherent, and everything we call self-deception is just motivationally biased belief formation — wishful thinking, lopsided evidence-gathering — with no intention and no deception anywhere. Write the joint statement they could both put their names to. I want the claims both can sign about the phenomenon itself, including the cases nobody disputes (the spouse who somehow never notices the affair; the person who sincerely reports they'll quit smoking next month and keeps not quitting); the exact disagreements, each phrased so the side that holds it would sign off on its own wording; the cruxes — I'd guess one is whether intention is required and another is whether the self-deceiver really knows the truth somewhere; and for each crux, a case or experiment both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If a chunk of the fight turns out to be about the word deception rather than the phenomenon, say which chunk and why. And make them both deal with the objection I keep raising: if you really beleive it, you should take a bet on it at decent odds, and self-deceivers won't. What does that show, and do they agree about it?
