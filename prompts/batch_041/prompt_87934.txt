Write a ghazal of six or seven couplets on the special composition question — the question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing. Let the form do the arguing. Keep the couplets autonomous, as the ghazal allows, so that read one by one the poem is a heap of independent things and nothing composes anything; the monorhyme and the radif — the identical word or phrase closing every couplet — are then the only candidate unifiers, and the poem should end without settling whether they succeed. Give the competing answers their due across the couplets without naming anyone: that composition never occurs; that it occurs wherever you like; that it occurs only where the parts are swept up into a single life; that no informative rule can be given because composition is a brute fact; and that any rule one proposes will have borderline cases. Let each answer arrive as an image or a scene rather than a thesis, and let the closing couplet put the poem itself on trial — one poem here, or only couplets arranged poem-wise? A philosopher who knows the debate should be able to match each couplet to its answer; a reader who does not should finish unsure whether they have read one poem or six.
