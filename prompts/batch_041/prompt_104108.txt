I'm a second-year doctoral student in the philosophy of science, working mainly on model-based explanation in economics, and I've been drawn into a running argument with a colleague about the F-twist — the idea, usually traced to Friedman, that the unrealism of a theory's assumptions can itself count in the theory's favour. Her version of the position is a careful one: she grants that assumptions are frequently false, but maintains that their falsity is a virtue only instrumentally, and only relative to a target that has been fixed in advance; let the target float, she argues, and the F-twist degenerates into a blanket licence for any tractable fiction whatsoever. I think this is half-right but concedes too much to the critics, and I want to work out where I actually stand.

Please write an essay that adjudicates the controversy and defends a verdict. I'd like you to organise it around a distinction I've been trying to formulate, between load-bearing unrealism and scaffolding unrealism: an assumption is load-bearing when the result depends on its being false — relax it and the result disappears or inverts — and scaffolding when it merely makes the derivation tractable and would wash out under relaxation. Test this distinction against the usual stock cases (frictionless planes, point masses, perfectly competitive markets, rational expectations, infinite populations) and tell me whether it does the work I want or whether it collapses under pressure. In particular, I'd like you to press on the awkward case in which relaxing the assumption is not well defined at all, so that 'washing out under relaxation' has no clear content; and to consider whether an assumption can be scaffolding with respect to one target and load-bearing with respect to another without the distinction thereby becoming merely target-relative — which is roughly where my colleague wants to land.

The essay should engage the strongest version of the opposing view rather than a straw man, and should not end by splitting the difference or announcing that both sides have a point. Reach a conclusion about whether an assumption's descriptive falsity can ever be an epistemic virtue in its own right, and justify it. If your conclusion is a qualified one — for instance, 'yes for scaffolding unrealism, no for load-bearing unrealism' — state the qualification precisely and defend its boundaries. Assume I already know the standard literature; I want the argument, not the map.
