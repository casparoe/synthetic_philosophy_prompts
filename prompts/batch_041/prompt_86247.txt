Some context, because the committee is smaller than the issue: I sit on the ethics and compliance sub-committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds rural water and sanitation in two regions, we meet Thursday, and I have to circulate a short written opinion by Tuesday night. The case on the table is this. Our main local partner in one district, a few weeks before a tender for four borehole contracts, distributed sacks of rice and small cash amounts to a handful of village elders, and seperately sent an envelope to the district water officer who sits on the tender evaluation panel. The partner's director is the district officer's nephew. A junior staffer at the partner organization reported it to our country office, which sat on it for six weeks before forwarding it up, and now wants to stay anonymous. The partner's response is that the elders' share is customary hospitality they'd be shamed not to reciprocate at the next harvest, that the envelope was a facilitation payment to unblock a permit that had been sitting for months, and that the wells are finished and running. Our country director's recommendation is to continue the grant with a written warning: suspending it punishes the villages, we'd just be replaced by outfits with no oversight at all, and our own standard is a foreign import that our auditors apply selectively anyway. My suspicion is that the committee is having a fight about moral philosophy without noticing, and I'd rather the memo actually argue the point than recite that we have a zero-tolerance policy.

So what I need is the written opinion I'll circulate: a clear recommendation on the grant (continue, suspend, continue with conditions, terminate -- whatever you conclude is right) with a time frame attached to each step, and reasons the partner and our country office could actually respond to. A few things I want worked through rather than waved at, since they're what the committee keeps talking past each other on. First, whether a gift that creates a standing obligation between two people is the same kind of thing as a payment that buys one decision, and whether that distinction survives when the same person is doing the giving and the deciding. Second, whether what we're looking at is better described as clientelism than as corruption -- a durable, fairly open, reciprocal relation in which a patron is expected to redistribute to his people, which some people argue does a crude kind of accountability work where formal accountability is missing, as opposed to a covert one-off market exchange -- and if it is clientelism, what that actually changes about what we should do. Third, whether the norm we'd be enforcing, that officials allocate by impersonal rules, is a genuine universal requirement or the export of one particular state-building history, and what it would mean for our decision either way. There's also a test I keep coming back to that may or may not be a real distinction, I might have made it up in a meeting: ask whether the payment changed who got the contract or only how fast it got done, and ask whether a village assembly hearing the whole arrangement described in their own terms would endorse it or feel cheated. If those tests are confused, say so. Please also give the strongest honest version of the country director's position and explain why it doesn't win, and don't skip the practical bits -- whether we can protect the staffer, what we tell the other partners in the region, and what happens to the four wells and the villagers' own contributions if we pull out. I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, obviously, just the argument and the decision.
