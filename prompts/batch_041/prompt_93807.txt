Two of my colleagues have been at each other for a year now. One's a Quinean about radical translation: translation is indeterminate, there's no fact of the matter about what a speaker of an alien language means, only manuals that fit all the behavioural evidence, and the gavagai case is just the cute version of a completely general point. The other thinks that's at best underdetermination — an epistemic predicament, our problem, not the world's — and that what a speaker means is a perfectly real fact fixed by whats in their head or by the causal history of their words. Write the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they agree on (about the evidence base, about holism, about what the analytic/synthetic business has to do with any of it); the residual disagreement written twice, once in wording the Quinean would sign and once in wording the determinacy person would sign; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether saying there's no fact of the matter adds anything at all to saying no evidence could decide, and another is whether the whole thesis collapses the moment you stop being a behaviourist about meaning; and for each crux the case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two things I don't want them waving away. First, a case I made up for seminar: a community whose word for some animal can be read either as a count noun for the animal or as a mass term for its stuff, where the two rival manuals diverge only over a sentence nobody has ever uttered yet, and where whichever way the speakers then react, both manuals survive by re-translating an unrelated hedge-word they use — so holism means no test ever isolates the term. Second, radical translation at home: is the indeterminacy supposed to apply just as hard to your own past self, or to the dialect across the river, and if so can the fact-of-the-matter crowd even state their view here without circularity? Say too whether any part of this fight is really verbal — over meaning, over fact, over evidence — and which part.

Format: exactly five numbered sections and nothing else, no intro and no closing paragraph, each section kept to a sentence or two per point (bullets inside a section are fine): (1) agreed claims; (2) each side's own wording of the disagreement; (3) the cruxes; (4) the shared cases or arguments with each side's advance prediction; (5) what would change each side's mind — and in (5), tell me where the drafting is most likely to be slanted toward one side or quietly question-begging, and which single argument or consideration would most likely change your own read on where the indeterminacy really lies.
