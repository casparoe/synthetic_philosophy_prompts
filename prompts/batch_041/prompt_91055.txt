I am in the upper sixth and writing an Extended Project Qualification on whether the self is really a single thing, and I have reached a sticking point in the section on split-brain patients that I would like help with.

The account I have been working from is a popular-science one, and its central move is a picture rather than an argument. The corpus callosum is described as a corridor between two rooms, the operation as bricking up that corridor, and the patient afterwards as two people who can no longer speak to one another: two minds sharing one skull. The book then leans on the picture to reach a fairly strong conclusion — that the patient literally is two subjects rather than one — and, in a more speculative chapter, that the ordinary single self may be a similar fiction, a crowd that passes for a person only because the crowd has one mouth.

I can see why the picture is attractive, and I can also feel it doing more work than it has earned, but I cannot yet say precisely where. I would like an audit of the comparison.

First, set out the correspondences explicitly: what plays the role of the two people, of the corridor, of the separate rooms, of the locked door, of the outside world, and of the single voice that answers the experimenter. A table would suit me.

Second, say where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the features the picture quietly borrows from its source and that the real case may not supply: that each person has a private stock of information, that they have separate interests and can come into conflict, that each is a complete agent rather than half of one, and that each has a life of their own in the world.

Third, separate the failures that matter for the conclusion the book wants to draw from the ones that are merely picturesque. If the picture fails only in ways a defender could patch, say that too; I do not want the disanalogies padded out to look more damaging than they are.

Fourth, tell me whether the conclusion survives. If the honest answer is that the picture cannot carry the metaphysical claim it is being used for, but that something weaker and more interesting does survive, I would much rather have that than a flat verdict that the picture is simply wrong.

Fifth, if a better comparison is available — two shift workers sharing one desk, two governments in one country after the telephone lines are cut, a committee whose members cannot hear one another — say which does the most work, and where it fails in turn.

There is one further point I would like you to press on. The picture makes one of the two people mute, since only one hemisphere can speak, and it is always the speaking hemisphere that we get to interview. That looks to me like a loaded set-up: it turns the speechless hemisphere into a person we can only ever know about second-hand, and it makes the talking hemisphere the one whose self-description counts as evidence. Is that asymmetry a genuine feature of the case, or an artefact of how the evidence is gathered, and does it affect what the two-people picture can be used to show?

I would like you to be genuinely self-critical about how much any of this settles. Where the comparison plainly breaks, say so flatly and do not soften it; where the answer depends on a theory of the self that one has already accepted, mark that clearly rather than letting the picture appear to decide it. And if my framing is confused — if the two-people picture is not really an analogy at all but a literal empirical hypothesis, or if no comparison could settle how many subjects there are because that is a conceptual rather than an empirical matter — then please say so plainly instead of answering the question as I have posed it. I would rather discover now that my question is the wrong one than at the end of the project.

Please write your answer in British English.
