I run a small reading group that meets in the back room of a pub — nine of us, no two from the same trade: a litigator, a paediatric nurse, a machinist, a software tester, a retired surveyor, three graduate students from assorted departments, and me, a lapsed philosopher who now does data work for a health authority. We never set reading in advance, because half the group will not do it; the format is one idea per session, seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and the leader has to get the idea across with no text in front of anybody. Next month is my turn, and the idea I have picked is the one usually called the intuition pump: a thought experiment understood not as an argument with suppressed premises but as a device for producing a verdict, whose force is carried by details it never defends, so that moving a detail the reader does not notice moving can reverse the verdict while the case still feels like the same case. I have been calling the detail a case invites you to grab its handle and the detail that actually does the work its pivot, and I would like the session to end with the group able to find the pivot in a case they met five minutes earlier.

What I want from you is not the explanation — I can explain it — but the plan for delivering it to this particular room. Tell me the order in which the pieces have to go so that each one lands as a consequence of the last rather than as another item on a list, and say what has to be in place before the question of whether a pump can mislead even makes sense to them. Then tell me which single case should carry the session: the one we return to four or five times, each return showing us something the previous look missed, and what that case must have and must lack. I take it that it must produce a strong near-unanimous verdict on first hearing, that its pivot must be dial-able in at least three directions, and that it must not be a case whose ethics is the interesting part, or the group will spend the hour litigating the ethics instead of watching the pump. I have drafted two candidates and I want your judgement on them rather than a third option chosen to spare my feelings. The first is a hospital case: a junior doctor covering a ward at night is called to one patient, and while she is with that patient another patient, whom she has never met, deteriorates and is found dead at the morning round; the pivot is the size of the ward, which is stated as scenery in the first clause and does all the work, and it can be dialled further by changing how long she has known the dead patient and how close the discovery falls to the end of her shift. The second is an identification case: a witness picks a man out of a line-up, and the pivot is everything the description omits about how the line-up was built — whether she was told the culprit might not be present, how many fillers there were, how long she was given to look. Tell me which of these carries better and why, or whether neither does, and if you propose a replacement, say what it has that mine lack.

I also want the misconception map, since this is the part I expect to get wrong. My guesses, to be corrected if they are wrong or badly ordered: first, that a thought experiment is an argument with some premises left out, so that it can be defeated by denying a stipulation; second, that the verdict is a fact about the case rather than a fact about the reader; third, the overcorrection, that because a verdict can be flipped by an unnoticed detail, intuitions are worthless and the whole exercise is rigged — I expect the litigator to make this move in the first ten minutes, and I need to know whether to let it run. For each misconception, say exactly where in the sequence it has to be defused and what the defusing move is. Finally, give me one question or task to close on that would show whether the idea has landed rather than whether they enjoyed themselves — something they can visibly fail — and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in seventy-five minutes with this room, so that I can say so out loud at the start rather than be dragged into it at minute fifty.
