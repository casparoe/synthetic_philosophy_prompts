I'm in a small reading group that's been going through evolution-and-behaviour stuff — some of the classic selfish-gene material, a couple of the older group-selection papers, then some recent modelling papers — mostly because two people in the group have been going at each other about this for about a year now and the rest of us would like it to stop. I'm a philosophy person (I mostly do mind stuff, aestetics on the side), so I'm the least equipped person in the room, and I said I'd try to get a straight answer from somewhere. Here's what happened last week. We were discussing a toy model from one of the recent papers — a made-up diploid beetle, I think, not a real species — where some females stay at the communal nest and guard the brood instead of going off to breed on their own. Guarding is costly (guards end up with fewer offspring of their own) but nests with guards survive and fledge more young, and in the model the average relatedness between a guard and the brood she's protecting comes out at only about an eighth, with the cost and benefit numbers falling well short of what the usual rule (the one with r times benefit greater than cost) would need.

R. said: so this is kin selection, full stop. The guards are related to the brood, the allele spreads because it's helping copies of itself in relatives, and "group selection" here is a redescription — nothing causal is going on at the level of the group that isn't already going on at the level of the gene. He said the group-selection vocabulary survives in textbooks only because people refuse to do the relatedness arithmetic, and that once you do it the group-level story evaporates; he added that the only things that replicate with any fidelity are genes, so genes are the only things that can be units of selection and everything else is a vehicle. S. said: your relatedness number is doing nothing but restating the conclusion — either you use the genealogical figure and the rule plainly fails, or you pick whatever value of r makes the rule come out right, and then you've just relabelled the group-level effect as "relatedness." The guards in the model are barely related to the brood; what's actually happening is that groups containing guards out-produce groups without them, and if you decompose the total change in frequency into a between-group part and a within-group part, the between-group part is carrying it. She said calling that kin selection because the individuals happen to be somewhat related is exactly the move that made the field miss group selection for decades, and that the arithmetic doesn't care what you call it.

R. came back with: you can always do that decomposition. Take any population, any trait, any fitness differences, and you can write the total change as a between-group term plus a within-group term, and the sum comes out identical to what the gene's-eye account gives you; you can even shift the boundary between the two terms by choosing how to carve the population up. If the two descriptions are algebraically equivalent and the split point is a free choice, then saying one of them names the "real" cause is a fact about your explanatory tastes, not about the world. S. said equivalence of the algebra isn't equivalence of the causal story — two ways of writing the same number can pick out different processes — and that the descriptions stop being equivalent the moment you push on them, since change the group size or the migration rate and the two accounts disagree about what generalises and what you should expect next. R. said fine, then it's an empirical question, and the burden is on her to say what experiment would tell the two apart; she said the burden is on him to say what "nothing causal at the group level" even means, given that he'd just conceded the between-group term was doing the work. Nobody would move, and the rest of us sat there.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — rate each of the moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who's right about which specific point — and then tell me what's actually left of the dispute once you grant R. his bookkeeping point? I keep suspecting they're talking past each other on something definately definitional, but I can't work out which part is definitional and which part is a real disagreement about nature. And what are both of them missing?
