Construct a thought experiment that pulls apart two pictures of how a name or a natural-kind term gets its reference. I state the pictures here, so that nothing in the task hangs on your recall of the literature. Picture A, the descriptive picture: the description associated with the term gives its meaning, so in any world the term applies to whatever uniquely satisfies the description, and the sentence linking the term to the description is analytic and knowable a priori. Picture B, the rigid picture: the description only fixes what the term refers to in the actual world; the term is rigid, designating the same thing in every world in which it designates anything at all, so the linking sentence, if true, is contingent and knowable only a posteriori, while the identity between the term and the thing that actually satisfies the description is necessary but knowable only a posteriori. A third picture may be given a middle verdict if you want one: the term is rigid, but its reference is fixed by description, so the linking sentence is contingent yet knowable a priori.

The specifications are these. First, Pictures A and B must agree about what the term refers to in the actual world; the divergence must concern the term's reference in other worlds, and the modal and epistemic status of the linking sentence. Second, the description must be satisfied only contingently by the object: there must be a nearby, intelligible counterfactual situation in which that very object exists and does not satisfy the description. Third, the setting must be ordinary — a trade, a sport, a family, a workplace, a legal or medical practice — with no Twin Earth, no brains in vats, no stipulated physics, no unicorns or fictional names, and none of the stock examples (nothing with Hesperus and Phosphorus, Gödel and Schmidt, or water and H2O). Fourth, the term must be introduced the way ordinary speakers introduce terms, not by an explicit definition of the form "let this term mean whatever satisfies the description," which would settle the question by stipulation.

This is for a teaching unit, so the divergence has to fall out of the two pictures rather than being built into the case. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph, then give, for each picture, what the term refers to in the counterfactual situation, which proposition the linking sentence expresses there, and whether that sentence is knowable a priori; say whether the case exhibits a necessary a posteriori truth and why. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the discriminating work, what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the picture that fares worst would resist the example.
