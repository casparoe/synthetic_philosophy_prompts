I teach an upper-level aesthetics seminar and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on fakes and forgeries that cant be answered by reciting the usual two moves — the one about a forgery misrepresenting somebody's achievement, and the one about it corrupting our understanding of a tradition. I dont want them name-dropping either move; I want them to have to work out whether those are actually the same argument wearing two hats. So I've built the question around three hypotheticals that aren't in anything they could have read, and now I need the model answer and a marking scheme. Keep the whole reply under 1500 words and the model answer itself under 700.

Here's the question as I've drafted it. Tighten the wording if you want, but keep all three cases and the follow-up:

A foundation has inherited three paintings, each catalogued under the name of a long-dead celebrated artist. It must decide, for each, whether its attachment to having the original is doing any aesthetic work, and if so what work.

The Signature Case: the canvas was painted entirely by the artist's own hand, but the signature was added decades later by a dealer, along with fabricated letters of provenance. Nothing about the painted surface is deceptive. Only the paperwork is.

The Studio Case: the painting was made by the artist's apprentice, following the artist's designs and under the artist's supervision, and the artist signed it. Later scholarship downgraded the attribution to school-of rather than by-the-artist. The apprentice later became a celebrated painter in his own right.

The Labeled Forgery Case: a modern painter made it deliberately as a forgery, got caught, and it now hangs in a museum with a prominent label announcing that it is a forgery. Critics agree it is a better painting than the real works of the artist it imitates.

Using the three cases, say whether the achievement argument and the corruption-of-understanding argument are one argument or two, and whether either survives the Labeled Forgery Case. Then say what a defensible account of the value of originals has to concede. Follow-up: would your answer change if the only surviving evidence of what the original looked like were a perfect copy?

Now give me:

1. A model answer of about 650 words that a strong student could plausibly produce in 90 minutes. It should not just survey positions; it should commit to a line.

2. A marking scheme worth 25 marks, built as five must-hit points at 5 marks each, each point phrased as a specific move a top answer has to make rather than a topic it has to mention, and each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong.

3. One sentence on where the A/B boundary falls — what an A answer has that a solid B answer usually lacks.
