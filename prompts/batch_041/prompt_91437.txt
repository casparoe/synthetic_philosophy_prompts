I teach a 75-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of money and credit, with about 18 students. The session is on George Berkeley's economic queries, published in the 1730s under the title The Querist, in which he treats wealth as a power to command the industry of others and asks whether money's true idea is not that of a ticket or counter rather than a commodity with intrinsic value. I want students to feel the force of that thought before I name it: that the token's value is not in its material, that trade might survive the disappearance of the bullion, and that a smaller quantity of money circulating quickly can do the work of a larger quantity circulating slowly.

Design a classroom game that stages this, runnable in the session, with a debrief at the end. Do not tell participants Berkeley's theses in advance; the rules and the sequence of rounds should make them run into the problem themselves. The deliverable should include the following.

Setting and materials. Specify a role card for each participant, giving a production capacity and a set of needs; a supply of tokens; a small stock of a commodity that initially backs the tokens; and any ledgers, timers, or scoreboards you need. Assume no real money changes hands.

Payoff scheme. Give a points scheme with stakes high enough that participants care about the outcome but low enough that they do not defect into unilateral charity. Explain how points are earned and lost, and how the scheme keeps the pressure on without making the game feel like a mere arithmetic exercise.

Rounds and timeline. Lay out three or four rounds with timings. At minimum: a barter round; a round in which tokens circulate and are redeemable for the backing commodity; a round in which the backing is removed or discovered never to have existed, with the group free to keep accepting the tokens or to refuse them; and a round in which either the denomination is changed or the quantity of tokens is halved. Include a moment where hoarding is possible and its costs become visible, and a moment where the group's continued acceptance of the token is a live collective decision rather than a background assumption. If it helps, Berkeley also treats the current of industry and commerce as determined by the prevailing will or fashion of a nation; consider whether one of the rounds can make that visible without turning into a lecture.

Debrief. Map specific moments in the game onto Berkeley's queries: the ticket-or-counter idea; the identification of wealth with the power to command industry; the irrelevance of the material of the ticket; and the equivalence between less money circulating quickly and more money circulating slowly. The debrief should show participants where in the game each point was already operative.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure without abandoning the exercise. Finally, be frank about which features of Berkeley's view the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in the debrief. Address in particular whether the game can capture anything of his doctrine of signs or his idealist metaphysics, and what it necessarily leaves out about the Irish political and economic context in which he wrote, including his interest in a national bank.
