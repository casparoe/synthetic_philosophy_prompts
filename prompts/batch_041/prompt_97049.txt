Here is the framework, set out so that there is no ambiguity about the target. Consider a gamble whose possible outcomes are \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\), listed from worst to best, where outcome \(x_i\) occurs with probability \(p_i\). Let \(u\) be a utility function, and let \(r\) be an increasing function from \([0,1]\) to \([0,1]\) with \(r(0) = 0\) and \(r(1) = 1\). The risk-weighted expected utility of the gamble is

\[ u(x_1) + \sum_{i=2}^{n} r(p_i + p_{i+1} + \cdots + p_n)\,[u(x_i) - u(x_{i-1})], \]

where the first term is simply the utility of the worst outcome, since the probability of doing at least that well is 1 and \(r(1) = 1\). The same quantity can be written as a sum in which \(u(x_i)\) is multiplied by a decision weight, namely \(r(P(x \geq x_i)) - r(P(x \geq x_{i+1}))\), taking \(P(x \geq x_{n+1}) = 0\). Note that these weights are non-negative and sum to 1, so they behave like a probability distribution over the outcomes even though they generally differ from the \(p_i\). The intended reading of \(r\) is that it encodes the agent's attitude toward risk — how much she cares about the way probability mass is arranged across the ranking — and this is supposed to be a component of her psychology distinct from both her degrees of belief, given by the \(p_i\), and her desires, given by \(u\).

The position I want you to argue for is that this reading cannot be sustained. Once the theory is put in the second form above, it becomes apparent that the very same preferences can be generated by an agent whose degrees of belief are themselves distorted, an agent for whom the credence assigned to a disjunction of mutually exclusive possibilities need not equal the sum of the credences of the disjuncts; and nothing internal to the framework privileges the risk-attitude story over the distorted-belief story. You should argue, further, that this is not a harmless instance of the underdetermination of theory by data of the sort that afflicts any representation theorem, but a point about the content of the theory: the theory's own machinery supplies a doxastic interpretation of the numbers that does all the same explanatory work, so the risk-attitude interpretation earns its place only if there is an independent argument for it, and there is none.

Please also make two subsidiary points. First, that the difficulty is invisible for gambles with only two possible outcomes, where the framework collapses into ordinary expected utility with the probability of the better outcome replaced by its transform, and becomes visible only once a gamble has three or more outcomes, so that the weight on an outcome depends on where it sits in the ranking and on how the probability mass is distributed above and below it. Second, that the theory's defenders cannot rescue the risk-attitude reading by insisting that the risk function is fixed across all the gambles an agent faces, since a fixed function of the objective probabilities is itself a perfectly good object for a doxastic interpretation — a rule that assigns to each event a distorted credence — and the constancy, if anything, makes the doxastic reading more attractive, because it gives the agent a single stable non-additive system of credences rather than a family of ad hoc distortions.
