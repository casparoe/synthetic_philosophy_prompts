I'm writing a dialogue for a seminar exercise on Turing's 1950 paper, and I need it to actually get into the weeds rather than rehash the usual "can machines think" stuff. The two speakers should be arguing about the section near the end where Turing raises extra-sensory perception as an objection to the imitation game: the worry that if the interrogator can read the machine's mind, or the machine the interrogator's, the test's verdicts get contaminated, and Turing's response is essentially to say that we'd need to shield the interrogator — put them in a room that blocks telepathy — and then see whether the machine still does well.

One speaker thinks this is a serious methodological point that deserves to be taken at face value: that the imitation game is a behavioural test, and every behavioural test is only as good as its assumptions about what channels of information exist, so the validity of the whole thing is contingent on an empirical fact about physics that Turing couldn't check. The other thinks the section is basically a reductio or a bit of mischief, and that the right lesson isn't "ESP is a live threat to the test" but something like "any test that can be defeated by positing a new information channel is a test with a structural weakness, and this happens to be a vivid way of showing it" — or maybe even that Turing is quietly showing that his own criterion is hostage to whatever the world turns out to be like.

Let them actually go back and forth. I want the first speaker to press things like: if ESP is real, does the machine pass or fail, and is the failure the machine's fault? What would a controlled version of the experiment even look like, given that the experimenter's own beliefs might be part of the effect? And the second to press back: is this objection specific to machines at all, or does it also threaten any empirical test of human intelligence, in which case it's a problem for testing in general rather than for Turing's proposal? Is the hypothesis doing any work beyond being unfalsifiable in practice? Does it matter that Turing treats it as an empirical contingency rather than a conceptual objection, and if so what does that tell us about which of his nine objections he thought were the serious ones?

Don't let them settle whether telepathy is real — that's not the point. The point is what role the objection plays in Turing's argument and whether it exposes something about behavioural criteria for intelligence or is just a period piece. End the dialogue without a tidy resolution, but with each of them having conceded something. Also don't have either of them cite page numbers or claim what some commentator said; I want them reasoning from the structure of the argument, not from the secondary literature.
