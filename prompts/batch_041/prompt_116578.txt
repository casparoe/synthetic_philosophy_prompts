I have been designing a habit-tracking app for people who want to work seriously on their character, and the obvious ancestor to borrow from is Benjamin Franklin's thirteen-virtue scheme in his Autobiography. My reading of it is that the whole thing was an early experiment in quantified self-improvement: a page for each virtue, seven columns for the days of the week, thirteen lines for the virtues, a little black spot recorded for every fault, and a weekly rotation in which one virtue at a time was supposed to be brought to zero spots before attention moved on to the next. On my reading it collapsed for a straightforward reason. The target was a clean page, Order was the leak that ruined the whole board, and Franklin himself concedes that he never arrived at the perfection he had been so ambitious of obtaining and that he would settle for a speckled axe rather than keep grinding. So my question is a design question. If I want to preserve the structure of his method while repairing what broke, what should I change first? Should I weight the virtues so that a lapse in Order does not swamp an otherwise clean week? Should I replace the binary spot with a graded scale? Should I add streaks and decay so that a single relapse does not erase visible progress? And should the weekly rotation be adaptive, holding someone on a virtue until they cross some threshold rather than moving them on after seven days regardless of how they did? I would like a concrete priority order for the redesign, the reasoning for why each change belongs where it does, and a frank statement of which of these fixes would actually have helped Franklin and which are solutions to a problem his method never had.

I am aware that I may be importing assumptions from modern productivity culture into a text that is not doing what I think it is doing, which is precisely why I am asking before I build anything. So please also tell me where my framing is most likely to be distorting his project. Specifically: if the success condition of the scheme is not a clean page, what is it? What role are the recorded faults supposed to play in the practice, given that they are faults rather than accomplishments? What is the daily examination for, and how does it relate to the morning resolution that opens the day? What is going on with the virtue that was added to the list after a friend's reproof, and with the fact that Franklin claims only a limited sort of success in acquiring it while claiming considerable success in appearing to have it? And what is the axe anecdote doing in the argument, if it is not signaling that he gave up on perfection? I would much rather be corrected on the reading than receive a polished specification built on a misreading.

Finally, I would like the answer to be self-critical rather than merely confident. Say what would change your mind: if the case for my reading is stronger than you think, which feature of the text or which consideration would move you toward it, and where is your own reconstruction of Franklin's intent most vulnerable to over-reading? If the honest answer is that parts of the evidence underdetermine the dispute, say so plainly and mark which of your claims are inferences rather than things the text settles.
