Please explain the conceptual difficulty that Ayer's verificationism creates for claims about life after death, and why the difficulty is not merely that such claims are hard to test. Ayer's view implies that statements about surviving one's own death are not false but meaningless, since no possible observation could verify them. But this verdict is usually stated too quickly, and it has an uncomfortable symmetrical consequence: if the survival claim is meaningless, so is its denial.

In working this out, address the following three considerations and say which one does the real work. First, if we try to make survival claims meaningful by tying them to experiences, the best candidates are reports from people resuscitated after cardiac arrest. Even if such reports are veridical, they concern experiences during a period when the brain is still or again functioning; they are events before death, not after it. Explain why this gap is not a mere practical limitation that better evidence could close. Second, if we insist that it is the same person who survives, we need a criterion of personal identity that can hold across the destruction of the body. Ayer's own treatment of the self as a construction out of experiences makes that criterion hard to state without either circularity or the introduction of a further fact that no observation could reveal. Third, if we treat survival as a decision about how to apply words rather than a factual discovery, we seem to lose the very thing at issue — not that some experience continues, but that I continue.

Then consider the following reported episode. Ayer is reported to have had a near-death experience late in life, and to have said afterwards that it slightly weakened his conviction that death would be the end of him. Explain what a verificationist should say about the evidential weight of that experience for Ayer himself, given that his own theory predicts that a dying brain can produce experiences of exactly that kind. Does the first-person perspective give the experience a force that the third-person perspective cannot capture, or is that appearance itself something the theory can explain away?

Finally, state what would have to be true for a claim about life after death to be genuinely verifiable, and say whether that condition can be met without the claim collapsing into a claim about experiences that occur before death — or, alternatively, without making the denial of survival equally meaningless.
