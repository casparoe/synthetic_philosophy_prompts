I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to work out what the world's three main approaches to commercial sex actually commit themselves to. As I understand them: the Nordic model makes buying sex a crime while leaving the seller unpunished; Germany and the Netherlands license it as a regulated occupation, with registration, taxation, zoning, and health requirements; New Zealand decriminalized it and largely lets ordinary labor and safety law govern, so workers can organize and bring workplace claims. Each approach is defended by serious people, and each seems to rest on a different picture of what the transaction fundamentally is.

What I want is the implicit theory behind each one, reconstructed rather than assumed. Concretely: what does each presuppose about consent — in particular, does the Nordic model require the view that consent to sell sex is impossible or invalid in a way that consent to other risky work is not, or can it get by with a weaker claim about background coercion? What does each presuppose about where the harm lies — in the buyer's character or the community's moral order, in the conditions the seller works under, or in the ordinary workplace risks that labor law already addresses? And what does each assume about the state's proper role — protecting a class of people from themselves, regulating a market like any other, or simply getting out of the way?

I'm especially puzzled by two things, and I'd rather they be addressed than smoothed over. First, the Nordic asymmetry: the seller is treated as too compromised to be blameworthy, yet also as competent enough that her agreement does not excuse the buyer — is that a coherent single view of agency, or two views stapled together? Second, if Germany and the Netherlands really hold that this is work like any other, why do they still impose special registration, location, and health rules that no comparable occupation faces; and if New Zealand really decriminalized it, why hasn't the full apparatus of employment protection (minimum terms, employer liability) followed, the way it would for an ordinary job?

Finally, distinguish for me what each regime strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect several different theories would license the very same statutes — and tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival: what would a genuinely "sex work is work" regime look like in practice, and what would a regime built on the premise that the transaction is inherently harmful have to do differently?
