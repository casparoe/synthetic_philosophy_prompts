I chair the competences working group of the constitutional assembly that is drafting a charter for a union of twelve formerly sovereign regions. The assembly votes in six weeks on the charter's competence clause, and my group has to put a single recommended text in front of it. I would like a written opinion I can circulate before our next drafting session.

Where we are stuck. Three drafts are on the table, and the group is split roughly three ways.

Draft A (political). The union may exercise a shared competence only where action by one region, or by several regions acting together, would be manifestly insufficient to secure the objective, and where union action would secure it at materially lower cost or on a materially broader scale. Enforcement sits with the Regions' Chamber: within eight weeks of a proposal's publication, two thirds of the chamber may serve a reasoned objection, to which the commission must respond publicly within sixty days. The commission may proceed anyway. Courts may not review compliance.

Draft B (judicial). The same substantive test, but any region may challenge a union act in the Constitutional Court within three months; the court must annul acts that fail the test; the burden of showing necessity rests on the union; and the court may not second-guess the union's assessment of how important the objective is.

Draft C (weak-form). The court may declare an act non-compliant and remand it to the legislature, but may not annul; the Regions' Chamber then gets a second reading with a lower threshold. Any union competence exercised under the clause lapses after twelve years unless re-enacted.

What is actually dividing us. One faction reads subsidiarity as a principle of autonomy: a higher body may act only to help a lower one do what it cannot do for itself, never to supplant it. The other reads it as a principle of efficiency: allocate each task to the level that does it best, with no presumption either way. I have also noticed an inconsistency I cannot resolve. The members who want the strongest possible protection for regional control over schooling, language, and culture are the same members who want the union to override regions on the bill of rights, which binds both levels, and on free movement, which some poorer regions say is strangling them. We are also studying how the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union have handled this, though the group does not want a survey; it wants reasoning it can argue with. No literature review or citations needed.

What I want from you. Act as a constitutional lawyer and political philosopher who works on comparative federalism and on subsidiarity as a normative principle. Spend a few sentences at the start brainstorming the real choice here — what is genuinely at stake, and which of our three drafts actually differs from which — and label that part as brainstorming. Then write the opinion, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.

It should: recommend one draft, or a specific hybrid whose clause language you supply; specify the trigger, the majority required, the review timeline, and the standard of review, since those details are where the drafts do their real work; explain what conception of the political community each option presupposes, and say whether the choice among them is a substantive moral choice or merely an institutional design choice; and distinguish what a subsidiarity clause strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect some of our disagreement is about outcomes rather than about who decides. Give the strongest argument against your own recommendation its full weight, especially the charge that local control has historically shielded local injustice, and the charge that a justiciable necessity test simply transfers contested policy choices to judges while pretending to be neutral. Close with a short section headed "What would change my mind": the argument or consideration that would move you to a different recommendation, and the point in your reasoning where you think you are most likely to be wrong.

Write in American English.
