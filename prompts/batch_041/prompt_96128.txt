I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been working through the consciousness literature that keeps surfacing in AI discussions. Recently I read a blog post on a popular explainer site — anonymous author, no credentials given — summarizing global workspace theory and its relation to the access/phenomenal consciousness distinction. I've condensed the central section below, paraphrasing but keeping the author's key claims intact, and I'd like an audit of how much of it I should believe.

The summary runs roughly like this. Global workspace theory, developed by Bernard Baars and given a neural implementation by Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues, holds that the brain is a collection of many specialized unconscious processors competing to get their output into a limited-capacity workspace. Information that wins the competition is broadcast widely, and that broadcast is what we call conscious experience; information that stays local remains unconscious. Dehaene's masking experiments support this: a stimulus that reaches awareness produces a sudden, widespread ignition of prefrontal and parietal networks, while the same stimulus presented subliminally stays confined to sensory areas. The author then argues that this renders the older distinction between access consciousness (what is available for report and reasoning) and phenomenal consciousness (what it is like to experience something), associated with Ned Block, largely obsolete. The workspace, he says, is precisely the thing whose contents we can report, and its contents are what we experience, so the two are one phenomenon described at two levels. He concludes that because everything science can measure about the mind amounts to information being made available to the right systems, there is nothing left over for phenomenal consciousness to be, and the hard problem is dissolved rather than solved. He adds that the global workspace is essentially the brain's working memory, housed mainly in the prefrontal cortex, which is why the prefrontal cortex is so central to consciousness.

I'm inclined to accept this, since it is the only mechanistic and testable story about consciousness I've encountered. But I have a nagging sense the author is moving faster than the evidence allows. Please go claim by claim and tell me what a researcher working on this would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I especially want to know whether the step from "the workspace is what we can report" to "the workspace is what we experience" is a result or a stipulation; whether the hard problem can be dismissed in that way; and whether identifying the workspace with prefrontal working memory is the theory or a caricature of it. Then give me a bottom line: after the corrections, does the author's overall picture survive?
