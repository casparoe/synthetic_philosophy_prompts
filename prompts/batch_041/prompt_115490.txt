I run the research desk at a small human rights documentation outfit -- five of us, no lawyer on staff, and our country reports get quoted by journalists and diplomats. A few times a year we have to decide whether to use the word genocide about a situation we're documenting, and honestly we keep making it up as we go. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run in a day or two and still defend six months later.

Two things have to be in it, because they pull in different directions. First, the word as Lemkin built it was about destroying a group's way of life -- its political and social institutions, culture, language, national feeling, religion, economic existence -- and replacing it with the perpetrator's own pattern, which is a lot broader than what most people picture. Second, the 1948 convention version is much narrower: it needs specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such, and the acts basically come down to killing, serious bodily or mental harm, conditions of life meant to destroy the group, preventing births, and taking children. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clean no, and where it hands the call to our board because the question is values or politics rather than facts. Say which steps need evidence we won't realistically have in two days, and what to do when it's missing.

Then run it on three cases: a state that has killed nobody but is steadily erasing a minority's language, monuments and churches while settling its own people onto the land; a massacre of 400 villagers where we can't find anything showing intent to destroy the group rather than crush a rebellion; and a government wiping out an armed political movement and its families, where publishing the word would probably get our two local staff arrested. I expect to dislike at least one of the verdicts. Also tell me what the procedure can't do -- where it will misfire, and where the honest answer is that we're making a political choice and dressing it up as a finding. I dont want a reading list, we need this usable tomorrow.
