I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of math -- three of us, and one is a card-carrying mechanist who keeps saying the whole Lucas-Penrose thing was dead on arrival and only survives because philosophers don't read proof theory. I've been trying to state a version of the Gödelian argument that can't just be waved away, and I want an actual number for how likely it is to work. Here's the version I care about. Forget trying to show in one shot that the human mathematician isn't any Turing machine. Grant the mechanist that at any given moment a human's mathematical knowledge is captured by some sound formal system S_i. The anti-mechanist claim is then an open-endedness claim: that the human can always see the truth of G_{S_i} (the Gödel sentence for S_i, whose truth follows from S_i's soundness), move to a stronger system, see the truth of that one too, and go on with no pre-set stopping point -- and that no single Turing machine can model this because the sequence never closes. My mechanist friend's reply is that you can program a machine to iterate reflection principles too, and that the only way to block that is to insist the human's reflection is unbounded, which really just means the human isn't any one machine but a family of them, and a family of machines is still something an algorithm can generate. He puts the whole thing at maybe 0.15. I'd have said 0.6 before he started talking, and now I'm at sea.

So: give me your credence that this open-endedness version establishes its conclusion, namely that human mathematical understanding is not equivalent to any Turing machine or formal system. State it as a number and decompose it. I want the sub-claims it rests on, each with a rough probability and one line of why: (a) that the logical core is valid, i.e. soundness of S_i really does hand you the truth of G_{S_i}; (b) that a human can have non-question-begging knowledge of her own soundness or consistency at each stage, rather than just knowing the conditional if I'm sound then G is true; (c) that this self-knowledge doesn't evaporate as S_i gets strong enough that nobody plausibly knows its soundness -- set theory and beyond, where we aren't even confident about consistency, let alone the truth of the axioms; (d) that human mathematical practice being error-prone, or possibly even inconsistent, doesn't sink the argument; (e) that open-endedness isn't algorithmically capturable. Then combine them, and tell me which single sub-claim my number is most sensitive to and what would move it most: some proof-theoretic fact I could learn, some empirical fact about how mathematicians actually extend theories in practice, or nothing at all because the whole question is conceptual and evidence can't touch it. And if you can, tell me which of those sub-claims is where my friend and I actually disagree, since 0.15 vs 0.6 is a big gap to just be taste.
