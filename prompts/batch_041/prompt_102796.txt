I am a doctoral candidate in applied mathematics, working on nonlinear dynamical systems, and I have been reading in the philosophy of history on the side. I would like you to take four accounts of historical change — Polybius's cycle of constitutional forms, Ibn Khaldun's account of the rise and decay of dynasties (together with its central notion of group solidarity, asabiyya), Spengler's morphology of cultures treated as organisms with a determinate life-course, and Toynbee's pattern of challenge and response — and restate each, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: state spaces and trajectories; attractors of various kinds (fixed points, limit cycles, and the like); invariant measures and ergodicity; stationarity versus non-stationarity; hysteresis and path dependence; timescale separation; feedback and thresholds; deterministic chaos; and, crucially, the distinction between a recurrent dynamics on a fixed state space and a single trajectory running once from a given initial condition.

For each of the four, do three things. First, give the best translation you can. Second, say exactly where the translation distorts the original: what you had to idealise, what you had to fudge, and which of the original's claims you had to strengthen or weaken in order to make them expressible at all. Third, state what has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever.

Then give me the residue report in aggregate. I want to know whether these historians are claiming anything that genuinely cannot be said in dynamical-systems terms — claims about meaning, purpose, or the normative significance of decline would be the obvious candidates — or whether the apparent residue is merely the vagueness of the originals, which the formal vocabulary exposes. Where the translation fails, adjudicate: is the failure a defect of my framework, in that it cannot represent something real, or is it evidence that the original claim was never a well-formed claim about a process in the first place? In particular, wherever a thinker's 'cycle' is meant to be lawlike, tell me whether anything in the source supports a genuine recurrence — a closed orbit or an invariant measure — as opposed to a merely repeated sequence of stages; and wherever what is called a cycle is really a lifecycle, say so plainly. Be specific and adjudicate rather than merely listing parallels.

Please write your answer in British English.
