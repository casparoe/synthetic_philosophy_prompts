Over the weekend a friend of mine and I argued for most of an evening about a glass goblet that has been in her family for six generations, and we broke off without either of us giving ground. The goblet was blown by her great-great-great-grandfather, who ran a small furnace on a wind-beaten stretch of coast; it is thick, green, slightly lopsided, and it has been broken at least four times — once by a child, once in a house fire, once during a move, and once, famously, by a cousin who tried to drink from it after a funeral. Each time, the family gathered the shards, melted them down, and blew them back into a goblet of the same rough shape, adding a little new glass to make up for what had been lost or spilled. My friend says it is the same goblet: it has never stopped being the family's cup, it is still used at weddings and funerals, and anyone who called it a replica would be asked to leave. I said that at some point the last original atom departed and what remained was a copy with a good story attached, and that the reason we cannot locate the moment is a fact about our concepts rather than about the glass. We also discovered, late, that we disagreed about a case neither of us had considered before — a year when the goblet broke and, because two cousins were feuding, two separate goblets were blown from the recovered shards, both of which are still in use — and we could not even agree on what the question should be.

I would like you to write a short story, of about fifteen hundred words, that stages this kind of dispute without settling it. Please invent the setting rather than reusing my friend's family: give me a coastal town with its own trade and its own rite, a particular made thing with a name the townspeople use, and a small cast — say, a craftsperson, an heir or custodian, an outsider such as an appraiser or a scholar, and someone whose livelihood depends on the object's status. The story should move through at least three episodes: one in which the object is broken and remade from its own remains; one in which it is altered piece by piece over years, so that no single day marks a change but the whole is eventually transformed; and one in which a single object is divided or duplicated, so that two claimants now hold something each calls by the same name. I want the practical stakes visible — an inheritance, a loan to a museum, the validity of a rite — so that the metaphysical question has teeth and the characters cannot simply agree to disagree.

What I care about most is that the story dramatize, rather than lecture about, the several things my friend and I kept running together: whether a thing's persistence depends on its matter, on its form or function, or on a community's continued recognition of it; how one and the same parcel of stuff can be, at once, a quantity of glass and a vessel and an heirloom, each with different conditions for surviving; and the difference between asking which object this is and asking which object we ought to care about or preserve. Let each of the main characters embody one of these answers in how they act rather than in what they announce, and let at least one of them hold a view on which identity is conferred by practice rather than found in the object. Do not resolve the dispute in the last paragraph, and do not let the narrator win it; I would rather the story end with the disagreement still live and the reader unsure which verdict the story itself endorses. A reader who knows the puzzle literature should recognize the moves, and a reader who does not should still be gripped by the people.
