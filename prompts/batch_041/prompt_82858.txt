I taught experimental physics for thirty-one years, retired four years ago, and have spent the retirement reading philosophy in earnest — to my considerable surprise, I have ended up teaching the final-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of economics at my old university's continuing-education faculty, learning the material a week or two ahead of the students. The final examination is in three weeks and I must contribute one essay question. I would like your help drafting it, together with the standard against which I will mark it.

The question I have in mind is a single sixty-minute essay, twenty-five marks, on what utility can mean when choice is the only evidence available. I want it to be unanswerable by reciting the ordinalist revolution, Samuelson's revealed-preference programme, and the weak and strong axioms of revealed preference. My students will have no books and no internet during the examination, and I do not wish to reward name-dropping, so the question must be answerable from the material in the case itself.

Here is the case I intend to build it around. A subject chooses among three options, A, B and C, in two laboratory sessions. In the first session the options are displayed as separate items with no default; she chooses A over B, B over C, and A over C. In the second session the same three options are displayed as a single bundle with one option pre-selected; she chooses C over B, B over A, and C over A. Each session's data, taken alone, is a complete strict ordering and passes every standard consistency test — a textbook rational agent. Pool the two sessions, treating the options as the same objects, and the data violate the weak axiom.

Two things about this puzzle interest me and I want the question to force students onto both. First, the theory seems to have a choice between two descriptions — pool the sessions and the subject is inconsistent, keep them apart and the theory is simply silent — and I want students to say what that choice presupposes about what the objects of choice are. My colleague's immediate reaction was that the options in the two sessions are not the same objects at all, because the presentation differs; I would like the question to make students confront what that escape costs. Second, I want them to notice that a theory which can always re-describe its objects of choice in order to keep its axioms intact is in a certain amount of trouble, though I am not confident I have put my finger on exactly what kind.

I also want to put a proposal of my own in front of them, in my own words, so that they must argue with it rather than recall it. In my own field we are content with idealizations obtained as limits — an ideal gas, a frictionless plane, a perfectly sharp spectral line. My proposal is that the utility of a subject whose choices vary with presentation is the limit of her orderings as the presentation is made progressively more neutral, and that this frame-free ordering is the one that should count for welfare purposes when the frames disagree. I would like the question to ask whether that limit is well defined, what operation would produce it, and whether the order in which neutrality is approached could matter. I am aware that I may be misdescribing the crux, and I would rather find out now than from the scripts.

The question should therefore require students to say: what a strict revealed-preference theorist must say about this subject's utility; what someone who holds that utility measures well-being must say; whether those answers come apart; what the case shows about the several things utility is asked to do — represent preference, index decision under risk, and measure welfare; whether my limit proposal is a definition, an empirical hypothesis, or a normative stipulation; and what a policy analyst should do who must choose between two policies for this subject, one better under the first session's ordering and one better under the second's.

Please give me the following. First, the question as the students will see it, with whatever sub-parts you judge sixty minutes can carry. Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred and fifty words, written as a strong student would write it rather than as a survey. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five, built on five must-hit points of four marks each; for each point, name the specific move a top answer must make and, in one line, the characteristic error that costs the marks. Reserve the remaining five marks for a closing paragraph of no more than a hundred words in which the model answer states what would change its mind and where its own reasoning is most likely to fail — I intend to award those marks precisely to students who can name the weak joint in their own argument, so the model answer should genuinely model that rather than gesture at it. Finally, tell me in a sentence or two what separates an A script from a B script overall, and add two or three sentences for my own benefit on what would make you revise the question itself — what a student could write that would show the question is rigged, or that I have misdescribed the difficulty.
