I'm a third-year philosophy student in a course on moral responsibility, and for our peer-assessment unit I have to grade another student's answer to the following take-home question. The question is based on a distinction from our course handout, so please treat the handout's framework as the relevant standard rather than importing a different taxonomy.

Question: In the handout, we separated three questions about an episode of blame: (1) Is the blame warranted or fitting, in the sense that the target really did the wrong-making thing and the blame accurately represents it? (2) Does the blamer have standing to blame, in the sense that the blamer is not disqualified by, for example, hypocrisy or complicity? (3) Is the target liable to blame, in the sense that it is fair to hold the target to the relevant demand? Consider Maya, who has repeatedly lied to her partner Sam about where she spends money. Sam has also lied to Maya about money in the past and has never acknowledged it. Maya now blames Sam for lying. Using the three questions, assess Maya's blame. Then consider the further claim that even if Maya lacks standing, her criticism may still have force as a moral observation.

Student's answer:
Maya's blame is clearly warranted in one sense: Sam did lie, and lying to a partner about shared finances is a genuine wrong. So the content of Maya's accusation is accurate. But the question is whether Maya can be the one to make it. Because Maya has done the same thing, she is a hypocrite, and hypocrisy removes standing. The standard same-fault condition means that a blamer lacks standing only when she is guilty of the very same fault she is blaming. Here Maya and Sam both lied about money, so the condition is met. Therefore Maya's blame is not warranted. Some people say that Maya could still make a moral observation, but if she lacks standing then her criticism has no normative force at all; it is just an attempt to deflect from her own behavior. This is why tu quoque is a legitimate form of objection: it shows that the accuser is in no position to accuse. The only way Maya could regain standing would be to apologize, and even then Sam would not be liable to blame, because liability is simply a function of whether the blamer has standing. If Sam had lied about something completely unrelated to money, by contrast, Maya would have standing, since the same-fault condition would not be satisfied.

I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or oversimplifies, and how much each problem should affect the mark. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the grade—which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict—and where your grading is most likely to be mistaken.
