I am the outgoing director of a small arms-control organisation that is being wound up at the end of this month, on the grounds that its central demand has been met: a treaty limiting a particular class of weapon has been signed, and the board has concluded that the organisation has no further reason to exist. I am giving the closing remarks at the farewell dinner. Roughly half the room are veteran negotiators who know Kant's essay on perpetual peace closely and will notice if I soften, inflate or misattribute anything; the other half are donors, former interns and partners' families who have never read a word of it and will not sit still for a lecture. The difficulty I want the speech to negotiate is this. One verification provision in the treaty has been deferred by a confidential side-letter, and I helped to negotiate that deferral on the understanding that it would be revisited within two years. Our organisation has had exactly one founding rule since its first week, and it is the only piece of text I am willing to quote: we publish every position we take at the table within seven days. We have now broken that rule once, and I am the one who decided to break it. I want the speech to be honest about the deferral without detonating the treaty, and I want Kant to do the structural work rather than decorate it.

Please write the speech as exactly five numbered movements, each a short paragraph of three to five sentences, with no framing text before the first movement or after the fifth, and no headings other than the numbers. The five movements must be distinguishable in function — not five variations on one note — and the Kantian material must determine what can and cannot be said in each rather than being summarised anywhere. In particular, the speech should make structural use of the following without ever naming them as doctrines or listing them: the difference between a peace and a mere truce, the idea that a treaty can carry a reservation of material for a future conflict, the signboard of the Dutch innkeeper who hung the words over a picture of a churchyard, the thought that a maxim which cannot be avowed publicly is already discredited, and the contrast between someone who bends policy to morality and someone who bends morality to policy. Let the signboard be retold in my own words rather than quoted. The speech should leave the room able to repeat one image and one uncomfortable question, and it should not resolve the deferral: I want the fifth movement to say plainly what I am still refusing to decide, and why refusing is not the same as lying. Write in British English, avoid jargon, avoid citing editions or page numbers, and keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words.
