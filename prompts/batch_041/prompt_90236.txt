Question. Neural networks are trained by gradient descent on a loss that never mentions features, concepts, or representations — only next-token prediction, or whatever the task is. Yet interpretability researchers train linear probes on hidden activations and decode properties like part-of-speech, sentiment, or the state of a board game with high accuracy, and sparse autoencoders pull thousands of apparently meaningful features out of activations that look like undifferentiated noise at the level of individual neurons. A skeptic says: the network contains weights and activations, full stop; the features and representations are artifacts of the researcher's probe or dictionary, useful for us but not discovered in the network. A realist says: a probe is an instrument, and an instrument that reads something off doesn't invent it — a thermometer doesn't create the temperature. Who has the better of the argument, and what would actually settle the question? In your answer, distinguish information being present in the activations from information being represented by the network, say what role causal interventions play in the dispute, and say whether the question is ultimately empirical or conceptual, or both.

Student's answer. The skeptic's basic point is right: a linear probe is a separate model trained on frozen activations, so a probe that hits 90% accuracy shows only that the property is linearly decodable from the activations, not that the network computes with it. Decodability is cheap — you can decode a lot from a lot — and since nothing in the training loss mentions concepts, nothing forces the activations to be organized around the properties we happen to care about. So far the instrumentalist has the upper hand, and the realist owes us more than probe accuracy.

Superposition is where I think the realist gets traction. If a network has to track more features than it has dimensions, it can't give each feature its own neuron, so it stores features as overlapping, non-orthogonal directions; that's why individual neurons look polysemantic. Sparse autoencoders try to undo this by finding a sparse, overcomplete set of directions that reconstruct the activations. Because a sparse dictionary like this is the unique decomposition of the activations into independent features, the features an SAE recovers are the network's actual representational units rather than a modeling convenience.

The decisive evidence, though, is causal. If ablating a direction or patching an activation changes the behavior in the predicted way, we've shown the network uses the information, and that is exactly the challenge the instrumentalist set. Once an intervention shows a feature is causally necessary for the behavior, the instrumentalist has basically lost, because the feature is now doing explanatory work that can't be paraphrased away as a mere summary of the weights.

So I'd come down on the realist side, with one caveat. The caveat is that 'represents' still needs a gloss, and the natural one is belief: for the network to represent a property is for it to take the property to be the case, which is what lets us ask whether the representation is accurate. On that reading, realism about representations is just the thesis that networks have beliefs about their inputs, and the interpretability evidence makes that very plausible.
