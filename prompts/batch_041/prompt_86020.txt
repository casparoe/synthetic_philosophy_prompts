I'm a second-year PhD student in a cognitive science group that runs a weekly reading group, and we have spent the last three sessions on heterophenomenology: Dennett's proposal that a science of consciousness should treat a subject's reports the way an anthropologist treats a text in an unknown language — as material to be interpreted alongside every other piece of evidence, with the subject granted authority about how things seem to her and about nothing further, and with the first-person methods that Dennett dismisses as lone-wolf autophenomenology bracketed rather than believed. We ended the last session split down the middle, and a few days later one of our postdocs circulated a short note proposing what she calls calibrated heterophenomenology, which she insists is a repair rather than a rejection. Her proposal has two rules. The demotion rule: when a subject's report conflicts with a robust non-report-based measure — the standard illustration being a subject who denies seeing the changed item while a classifier reliably separates the change trials from the no-change trials — the denial is to be reclassified from a claim about the subject's experience into evidence that the reporting process failed, and the subject's state on those trials is to be characterized by the third-person measure instead. The promotion rule: when a trained observer's report survives repetition, re-description under different framings, and the experimenter's attempts to talk her out of it, that report graduates from a claim inside the heterophenomenological world to a datum that constrains theory, so that a third-person account must accommodate it rather than merely explain it away.

I would like you to write the dialogue our next session should have had. The two speakers are both postdocs in the group, and neither is a fool or a caricature: one works squarely within heterophenomenology, the other was trained in phenomenological method and works in the neurophenomenological tradition that treats first-person and third-person investigations as mutually constraining. Each should begin by reconstructing the other's strongest objection in terms the other would accept before answering it, and each should engage the other's actual last point rather than sliding back to a prepared speech. I want the argument to turn on real joints: whether the demotion rule is a discovery about the mind or a stipulation about what will count as evidence; whether a subject can be mistaken about her own experience, and what the phrase failure of the reporting process presupposes; whether stability under the experimenter's own re-descriptions is a mark of validity or merely of a shared vocabulary; and whether calibration between two kinds of data is even coherent when only one of them is allowed to set the scale. They should agree on the facts of at least one concrete case and disagree about what it shows. Neither should win by appeal to what Dennett or Varela really meant — they should argue the substance — and neither should be allowed to dissolve the dispute into a difference of emphasis. Let them make at least one concession each, and end with each stating precisely what would have to be true for her to abandon her position, or what the residue of disagreement finally consists in. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words of dialogue, with only enough stage business to make the speakers distinct.
