I recently joined the scientific advisory board of a small synthetic-biology company, and it has made the philosophical debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function unexpectedly personal. The company builds minimal bacterial cells with inserted genetic circuits, and we keep arguing about whether the activity of a newly inserted sequence should be called its function. The molecular biologists say yes: the sequence contributes to the cell's capacity to survive and produce our product in the bioreactor, and that is what function means. The evolutionary biologists on the board say no: the sequence has no history of selection, so its activity is at best a useful effect, not a function. Both sides know the standard positions and the standard counterexamples — vestigial organs, exaptations, novel traits, malfunctions — and neither has moved in months. The literature seems similarly stuck: the same examples circulate, the same replies are made, and the two camps largely talk past each other. Why has this debate stalled, and what kind of disagreement is it really? Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of function, a verbal dispute about how to use the word 'function', or two different explanatory projects — one about how a trait is maintained in a system, one about why it exists at all — being run under a single heading? Is the hidden issue the normativity of function claims, i.e., whether a malfunction can still count as a function, or the relative explanatory priority of selection history versus current causal contribution? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a decision procedure for ascribing function in cases like ours, a formal result about the extensions of the two accounts, or an argument that no single account should be expected? Please engage with what each side actually says, including the standard replies to the standard counterexamples, and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is.
