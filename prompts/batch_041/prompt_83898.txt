I'm a software engineer who ended up designing the voting mechanism for the governance of a large open-source foundation: members submit a ranking of proposals and, separately, a weight saying how much the outcome matters to them, and the system runs a randomized lottery over pairwise comparisons with probabilities derived from both inputs. Next month I have to defend it at a workshop on computational social choice, and I want to walk in ready. The room is mostly social choice theorists who know the impossibility literature cold and will read my design as a naive attempt to sidestep the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — one of them works on domain restrictions and will tell me that single-peaked preferences already buy strategy-proofness without any of my machinery — plus two cryptographers who care only about Sybil resistance and will ask why anyone would submit an honest weight. My line is that the theorem doesn't apply to what I built, and that this is a feature rather than a cheat: I fail its hypotheses on purpose (I'm not onto, I'm not purely ordinal, and I'm settling for a weaker incentive property than dominant-strategy truthfulness), because I think the failure modes I buy are cheaper than the ones the theorem leaves me. Prepare my Q&A sheet: six to eight objections I'll actually get, in the order this room will raise them, each phrased the way a social choice theorist would put it — axiom first, mechanism second — ordered by how much damage it does, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, especially which hypotheses I should admit I'm evading rather than escaping, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
