In my seminar on the intentional stance and artificial minds, the professor has been developing a distinction between thin and thick intentionality. A system has thin intentionality when its behavior is most efficiently predicted by attributing to it states that are about things, even if those states are not the system's own in any deeper sense. A system has thick intentionality when its states are about things in a way that is not merely relative to an interpreter's predictive convenience—when they can be correct or incorrect independently of whether anyone finds it useful to attribute them. Her verdicts so far: a thermostat has neither; a chess engine has thin intentionality but no thick intentionality; a honeybee has thin intentionality but no thick intentionality; a corporation has thin intentionality but no thick intentionality; a simple reinforcement-learning agent that navigates a grid has thin intentionality but no thick intentionality; a human adult has both. Next week we are discussing a current large language model—specifically, a chat model with no persistent memory, no embodiment, and no goals beyond producing likely continuations, but which can fluently answer questions about what it believes, revise those answers when prompted, and pass many tests of commonsense reasoning. Its explanations of its answers are generated after the fact and do not reliably reflect the computations that produced those answers. The professor has not yet given her verdict. I have to write a short position paper predicting it. What would she say about the language model's thin intentionality and its thick intentionality? Identify the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts about the language model, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case or experiment that would best distinguish the rules. Also tell me what would change your mind about the prediction—which consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
