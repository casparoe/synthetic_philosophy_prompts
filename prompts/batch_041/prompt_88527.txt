I'm an undergraduate taking a module on the epistemology of testimony, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when we're entitled to believe what people tell us. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher working on epistemology and help me do reflective equilibrium properly: begin by brainstorming the main positions in the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate, then test my verdicts against them, and finish with a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles. Please think carefully and reason about the cases rather than simply reporting what the literature says.

Here are my verdicts. A passer-by tells me the post office closes at five: I believe them at once. A stranger online tells me the election was rigged: I don't, though I know nothing about their reliability. My flatmate, whom I've caught exaggerating before, tells me she's just back from Portugal: I believe her. The same flatmate tells me a restaurant is worth the money: I check the reviews. A doctor tells me my ankle isn't broken: I believe her. The same doctor tells me her hospital is the best in the region: I don't. I believe a well-referenced encyclopaedia entry on the Battle of Hastings but not an anonymous blog post making the same claim, though I've verified neither author. My tentative principle is that I'm entitled to believe an assertion unless I have some specific reason not to: trust is the default, and defeaters do the work. But that seems to leave the encyclopaedia-versus-blog verdict and the timetable verdict unexplained — one turns on the source being structurally unlikely to be wrong, the other ignores reliability altogether. Can a single principle capture all of these? If so, state it precisely, say which clause each verdict motivates, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't mentioned: believing a stranger's report of what a smell reminds them of, and believing a stranger about which way the parade goes. If no single principle works, say which verdict I should abandon and why. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your favoured principle cannot accommodate.
