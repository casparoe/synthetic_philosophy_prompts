I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on work, leisure, and the meaning of life, and I close the term with Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for our grandchildren — the one in which he predicts that within a century the economic problem will be solved, that something like a fifteen-hour week will be normal, and that the real difficulty will be learning how to use the freedom from pressing economic cares. My students reliably take the essay as a failed forecast about hours and miss the more interesting philosophical claim: that once absolute needs are met, the remaining needs are relative ones — needs we have only because their satisfaction lifts us above our neighbours — and that the accumulation of money as an end in itself is a passion Keynes regarded as quasi-pathological, one we could in principle discard once abundance arrived. Last year's discussion stalled on the prediction itself; one student insisted the essay had simply been refuted because hours have not fallen, and I could not get the class to see that the interesting question is what Keynes thought the point of the leisure would be. I want to end the unit with a film that takes his thought experiment seriously rather than mocking it, and Groundhog Day seems right: Phil Connors is handed exactly the thing Keynes thought we would have to learn to use — unlimited time, no economic necessity, no consequences that stick — and the film tracks him through self-indulgence, despair, and eventually a kind of cultivation. What I want from you is a reading of the film through Keynes's account of the good life. Where does it dramatize the problem he identified — that abundance, once achieved, leaves us with the question of how to live — and where does it complicate or contradict him? In particular, take seriously the possibility that Phil's final phase is not Keynesian at all: that the piano, the ice sculpture, the French, and the rescues of the townspeople are a work ethic in leisure clothing, and that the film's real answer to the question of the good life is love and community rather than the aesthetic and personal goods Keynes's Bloomsbury milieu prized. But I also want the reverse pressure: where does the film vindicate Keynes against his critics, and where does his essay anticipate something the film only dramatizes?

Please structure the response under the following headings, in this order: (1) The reading in brief (about 150 words); (2) Keynes's picture of the good life, reconstructed (about 300 words), making clear what he thought would remain to be done once the economic problem was solved, and why he thought the habits of acquisition would outlive their economic justification; (3) Where Groundhog Day dramatizes the problem (about 400 words), anchored in specific scenes and phases of Phil's repetition; (4) Where the film resists the Keynesian reading (about 400 words), including the objection that Phil's self-improvement is still a form of accumulation, and the possibility that the film's good life is moral rather than aesthetic; (5) What the film adds to the philosophical debate (about 250 words), saying what it shows that argument alone does not; and (6) A short verdict (about 100 words) on whether Groundhog Day is finally Keynesian, anti-Keynesian, or something the essay cannot classify. Please paraphrase rather than quote — no quotations from the film or the essay — and no bibliography.
