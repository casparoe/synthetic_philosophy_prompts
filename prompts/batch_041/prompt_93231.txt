I'm applying for a one-year fellowship and I need a two-page research proposal out of it (so maybe 1100-1200 words), pitched to a reading panel that's mostly cognitive science and philosophy of mind people. I've got a half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into something with actual bones. The idea: Hofstadter's line that creativity is basically knob-twiddling. You almost never invent a new game, you just turn the knobs on an old one, and the only thing separating a minor variation from a stroke of genius is how far you turn them. Everyone nods along, because it's obviously right for bebop and sonnets and the blues. But I think the thesis is either trivially true or false, and nobody has made it sharp enough to tell which.

My hunch is that it only has teeth if "how far you turned" can be defined, and that this is always relative to how the space of possibilities has been carved up. Which would make creativity a two-place predicate: creative-relative-to-a-space-S, not creative, full stop. And then a lot of what look like substantive disagreements about particular works — this one's derivative, that one was a paradigm shift or just a big nudge — turn out to be disagreements about where to draw S, not about the work at all.

The part I want to actually do rather than just assert: there seem to be two very different things getting lumped together. (1) Turning a knob the space already has a slot for — a new chord substitution, a faster tempo. (2) Changing the panel: introducing a parameter that nobody had treated as a parameter, after which the old knobs have to be re-described. My suspicion is that (2) isn't a different kind of act, it's the same act relative to a bigger space, and that there's no non-arbitrary fact about which space is the real one. If that's right, the Hofstadter picture either collapses into "novelty is relative to a reference class," which is true but toothless, or there's a principled way to fix the space — from the internal structure of the artifact itself, or from the practitioners' own sense of what falls inside the tradition — and then the picture has content again and starts making checkable predictions.

So, two case studies, picked so that one space is contested and the other is fully explicit. Contested: jazz contrafacts, where dozens of tunes get written over the chord changes of a single old standard and musicians genuinely argue about how far you can wander before it stops being that tune. Explicit: the letter-string microdomain Hofstadter's own analogy programs work in, where the space is written down in code and you can compute how far a move actually travels. Plus a small formal model — a parameterized family with a metric on it — where I define "size of variation" two different ways and check whether they agree with what the practitioners say in the jazz case. And I'd definately want his book that translates one short Renaissance French poem dozens of different ways as a third case, since there the theme is nailed down and everything else is variation, so you can ask whether the versions people praise are the far-travelling ones or the ones that find a dimension nobody had carved.

Keep the scope realistic — if this is really two years of work, say so and cut it down to something one person can finish in a year.
