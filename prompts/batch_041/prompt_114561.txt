Had a three-hour argument with my friend Rowan about Ellsberg's urn and I need to know if I'm the dogmatic one. My line: with 30 red balls and 60 black-or-yellow in a proportion nobody will tell you, preferring the bet on red to the bet on black is just a mistake — a bias that gets called a preference because it's popular. She came back with something I can't shake. Suppose the 60 balls were drawn one at a time by a fair coin, so you know exactly how the urn got filled, you just dont know the outcome. Then black has a clean 1/3 chance, same as red, and I'd happily take either. So in the original case, what am I actually averse to — the balls, or not knowing who filled the urn? And if it's the second thing, is my "it's just a bias" claim even a claim about rational choice anymore, or am I just insisting that a rational agent must have a prior when in fact she doesn't? So: what would count against me? Formal results, cases, arguments about whether the state space is even well-defined — whatever should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of these I'd have to concede are real problems rather than the usual "people just feel it's riskier". Also a straight answer on whether I can defend this view against every counterexample by redescription — if it's basically unfalsifiable, say so, and say whether that matters.
