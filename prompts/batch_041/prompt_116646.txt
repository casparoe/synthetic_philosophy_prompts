I've been going down a rabbit hole on Thomas Szasz lately — the psychiatrist who wrote The Myth of Mental Illness and spent his whole career arguing that what we call mental illness is really just problems in living, and that pinning a diagnosis on someone is usually a way of either excusing what they did or locking them up. He died in 2012. I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy training (so sorry if I'm butchering the terminology), and what got me thinking about this is that two things have happened since he died that I'd really like his take on.

The first is AI. There's a whole crop of therapy chatbots now, plus systems that score your crisis risk from your texts or your voice, and on top of that people are increasingly just typing their problems into an LLM and treating whatever it says as a diagnosis — walking away from a chat window convinced they have ADHD, or PTSD, or autism. The second is the way trauma talk has taken over: complex PTSD, nervous system regulation, trauma stored in the body, the whole genre where the message is that your symptoms are the physical residue of what was done to you and therefore not your fault. My friend who works in HR says "trauma-informed" about basically everything now, which is partly what set me off. That whole way of talking has blown up in a way I doubt he ever saw coming.

So what would Szasz have made of these? I'm less interested in "he'd say it's all a myth" than in which of his own commitments each one would have put pressure on. He was obsessed with people being responsible for what they do, and he was basically a libertarian who thought consenting adults should be able to do whatever they want with their own heads — those two seem to pull in opposite directions here, and I can't tell which way he'd bend. Also, would he have read the chatbot self-diagnosis thing as a kind of vindication (nobody's even pretending a real doctor is involved anymore), or as the same myth in a new costume? Give me your best guess at what his actual positions would've been, and be explicit about where you're extrapolating versus going off something he definately said.
