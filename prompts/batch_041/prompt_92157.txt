Write a polished dialogue between two philosophers, Mira and Jonas, on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, jaywalking—are moral obligations, and whether visitors are bound by the local versions of them. Mira defends the convention-as-obligation view: conventions can generate genuine, directed moral obligations because they are load-bearing for practices of mutual accountability, fair cooperation, and respect; a visitor who ignores them does not merely breach etiquette but wrongs those who sustain the practice. Jonas defends the convention-as-coordination view: conventions are solutions to coordination problems, not sources of moral obligation; they bind only those who have tacitly accepted them, and visitors owe only the universal duties not to harm, exploit, or disrespect in a thicker sense. Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman.

They should debate a concrete case: Nadia, a visitor from a country where tipping is not customary, spends a week in a city where servers depend on tips, queueing is strict, and jaywalking is widespread but illegal. Mira argues that Nadia has a moral obligation to tip and to queue properly; Jonas argues that she has at most prudential or civility reasons, and that the moral case for tipping is weaker than it looks because it may sustain an unjust wage structure. They should also discuss jaywalking, where the local practice and the formal rule come apart, and ask whether a visitor who jaywalks is wronging anyone.

In the course of the dialogue, have them develop and contest a principle: the load-bearing convention test, according to which a local convention generates a moral obligation for a visitor only if it is load-bearing for a practice that protects urgent interests or fair cooperation, and if compliance is not disproportionately costly. Have them also consider the guest's discount: the idea that visitors are entitled to a discount on the moral force of local conventions proportional to how hard the convention is for an outsider to know, how costly compliance is for them, and whether non-compliance would be legible as ignorance rather than as a refusal to comply. They should distinguish between a directed moral obligation, owed to someone who can hold the visitor to account, and a non-directed requirement of local practice.

Each character should concede at least one point and refine their position in response to the other. Every reply should engage the previous argument rather than talk past it. End with a short joint statement of what they now agree on, what remains unresolved, and what conceptual question would need to be settled to resolve it. Do not cite real authors, texts, or empirical studies; keep the dialogue self-contained and conceptual. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
