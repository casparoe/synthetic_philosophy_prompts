I run a small reading group that's spent the last few months on late Nietzsche, and we've gotten stuck in an argument I can't resolve: how seriously to take all the stuff about diet, digestion, climate, walks, sleep, alcohol and the rest — the part of Ecce Homo where he goes through his own regimen and treats it as belonging to his philosophy rather than as an aside. Half the group thinks it's just biography, a sick man's notes on managing a sick body, and that it tells us nothing about the doctrines. The other half thinks it's the reverse: that the physiology is load-bearing, that when he treats the state of a person's gut as a philosophical matter, or treats a mistake about diet as a mistake about life as a whole, he's making a claim and not just a confession. Then there's a third camp that says it's all performance — the regimen is part of the persona, the self-fashioning, and reading it as a theory misses what it's doing. Nobody can even say what the live options are, so I want to build a "which reading of Nietzsche on diet and health do you actually hold?" quiz: 8–10 questions, each one a concrete case or a claim someone can react to without knowing the labels, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

Some things I'd want the questions to get at, in whatever order works: whether the collapse of his health in the late 1880s counts as evidence against a philosophy that recommends a regimen; whether dietary advice for him is universal or always indexed to a particular person's task and forces (I'm interested in the possibility that he has no general theory of health at all, and that health is relative to what a person is for — if that's right, what's left of health as a standard?); whether sickness is something to be cured or something to be used; whether vegetarianism and the moralizing of diet come out as errors about physiology or errors about morality; whether alcohol and religious devotion function as the same kind of anaesthetic, so that the question of diet is really a question about what you do with your suffering; and whether the whole business is continuous with the old tradition of dietetics — the regimen as a way of life, the cultivation of the self through the care of the body — or a break from it. If you can find two questions where the camps split in opposite directions off the same passage, include them; that's more useful to me than a question per camp.

Then the scoring key: at least five named positions, each with its most awkward commitment in one sentence. I'd expect roughly a deflationary/biographical reading, a physiological or naturalist reading where the medical claims are load-bearing, a performative or self-fashioning reading, a symptomatic reading where the diet talk is itself a symptom to be explained rather than an explanation of anything, and a therapeutic reading where the regimen is a practice rather than a theory. If one of those is a fake position — a view nobody could hold because it collapses into another — say so and show the collapse rather than padding the list. Also flag any two positions the quiz can't separate by cases alone, and note any combination of answers that lands between positions, i.e. a coherent view that nobody seems to occupy, and whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I don't need citations, I need the instrument and your reasoning about why each question is diagnostic.
