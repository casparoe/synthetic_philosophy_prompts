I would like help identifying a pattern I keep encountering in accounts of scientific priority, and I am not certain whether it already has a name.

The pattern concerns the way the fact of independent discovery is used as evidence about the discovery itself. Two people arrive at the same result, working separately and without contact, and the coincidence is taken to show that the result was waiting to be found — that the field had assembled everything needed, that anyone competent would have reached it soon, and therefore that the question of who got there first matters far less than the discoverers imagine. The two names become interchangeable, and the achievement is deflated by its own duplication. I understand why this feels compelling. What I cannot get past is that the evidence is drawn from a set that is guaranteed to be unrepresentative. We can count only the discoveries that were made. We never see the near misses that a second person almost reached and did not, the ideas for which all the ingredients were present and which nonetheless went unfound for another generation, or the results that were perfectly findable and never found at all. The number of independent discoverers is a numerator with no visible denominator, and yet it is routinely read as a measure of how inevitable the discovery was. One discoverer feels like proof that the thing could have been missed; two discoverers feel like proof that it could not; and both impressions are produced by the same narrow window.

I suspect this is adjacent to two things I already know by name — hindsight, which makes an outcome look inevitable once it is known, and the bare fact of multiple discovery, that the same thing sometimes gets found twice — but neither of those is quite the thing I am describing. Hindsight is about how a known outcome feels; multiple discovery is a descriptive fact about how often duplication occurs. What I am after is the specific inference from the count of independent discoverers to the inevitability of the discovery, together with the bias that makes that count untrustworthy.

Does this inference have an established name, and if so, how does it relate to the nearest concepts? If it does not, please coin a term for it and define it precisely, distinguishing it from hindsight bias and from the sociology of multiple discovery. I would also like the boundaries drawn: say whether it applies to a case in which three people independently reached the same result and everyone afterward agreed the result had been inevitable, and to a case in which one person reached it and everyone afterward treated it as a lucky accident. A bare name will not help me; what I need is the carving.
