I'm in a grad seminar on the philosophy of emotion and I've been put down to lead discussion on William James's 1884 paper "What is an Emotion?" (the Mind one). The physiology talk I can follow okay, but there's a cluster of sentences in the opening pages that I've read maybe fifteen times now and I still can't pin down what he's actually claiming, and I have to present on it Thursday. Here's the bit I keep staring at: he starts with a concession, that "the only emotions I propose expressly to consider here are those that have a distinct bodily expression." Then the thesis: "My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion." Then, a little later, the line everyone quotes, that "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be." And then: "Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

What trips me up is that this seems to say two things that pull against each other. Half the time he sounds like a pure "feeling" theorist — the emotion just *is* the felt bodily state, a sensation of the viscera, nothing more. But the bear sentence seems to be drawing a line between a cognition (seeing, judging it best to run) and the emotion proper, as if the cognition can't get you all the way to fear on its own — which sounds like he's giving cognition some role and the feeling the rest. And a friend in the seminar said James is really closer to a *perceptual* theory than a feeling theory, because for him the emotion is a perception of the body; but another friend shot back that if that's right, then my fear is about my racing heart rather than about the bear, which is obviously wrong, so the perceptual reading must be a misreading. Neither of them could point to which sentences actually settle it, and now I don't know what I'm defending.

Can you help me untangle this? Take the role of someone who really knows this text and the history of the James–Lange theory, and please think carefully and step by step rather than jumping to a verdict. I'd like you to first brainstorm the three or four plausible readings of that central sentence about the feeling of the changes *being* the emotion, then go through them one at a time, saying for each what in the passage supports it and what it would commit James to. Then tell me which reading the text best supports and why, and be explicit about where you're doing textual exegesis versus where you're reconstructing an argument he never quite states. I especially want your take on four things: (i) whether the emotion here is a feeling, a perception, or something else, and whether it's *about* anything; (ii) whether the bear passage is meant as an empirical prediction (strip the body away and you'd literally have no fear) or as a claim about what we'd even be willing to call fear; (iii) whether his upfront restriction to emotions with "a distinct bodily expression" changes the answer or just dodges the hard cases; and (iv) whether "perception of the exciting fact" is supposed to already include the fact's *being exciting* — because if it does, the whole thing looks circular to me.
