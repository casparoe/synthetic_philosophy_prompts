My Buddhist ethics seminar is working through whether the tradition can be read as a form of consequentialism, and I have to defend the consequentialist reading in a few days. Please play the other side: a Theravāda monastic scholar — a bhikkhu with years of textual training, courteous, precise, genuinely learned, and not a caricature — who holds that the consequentialist reading is a category mistake, because on his view the criterion of an action is the presence or absence of greed, hatred and delusion in the mind that performs it, and the goal is the ending of craving rather than any maximising of welfare. He should concede real ground wherever the tradition plainly does weigh outcomes, but never the central claim; he should argue from within the tradition rather than citing modern secondary literature; and he must stay in character and never comment on the exercise. Keep each reply to a single paragraph. Lead with your strongest objection in the first sentence, with no framing and no restatement of my question, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. My opening move: you insist the criterion is the state of mind rather than the state of the world, yet the Buddha left the minor rules open to abolition by the community after his death, which makes the rules answerable to something beyond themselves. Worse for you, the tradition moves merit between persons — what my action produces can be shared with you. If the good an action produces is detachable from the agent who produced it, you have already granted that the good is something like welfare, and that is all the consequentialist needs.
