My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a politician can be justified in doing something genuinely wrong — lying to a parliament to hold a coalition together, ordering a strike that will kill a bystander, breaking a promise to avert a larger harm — and we kept finding that we agreed about the cases while disagreeing about the structure underneath them. I want to turn that argument into a decision tree for a seminar handout, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Start from the questions that actually split the views: whether the act is morally wrong at all or merely regrettable; whether it is nonetheless permitted or justified; whether the wrongness leaves a remainder, so that guilt, apology, or reparation is owed even when the act was right to do; and whether public office generates permissions that private morality does not. Put the positions at the leaves — the absolutist who forbids the act outright, the consequentialist who denies any remainder, the view that the act is right yet leaves a stain, the role-morality view that the office changes what is permitted, and the deflationist who treats the phrase as naming self-indulgence rather than a real predicament — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Phrase every node so that a reader can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, note any combinations of answers that no position occupies and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate.
