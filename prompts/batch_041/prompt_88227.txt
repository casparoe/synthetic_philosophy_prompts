I've been going down a rabbit whole on the van Meegeren affair and I want you to treat it as a test case for a theory rather than a bit of trivia, so let me set it up.

The facts, briefly, in case you don't have them: Han van Meegeren was a Dutch painter working in the 1930s and 40s whose own work the critics had dismissed. He started producing "lost" Vermeers, and the best-known one was accepted by Abraham Bredius -- a genuinely eminent Dutch art expert, one-time director of the Mauritshuis -- who published it as a newly discovered masterpiece. A museum bought it. The public adored it; people saw in it a whole new phase of Vermeer that nobody had known existed. During the war Göring bought one of the "Vermeers" and prized it. After the war van Meegeren was arrested, not for forgery but for selling a Dutch national treasure to the Nazis, and facing a possible death penalty he confessed that the paintings were his own -- then painted another "Vermeer" in custody to prove it. And here's the bit I actually care about: once the confession landed, the same canvases stopped being masterpieces in the eyes of the same people. The paint didn't move.

The theory I want you to run this through is the debate over aesthetic testimony. Short version, in case it isn't at your fingertips: can you get aesthetic knowledge, or aesthetic justification, from someone else telling you? Pessimists say no -- a judgment of taste has to be yours, first-hand, you can't just borrow one. Optimists say aesthetic testimony is no worse than testimony anywhere else. Both sides argue mostly over toy cases (can I know the sunset was beautiful because you said so?), which is exactly why I want a real one.

So map the episode onto the debate properly. Who is testifying to whom, about what, and what would count as the audience knowing rather than merely believing? Take the public who loved the painting on Bredius's say-so -- did they have aesthetic knowledge, a borrowed true belief, a real experience that simply wasn't warranted, or what? Then take Bredius himself, who did judge first-hand and got it spectacularly wrong: what does that do to the pessimist's picture of first-hand experience as the privileged route to aesthetic knowledge? And then the strangest part, the same canvas flipping from sublime to cold the moment a piece of historical information arrives -- is that a change in aesthetic experience or only in aesthetic judgment, and does the debate even have the vocabulary to tell those two apart?

Then the other direction, which is the part I most want: what does the case do to the theory? Does it support the pessimists (look what happens when you outsource taste), or does it support an optimism neither side really defends -- that some aesthetic properties are historical rather than perceptual, things like originality, achievement, integrity, the visible trace of a real hand and a real life, and that those can only ever be known by testimony? If it's the second, say so, and say what it costs the debate, because it would mean the whole thing has been framed around the wrong kind of aesthetic property.

And be honest about where the framework just goes silent, instead of stretching it to fit.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind. If you come down on a verdict, give me the consideration or arguement that would push you off it, and flag the step in your reasoning you'd bet is most likely to be wrong.
