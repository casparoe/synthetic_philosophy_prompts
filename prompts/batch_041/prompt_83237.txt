There's a popular book on causation I picked up at the airport (written by a journalist, not a philosopher) that explains causation by omission like this:

'Philosophers used to insist that a cause had to be an event — something that happened. Then came the counterfactual theory: X causes Y just when, had X not occurred, Y would not have occurred. Once you have that, omissions drop neatly into place. The doctor's failure to treat caused the death, because had she treated, the patient would have lived. So omission causation is just ordinary causation after all, and counterfactual dependence is what proves it. The theory's one embarrassment is that it makes far too many things causes — the gardener who didn't water the plant is as much the cause of its death as the neighbour who never even knew the plant existed — but that's a general problem with counterfactual theories, not a problem about omissions in particular. And since the account is purely counterfactual, whether a failure counts as a cause has nothing to do with whether anyone was expecting you to act. Duty, blame and expectation are seperate questions that come in later. Which is why the law treats the doctor who withholds treatment and the doctor who injects poison as equally the cause of death.'

How much of this should I believe? Go sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on causation by omission would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong.
