I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we've spent the last few weeks on panpsychism -- the view that consciousness or experience goes all the way down, that it's a basic feature of matter rather than something that only appears once you wire enough neurons together. I get the appeal, honest, but the piece I keep tripping over is the combination problem: fine, suppose electrons or whatever have some tiny sliver of experience, but how do a bunch of those micro-experiences add up to the one single unified experience I'm having right now? Everyone in the group just says "emergence" and waves their hands and I want something with actual teeth.

So here's the case I want to run through it. I've been reading about the split-brain patients -- the people who had their corpus callosum cut to treat really severe epilepsy, mostly in the mid-20th century, and afterwards the two hemispheres seem to process things separately: one half can talk about what it saw, the other half can't, and sometimes the two disagree or even work against each other, one hand doing something the other hand quietly undoes. Alot of people took this to mean that a person can, in some sense, be two subjects of experience instead of one. I want you to take that whole episode -- the surgeries, the experiments, the interpretation that there might be two minds sharing a skull -- and look at it through panpsychism and its combination problem. What does the panpsychist say is actually happening in these patients? If each hemisphere has its own experience, does that mean the combination relation that was supposedly gluing the micro-experiences together got undone by a scalpel? Is the unity of consciousness supposed to be that fragile on this view, or is that a problem it can't absorb? And where does the theory just go quiet?

Then flip it around. What does the split-brain case tell us about panpsychism's story about combination -- is it a friendly case, a counterexample, or something the view never anticipated? Please don't give me a tour of what various famous people supposedly said about split brains; I want you to actually reason about whether a view that says experience is fundamental and combines into bigger and bigger subjects can handle a case where the biggest subject apparently comes apart. If you think the move is to deny there were ever really two subjects, or to say the combination was never genuine to begin with, then say that and tell me what it costs the panpsychist. Run the mapping both directions and be straight with me about where it definately breaks.
