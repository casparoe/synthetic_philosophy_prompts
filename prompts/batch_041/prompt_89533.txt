Read Francis Ford Coppola's The Conversation (1974) through Davidson's account of radical interpretation, and say what each does to the other. Harry Caul is a radical interpreter by accident: his evidence is a few sentences recorded in Union Square; the same words can be heard as fear of the Director or as a justification for killing him first, depending on where the emphasis falls; and Caul's reading is settled by his own guilt and by what he assumes about the couple. Davidson holds that belief and meaning must be assigned together, by charity, against the totality of the evidence, and that this is what keeps interpretation from being arbitrary.

I want the reading to press on two joints. First: is Caul's error a failure of charity, or an excess of it, given that he attributes to the couple the fears he would have had in their place? Does the film show the total-evidence constraint doing the work Davidson assigns it, or an interpreter for whom only his own prior beliefs are load-bearing? Second: Davidson thought genuine indeterminacy of interpretation was limited — an uninterpretable alien scheme is not a coherent hypothesis — whereas Quine thought the evidence leaves meaning underdetermined. Say which, if either, the film illustrates.

The film also makes us interpreters, and withholds precisely the evidence Davidson says we would need; say what that does to the reading. Close by saying what the film shows about interpretation that Davidson's argument alone does not — including whether it matters that the people Caul interprets never consented to being understood.
