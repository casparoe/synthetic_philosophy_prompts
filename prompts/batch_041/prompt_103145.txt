The following is a short-answer question from a second-year course on perception and epistemic justification, followed by one student's response. I am grading it and want your assessment: assign a grade out of 10 and give me the specific comments that justify it — what earns credit, what is wrong, misleading, or unsupported, and what a fully adequate answer would have said at each point. Assess the response on its own merits rather than as a draft to be improved.

Question: Cognitive penetration is standardly characterized as the influence of a subject's beliefs, desires, or other cognitive states on the contents of her perceptual experience. (a) Distinguish cognitive penetration from two other kinds of top-down influence on perception that are usually not counted as penetration, and say why the distinction matters. (b) Explain what turns on the existence of cognitive penetration for the thesis that perceptual experience can justify empirical belief. (c) State whether the empirical evidence establishes penetration and defend your answer.

Student's answer: "(a) Cognitive penetration is when what you think or want changes what you see. The clearest cases are attention and perceptual learning: attending to a feature makes it more salient, and once a radiologist has learned to read scans the tumours start popping out at her, which shows that her beliefs about tumours are shaping her visual experience. Cross-modal cases like the McGurk effect get cited for the same reason, since vision and audition influence each other. So penetration comes in degrees, and the real question is how far up the processing stream the influence reaches.

(b) What turns on it is that perception is supposed to be the tribunal against which belief is tested. If beliefs can reach down and alter experience, then experience cannot be a neutral court of appeal, and the claim that a perceptual experience justifies a belief collapses: a justified belief would have to rest on something theory-neutral, and there is nothing theory-neutral to rest on. This is the lesson the Churchlands draw from perceptual plasticity, and it is why the modularity thesis matters. Fodor does not really deny the phenomenon — he grants that beliefs affect perception, but insists the influence is slow and mediated, so his disagreement with Churchland is largely about how fast it happens.

(c) I think the evidence does establish it. Critics object that many alleged demonstrations are confounded by memory, response bias, or task demands, but that only shows that some studies are flawed; it does not show that none are, and the evidence has accumulated to the point where the phenomenon is settled."
