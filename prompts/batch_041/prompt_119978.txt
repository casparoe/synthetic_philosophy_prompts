I'm in the middle of a dissertation chapter on Scanlon's contractualism and I've hit a wall that I don't think is my fault. The setup, as I understand it: an act is wrong just in case no set of principles that no one could reasonably reject permits it, and the whole selling point is that justification has to run to each person individually — you can't just add up how much people would gain, because that rides roughshod over the fact that people are separate. The problem everyone knows is that this looks like it can't handle numbers at all: save one person from drowning or two from a slightly less bad fate, and the one has a complaint against the principle that tells you to save the two, so the principle gets rejected and you're stuck. Then there's the zoo of rescue moves — numbers matter as a tie-breaker between objections of comparable strength, numbers enter through the reasons that each individual separately has, numbers don't matter and that's a feature not a bug — and I've read alot of it and honestly nobody has moved in twenty-five years. My friend who works on population ethics says the whole thing is a pseudo-problem caused by everyone assuming that 'a reason' has to be somebody's reason, and she might be right but I can't tell whether thats a diagnosis or just a restatement of the problem in different words.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse, not another rescue attempt. Concretely: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about whether we're allowed to call a fact about numbers 'a reason', or two groups answering different questions under one heading — one asking what makes an act wrong, the other asking what a person could be asked to accept as a justification for a principle that burdens them? My own working hypothesis, and I'd rather you push on it than just agree — is that the real disagreement is about whether the thought that persons are separate is a metaphysical constraint on the content of acceptable principles or a constraint on the form of justification, and that once you prise those apart most of the standard counterexamples lose their grip, but a smaller and nastier problem survives for whichever of the two readings you take. Tell me if that's the right cut or if there's a better one, and argue it from what the positions actually commit you to rather than giving me a survey. Then be honest about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a counterexample, a result about what a principle that refuses to aggregate can and can't rank, or nothing at all because the question is ill-posed and the whole debate is downstream of a bad question. Skip the citations, I've got the literature; I need the shape of the thing.
