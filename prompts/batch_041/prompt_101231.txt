I'm putting together the final for an upper-level seminar on rationality and decision theory, and I want one essay question on Parfit's hitchhiker — the desert case in Reasons and Persons where you're stranded, a stranger offers to drive you to town on the condition that you promise to pay him once you get there, and you know perfectly well that once you're in town you'll have no reason to keep the promise (he can't catch you, you'll never see him again). I'm sick of answers that just retell the case and say, ah, rational irrationality, how clever. I want a question that makes them say what the case is actually meant to establish and then defend it against two variants I've cooked up for the exam:

(a) the fare up front — you happen to have a $100 bill in your pocket, and the stranger points out you could just hand it over now and skip the promise entirely;

(b) the witness — there's a second traveller stranded a few feet away who watches the whole thing, and next month she's the one with the car and you're the one stranded.

The students should explain the original, work through (a) and (b), and say whether the irrationality in the original is still doing any work once you've got a way to commit up front (a) or a reputation to protect (b). I want them to notice the two variants pull in different directions — one dissolves the puzzle by removing the need for a disposition, the other by making the paying actually rational — and I want them to deal with the thing everybody skips, which is that the whole set-up only bites if the stranger can tell what kind of person you are; if he cant, you just lie and the puzzle evaporates. Then, finally, take a side: is rational irrationality a genuine phenomenon, or does it collapse into ordinary instrumental reasoning about which dispositions to cultivate?

50 minute essay, so aim the model answer at roughly 700 words. Then give me a marking scheme: five must-hit points at 5 marks each (25 total), and for each point a one-liner on the specific move that separates a strong answer from a weak one, plus a line on how students typically get that point wrong. In the model answer please actually commit — pick a side on the genuine-or-not question and argue for it, don't just lay out the options and shrug. And no citations or reading lists; they won't have access to that and neither will I when I mark it.
