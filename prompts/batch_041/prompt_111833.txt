I am a third-year medical student who came to medicine from a philosophy background, and I cannot stop noticing something odd about how we decide who is sick. Every laboratory result I order comes back with a reference range — the interval that, by construction, contains the middle ninety-five percent of whatever population the laboratory has chosen as its healthy reference group. That interval is a fact about a sample, not a boundary inscribed in anyone's body: it shifts when the reference population changes, when the assay changes, and when a committee decides to move it. And yet we read it as though it marked a seam in nature — inside is well, outside is pathological — so that a value one unit past the line licenses a diagnosis, a prescription, and decades of surveillance. The same thing happens one level up, at the level of syndromes and cutoffs: when a professional body revises a threshold downward — the blood-pressure guidelines are the familiar case — millions of previously healthy adults become patients overnight, without any change in a single artery. We have also invented whole categories that consist of nothing but proximity to a line, such as pre-diabetes, pre-hypertension, and osteopenia, which would be strange if the underlying kinds were as robust as we treat them. And yet nobody in the clinic talks as though we had stipulated anything; we talk as though we had found it.

What, then, is this practice committed to? Does treating a percentile as a diagnostic boundary presuppose that health and disease are natural kinds with real thresholds — that there is a fact of the matter about who is hypertensive — or does it presuppose only something much weaker, that a statistical boundary is a serviceable practical kind for a given clinical purpose? Can the practice be made internally consistent, or does it quietly equivocate between a realist reading, on which we discover disease, and a conventionalist one, on which we draw the line? Which rival accounts of medical categories would license what we actually do, and which would be ruled out: a strict natural-kind realism about disease; a homeostatic-property-cluster view on which a disease is a stable, self-sustaining bundle of mechanisms rather than a point on a continuum; and a thoroughgoing pragmatism on which disease names whatever it is useful to treat? And if we took the cluster view seriously — asking whether a person harbors a coherent pathological process rather than whether a number crossed a line — what would have to change: the reference range, the diagnostic threshold, the status of the merely at-risk, the design of trials that enroll people defined only by a cutoff, and the way we explain a result to a patient? I am less interested in whether medicine is really discovering or really constructing its categories than in reconstructing what our actual practice presupposes, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, locating the points at which it is genuinely underdetermined between the realist and the pragmatist, and saying what would have to give if we committed to one side.
