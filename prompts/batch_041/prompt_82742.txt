I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I spent a conference dinner wedged between two people having an arguement I couldn't referee. I'd like you to do it for me, move by move.

The historian went first: the balance sheet on the Raj is positive. Britain built the railways, the telegraph and the universities, knitted a subcontinent that had never been one political unit into a single state, and handed over parliaments, a civil service and the rule of law. Take the British out of the counterfactual and you get a patchwork of princely states, probably poorer and definately less unified.

The economist said you can't just run that counterfactual and assume the alternative is chaos, and anyway the railways weren't a gift — Indian revenues paid for them, British investors got guaranteed returns, and the home charges and the drain ran the other way. He added that the real entries are the famines, Bengal in '43, and the collapse of Indian textiles while Lancashire was protected at home.

The historian came back: call it extraction if you like, but extraction that leaves ports, rails and institutions behind is a different animal from extraction that leaves nothing; you have to count the assets, not just the invoices.

The economist: the assets are the wrong unit of account. A railway that carries grain out at a price people can't pay doesn't feed anyone. And the institutional-legacy line proves too much — the same case gets made for every empire, and the countries that got the strongest dose of British institutions didn't obviously do best.

The historian: then famines are a bad unit too — independent states have starved their own people, and if every bad harvest is a British crime then every good one is a British credit.

The economist: I'm not assigning crimes, I'm asking what the railways caused that wouldn't have happened anyway, and the honest answer is that they paid for themselves in trade while the returns went to Britain.

Then they both looked at me. Can you take each move in turn and say how defensible it is — which claims are empirical, which are just accounting conventions, where somebody's smuggling in a baseline — and then say what the fight is actually about underneath: whether a balance-sheet framing of empire can be answered at all, or whether the two of them are answering different questions. Don't just pick a winner.
