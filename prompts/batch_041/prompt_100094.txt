I'm putting together a episode of my podcast (small philosophy-of-AI thing, 40 min or so) and next weeks guest is a philosopher of AI whose book argues that the real Turing criterion for machine intelligence isn't the imitation game at all, it's educability. Her line: the child-machine remarks that everyone treats as a charming aside are actually load-bearing, and the imitation game got overpromoted later by people who wanted a pass/fail test. Her gloss on educability is that a thing is intelligent to the degree it improves under a teacher's correction on problems nobody trained it on, not just on the problems that were in the curriculum. She also insists Turing himself saw this and that the 1950 paper is misread if you stop at the interrogation room.

Can you draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? No softballs, nothing like what drew you to this. I want the questions to make her state the view precisely once and then spend the rest of the interview defending it. Please cover:

- the trainable versus educable distinction, and whether that distinction is hers or actually Turing's
- the teacher problem: if competence is bounded by what the teacher can teach, hasn't she made machine intelligence a fact about pedagogy rather than about the machine
- whether educability can be assessed without smuggling the imitation game back in through an exam, and what a non-imitation test of educability would even look like
- the rewards and punishments channel Turing floats as the instruction signal, and whether that is teaching or just conditioning with extra steps
- why a dog, a child, or a very good feedback controller doesn't qualify, or if it does, what her criterion is still excluding
- the unfalsifiability worry: we can always say the machine wasn't educated properly, so what would count as a machine that is genuinely ineducable rather than badly taught
- the historical objection I think is strongest against her reading: that the child machine was an engineering expedient, a way to avoid hand-coding an adult mind, not a criterion of intelligence at all — press her on what textual or conceptual grounds she has for the stronger claim
- the social dimension: education implies a teacher with authority, a shared language, a community of correction — does she accept that this makes intelligence constitutively social, and is she willing to pay that price
- the alignment angle, and here I want the follow-up to bite: an educated machine is educated toward somebody's ends, so does her criterion measure competence or docility, and can she distinguish the two without appeal to the teacher's intentions
- the speed point, since Turing suggests machine education might go faster than a human child's — if it's compressed, does the machine end up with the same concepts or just the same outputs, and what does she think is lost if the developmental path is skipped

For each one, give me the question and then the follow-up, and make the follow-up the thing she would least want to be asked next. If a question has two plausible versions, pick the sharper one.
