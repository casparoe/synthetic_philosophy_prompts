I run a monthly working session on open problems in AI safety for a group of about fifteen people: mostly machine-learning engineers who build and evaluate models, a couple of philosophers, and a cryptographer who attends irregularly. Next month I am leading the session on the proposal — much discussed in the alignment literature — that two AI systems argue before a weaker judge, and that the judge's verdict then supplies the ground truth we cannot supply ourselves. I have sixty minutes, a whiteboard and a projector, and no assigned reading: nobody will have prepared anything. I do not want the explanation; I want the plan for the session.

What the hour has to accomplish: the group should end up seeing why the objection that literature files under the obfuscated-arguments problem is not the familiar worry that debaters will lie, and why it bears on the scaling claim rather than merely adding an engineering headache. Most of the group's mental picture of oversight is supervised fine-tuning plus human preference labels; two of them encountered the debate proposal at some point and are enthusiastic about it. Every piece of the sequence should be there because something later depends on it, and I want to be told what that dependency is.

Specifically, I need:

- The order of the pieces, with the reason each must precede the next: what the protocol assumes about the judge's competence and about both debaters' incentives; why the interesting case is not a judge who is fooled but a judge who is being told the truth in a form he cannot use; and where the recursive-decomposition move belongs in that order, given that it is the natural response and, on the pessimistic reading, does not work.
- The carrying example. I have been using the following and I suspect it is bad: two debaters dispute the output of a long computation; the dishonest one recites a chain of intermediate steps that looks locally plausible, and the only way to see the chain break is to perform the whole computation, which the judge cannot afford; the honest one can say only "run it." My worry is that this makes the problem look like a budget shortfall — as though a stronger judge or a cheaper check would dissolve it — and that it invites the audience to conclude the dishonest side is lying about the intermediate steps. Tell me whether to keep it, and if not, give me the example that should carry the session, stating what it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack.
- The misconceptions, in the order this group will actually form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each must be headed off: that obfuscation is just deception and cross-examination catches it; that the honest debater will always have a short, checkable refutation available because the truth is on his side; that this is merely the weak-judge problem, so a stronger judge fixes it; that it is a complexity-theoretic curiosity with no bearing on practice; and that requiring debaters to submit checkable proofs is a solution rather than a restatement of what was to be explained. Add whatever I have not anticipated.
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud. I assume the formal results, the proposed protocol repairs, and any comparison with interactive proof systems are out of reach in an hour, but tell me which refusal would be a mistake.
- One question to put to the room at the end that separates someone who has understood the objection from someone who has merely been persuaded that debate is risky. It should require them to produce something, not to agree with something.

Two regulars hold the settled view that this is an engineering problem a cleverer protocol will route around; I think it marks a structural limit on what a debate-like procedure can certify. Do not settle that by fiat in either direction, but leave the group able to say what would have to be true for the optimists to be right.

No reading list and no citations: I want this to work for people who have memorized nothing about the debate literature.
