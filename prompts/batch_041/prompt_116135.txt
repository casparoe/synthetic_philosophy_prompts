In my school's philosophy club, two members got into an argument that I've been asked to referee, and I'd rather not simply guess. One of them claimed that a sentence like "If it rains, the picnic will be cancelled" does not state a fact at all — it merely expresses the speaker's conditional probability that the picnic will be cancelled given that it rains — and he said that David Lewis's triviality results prove that conditionals cannot be treated as propositions with truth conditions. The other said this is a misreading: the theorem shows only that no proposition can have, for every probability function, a probability equal to the corresponding conditional probability, except in trivial cases, and that this is a problem for one particular theory of conditionals rather than a proof that conditionals have no truth conditions at all. The first replied that if a conditional did have truth conditions, its probability would just have to be the probability of "Either it doesn't rain or the picnic is cancelled," which is plainly wrong, since the two sentences do not even mean the same thing. The second answered that this rules out the material-conditional analysis but not truth-conditional accounts as such, and that which truth condition is correct is an open question rather than a settled one. They want me to rule on the exchange. Please adjudicate point by point rather than just declaring a winner, telling me who is right about what, and separating what is genuinely settled in the philosophy of language from what is contested, without presenting a contested position as the consensus. Give me exactly five numbered items — one for each of the four moves and one for what both of them are missing — each a sentence or two, with no separate introduction or conclusion.
