A friend and I argued past midnight about whether the international community killed people in Rwanda, and we broke off with neither of us conceding, so I would like our two positions mapped rather than adjudicated. The facts are not what we disagree about; take them as given. In January 1994 the commander of the United Nations force in Rwanda sent a cable warning of preparations for mass killing and asking leave to raid arms caches, and leave was refused. After the president's plane was shot down in April and the killing began, ten Belgian peacekeepers were murdered on the first day and the Security Council answered by cutting the force from roughly 2,500 troops to a token few hundred; the proposal to jam the radio station that was directing the killers was discussed and dropped; the reinforcements the commander asked for did not arrive while the killing was at its height; and it went on for about a hundred days. My friend holds that it is a category mistake to speak of a cause here at all: an omission is not an event, nothing happened, and nothing cannot kill anybody; all we may say is that the dead would probably have been fewer, or would be alive, had matters gone otherwise, which is a claim about a counterfactual and not about the United Nations. I hold that the Council did act — it voted to cut the force, it refused the raids, it adjourned — and that the omission, if that is what it is, is made of decisions.

What I want is the claims separated. First, say which of three things each of us is really asserting when we say the non-intervention caused the deaths: that the absence is itself a cause alongside the others; that the absence is no cause but the deaths counterfactually depended on it; or that the absence is no cause but belongs in the best explanation of why the killing was not stopped — and say which of these can be defended, and which of my friend's objections it survives. Second, test the account against this case rather than a tidy one: the omission is composed of decisions, it is distributed across many agents each of whom did something individually innocent, the counterfactual is genuinely indeterminate, and it stretches over a hundred days rather than an instant. Say which of these the theory absorbs and which it cannot. Third, answer the move my friend used to close the argument — that we fasten on the United Nations only because we disapprove of its inaction, and that had the same thing happened where no one cared, no one would speak of a cause at all. If he is right that the causal judgment here tracks a moral one, does that debunk the causal claim, or does it show that our notion of causing is normative through and through? Finally, run it the other way: if omissions can cause, what becomes of the doctrine that killing and letting die differ in kind — does the metaphysics underwrite the moral difference, or leave it looking like one we impose? Where the theory goes silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak.
