I write about politics and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on an essay about the question that refuses to die: which of the two Scandinavian neighbours, Sweden or Norway, is the more liberal? I would like you to argue with me rather than inform me. Play a comparative political scientist who is convinced the question has a genuine answer, who has already gone on record with a verdict, and who will hold that verdict across our exchange. You may concede small points and you may refine your reasoning, but you may not abandon the verdict, and above all you may not quietly change the yardstick you are measuring by. If you think my framing is rigged, say so and repair it. Reply in two or three tight paragraphs at a time, so that this feels like an argument and not a lecture, and stay in character until I say we are done.

Three things to keep in view, since neither of us can look anything up. First, the two countries are close on most of the obvious dimensions: both are wealthy constitutional monarchies with large public sectors, high taxes and generous welfare states, so the real work lies in deciding which differences are decisive. Second, the word liberal splits at least three ways in ordinary usage — the American sense of socially permissive, the classical sense of a state that leaves people alone, and the European sense of liberal as the opposite of authoritarian — and a serious answer has to say which of these it means and why that one deserves the name. Third, your verdict, the one you have already defended in print, is that Norway comes out ahead, and your yardstick is the amount of ordinary life a person can conduct without the state's permission: buying a drink, opening a shop, hiring and firing, choosing a school, driving, keeping an animal. You believe that yardstick, rather than welfare generosity or social permissiveness, is the one that deserves the name, and you are prepared to defend it against the charge that it merely encodes a preference.

My opening move: I have been tinkering with exactly that yardstick, and it keeps betraying me. Norway sits outside the European Union while Sweden sits inside it, and membership lets Swedes live and work across a continent without asking anyone's leave; meanwhile the Norwegian state owns large stakes in some of the country's biggest companies and banks an oil fund that makes the government a part owner of the nation's wealth, while Sweden since the 1990s has let parents carry public money to a school of their choosing, including one run for profit. By your own measure those facts pull in opposite directions, and I can make either country win simply by deciding whether permissions to move and to choose count for more than the absence of a state owner. So tell me plainly: on your yardstick, why does Norway win, and what makes that weighting a finding about the two countries rather than a report of which freedoms you happen to prize?
