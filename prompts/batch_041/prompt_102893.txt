I'm a novelist — I'm about halfway through a book set in and around a state legislature, and because I want the arguments in it to sound like arguments real people actually make, I've been reading a lot of testimony and op-eds about what should and shouldn't be against the law. There's one move I keep running into and I can't figure out if it has a name. It goes like this. Somebody wants to keep some practice illegal — take your pick: selling a kidney, paying a surrogate, a doctor helping a terminal patient die. They don't actually claim that any single instance of the practice hurts anyone. What they say is that if we stop criminalizing it, people will gradually stop regarding it as shameful, and once it stops being shameful the people who are currently shielded by the fact that everyone around them disapproves will lose that shield. The harm is real, they insist — it just never shows up as a victim. I saw the same shape of argument last month in a piece about decriminalizing something much more mundane, and the writer did the identical thing: no victim, just a predicted shift in what people are willing to tolerate, and a warning that the vulnerable will suffer the shift.

Here's the part that nags me, and I'd like help putting my finger on it. The harm they're predicting is made out of other people's attitudes — including, as far as I can tell, their own. So the argument kind of conjures the very disapproval it's warning me about, and there's no event you could point to and say "there, that's the harm," and no way to check the prediction, because the alternative is a whole different society with different people in it who feel differently. A friend who studied law told me this is just the old Hart–Devlin thing over again, but that doesn't feel right to me — that argument seemed to be about whether the practice itself is immoral and whether the law may enforce morality, whereas what I'm chasing is the *mechanism* (disapproval doing the damage) and the strange self-referential bit where the arguer's own disapproval is part of what's supposed to hurt us. Is there an established name for this specific shape — the harm-without-a-victim that runs entirely through a predicted change in social meaning? And if there isn't one, coin me a term, but please be exact about the edges: what it is, what it isn't, whether it's really just a slippery-slope argument wearing a disguise (and if so what gets lost by calling it that), and how it differs from a plain appeal to a shared morality. A bare label won't help me — I need the carving, because my character is going to have to answer this argument on the page and I don't want her to answer the wrong one.
