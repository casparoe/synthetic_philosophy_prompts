Bit of an odd request, this one, and I'm not sure it's even the sort of thing you get asked. I'm a journalist — I write longform stuff on science and ideas, moral psychology, the effective-altruism-adjacent world, that sort of thing — and a philosophy department has invited me to a small workshop next month to give a talk defending a claim I made in a piece last year, namely that 'better than' isn't transitive, at least in the cases that actually matter for ethics. The case I use is a spectrum. Start with one person living a life of steady, unglamorous chronic pain, plus a billion others getting on with things. Now imagine a series of five thousand worlds. Each step takes a sliver of the sufferer's pain away — small enough that they'd never notice the difference between one world and the next — and redistributes it across the billion, so thinly that no single person's life changes in any way they could detect or care about; the sliver gets replicated rather than divided, so a billion faint echoes of it now exist where one solid thing used to be. By the end, the sufferer is free of pain, and each of the billion is carrying something so faint it never surfaces. I want to say two things: each step is an improvement on the one before, or at any rate not a worsening, and the world at the end is worse than the world at the start, because a real burden has been dissolved into a billion nothing-burdens and nobody is better off in any way they could name. I want to keep those two judgements seperate and see what's left standing if I do, which I think is transitivity. Thats the talk.

What worries me is the room. It's mostly moral philosophers — value theory and population ethics people, who've seen a spectrum argument before and will be quick to tell me which of my three options I'm really taking — but there are also two decision theorists from the economics side who, I'm reliably informed, will not let a money-pump argument go unmade, an economist who thinks 'better than' is only ever a partial order anyway and that I've manufactured a paradox out of incomparable dimensions, and one person who works on vagueness and is definately going to say the whole thing is a sorites in fancy dress. The bit I genuinely can't defend is why I'm so confident that no individual's loss counts for anything at any step, and I have a nagging suspicion someone will say the intermediate worlds aren't comparable with each other at all and that I've smuggled comparability in at the door.

So: give me a Q&A prep sheet. The objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones I'd get from a general audience, and not the ones from people who've already decided I'm right — ordered by how much damage each does to the thesis rather than by how loud it is, each phrased the way that particular person would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, which objection is the real threat as opposed to the showy one, and the one question I'm hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask. No citations or reading lists please — I know roughly who's said what and a bibliography is no use to me from the lectern. And could you do it in British English, since the whole thing's over there? I've got twenty minutes of talk and the rest is questions, so keep the replies actually sayable out loud.
