I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology and democratic theory, about twenty students. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel, rather than hear, why Condorcet's jury theorem works and why its assumptions carry more weight than its conclusion suggests — above all the independence assumption, which deliberation is built to violate. They should experience three things in sequence without being told what they are: a room of mediocrely informed individuals whose majority is far more reliable than any of them; the same room becoming individually better informed and collectively worse; and a room whose individual competence sits just below the knife edge, where a larger majority is worse than a coin and worse than its own best-informed member. The theorem's name and conditions should not be mentioned until the debrief.

The mechanism I have in mind, which you should improve or replace if you can do better, is a deck of signal cards. The facilitator fixes a binary state (two jars, A and B) and deals every participant a private card generated from a distribution the participants never see. In one deck the card matches the state about 70 percent of the time, independently across participants; in a second deck that same private clue is supplemented by an identical public clue on every card, or read aloud, matching the state about 60 percent of the time; in a third deck the cards match about 40 percent of the time. Each round every participant makes a secret simultaneous bet on the state, and in some rounds the group must also submit one answer after a fixed interval of open discussion. Tell them in advance only that the reliability of the clues varies from round to round and will be disclosed afterwards. Roughly, at twenty participants the first deck should make the majority right about nineteen times in twenty, the second should drag the majority down toward the individual figure, and the third should leave the majority wrong most of the time and less accurate than a single participant — check these expectations yourself and tell me if the deck design needs calibrating to produce the contrast.

Deliver: the setting and a minute-by-minute timing; materials and a procedure for generating the decks from a random number generator, with no statistics required of me; a payoff scheme that needs no real money but creates a genuine pull toward consensus in the discussion rounds; the exact sequence of rounds; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the theorem's parts — independence, the competence condition, and the difference between the majority beating a random member and beating the best-informed member. If time allows, add a coda that replaces the binary state with three and runs pairwise votes, so they run into intransitivity. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the statistical one: three rounds per condition gives too few group-level observations to see the effect, so say how to use the individual-level data instead. Be frank about which features of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce — the limit as the group grows without bound, genuine independence, and any assurance about whether real competence lies above the knife edge — and which the facilitator must therefore supply by argument rather than demonstration.
