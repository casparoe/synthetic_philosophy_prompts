I chair the Standards Committee of the professional association that accredits independent financial advisers in my jurisdiction. Our Code of Conduct provides that a member "shall not knowingly make a statement that is false, or that creates a materially misleading impression." Elsewhere the Code defines a "statement" as any oral or written communication, and calls something "material" if a reasonable client would consider it important in deciding whether to engage or remain with the firm. The Committee meets Thursday to dispose of a complaint, and I need to circulate a short written opinion beforehand.

The facts are not in dispute. During a mandatory annual disclosure call, a client asked the member whether his firm had ever been the subject of a regulatory investigation. He answered: "The firm has never been fined." That is true. The firm was in fact investigated by the regulator two years ago and entered into a settlement; no monetary penalty was imposed, but the settlement included a public censure and a binding requirement to revise the firm's procedures. The client says she took the answer to mean the firm had a clean regulatory record, and that she would have moved her account had she known otherwise. The member says he answered truthfully, was not asked about investigations or settlements, and that she was free to ask a follow-up. One member has taken to calling this "paltering" — using a true statement to produce a false belief — and wants the Code to name the practice explicitly.

The Committee is split four ways, and I want each view met on its merits rather than waved away. One reading holds that "knowingly" governs only the false-statement prong, so the misleading prong requires no intent and the answer violates the Code whatever the member's purpose. A second reading holds that "knowingly" governs both prongs, and that the member did not know his answer would mislead — he knew only that it was incomplete. A third draws a principled distinction between lying and misleading: a lie asserts something false and is a direct breach of trust, whereas a true-but-misleading answer merely exploits an inference the listener draws, and is either a lesser wrong or no wrong at all, since the listener can probe. A fourth holds the reverse — that misleading is the graver wrong, because every word was true, so the victim has no grounds to object and is covertly enlisted in her own deception. A prior Committee decision, which the firm now seeks to distinguish, found a violation where a true statement placed in a footnote left a misleading impression.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate and the Committee will vote on. It should recommend a concrete disposition — violation, no violation, or violation with a reduced sanction — state the remedy and a time frame for it, and give reasons the member and the complainant could each actually respond to. It should work through, at minimum: how "knowingly" ought to be read and why; whether the Code's misleading prong reaches true statements that mislead through conversational implication rather than entailment; whether any genuine moral difference between lying and misleading belongs in the Code's liability standard, in its sanctions, or nowhere; and whether the client's opportunity to ask a follow-up bears on the outcome. Close by saying what the Committee's ruling will commit us to in future cases, and note honestly which reading I am rejecting and the strongest thing that can be said for it.
