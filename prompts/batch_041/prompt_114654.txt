I teach a 75-minute session on rationality and decision theory to roughly 18 second- and third-year undergraduates. They have all seen expected utility theory; none of them has read anything about dynamic choice. I want an in-class trading game that makes them feel the force of the money-pump argument from the inside, and that makes the difference between a loop of choices made at a single time and a sequence of choices made across time feel like a substantive difference rather than a technicality.

Design the game so the point arrives through what happens rather than through what I say. Nothing in the rules, the materials, or the name of any round should use the vocabulary of consistency, transitivity, cycles, or pumping, and I should not have to introduce that vocabulary until the debrief. Every trade a participant makes must be one they have a real, local reason to accept at the moment they accept it.

Constraints and shape:

- No real money. Invent a scrip currency with a modest conversion at the end, or points toward something the class already cares about: small enough that nobody feels cheated, large enough that people care about their holdings.
- It has to work with 18 people in 75 minutes including set-up and debrief. Say explicitly whether everyone trades or a subset does, and how the non-traders stay involved rather than bored.
- The design has to survive the obvious failure. Within two rounds someone will announce that they see the trick and stop trading, and someone else will have memorized the starting bundle. Solve this structurally, through the order of the rounds, who knows what, whether trades are public, and how long the intervening business is, rather than by asking me to be a convincing actor.
- Assume I can use one confederate if the design needs one, and say how to keep that hidden.

The debrief must do all of the following, and you should map each item to the specific moment in the game where it showed up:

1. The pump's dependence on its market assumptions: an always-willing counterparty, costless trades, a guaranteed redemption price. What happens if I charge a small spread, or if the counterparty becomes unavailable at some point? Does that merely hide the inconsistency or genuinely defuse the argument? I want them to see that the conclusion is conditional on the market, not on the preferences alone.
2. The synchronic versus diachronic distinction. In a single session the same person, with the same memory and the same tastes, makes every trade, so what they experience is a loop inside one preference state. A genuinely diachronic pump needs something to change between choices, what the chooser knows, wants, or can do, and it needs an account of why the later self is bound by the earlier self's choices. So build in at least one round where a participant's situation or valuation shifts in a way I control, so that the sequence they get caught in runs across a change rather than around a fixed loop, and make them articulate afterwards what, if anything, the earlier and later selves owe each other.
3. The escapes and what they cost. Refusing to name a price for anything; refusing to be indifferent, by treating two options as neither better nor worse nor equal; committing in advance to a policy and then executing it even when the policy is locally unattractive. Each is available, and each has a price. The game should make at least one of these visibly tempting and visibly costly.
4. If there is time, a second, dial-shaped sequence: every individual step is a small improvement over the participant's current state, no single step is the mistake, and yet the endpoint is worse than the start. Ask them where they would have stopped and why; they should notice that any stopping point is arbitrary.
5. An honest note on what the game cannot show. It cannot show that cycling preferences are irrational as such, only that they are costly under conditions I chose, and it cannot reproduce the argument's generality, which is that the same structure applies whatever the goods are. Be explicit about which of these I have to supply myself.

Deliverables: rules and materials, a payoff and conversion scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, roles including the confederate if any, the debrief with its mapping and its stated limits, and a short section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure.
