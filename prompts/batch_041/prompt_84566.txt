I am a high school student trying to referee an argument in my ethics club. We were discussing a visiting student from a country where tipping is not customary and pedestrians cross whenever the street is clear. In her first week here, she left no tip at a sit-down restaurant, crossed against the light when no cars were coming, and cut into a long line at the cafeteria because she assumed it was just a crowd, not a queue. Two members, Maya and Jonah, disagreed about whether she did anything wrong.

Maya said: "These aren't just local quirks. Tipping, queueing, and jaywalking are the terms on which a society coordinates, and a visitor who takes the benefits -- safe streets, paid service workers, orderly lines -- while ignoring the terms is free-riding. At minimum, she owes locals the courtesy of following visible conventions, even if she doesn't endorse them. Consent isn't the only source of obligation: when you enter someone's home, you follow their house rules even if you didn't vote on them."

Jonah replied: "That proves too much. If convention alone created moral obligation, any oppressive or arbitrary local rule would bind visitors. She didn't consent to these norms, and some of them -- especially jaywalking laws almost no one obeys -- aren't moral rules at all. Tipping is a private transfer, not a duty; queueing is just efficient; crossing an empty street harms no one. We should distinguish morally load-bearing conventions from mere habits, and not demand that outsiders treat the second as the first."

Maya answered: "But the examples aren't alike. Queueing is a coordination convention, tipping is a burden-shifting convention, and jaywalking is mostly a legal convention with little moral content. She can't be bound by all three in the same way, but she is bound by the first two because they affect others' expectations and burdens. She isn't being asked to adopt the norms as her own; she's being asked not to impose the costs of her noncompliance on people who follow them."

Jonah said: "That still assumes the norms are fair. If a tip is expected because employers underpay staff, the visitor is being conscripted into fixing a labor problem she didn't create. And if the queue is just a local preference, cutting in is rude, maybe, but not immoral. There is a difference between breaking a rule and wronging a person."

They asked me to decide, and I couldn't. Please adjudicate point by point: for each move, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and why. Then say which side has the stronger overall position on whether everyday conventions like these are moral obligations, and on what visitors owe locals. I am especially interested in whether tipping, queueing, and jaywalking should be treated differently, and in where Maya and Jonah are talking past each other. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning.
