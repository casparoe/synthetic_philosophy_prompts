Question 3. Slurs and the semantics/pragmatics boundary.

Take a slur term, S. Consider the following bits of data:

(i) 'That S is a thief' comes out as derogatory whether or not the person is in fact a thief.
(ii) 'If that S turns up, I'm leaving' — the speaker isn't asserting that anyone is coming, yet the derogation is still there.
(iii) 'It isn't the case that the S stole the money' — the derogation survives the negation.
(iv) 'He's an S, but I don't mean anything derogatory by it' sounds close to contradictory rather than like a clarification.

Assess this arguement: 'Because the derogation in (i)-(iv) isn't cancellable and survives embedding, it can't be a conversational implicature. Since it's part of the meaning and isn't up for negotiation, it has to be part of the truth-conditional content of S — S just encodes a derogatory predicate on top of its descriptive one. So the semantics/pragmatics boundary is clear enough here: the derogation is semantic.'

Say what the data in (i)-(iv) do and don't establish about where the derogatory content sits, and whether the argument's conclusion follows.

Student's answer:

"The argument's first step is basically right. The data do make trouble for a conversational-implicature account. The decisive point is (iv): conversational implicatures are cancellable, so if the derogation were one, 'he's an S, but I don't mean anything derogatory by it' should read as a clarification, the way 'some of the students passed, but I don't mean to suggest not all did' does. Instead it's contradictory. So the derogation is not merely conversationally implicated. (The embedding in (ii) and (iii) is less decisive on its own, since some conversational implicatures survive embedding, but it's consistent with this.)

The second step is where it comes apart. The argument treats non-cancellability as a mark of truth-conditional content, but the projection data actually tell against that. Content that survives negation and embedding is content that isn't at issue: when we negate 'the S stole the money', the negation is aimed at the descriptive claim, and the derogation is precisely what slips past untouched. If the derogation were truth-conditional and at issue, (iii) is just the sort of case where it would be negated along with the rest. So (iii), which the argument cites in its favour, is really evidence against the conclusion it wants.

Put positively, the data point to a conventional implicature: conventionally attached to the word, non-cancellable, and not part of what is said. But that's inconsistent with the argument's own conclusion. The Gricean notion of a conventional implicature is defined precisely by the fact that such content is not truth-conditional and doesn't affect the sentence's truth conditions. So the argument both misreads the projection data and runs together two categories — conventional implicature and truth-conditional content — that have to be kept apart.

Finally, non-cancellability isn't the clean semantic/pragmatic test the argument assumes. Presuppositions can't be cancelled either — that's the whole point of calling them presuppositions — so showing that the derogation won't cancel only rules out the conversational-implicature view; it doesn't yet tell us we've crossed over into the semantics. So (i)-(iv) get us as far as 'not a conversational implicature, probably a not-at-issue conventional or presuppositional content'; they don't get us to 'truth-conditional'."
