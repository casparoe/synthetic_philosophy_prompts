Please describe the most important implications of the theory of public goods and common-pool resources for the governance of a small, invitation-only online community — a few hundred members, mostly long-time readers of the same blogs and forums — whose shared resource is what I have come to call the presumption of good faith.

Some background, because the details are what make the case awkward. The resource is the standing willingness of members to read one another's contributions charitably and to extend the benefit of the doubt when a claim is underspecified. It is non-excludable: I cannot reserve charitable reading for the members who replenish it. It is not consumed by any single use, since each draw is imperceptible and the harm shows up only in aggregate. And it is, as far as I can tell, unobservable from the inside. Depletion does not present as rudeness; people go on writing in the same courteous register while the reserve empties, and the first unambiguous signal is that a certain kind of member quietly stops posting. By the time that signal arrives, the thing it signals is already gone.

There is a second feature I find more interesting than the first. The obvious institutional remedies — stating the norm explicitly, monitoring who draws on it, sanctioning members who draw too hard, appointing someone to keep the ledger — all require making the resource legible. But a charitable reading that has been made a matter of explicit policy stops being charity; it becomes a service that members can demand and resent, and the announcement that we are tracking who supplies it converts an uncalculated kindness into a trade. So the standard machinery for managing a commons may itself be a large draw on this one.

I want implications I can use, not a list of textbook properties. Assume the community is mine to govern, that my only coercive power is revoking invitations, and that explicit rule-making is expensive in exactly the currency described above. Which of the usual prescriptions for common-pool resources transfer here intact, which break because of the observability problem, and which break because the cure consumes the resource it treats? Where the standard remedies fail, what does the theory suggest instead — indirect indicators, substitutes for monitoring, deliberate slack, some form of subsidy to the replenishers, something else? And which two or three implications should I act on first, with your reasoning for why those dominate the rest? I would rather have four that bite than a survey of the field.
