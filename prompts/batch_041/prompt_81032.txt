Ok, bit of a long one, sorry. I never studied any of this properly — I did a coding bootcamp and I read alot of blogs — but I've had a bee in my bonnet about "the alignment problem" for about two years now and I want to pressure-test the view I've ended up with.

Everyone says "the alignment problem" like it's one problem with one difficulty curve. I think that's basically a category mistake, and that there are at least four things people mean, which only look like one thing because they're always said in the same breath.

First, getting a system to do what you literally asked it to do — where the written-down objective and the actual behaviour come apart, so it games the metric and technically satisfies the goal while missing the point.

Second, getting it to do what you would have wanted if you'd thought harder or known more. That one is about us, not the machine: we can't write down what we want, and the spec is the bottleneck.

Third, the one where the system ends up with its own goals and pursues them against us — the power-grab, treacherous-turn story. This one isn't about our spec at all, it's about what a capable optimizer does when its objective and ours come apart, and I think it quietly assumes something about how the thing is built — that it has a stable objective in the first place, and that it's the kind of thing you can sensibly say wants anything.

Fourth, who decides. Even with a perfect fix for the first three, somebody has to pick the values, and the picking is politics, not engineering.

My claim is that these are genuinely different problems — different failure modes, different evidence, different fixes — and that the conflation is doing real work in the argument: the scariness of the third one gets borrowed to make the first two feel urgent, and any progress on the first two gets read as progress on the third, which I don't think it is.

What I want is for you to try to take this apart. What would have to be true for it to be one problem after all — is there some single formal core (something like "you optimize a stand-in and the real thing slips away") that all four are instances of, and if there is, is the fourth one the odd one out rather than the third? What would progress on one of them actually transferring to another look like, and has anything like that happened yet? If someone insists it's one problem, which of the four do they really have in mind — am I just slicing one thing at a different level of description and calling it four? Sort whatever you give me from "this should make me drop the view" down to "this is a quibble", say which of these a defender of the one-problem picture would themselves concede as a real cost, and be honest about whether my view can be wrong at all. If the four are just four descriptions of one situation, then I've said something true and empty, and I'd rather hear that than have it dressed up. Don't just point me at papers, I want the argument itself.

The one thing that would really move me: a single intervention that reliably improves all four at once, or evidence that two of my four always rise and fall together across real systems. If nothing could move me, say so.
