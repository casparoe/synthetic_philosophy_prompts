Fifteen years of reading rationalist blogs and forums has left me with one durable conviction: a principle that cannot be turned into a procedure somebody can run under time pressure is a mood, not a rule. I am writing because I need a procedure, and because I suspect the relevant philosophy will make my job harder rather than easier.

I chair the member disputes panel at a sixty-unit housing cooperative. The panel is five residents, no lawyers, and I am the only one who reads anything like this material. We meet monthly, hear three to six cases a year, and our rulings bind unless a household appeals to the full board, which happens perhaps once a year. The stakes are people's homes and their standing with their neighbors, and the panel's legitimacy depends on our rulings looking principled rather than personal. I get about forty-five minutes per case before the meeting, and no time to consult anything while the panel is sitting. Whatever you give me has to fit on two pages and be usable by someone who has never heard of any of this.

Here is the recurring shape of our cases, which I gather has a name. Our bylaws contain two commitments I always assumed were compatible and now suspect are not. First, each household is sovereign over its own interior: what we display, what we practice, whom we host, what we keep, what we do inside our own walls is ours to decide. Second, we are a consensus body: a proposal that leaves no household worse off is adopted, and where one option is unanimously preferred to another we take the preferred one. The trouble is that members have preferences about what other members do inside their own households — about a shrine on a balcony, a flag, a home business, a pet, a garden, a stream of guests. When a majority prefers that a household stop doing something self-regarding, the consensus commitment says honor that preference and the sovereignty commitment says it is none of our business. I believe the impossibility result bearing Amartya Sen's name tells me I cannot keep both commitments without some restriction, and that the restriction has to fall on whose preferences we admit in the first place. Build the panel's procedure around that, and be candid that this restriction is the load-bearing move rather than something the bylaws already contain.

Write the procedure as an ordered set of questions the panel asks, specifying for each (a) what counts as a sufficient answer, (b) which answers end the case with a ruling for one side, (c) which answers hand the case to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts, and (d) where the procedure is known to misfire. If it leans on the word harm, make it say what harm means operationally.

I need it to handle the following, in ascending order of how much they worry me:

A test that separates a genuine externality — noise, smell, pests, blocked light, traffic, structural risk — from disapproval of what a neighbor does. The hard case is symmetric: the objection is religious and the conduct is religious, so I cannot simply ask which side is being sectarian.

A rule for when the objecting households are a majority, or a near-unanimity, and are quite sure of their own moral standing. This is where I expect the procedure to tell me things I do not want to hear.

An instruction for adaptive preferences: a household that reports no objection to a restriction I suspect it has simply stopped expecting better than. Say what evidence would move me and what to do when the evidence is inconclusive.

A rule for when the objectionable conduct is aimed at a particular neighbor rather than at the world in general. I think there is a real difference between a symbol and a taunt, and I want the procedure to locate it without turning the panel into a mind reader.

A one-page intake form: the questions we put to both households before the hearing, phrased so a resident can answer them without knowing any of the theory.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing the reasoning at each step.

1. A household has installed a small religious shrine on a balcony visible from the courtyard. Two neighboring households object on religious grounds, saying that the shrine's presence in shared view is itself a religious statement they are being made to live with.

2. A household bakes and sells bread from its kitchen. Deliveries arrive at odd hours, the smell is constant, and the shared wall vibrates. The baker is a friend and the co-op is better for her being here, but I do not think that is relevant and I want the procedure not to care either.

3. A household flies a flag expressing a political position that I and most of the co-op find repellent. The objection is offense, not externality, and I would very much like to rule against it.

I expect the procedure to tell me to leave the flag alone and to let the shrine stand, and I would rather you say so plainly than bend the procedure toward my sympathies. I also expect it to say something I do not anticipate in the bread case.

Close with a short section on what this procedure cannot do: where the actual dispute is over the boundary of the sovereign sphere rather than over what happens inside it, and any case where you think the framework should be set aside altogether. Do not give me a reading list or citations; assume I know the names and want the machinery.
