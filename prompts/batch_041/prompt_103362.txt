I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind, and I am trying to formulate, for a chapter on the affective dimension of pain, the principle that underlies my verdicts about when a pain is bad for the one who undergoes it. My verdicts on a range of cases are as follows. An ordinary tension headache is bad for me, and bad in itself rather than merely because it distracts me. A patient with pain asymbolia, who reports a pain of normal intensity and bodily location but denies that it hurts or bothers her, and who may smile while withdrawing her hand from a noxious stimulus, has a pain that is not bad for her, even though the sensory state is present. A patient given morphine, who says that the pain is still there but no longer hurts, has a pain that is not bad for him, or at most marginally so. A stoic who feels an intense unpleasant pain but has trained himself neither to mind it nor to avoid it still has a pain that is bad for him; his indifference does not remove the badness. A masochist who feels an intense unpleasant pain but seeks it and finds it sexually arousing: I want to say that the pain is bad for him in itself, though the overall experience may be good, and that its badness is outweighed rather than cancelled. A patient with congenital insensitivity to pain, who never feels pain and repeatedly injures himself, does not show that pain is bad; he shows that the absence of pain can be instrumentally bad. A laboratory animal that withdraws from a noxious stimulus but whose species lacks the neural machinery for the affective component of pain: I am inclined to say that there is no pain there to be bad, rather than a pain that is not bad. And a person who reports a pain that is not unpleasant but who is distressed precisely because the pain no longer seems to belong to her body: I think the pain is bad for her, but the badness seems to come from the alienation rather than from the pain itself.

I have a tentative principle: a pain is bad for a subject just in case it is unpleasant to that subject, where unpleasantness is a distinct affective quality that can come apart from the sensory intensity, location, and motivational effects of pain. This principle seems to deliver the right verdicts for the headache, the asymbolic patient, the morphine patient, the stoic, and the masochist, at least if the masochist's pain is bad in itself but outweighed. Where it seems to fail is in the alienation case, where the pain is not unpleasant yet I still want to call it bad, and in cases where the badness of pain seems to attach to what the pain signals rather than to how it feels. I would like you to test this principle against the cases, repair it if it can be repaired, and if it cannot, tell me which of my verdicts I should give up. In particular, I want to know what the best principle predicts about a case I have not listed: a pain that is not unpleasant, not minded, and not avoided, but that is a reliable signal of a spreading infection that the subject will otherwise ignore. Is that pain bad for the subject, and if so, does the badness attach to the pain or only to the infection? Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
