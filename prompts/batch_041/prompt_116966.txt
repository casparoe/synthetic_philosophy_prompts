Two camps have been arguing past each other about weakness of will, and a long exchange has produced more restatement than progress. Call them, for convenience, the time-inconsistency camp and the agency camp. The first holds that every clear case of acting against one's own better judgment is a case of temporally inconsistent preference — the agent's ranking of outcomes shifts as the outcomes come near, roughly in the way a hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discount function implies — and that no further faculty and no distinct phenomenon need be postulated. The second holds that the discounting model captures at most the shape of the behaviour while missing the thing itself: that an agent can, at the moment of acting, hold one course to be better and take another, and be answerable for having done so.

Write the joint statement that these two camps could put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The statement should contain:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. It should include a shared and neutral characterization of the class of cases both sides are trying to explain; what each side concedes to the other about the empirical successes of the discounting model and about the reality of conflict within a single person; what both accept about the ambiguity of the words preference, judgment, and agent; and a statement of what the dispute is not about. Formulate each claim so that it is neither vacuous nor slanted toward one side.

2. A disagreements table. One row per genuine disagreement: the disagreement stated neutrally, then each camp's position in wording that camp would accept for its own view. Keep the rows few, and do not pad the table with disagreements that are really about words.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give (a) the case, argument, or body of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and (b) the prediction each side makes about it, recorded in advance. At least two cruxes should rest on cases that could in principle be settled by choice data; at least one should rest on a case that could not.

4. A falsification section: for each camp, the result or argument that would most seriously damage its position, and whether that camp would on reflection abandon the position; and, if there is anything both sides agree would actually move the debate, what it is.

5. A closing section distinguishing the parts of the dispute that are verbal — turning on how preference, judgment, or agent is defined — from the parts that are substantive.

Material to treat as given, not to be looked up:

- The discounting model represents intertemporal choice by a weighting of future outcomes that falls off faster in the near term than an exponential discount would. One consequence is easy to overlook: an agent so represented is locally consistent. Within any single moment its choices satisfy the ordinary consistency conditions, and the inconsistency lies entirely between moments. The model therefore does not represent an agent who, at one moment, ranks A above B and takes B; it represents a succession of moments at each of which the ranking differs. Whether this is a faithful representation of weakness of will or a change of subject is one of the things the statement must jointly settle.

- The running example, to be used throughout: at ten in the evening an agent judges it better, all things considered, to rise at six and run, and sets an alarm; at six it silences the alarm and stays in bed while reporting that rising is still better. As stated, the case does not distinguish the two accounts, since the evening and morning rankings differ. Both sides should say what further fact about the agent would distinguish them, and should agree in advance which way that fact would cut.

- The demand for commitment: the same agent buys a device that will force it out of bed, or pays to have the option removed. Consider whether this reveals a preference that the time-indexed representation omits — a preference about what the agent's own future choice situation is to be — and whether that counts against the completeness of the model or is representable within it.

- A stipulated three-way division of the apparent phenomenon, which both sides should either accept or reject explicitly: (i) diachronic reversal, in which the ranking at the later time differs from the ranking at the earlier; (ii) synchronic conflict, in which at a single time the agent's all-things-considered judgment favours one course while its contemporaneous ranking of the options favours the other; (iii) non-motivational failure, in which judgment and ranking are both stable but the action does not follow. If the division is tendentious, say so and replace it; if it is accepted, say which grade each side regards as the genuine article and why the others are not.

Do not cite literature, name authors, or supply references; write from the concepts alone. Where you are unsure whether a formulation is one a side would accept, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over.
