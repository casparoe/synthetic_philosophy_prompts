I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a metaphysics course on persistence and identity, and I am trying to work out what I actually believe about how things survive through time, rather than just which textbook position I can recite. In class we have been opposing the view that an object is wholly present at each moment of its existence against the view that an object is a four-dimensional whole composed of temporal parts, with stage theory and indeterminate-identity views hovering at the edges. When I test these against my own case-by-case verdicts, the verdicts refuse to line up behind a single view, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, unifies them, and which of them I ought to abandon.

My verdicts are as follows.

1. A wooden boat has its planks replaced one at a time over fifty years, remaining continuously seaworthy throughout. I say it is the same boat, and moreover that the boat is wholly present on each of those days — there are not fifty overlapping boat-candidates sharing the water.

2. Suppose instead that the removed planks are stored, and that after the final replacement they are reassembled into a second, seaworthy boat. I say the original is the continuously repaired vessel, not the reassembled one. If someone insists the reassembled boat is also the original, I take that to be a mistake rather than a second true answer.

3. A person's memories, dispositions, and character change beyond recognition over forty years. I say it is the same person, and again I want to say the whole person is present at each stage, not that the young person and the old person are merely two parts of a longer thing.

4. An amoeba divides into two daughter cells. I do not think either daughter is the original, and I do not think the original survives as both. I am inclined to say the original simply ceases to exist at the division, though each daughter inherits its history; I am unsure whether to say instead that identity becomes indeterminate.

5. A club has all of its members replaced within a year, retaining the same name, charter, and bank account. I say it is the same club. But a crowd that disperses and reassembles an hour later at the same spot, with the same people, I say is a different crowd.

6. A song is performed twice on different instruments and in different keys. I say it is the same song, even though the song is not "in" either performance the way the boat is in the water on a given day.

7. A bronze statue is melted down and recast as a different statue using all the same bronze. I say the bronze survived and the statue did not, so "same material" and "same object" come apart here.

My tentative principle is this: an object persists just in case its later states are causally continuous with its earlier states in the right way, and the object just is the whole causal-historical entity spanning those states, so that what we call "the object at a time" is really a stage of that entity. But this principle sits badly with verdicts 1 and 3, where I keep wanting to say the boat and the person are wholly present on a given day rather than merely partly present. It sits badly with verdict 7 for the opposite reason, since if an object just is its causal-historical span, it is unclear why the statue's span should count for less than the bronze's. And verdict 6 troubles me because a song survives changes of instrument, which seems to break material continuity altogether.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures 1 through 7 or close to it? Does the principle commit me to one side of the endurance/perdurance dispute, or to something else — stage theory, or a view on which identity is sometimes indeterminate? Which of my verdicts does it force me to overturn, and what reason is there to overturn that one rather than another? Please also state, for whichever principle you favour, what it predicts about two cases I have not listed: (a) an organism that is frozen solid, with all metabolic and neural activity ceasing for a decade, and then revived intact; and (b) two amoebas that fuse into a single cell which thereafter behaves as one organism. Where the principle cannot accommodate one of my verdicts, say so plainly and explain what the repaired principle would require me to give up.
