I'm in a small reading group on the moral emotions, and I've ended up holding a view that the others find provincial, so I'd like you to stress-test it rather than shore it up. The view has two parts. The first is that guilt is the morally better emotion: it takes an act as its object, which makes it answerable to reasons and dischargeable through apology and repair, whereas shame takes the self as its object, which pulls toward concealment, withdrawal, and status management, and so distracts from the wronged party — precisely the attention morality demands. The second part is the one I actually care about: a culture can choose between them. The choice is indirect but real. You change the practices — how wrongdoing is publicly marked, whether the address is to what someone did or to what someone is, whether apology is individual or collective, whether children are told that they are bad or that they did a bad thing — and the emotions follow the practices, the way the load follows the lever. I don't mean that a ministry can decree a feeling. I mean that if you move the practices, the feelings move too, and that this is the only sense in which a culture can be said to choose its moral emotions. I've been arguing about this with a friend from comparative literature who works on early modern Japanese fiction and thinks the whole framing smuggles in a Protestant-liberal picture of the self, and with a psychologist who thinks the arrow runs the other way — that our emotional repertoire is far less plastic than the practices built on top of it.

What I'd like is a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be shown or observed for it to be wrong, sorted by how decisive each consideration would be, including anything from history or anthropology that seems already to have happened and that cuts against me. I want the objections its defenders would themselves concede, not only the ones critics press, and I want an honest verdict on whether the second claim — the one about cultural choice — is even falsifiable as I've stated it, or whether I have quietly built it so that no evidence could touch it, and if so whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind. Skip the citations and reading lists; I can't check them, and I would rather have the arguments on their merits. Write in American English.
