I'm a software engineer by training — distributed systems, nothing to do with any of this — and for the past couple of years I've co-hosted a small interview podcast with two friends from a strategy-and-decision reading group. Next week's guest is a game theorist who has spent most of her career defending a particular line on nuclear deterrence: she holds that the credibility of a nuclear threat has almost nothing to do with whether anyone believes the threatener would calmly choose to launch, and almost everything to do with the threat's leaving part of the outcome genuinely outside the threatener's control. On her account, what deters is the standing possibility that events slip the leash — the risk of loss of control — rather than any settled intention to execute. She is also known for drawing what she treats as the uncomfortable corollary: that measures which reduce the chance of accidental or unauthorized use (hotlines, tighter custody arrangements, more deliberate crisis management) can, at the margin, make a deterrent less credible, so that some of the most dangerous moments in a crisis may also be the most stabilizing. Her stock move when challenged is to insist that a threat whose execution you would never choose is not a broken threat but the only kind there has ever been.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for her, pitched at a serious but non-specialist conversation — I can follow an argument, but I have no formal background in game theory or international relations, and neither do most of my listeners, so the questions should be self-contained rather than assuming we have read the literature. I want the questions to get past having her simply restate the view. In particular, I would like at least one question on why a threat that it would be irrational to carry out can still change an adversary's behavior, and on what exactly is doing the work if not the intention to launch; at least one on the tension inside her own position, namely that the very uncontrolled risk she credits with deterring is also the risk of the outcome we most want to avoid; and at least one on the idea that a stable nuclear standoff can make lower-level, non-nuclear conflict more thinkable rather than less, and how she reconciles that with the claim that nuclear weapons keep the peace. For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising when she takes the expected line. It would also help to have one or two questions pressing on whether her account does any explanatory work at all — whether it predicts anything a cruder story, on which everyone is simply afraid of the bomb, does not — and a closing question about what would actually change her mind.

Please give me the questions numbered, each with its follow-up listed directly beneath it and labeled as a follow-up, and write everything in American English. A one-line note on the purpose of each question would be welcome, but keep the questions themselves as the main event.
