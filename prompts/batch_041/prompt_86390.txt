I need a short research proposal for a term-length, part-time project aimed at a workshop paper, on a topic in the conceptual foundations of interpretability. The topic is mechanistic anomaly detection: the idea that a model's behavior should be flagged when it is produced by a mechanism different from the one that produced it during training, or from the one we took to be driving it, even where the visible outputs are unchanged. My half-formed worry is that the whole notion rests on an unexamined criterion of mechanism identity. Make the criterion too fine — identity of components or of weights — and every retraining, reimplementation, or hardware change registers as an anomaly and the notion is useless; make it too coarse — sameness of input–output function — and nothing is left but behavioral equivalence. I suspect the criterion should be a causal-role one: two structures count as the same mechanism when they occupy the same node in a causal graph that is itself relativized to a task and to a class of permitted interventions, so that "same mechanism" is always relative to a choice of grain and a choice of interventions. The philosophical work I want the proposal to promise is showing either that this relativization can be made precise enough to do real work, or that it collapses into arbitrariness and the technique cannot be rescued as stated. Test cases I want it to lean on: a computation rerouted through different components while preserving its role; a computation duplicated, with one copy causally idle; and a function carried by a distributed code that has no persisting parts across runs.
