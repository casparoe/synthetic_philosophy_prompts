I've spent the better part of fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and the recurring irritation I want your help with is a particular move in those threads: someone drops a well-worn formula as though it closes a question rather than opens one, and the thread then proceeds on the assumption that everyone agrees what the formula means. The formula in question is Berkeley's, that to be is to be perceived — esse est percipi. I ran into it in a sprawling comment thread about whether it makes sense to say that regions of the universe no one has ever observed, the interior of a distant star or the deep past before any observers, really exist, or whether for such things 'exists' is just shorthand for 'would be observed if anyone were there to observe it.' Three people were invoking the same formula for what looked like three different conclusions. One used it to settle the question in favor of the strong claim that unobserved things do not exist and that asking about them is simply confused. A second treated it as obviously true and therefore trivial, no more than the observation that you can only talk about what you can in principle encounter. A third said it was just the observer effect from physics dressed up in Latin. My suspicion is that each of them is latching onto a different thesis, and that at least one of the theses is not Berkeley's at all but a much later idea wearing his name.

What I want is a disambiguation I can carry back into that thread. Lay out the distinct precise theses the formula could be expressing, ordered from the near-tautological to the genuinely radical. For each, I want to know exactly what it commits you to, which word or assumption is doing the heavy lifting — whether 'perceived' means actually perceived by some mind at the moment or merely perceivable in principle, whether 'be' ranges over sensible objects only or over everything whatsoever, whether the perceiver must be finite or may be God — and whether the reading is trivial, a modest but defensible claim, a substantive and contentious metaphysical thesis, or simply false. Crucially, for each reading, say whether it would actually support the move the first participant made, namely that unobserved things do not exist and that questions about them are confused. And tell me which reading the participants most likely meant, and whether that reading is Berkeley's own position or a popular distortion of it.

Please give me the answer as structured data rather than an essay. Return a single JSON object with the following fields. The field readings holds an array, one element per distinct thesis the formula could express, and each element is an object with these fields: label, a short name for the reading; precise_claim, the thesis stated as sharply as you can in one or two sentences; what_it_turns_on, the specific word, scope, or hidden premise that distinguishes this reading from its neighbors; verdict, one of the four categories above, stated in a few words; supports_the_settling_move, one of yes, partly, or no, saying whether the reading would license the claim that unobserved things do not exist and that asking about them is confused; and attribution, noting whether this reading is Berkeley's own, a defensible development of it, or a later idea commonly mistaken for it.
