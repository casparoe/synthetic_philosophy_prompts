Two people in my cohort had a long, increasingly loud argument about Jeffrey conditionalization and I somehow got appointed referee. It started from a toy case one of them wrote on a napkin: a bag with one ball in it, red, blue or yellow, priors 0.5 / 0.25 / 0.25, and two uncertain observations -- the first moves you to credence 0.25 that the ball is red, the second moves you to 0.5 that it's blue, with the rigidity condition assumed for each shift (i.e. each shift leaves your conditional credences inside the cells of the relevant partition alone).

Mira did the arithmetic and says: red-shift first, then blue-shift, lands you at (0.2, 0.5, 0.3) over red/blue/yellow; blue-shift first, then red-shift, lands you at (0.25, 0.5625, 0.1875). Same two observations, different order, different answer -- so Jeffrey conditioning can't be the rule for uncertain evidence. And worse, she says, it doesn't even tell you what to do: it takes your posterior over the partition as an input, so it presupposes the very thing an updating rule is meant to deliver. Her fix is that your evidence is really the observations themselves -- it looked reddish, it looked blueish -- which you can be certain about, so you should just conditionalize on those propositions. Then order stops mattering, because conditionalizing on A and then B is the same as conditionalizing on A-and-B, which is the same the other way round.

Theo says that move makes your own experience infallible and pretends you can never be unsure whether you really saw what you think you saw. He also says conditionalizing on the experience still needs likelihoods -- how likely is it that it would look reddish if the ball were red, blue, yellow -- which you typically don't have, whereas Jeffrey lets you plug in the resulting credence and skip all that. And he says the order-dependence isn't a bug. You can't have two uncertain observations at once; the order is part of what happened to you. In the second order the blue-observation already moved your red credence before the red-observation even arrived, so the two orders just aren't the same total evidence. Each individual shift is impeccable on its own.

Mira came back with: calling the order part of the evidence just renames the update as evidence, and if the total evidence includes the whole sequence of shifts then you owe me a rule for what to do with a sequence, which is exactly what's missing. And on Theo's view, two people who saw the same two things in the opposite order are rationally required to disagree about the ball forever. Theo said they didn't see the same things -- order is part of what they saw -- and that insisting the order is irrelevant is just assuming the shifts commute, which is definately false.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Who's right about the order-dependence, about the charge that the rule presupposes its own input, about the conditionalize-on-the-experience alternative, and about the likelihood point? And say what they're both missing about what 'the evidence' is supposed to be in the first place.
