I am a PhD student in philosophy of science with a background in machine learning, and I co-run a weekly reading group on AI alignment that draws about sixteen graduate students — philosophers, computer scientists, and cognitive scientists in roughly equal measure — for a single ninety-minute session. I have the next session to myself, and I want to run an activity rather than discuss a paper.

The idea I want the group to feel is the "debate" proposal for scalable oversight. It trades a weak judge who evaluates a hard claim directly for two agents arguing that claim on opposite sides of a zero-sum exchange, the judge deciding only which side made the better case. The claim is that a truthful debater can win even when the judge could never have established the truth alone, because the honest side can point to precisely the step at which the dishonest side's argument breaks. I want them to arrive at the pull of this from the inside — to feel why handing a judge two adversaries is supposed to be stronger than handing the judge one answer — before I name the protocol.

Then I want them to feel the other edge. There is a failure mode, sometimes described as the obfuscated-arguments problem, in which the dishonest debater assembles a case that is locally valid end to end, whose sole defect is a step the judge has no independent means of checking; the honest debater's rebuttal then turns on the very same uncheckable step, so the judge, with no more resources than before, cannot tell which side is right. I want the activity engineered so the group first wins by debate and then gets stuck exactly here.

Please design it. I need the setting; the roles and the private information each player holds; a win condition and a scoring scheme that needs no real money; the cross-examination structure and its limits, with a hard cap on statements or exchanges; and a judge whose power is explicitly bounded — a concrete verification budget of checks or lookups it may spend — so that the gap between what the debaters can establish and what the judge can verify is a quantity they feel rather than a slogan. Give me materials I can print, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the concealed piece of information or rule that turns the first round into the obfuscation round. The judge's ignorance should be structural — a budget, not a lapse of attention.

Then a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the parts of the proposal: the judge's bounded power; the zero-sum turn structure; the claim that the truthful debater can win; and the assumption the guarantee actually rests on — that the honest side's winning move is one the judge can follow. If the analogy to complexity classes earns its keep (that direct judging is supposed to reach only a weaker class of problems than debate does), use it, but do not overclaim it.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — where participants will game the rules, where the judge will feel coerced into a verdict, where the twist will land as a gotcha rather than as a discovery — and how to handle each. And be honest about what a live role-play cannot reproduce: name the parts of the actual proposal it cannot stand in for, and tell me what I will have to supply myself in the debrief. No lecture up front; do not hand them the vocabulary until after.
