I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the value of finding things out, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my attitudes express.

The occasion is medical. I am waiting on a test result that will tell me whether I carry a variant associated with a neurodegenerative condition that nothing can prevent, and I have separately been offered a second test whose result I could not act on in any way. My reactions have surprised me, and in trying to defend them to my partner I went looking for what decision theory says about the value of knowing. I found a result usually called Good's theorem — I may have the attribution garbled, so please correct me if so — which as I understand it says that for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, the expected value of costless information is never negative, so such an agent should never pay to be kept in the dark. I see why that is meant to hold: she can always ignore what she learns and act as she would have acted. I am not inclined to think the result is false. But I cannot get my own verdicts to line up with it, and I suspect it is answering a narrower question than the one I am asking.

Here are my verdicts on particular cases.

1. Whether my train is cancelled tomorrow. I want to know, obviously. I would pay a small amount for the information and would resent being kept from it.

2. The result of the untreatable test. I want to know. I would rather have it than not, even though nothing follows for anything I do. If it comes back positive I expect to be miserable, and I still want it.

3. Whether my father, who died in March, read the letter I sent him before he died. I do not want to know. I would pay a substantial sum to be certain I never learn. This is not because I could act on it — I could not act on the test result either — and it is not because he mattered little to me.

4. The sex of my unborn child, in a case where I have no preference either way and knowing would change nothing about the pregnancy or the birth. I would rather not know, and I would pay to avoid being told.

5. Whether my partner had an affair ten years ago that is long over and would not recur. I do not want to know, and I think the wish to know here is a defect of character rather than a reasonable preference.

6. How a novel I am halfway through ends. I would pay to avoid finding out, and I take this to be the same attitude as in 4 and 5 rather than a separate aesthetic quirk.

7. A public prediction of mine that has turned out wrong, where I can still issue a correction and the record is open. I want to know as soon as possible, even though it will be humiliating.

8. The same wrong prediction, where the record is closed and no one will ever check. I am torn, and my inclination reverses from week to week.

What I take myself to be tracking is roughly this: information matters in the first instance because it feeds a choice, and its value in that role is not reduced by the news being bad; but information also changes my experience of things and my picture of my own past, and in that second role it can be worth less than nothing to me. The medical case is what strains the account. No choice hangs on it, so the first clause assigns it no value, and I want it anyway. The only defence I can mount is that a true belief about my own future is something I want for its own sake — which would seem to commit me to wanting to know about the letter, and there I emphatically do not. I cannot tell whether the difference between 2 and 3 marks a real difference in kind (the one concerns what I still have to live through, the other a relation to someone who is gone and can no longer be affected by anything I learn) or whether I am rationalizing a pair of attitudes that a single principle would have to condemn.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, gets all eight of these, or gets the most of them with the fewest moving parts? Where precisely does Good's theorem stop applying to my case — is it the expected-utility assumption, the assumption that the information is costless, or the assumption that the only thing information is for is guiding action? If the theorem needs an amendment rather than a rejection, what is the amendment, and does the amended claim still deserve to be called a theorem about the value of information? Which of my eight verdicts does your principle force me to give up, and what would it predict about two cases I have not listed — one in which I would have to act on the information, and one in which the information concerns a stranger rather than myself?

Please say how confident you are in the principle you land on, and identify the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
