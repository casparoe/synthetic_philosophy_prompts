You are a novelist who is also a careful philosopher — the kind of writer who can let a single abstract puzzle do structural work in a story, shaping the plot, the dialogue, and what the characters are able to see, without the story ever collapsing into a lecture. I would like you to write a short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,800 words, that takes seriously a question in the theory of value: whether the relation better than is transitive — whether a chain of improvements, each step of which is genuinely an improvement over the step before it, must end somewhere better than where it began. Assume your reader is intelligent but has never encountered this problem before. Before you start drafting, spend a few paragraphs thinking on the page about the scenario: who the protagonist is, what each rung of the ladder looks like in concrete human terms, what the two sides of the argument would actually sound like coming out of the mouths of people with jobs and mortgages, and where the story should stop. Then write the story.

Here is the seed I would like you to build on, though you should feel free to develop, complicate, or partly replace it. In the near future, a private clinic sells a service its clients call the Ladder. A client in the middle of some ordinary crisis — a divorce, a diagnosis, the slow collapse of a career — is offered a sequence of small, concrete revisions to their circumstances: a different apartment, a different medication, a different city, a slightly altered relationship, a slightly dulled memory. Each revision is vetted by the clinic's assessment system, which was trained exclusively on human judgments comparing two neighboring options, and which therefore answers one question reliably: is this better than the one just before it? The clinic's charter forbids any comparison between the current option and the client's original situation, and the paperwork is explicit that each step is justified only against its immediate predecessor. Clients who climb the whole ladder tend to end somewhere they would have refused outright on the first day, and not one of the steps along the way is a step they could have objected to.

Write the story from the point of view of someone inside the clinic — a counselor, a technician who maintains the assessment system, or a client partway up the ladder — and let two characters embody the two sides of the dispute. One should hold that transitivity is a condition on rational choice as such, and should have a concrete, unphilosophical way of showing what goes wrong when it fails, the way a broker or a rival clinic might have a business model built on extracting money from anyone whose preferences run in a circle. The other should insist that each neighboring comparison is obviously correct, that anyone can see it, and that the demand for transitivity is an artifact of theorists who never have to live in one of these lives. Do not resolve the puzzle with a tidy moral, do not have anyone deliver a lecture or name a philosophical literature, and do not let the machinery of the clinic become mere decoration — the argument should be visible in how the place works and in what the characters say to each other. End on something concrete rather than a summary. After the story, add an afterword of no more than two hundred words, in your own voice, identifying which link in the chain you take to be the weak one — the local judgments, the demand for transitivity, or the assumption that a life can be ranked against another at all — and saying what would have to be true for the Ladder to be safe to climb.
