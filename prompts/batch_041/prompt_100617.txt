I ended up in a three-hour arguement last night with an old friend from my PhD, who is a card-carrying Hempelian about historical explanation — she thinks the covering-law model was basically right, that historians use laws whether they admit it or not (mostly banal ones borrowed from common sense and the social sciences), and that explanation and prediction really are symmetrical in principle once you stop fussing about practice. I'm on the other side, and I'm roughly Popperian: what historicists take for laws of development are trends, and historical understanding works by reconstructing the agent's situation and what it made sense to do in it, so the covering-law model either collapses into platitude — the generalisation that people mostly do what their situation makes rational — or is just false of the generalisations historians actually cite. We went round in circles for hours, and my suspicion is that half the disagreement is about what historians do, a quarter is about what deserves to be called a law, and the rest is about whether the unity of method that Popper himself endorsed sits comfortably with his attack on historicism, because situational logic looks awfully like a covering-law schema with a rationality principle doing the work of a law, and I genuinely can't tell whether that principle is meant to be an empirical generalisation or a methodological rule for the inquirer.

So before we ruin the friendship, I want the document we should have written instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the covering-law naturalist and the Popperian anti-naturalist about laws in history. Start with a numbered list of claims we can both sign — about what historians actually do, about what has to be true for something to count as a law, about what the disagreement is not (I'd rather not sign anything that says history is unique in a way that just means complicated). Then a disagreements table, one row per genuine disagreement, with four columns: the question at issue; the formulation each of us would accept for our own side, worded so that neither of us is strawmanned; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us; and what we'd both accept as bearing on that crux, with our predictions written down in advance. I'd like at least one row where the crux is the status of the rationality principle in situational logic, and one where it's whether the trend-versus-law distinction is a real distinction or a terminological one — and if you think part of a row is a purely verbal dispute, say so in the relevant cell and say which part. No citations or reading list needed, we both know the stuff; and please keep the whole thing to 900 words maximum including the table, because its going into the reading group doc and I don't want to be the one who blows the page limit.
