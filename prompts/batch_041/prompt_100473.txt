My friend Dan and I got into an argument last night that ran almost two hours and I still dont know who won.

It started with a puzzle he'd read somewhere. Version one: twenty people in a room, some eccentric billionaire tells us that if exactly one of us sends him a telegram by noon tomorrow (sent collect, so it costs the sender nothing), that one person gets a billion dollars. If two or more telegrams arrive, or none do, nobody gets anything. And we're forbidden from talking to each other or making any deals. Version two, which he says actually got run for real by a magazine in the eighties: you mail in a postcard with a number on it, and the number is how many entries you want. The prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries the magazine receives, and one entry gets picked at random to win that whole prize, however tiny it has shrunk. So the more entries you send the better your odds, but the more entries everybody sends the less the prize is worth.

Dan says the whole point of these games is something called superrationality. His line on game one: you shouldn't just send a telegram, you should roll a twenty-sided die and send only if it comes up one, because you have to reason as if the other nineteen are running the exact same reasoning you are. If you're the sort of person who concludes "just send it," so are they, and then all twenty telegrams show up and nobody wins anything. If everyone randomizes, there's a real chance exactly one telegram arrives and somebody actually gets the money.

I told him that's nuts, for two reasons. First, sending dominates not sending: if nobody else sends, I get a billion by sending and nothing by refraining, and if somebody else does send, I get nothing either way, so sending is never worse. Why would I roll a die and sometimes throw the money away on purpose? Second, he's assuming the other nineteen are copies of me, but they're strangers with their own heads, and my decision doesn't make theirs happen. He said that's the same mistake people make about Newcomb-type problems, that the point isn't that my telegram causes theirs but that the same reasoning process is being run in all twenty skulls, so my conclusion is evidence about theirs. I said fine, evidence, but evidence isn't control, and the whole thing only goes through if everyone really is running the same process, which in the real world they aren't.

Then we got to game two and it got worse. Dan said superrationality tells you to roll a die with a number of sides equal to the number of people you expect to enter, and send in exactly one entry only if it comes up one, so that the prize stays close to a million and there's a genuine chance that exactly one person enters. I said that's a fantasy: the recipe needs everyone else to already be playing that way, you don't even know how many players there are, and according to him the actual magazine experiment ended with people scrawling numbers like a googolplex onto their postcards, so the prize rounded down to a fraction of a cent and they couldn't even tell who'd won. He said the fact that people played badly doesn't change what's rational. I said a principle that only does its job when everyone already believes it isn't a principle of rationality, it's a coordination norm, and calling it "rational" is just smuggling in the assumption that everyone is on the same team.

So I want an essay about this. Weigh both cases honestly, Dan's included, and attack my side where it's actually weak instead of setting up a strawman. Then land somewhere: what's the right play in the twenty-person telegram game, what's the right play in the postcard game, and is superrationality a real requirement of rationality, a mistake, or a coordination convention dressed up as one. Also say whether the two games deserve the same treatment or whether the fact that the second one has no fixed roster of players and no ceiling on how many entries anyone can send breaks the analogy between them. Don't wimp out at the end, give me a verdict and defend it. And write it as straight prose like you're emailing me back, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
