I have no academic training in philosophy or theology. I came to the problem of how God's foreknowledge can leave room for human freedom on my own, out of a personal need to make sense of it, and after a long time of thinking it through I have settled on the view that is usually called Molinism, or the doctrine of middle knowledge. I would like you to tell me how exposed that view is.

Here is the position in my own words. Before God decides anything about which world to create, God knows what every possible person would freely choose in every set of circumstances that person could be placed in. These are not guesses or predictions about what someone will do; they are facts about what someone would do, and they hold independently of God's decision to create anyone at all. God uses this knowledge the way an architect uses a survey of the terrain: by knowing what each possible person would do in each possible situation, God can choose a world and arrange its circumstances so that free creatures carry out what God intends, without overriding anyone's will. I hold this because it is the only position I have found that preserves three things at once: God knows the future exhaustively, human beings are free in a sense that really matters, and God is genuinely in charge of how history turns out. Give up any one of those and I lose the reason I care about the question.

I have also added something of my own, and I suspect it is where I am most vulnerable. I could not accept that these would-be choices are true for no reason at all, so I have tried to ground them in the individual nature of each possible person — in what that particular person essentially is, before they exist. On this picture, what I would freely do in any situation is already part of what makes me the person I am. When I have put this to people, they tell me it cannot be right, because it means my choices were settled before I existed and therefore are not really mine. My answer has been that a fact about what I would do is not a fact about what I do, and that being settled in advance is not the same as being compelled. If that answer fails, my fallback is to say that the truths are simply brute: true, with nothing further making them true. I am not sure whether that fallback is a legitimate move or a way of escaping the argument by fiat.

So: what would count against this? I do not want a reading list, citations, or names to look up — I cannot check them, and I care about the arguments rather than the sources. Tell me which observations, arguments, intuitions, or results would tell against the view, including anything from outside theology that bears on it; what the view commits me to that its rivals do not; which objections someone who holds the view would themselves admit count against them, as opposed to which ones only critics press; and whether the position has been qualified so heavily that nothing could count against it, and whether that would be a defect for a view of this kind.
