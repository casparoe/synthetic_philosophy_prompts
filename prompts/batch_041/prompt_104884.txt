At a reading group on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, a participant condensed the whole line of thought into a single sentence — "natural selection shaped our moral beliefs for their fitness, not for their truth" — and then treated it as though it had settled whether we may keep trusting our moral convictions. I doubt that it settles anything, but I could not say at the time exactly which work the sentence was doing, and the harder I press on it the more separate claims I seem to hear.

Separate them for me. What are the distinct, precise propositions that sentence could be expressing, arranged from the nearly analytic to the strongly empirical or metaphysical? For each, say whether it is true, false, or merely contentious, and what it would take to settle it. I am especially interested in the reading strong enough to license the debunker's conclusion — that our moral beliefs are unjustified, or that we have no reason to trust them — and in whether the standard rejoinders engage that reading or only a weaker one: the companions-in-guilt rejoinder, that the same reasoning would debunk logic or mathematics; the byproduct rejoinder, that a reliable capacity might be selected for its effects while tracking the truth only as a side effect; and the charge that the argument proves too much. End by telling me which reading the sentence is most charitably taken to express in this debate, and which weaker reading it tends to collapse into when people repeat it approvingly.
