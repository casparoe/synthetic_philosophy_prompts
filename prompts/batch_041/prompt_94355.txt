You're a philosopher of science who's spent years on underdetermination and scientific realism, so you know all the stock moves. Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses, then commit to one and price it out.

A friend dropped this on me and its been eating my week. (1) A theory is just a machine for churning out predictions, and whatever it posits 'behind' the predictions is its ontology. (2) Two theories that agree on every possible observation are empirically equivalent, and nothing could count in favor of one over the other — anything that could would have to show up in an observation. (3) For any theory you can build a twin that agrees on every observation but swaps the hidden machinery: relabel the states, or bolt on a whole extra layer of structure that no observation can ever reach. (4) By (2) the twin is just as good. (5) So when two theories posit different ontologies but make the same predictions, there's no fact of the matter about which ontology the world has. (6) But this trick works on any theory, so 'the world has ontology A rather than B' is never a fact. (7) That goes double for the parts of the world no observation can reach at all. (8) Yet we say atoms exist and phlogiston doesn't, and that the microstate of a gas is real even though nobody can read it off. So either (6) is false, or all our talk about unobservable ontology is empty.

Where does this go wrong? I'd most like to keep (1) and (3). If the answer is just 'underdetermination', tell me which step that word is supposed to be doing the work at and whether it earns its keep. And I especially want to know whether the verdict changes when the hidden stuff is inaccessible in principle rather than just for now — thats the bit I actually care about.
