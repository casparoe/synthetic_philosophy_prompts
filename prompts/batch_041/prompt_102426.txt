I have spent the better part of a term in a decision-theory reading group arguing about the Allais paradox, and I have come to accept that the group will not converge on whether the characteristic preference pattern — preferring a sure moderate prize to a lottery, then preferring the higher-payoff lottery once a common independent component is added to both — is irrational. What I want from you is not a verdict but a number with its skeleton showing. Specifically: what is your credence that the independence axiom, understood as the requirement that an agent's preference between two lotteries be undisturbed by the addition of an independent common consequence to both, is a genuine requirement of practical rationality rather than an artifact of a particular formalization or of an intuition we happen to share? Give me the number, then decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on — the probability that expected-utility maximization is partly constitutive of rationality, the probability that some risk-sensitive alternative captures the normative facts better, the probability that the Allais intuition is a framing or presentation artifact, and whatever else you think belongs — with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, and an explicit account of how the terms combine.

A colleague has offered two hypotheses I cannot decide between and would like you to treat as live sub-questions rather than dismiss. The first is that the paradox trades on a conflation between a constraint on a preference relation and a constraint on a choice function, and that once the two are kept apart the Allais agent is perfectly coherent; the second is that an agent who is permitted to have preferences over whole decision strategies, rather than over single acts considered one at a time, may be not merely permitted but required to exhibit the pattern. She also suspects that the common-consequence and common-ratio versions of the paradox deserve different rational verdicts, because only one of them can be underwritten by a coherent attitude toward risk. I would like a probability attached to each of these being correct, propagated into the final figure. I am additionally worried that the question may not be well-posed — that the notion of a requirement of rationality may lack determinate content — so part of what I am asking for is your probability that there is a fact of the matter here at all.

For what it is worth, my own credence is around 0.45 and hers is around 0.8. So tell me which single sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in, which term your own number is most sensitive to, and what argument, theorem, or conceptual distinction would move it most; I would much rather have the weak point marked than a confident-sounding conclusion.
