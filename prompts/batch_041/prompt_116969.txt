I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so what I want out of this is an argument rather than a survey. Karl Jaspers died in 1969. His short book, usually translated as The Question of German Guilt, grew out of lectures he gave to German students in 1946, and its central move is a four-way distinction: criminal guilt, for deeds punishable under law; political guilt, borne by all citizens of a state for what that state does in their name; moral guilt, before one's own conscience; and metaphysical guilt, before God, resting on the solidarity of all human beings and on the fact of still being alive when others were killed. Before you use them, restate the four in a line each in your own words, so I can see you have hold of them.

Then treat Jaspers not as a time traveller but as a careful reader of the last fifty years, and tell me what position he would most likely have taken on three things that could not have been on his agenda. Give me the position and the argument from his own premises, not a menu of possible readings; where his commitments pull against each other, say which one you think would win.

First: German reunification. The population of the former East Germany, whose state presented itself as the antifascist heir and therefore as having no continuity with the Third Reich, was absorbed into the very political community Jaspers had declared politically guilty. Does his account of political guilt carry over to them, or does it imply they stand outside it?

Second: climate change. A generation that is neither perpetrator nor victim, but heir to an industrial order that will harm people not yet born, and that benefits from the harm without having caused it in any ordinary sense. Can metaphysical guilt run forward in time toward future victims rather than backward toward past ones?

Third: the campaign, now conducted by people who were not alive during the crimes, to reverse the country's memory policy and stop treating the past as a standing obligation. Is that a moral matter in his scheme at all, or only a political question about what a state should teach its children?

For each case, say which of the four categories it falls under, which category he would have to refuse to apply, and whether his framework is being applied or stretched past its limits. Close by naming the single feature of his view that does most of the work in your answers. No bibliography, page references, or quotations I cannot check; if you are unsure what he actually wrote about something, say so and argue from the position rather than inventing a source.
