For the past few weeks a friend and I have been circling a deceptively simple question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? She insists there must be some shared essence running through all the wildly different 'a's one encounters across typefaces and handwriting, while I suspect the category is held together by something looser, and our argument keeps stalling because we are each silently picturing different examples. I would like you to design a single case that settles the matter by construction rather than by appeal to whichever examples happen to come to mind. Build me a small set of letterforms — described in words, since we are working without images and I want the design to be checkable by someone who cannot see it — engineered so that three theories of the category deliver three different, pre-specified verdicts: the classical view, on which membership is fixed by necessary and sufficient features; the prototype view, on which membership is a matter of similarity to a central or best example; and the family-resemblance view, on which members are linked by overlapping similarities with no single feature common to all. The set must include at least three shapes that any ordinary reader would unhesitatingly call 'a' even though no single feature is shared by all of them, and at least one shape that is a genuine but marginal 'a' — the sort of thing a reader would accept while feeling it is somehow not quite right. Hold everything else fixed across the set: same stroke weight, same x-height, same overall style-grid, so that the only variable changing from shape to shape is the one you intend to test, and state plainly which variable that is.

Then add a second layer: one case of context-dependence, in which a single shape's status flips when the surrounding letters change while the shape itself is left untouched, so that the neighborhood is the only thing doing the work. After presenting the scenarios, walk through what each of the three theories says about each shape and why, and identify precisely which feature of your design is responsible for the disagreements. Then run the checks: say what happens to each verdict once that feature is removed, and how a defender of whichever theory fares worst would attack the case — either by denying that a given shape is what you claim it is, or by arguing that the case has quietly stipulated its verdicts rather than generated them from the theories. The purpose here is adjudication, not illustration, so a case that simply announces its conclusions will not do: the verdicts have to fall out of the theories themselves, and if any of my three specifications cannot be met simultaneously, tell me so and explain which one gives way.
