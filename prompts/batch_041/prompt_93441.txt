Every spring our art department clears out the gym and hangs a one-night show — any student who wants a piece on the wall gets one, so it runs from the freshmen's first charcoal smudges to the seniors' portfolio work — and this year, because I have been in the art room longer than anyone else, the teacher has asked me to give the opening remarks. I have been trying to write them and I keep running into something philosophical. For three years, whenever any of us has asked whether a piece is really any good, she has answered that you cannot argue about taste, that a response to a painting is simply something that happens to you, and that all she can honestly teach is technique. I have never quite believed her. Some of what I now love I came to love only because someone I trusted stood me in front of it and told me to look again, and I do not think I was merely flattered or worn down into it — I think I learned something true about the work. Yet I do not want to stand up in front of a gym full of people and announce that my way of seeing is the correct one. Please write the speech for me, about four minutes long, that holds both of these together: one that says something real about why these particular pieces deserve an audience's attention while staying honest about what my own response to them can and cannot vouch for. It has to land for two audiences at once — the art students and teachers, who will notice immediately if I am fudging, and the parents, most of whom have never thought about any of this and mainly want to be moved and to be proud of their kid. The philosophy has to do the actual structural work of the speech, deciding what I may say about a work's value and how I may say it, rather than serving as a clever aside. When you are finished, add a brief note to me, separate from the speech, telling me how confident you are in the positions the speech commits me to and naming the single point in it you are least sure of.
