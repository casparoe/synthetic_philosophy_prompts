You are a professional philosopher who also writes satire, and you are good at making a piece land for readers who have never opened a philosophy book. Before you draft, think carefully for a moment: work out, briefly, the two or three real distinctions at stake below and how each one would get flattened into coaching-speak. Keep that part short, then write the piece.

I have no academic training in philosophy. This is something I have been working on by myself, from examples, and I want a satirical piece built on the examples rather than a lecture that uses them as illustrations. I have also deliberately not looked up whether any of this has a name; please do not let the piece depend on naming a theory, on a reading list, or on anything I would have to go and read.

Here is what I keep noticing. There are sentences that are perfectly capable of being true and still cannot be said by the person saying them:

- The 7:40 is cancelled, but I don't believe it is.
- The 7:40 is cancelled, but I don't know that it is.
- I believe the 7:40 is cancelled, but it isn't.

Say any of these out loud and something goes wrong — not with the world, but with the saying. What convinces me that this is not a plain contradiction is that the world could be exactly as described: the train is cancelled and I am in denial about it. Both halves can hold at once, and yet I cannot assert the whole thing, here, now, in my own voice.

My own guess about what is going on: when I say the first half, I am not reporting on the inside of my head, I am putting the cancellation forward as something to be accepted. "I believe the train is cancelled" is not like "my knee hurts"; it is mostly a timid way of saying the train is cancelled. So by the time I reach the second half I have already done the thing that half denies, and the second half tries to take back, in the same breath, what I just put forward. The state is fine. The utterance is a performance that cancels itself.

Three things I checked, because I wanted to know whether I was imagining it:

1. Past tense is fine. The 7:40 was cancelled, but I didn't believe it was — an ordinary sentence, as is: I thought it was cancelled, but it wasn't.
2. Third person is fine. He thinks the 7:40 is cancelled, but it isn't, and: she says it's cancelled, but she doesn't believe it. Both sound normal to me.
3. Ordinary exclamation gets in the way. "I don't believe it!" usually means "I'm astonished," not "I lack the belief." So a sentence can be wrecked by the tone of one phrase inside it, which seems to be a different kind of trouble from the one above.

Two I have not cracked:

4. First person plural. The 7:40 is cancelled, but we don't believe it is. Odd, I think — but odd in the same way as the singular, or in a different way? If I am one of the "we," have I not put my own belief forward and then denied it?
5. Add-on clauses. The 7:40 is cancelled, but I don't believe the timetable seems fine, and so does: but I doubt it. So the trouble is specifically the bare denial of belief about the very thing just asserted.

Now, what I want the satire aimed at. There is an entire industry — workplace communication training, "sincerity" and "alignment" coaching, internal comms policies — that has noticed something in this neighbourhood and drawn exactly the wrong lesson from it. The pitch is always that contradictions are poison and must be scrubbed out, and the fix is always a change of wording. Which means such advice will happily "repair" a sentence that was never a contradiction, and cannot tell the difference between a sentence that is false, a sentence that cannot be said, and a sentence that is both true and unsayable — the three piles I keep sorting my examples into.

So: write me a mock advice column of the kind that runs in a lifestyle magazine or on a coaching brand's blog, with a title. The letter is from a reader with a domestic problem that is really the puzzle above: their partner has started speaking this way and insists he is not contradicting himself, and the reader wants to know who is right. The reply is from the coach — an invented figure, not a real person, with his own method and his own vocabulary. Roughly 650–750 words in total: the letter about 200, the reply the rest.

Rules for the reply, because this is where the piece lives or dies:

- The coach must be good at his own job. He is not a fool and not a straw man: confident, warm, quotable, with a system that works well enough to have paying customers. The comedy has to come from a competent system meeting examples it cannot absorb, not from him being stupid.
- He must not use a single piece of academic vocabulary — no talk of pragmatics, assertion, sincerity conditions, self-knowledge, performatives, paradoxes. Everything he understands about this has to come out in self-help idiom (gut, alignment, ownership, clean sentences, saying what you mean), and those idioms have to be doing real analytical work rather than decorating. Someone who knows the territory should be able to see that his distinctions map onto the real ones and are merely wearing the wrong clothes.
- His advice must be internally consistent with his brand. Every fix he proposes should follow from his own stated rule, and the failures should be the rule's failures rather than slips of the pen.
- He should get at least one thing genuinely right, so that the reader cannot simply dismiss him.
- Deadpan throughout. No winking, no narrator, no author's note telling us what to think, no character announcing the moral. A reader should be able to tell what is being sent up without being told.

Do not reuse the train; invent your own domestic examples, and feel free to use the two puzzles I have not cracked as material.
