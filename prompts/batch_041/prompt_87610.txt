I'm an undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of probability, and we have just spent two weeks on the reference class problem. I keep noticing that the problem, which in class looks like a puzzle about which class a probability is relative to, is settled every day by an institution that has to put a number on it: the insurance industry. I would like you to work out what that practice presupposes.

Here is the practice as I understand it. When an insurer prices a policy, it does not compute a probability for me as an individual. It assigns me to a rating class — for auto insurance, typically defined by age, territory, marital status, the vehicle, my prior claims, and in many states a credit-based insurance score, which some states restrict or forbid; for life insurance, by age, sex, smoking status, and medical history — and then charges everyone in that class the same base rate. On top of the class rate it layers individualized adjustments: a discount for a clean record, or a surcharge or discount from a telematics device that watches how I actually drive. Health insurance under the Affordable Care Act is the sharpest case, because the law forbids insurers to rate on health status, medical history, or sex at all, and permits only age (within a compressed band), tobacco use, family size, and geography. So the law is not merely choosing a reference class; it is mandating one, and forbidding others that would predict an individual's costs better.

What I want to know is what this whole arrangement presupposes. Start with the theory of probability: does class rating presuppose a frequency view on which the probability of my crashing just is the frequency in my class, so that there is no further fact about my own chance for the practice to be right or wrong about? Does it rule out a single-case propensity view, or can a propensity theorist underwrite the same practice and merely add that the class rate is a good estimate of the individual chance? Does the practice presuppose that there is a uniquely correct reference class that underwriting is trying to find, or does it presuppose the opposite — that the class is chosen, and that the choice is practical and moral rather than a statistical discovery? Then turn to the ethics: the ACA's community-rating rules seem to presuppose that the reference class is a matter of fairness and solidarity rather than accuracy, and that it is legitimate to forbid a class even when it predicts better. Is that consistent with the insurer's own actuarial logic, or does the practice rest on two incompatible pictures of what a premium is for?

I would like the answer to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to say where it is philosophically underdetermined — where several theories would license exactly the same rates. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the institution if we replaced the presupposed view with its rival: if we took the individual's own chance seriously as the thing to be priced, what would underwriting, and the very idea of insurance as pooling, have to become?

Please structure the response as follows, in this order:

1. "The practice, restated" — about 150 words, just to confirm you have the institution right.
2. "The implicit theory of probability" — about 400 words, reconstructing what view of probability, and of the individual's chance, the practice presupposes.
3. "Strict presupposition versus comfortable fit" — about 300 words, distinguishing what the practice cannot do without from what it merely suits, and naming the rival views it quietly rules out and those it leaves open.
4. "The two rating logics" — about 300 words, on whether actuarial fairness and mandated community rating presuppose consistent things or pull apart.
5. "If the presupposition were replaced" — about 300 words, on what would change under an individual-chance view.
