I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a course on practical reason and value theory, and I'm picking a topic for a term paper that I also want to use as a writing sample. The course has given me the standard map of the terrain -- two options are ranked, equal, or incomparable -- but it doesn't fit the cases that actually stall me, and I want to write about that gap. In those cases the pair of options is fixed and my ranking of them seems to move with me: the same two careers look commensurable on a Tuesday, when I'm in one evaluative mood, and incommensurable on a Thursday, when I'm in another. That has pushed me toward the thought that incommensurability may not be a relation between the options at all but a feature of the agent's current evaluative repertoire -- of which distinctions and covering values the agent can bring to bear -- so that two agents, or one agent at two times, can stand in different comparability relations to the very same pair of options. I'll call this repertoire-relative incommensurability, and I want to know whether it can be made coherent, and if so what it displaces in the standard picture. If it survives, it seems to have awkward downstream consequences: hard choices might be evidence about the agent's development rather than about the values, and the usual move of resolving a hard choice by "discovering" a covering value would look less like discovery and more like acquiring a new instrument of comparison.

Assume the constraints are real: one term, one paper, no experiments, and a supervisor who works in value theory generally but not on this particular idea, so I'll be largely on my own with it. Which open questions in this area most deserve my time, and in what order should I take them? I care less about the inventory than about the ranking: for each, how much other work in the area depends on it, how tractable it is for someone with my constraints, and how neglected it is. Where a question could move up or down the list, say what argument or result would move it.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else. Each item should name one open question and, in a sentence or two, defend its position in the ordering -- including, where relevant, what would change it. No introduction, no conclusion, no closing summary.
