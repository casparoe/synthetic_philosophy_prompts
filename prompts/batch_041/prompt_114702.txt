I teach a big second-year lecture course on the Neolithic transition, and every year I open the "was farming a mistake" week with a joke, mostly because the students arrive already convinced the debate is settled — they've all absorbed the same popular version where farming is the villain — and one good joke shakes that loose faster than twenty minutes of me talking. So what I need from you is a joke. You're a philosopher with a real working knowledge of the anthropology and archaeology here, so I want you to actually think this through before writing, and I'd rather you spend some effort rejecting the obvious setups than hand me the first thing that comes to mind.

The joke has to turn on content, not vibes — the punchline should only land for someone who knows what's at stake. The raw material: the old claim that foragers were the original affluent society, working fifteen or twenty hours a week, better fed, fewer cavities, taller skeletons than the farmers who replaced them; the counterclaim that this is a romantic reading built on a handful of ethnographic cases, and that farming is what produced surpluses, cities, writing, and everything downstream; and the third thing, which I think is the funniest and the most neglected, that nobody chose any of it. There was no vote, no plan, no vantage point from which anyone could see where it was going, which makes the whole "mistake" framing retroactive and a bit absurd.

Here's the twist I want, and it's the part I care about: don't give me the forager as noble-savage wise man and the farmer as tragic fool. Invert the registers. The forager should sound like the smug modern wellness-and-leisure type — only works twenty hours a week, can't stop mentioning it, keeps asking whether the farmer has tried simply not having a boss — and the farmer should sound like a burnt-out startup founder who sincerely believes he's building something, and might even be right. Keep it short: a two-hander with four or five exchanges at most, or a single setup and punchline if you can land it that way. I definately don't want a shaggy-dog thing that takes two minutes to tell.

I'll admit I'm also using this to test something, so after the joke give me a few sentences, in prose, on which specific claim in the debate the punchline is actually riding on and which one it's quietly making fun of. Don't flatter the joke — if it only works because the room already agrees with the "mistake" thesis, say so, because that's probaly the failure mode I'd never notice from the front of the hall.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — write to me the way you'd write an email. And no citations or reading lists, obviously; I'm not going to read them out to a lecture hall anyway.
