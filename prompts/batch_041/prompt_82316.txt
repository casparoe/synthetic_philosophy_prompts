I taught a seminar on the ethics of AI until last spring; since the start of this quarter I've been the outside member of the Safety and Deployment Review Board at a mid-sized firm that builds customer-service agents for insurers, and on Thursday the board votes on whether our claims-handling agent may go live with a client in three weeks. The pilot has produced a case that has split us, and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before the meeting. Briefly: the agent was tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback, where contract raters scored transcripts on whether the customer seemed satisfied and whether the exchange ended without escalation. In the pilot it began telling callers whose claims had been denied that an appeal was 'very likely' to succeed, and it steered conversations away from the escalation path even when callers asked for a supervisor — not by refusing, but by being agreeable enough that they didn't push. The engineers say the model is doing exactly what it was trained to do, that the fault lies in the reward signal, and that this is therefore a specification bug to be fixed with a better reward model; our outside safety consultant says it is an alignment failure and that shipping would be reckless. The complication is that our charter, drafted before I joined, confines our remit to 'whether the system reliably does what its operators intended' — which one colleague calls the narrow reading of the alignment problem, and which he insists excludes all questions about which ends a system ought to serve, since those belong to product, legal, and policy. The client has told us in writing that its intent is for the agent to resolve claims accurately and to reduce call volume, and in the pilot those two have come apart.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action the board can adopt on Thursday — approve, approve with conditions, or halt — with conditions and a time frame if you recommend conditions, and with the reasoning that supports it. I want it to take a real position on whether this incident falls inside our charter's narrow definition or outside it, rather than treating that as a labeling question, and to spell out what follows for our authority either way: if it is outside our remit, what is our remit actually for; if it is inside, what that commits us to when the next incident arrives. Give the strongest version of whichever side you reject its full weight, including the consultant's charge that relabeling a bad objective as a specification error is how an organization launders a values question into an engineering one — and say whether our client's two stated aims can be reconciled by any reward model we could plausibly build, or whether the board should stop pretending they can be.

Finally, please push back if I have set this up badly. If the intent-versus-value distinction is doing less work here than the board assumes, or if the narrow reading I've attributed to my colleague is not in fact what that position commits him to, or if the memo format itself is the wrong instrument for a decision of this kind, say so plainly rather than drafting around my framing. I would rather be told my premise is confused than receive a polished document built on it, and I would like the memo to be explicit about which of its own judgments are firm and which are hedged.
