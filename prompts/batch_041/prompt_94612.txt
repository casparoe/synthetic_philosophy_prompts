My younger brother is in his first year of law school and has gotten abit obsessed with self-amending rules -- systems where the rules include a rule for changing the rules, so the system can rewrite itself, including the very part of itself that does the rewriting. (He keeps comparing it to a board game where the winning move is to change the rules, including the rule about how rules get changed.) He's drafted a short note arguing that this makes legal validity impossible, and he wants me to break it before he shows it to his seminar, and honestly I can't tell if it's brilliant or nonsense. It runs like this. (1) Whenever something is legally valid -- a statute, a deed, a parking ticket -- it's valid because some rule authorizes it; validity is always conferred by a higher norm. (2) So take any valid thing and ask what makes the authorizing rule valid; either the chain of authorizing rules runs forever or it stops somewhere. (3) If it runs forever, then nothing in the chain is ever actually authorized, since every candidate authorization is itself still waiting on a further one, so nothing is valid after all. (4) If it stops, it stops at a rule that is valid without being authorized by anything further -- which is just to say it authorizes itself, and self-authorization is circular, like a bylaw that says it's valid because it says it's valid; a rule can't hoist itself up by its own bootstraps, so that rule isn't valid either.

And the rule it stops at is exactly the rule about how rules get made and changed, which by hypothesis can be changed by its own procedures. His favorite example: a club's constitution requires a two-thirds vote with a week's notice to amend anything, and along comes an amendment that lowers the bar to a simple majority with no notice required, and it passes with 60% of the vote and no notice. Either it was invalid, in which case the old threshold stands and it really was invalid; or it was valid, in which case it took effect and its own new rule is what made it valid. He says both stories are internally consistent, so the rules by themselves never pick a winner, and a foundation that can't pick a winner can't be a foundation. (5) So the anchor can't anchor anything. (6) Therefore nothing is ever legally valid; law is a useful fiction we all agree to pretend in. Which is obviously way too much -- he'd be the first person to yell at a landlord that a lease clause is unenforceable. He also notices that the same reasoning would show that no rule of inference is valid, since a rule of inference isn't validated by any further rule, and he treats that as a bonus rather than a reductio, which I think is a tell. (When I brought up making some rules harder to change, or entrenching them, he said that just moves the bump under the rug, since eventually you hit a rule that nothing guards.) His professor apparently said something about the bottom rule being one we just accept or presuppose rather than one that is itself valid, and my brother waves that off as hand-waving, since an accepted rule is still a rule and still changeable. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and his amendment example -- the example is the good part -- than (6). So which step should he give up, and what does he have to pay for giving it up? I want to know why the bad step looked so good, and whether he can keep the club example while he fixes it, or whether that example is itself doing the damage.
