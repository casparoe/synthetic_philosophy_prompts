I am assembling a specification for a theory of biological function, and I want the desiderata rather than a tour of the literature: what must any adequate account of what it is for a trait to have a function be able to deliver? Treat the two live families — the etiological, selected-effects accounts, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and the causal-role accounts, on which a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the system that contains it — as candidates to be tested against the list, not as the subject of it. For every requirement you state, give the case or consideration that forces it, and separate the requirements that any account must meet from those that merely encode one camp's prior commitments.

The list should at least confront the following, and should say for each what it demands of a theory: a trait fixed by drift that now incidentally promotes survival but was never selected for doing so; a novel variant that confers an advantage on its first carriers before selection has had any chance to act; a trait selected for one effect that now reliably produces another; a vestigial organ whose original effect is gone; a trait that is harmful, so that function must be separable from health or proper working; a nearly neutral trait whose contribution to fitness is real but whose persistence is governed by drift rather than selection; two systems whose parts have identical causal roles but different histories, and two whose histories are identical but whose roles differ; and function talk about items with no history of selection at all — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an organism that assembles by chance, or a trait in a population with no heritable variation. Say also whether the theory is to cover only evolved biological parts or also artifacts and their components, and what follows either way.

I most want two things. First, whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular whether the demand that function be a matter of selection history can coexist with the demand that functions be ascribable to novel, drift-fixed, or historyless items; if it cannot, say which demand should give way and why. Second, which single requirement eliminates the most otherwise attractive accounts. Close with a short assessment of how the etiological and causal-role families fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
