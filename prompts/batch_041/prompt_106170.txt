I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly — and I've been circling a piece on sortition for the better part of a year. I've read the citizens' assembly stuff, Ireland, France, all the deliberative-polling experiments, and the weirdly cheerful literature arguing we should replace elections with random selection (some people call it lottocracy). Here's my problem: none of it lands on the page. Readers nod and think "oh, jury duty for policy, cute." The actual argument is sharper than that and I can't get it across in prose. So I've agreed to run a 90-minute session at a small ideas festival — 18 people, mixed ages, no particular expertise, a plain room — and I want the thing to be something we *do*, not a panel I moderate. Then I'll write up what happened.

What I want those people to feel in their gut, in this order, before I say one word about it:

First, that elections don't just aggregate preferences, they *select for a kind of person* — the kind who wants the job, who likes being watched, who can afford to spend two years campaigning. That's a bias baked into the inputs, and no campaign-finance tweak removes it. Second, that swapping the election for a lottery fixes exactly that problem and instantly hands you a different one: a body pulled at random has no institutional memory, no reason to be competent, and — the part I keep failing to explain — nobody to be accountable to, because you can't vote them out. If the whole lottocracy pitch is going to work, it has to solve *that*. I want them to walk into both walls themselves, in that order, and I want them to notice that the two fixes pull against each other.

So: design me the activity. Give me the rules, the materials (assume paper, a hat or a randomiser, a whiteboard, nothing fancier), and a payoff scheme that makes winning and losing mean something without real money changing hands — tokens, points, a pot they split, a treat someone does or doesn't get, whatever works, as long as there's a genuine winner and a genuine loser in the room. I want roles or rules that do the work, not a lecture. Please arrange it so the group runs into the first failure (the self-selection of candidates) under one selection rule, and the second (no memory, no accountability, capture by whoever talks most) under the other, and so that the second one stings precisely because the first one was just fixed. Don't name sortition, lottocracy, or citizens' assemblies until the debrief — if the word "lottery" has to appear early, fine, but nothing that tips my hand.

Include a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and tell me honestly how long each beat really takes rather than the optimistic version. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the specific ideas — descriptive vs substantive representation, why the sample-size argument needs hundreds of people and not eighteen, rotation as a substitute for re-election and what rotation costs you, the difference between using the lot to fill an office and using it to decide a policy — and give me the exact questions to ask so the group finds these rather than hears them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, what I'll be tempted to do, and what to do instead. And be straight with me about which part of the real argument this activity *can't* reproduce no matter how I run it, i.e. the thing I'll have to say out loud myself in the last ten minutes.
