I am a novelist, and I need help with a scene: two mathematicians, once collaborators, meet for dinner after twenty years and fall into an argument about a proof.

The first has spent those years on combinatorial games and is exhilarated by the strategy-stealing argument. In Hex — two players alternately claim cells of a rhombus of hexagons, each trying to link their own two opposite sides — one can prove the first player wins without producing any winning strategy. If the second player had one, the first could open with an arbitrary move and then play the second player's strategy, treating the extra stone as harmless; in Hex it is harmless, since your own stones never obstruct your own path. So the second player cannot win; and since a full board always connects exactly one player's sides, the first player must win. My character calls this knowledge of a fact about the game, obtained without a witness.

The second has drifted into constructivism and thinks the proof is hollow. He grants that it is valid where its premise holds, but says the premise — that an extra move can never hurt — carries all the weight: it is obvious in games we already understand, and in games where having the move is a liability the same form of argument would deliver a false verdict. And a proof that a winning strategy exists, in a game where no one has the faintest idea what that strategy looks like, gives a player nothing to do.

Write their exchange, a page or so, as a real argument between two equally sharp people: each must engage the other's actual point, each must concede something genuine, and neither may be a mouthpiece. Let them end on the question of whether it can be a fact about a game that the first player wins, when nobody will ever be able to say how.
