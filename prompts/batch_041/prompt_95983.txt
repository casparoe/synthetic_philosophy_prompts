I teach a graduate seminar on translation and formal constraint, and this term I want to build the final weeks around a single case study: Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life and Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, the film made from it. The framework I want the class working with is the one Douglas Hofstadter develops in Le Ton beau de Marot, where a short French poem by Clément Marot is translated dozens of ways, the versions varying in which features of the original they hold fixed — sense, rhyme, meter, syllable count, register — and occasionally obeying arbitrary extra rules imposed just to see what happens. The moral I want the class to take from the book is that translation is a species of constrained writing: the translator must decide which features to preserve and treat the remainder as slack, so fidelity is not one relation but a menu of them, and the interesting question is always which features a given translator chose to hold fixed and why.

My reading of the pair — which I would like you to test rather than assume — is this. The novella's central formal device is a narration addressed to a daughter who does not yet exist, in which the future is delivered with the same intimacy and specificity as the past and the prose gives the reader almost no help in sorting the two, so that the reader is made to occupy something like the heptapods' relation to time. The film cannot do that; it disguises its future scenes as memories and then reclassifies them, converting a standing condition of reading into a one-time revelation. Meanwhile the story's intellectual spine is the variational principle in physics — light taking the path of least time, a law that looks teleological rather than causal — and that is what makes the heptapods' physics and their language intelligible together; the film drops the argument entirely and substitutes a geopolitical countdown whose resolution turns on a phone call. There is also an asymmetry I find interesting: prose is a linear medium that must render a two-dimensional, non-sequential script in words, while film is a spatially simultaneous medium that can simply show the logograms but cannot show their grammar. Each medium is handicapped exactly where the other is at ease.

What I want from you, then: first, whether the film is better described as a translation or as an adaptation, and whether that distinction survives contact with Hofstadter's account. Second, an inventory of what the film held fixed and what it treated as slack, sorted into departures forced by the medium — the commercial feature's running time, the need for visual legibility, the need for a climactic action — and departures that were free choices. Third, and this is the part I care about: whether the novella's device is translatable into film in principle. If it is, say how. If it is not, give me a real untranslatability claim, of the kind that says a formal feature is bound to its medium, and say whether it survives the obvious counterexamples. Fourth, where the Hofstadter framework earns its keep here and where it fails — in particular whether a constraint-and-slack model can capture a work whose effect is an experience stretched across the reader's time rather than a pattern on the page. If my reading of the novella is wrong, say so and say what the device is actually doing. And finally, as an exercise for the class, produce one further translation of the novella into a medium with a genuinely different formal constraint — a radio play, a single screen of text, a five-minute short, a gallery installation, your choice — stating explicitly which features you held fixed, which you sacrificed, and why.

No citations, no reading list, and no quotation from either text; the class has both in front of them and I want the argument. As long as the argument needs.
