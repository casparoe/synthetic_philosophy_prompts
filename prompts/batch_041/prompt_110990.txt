I'm in a small reading group on Grice, and we've been going through a friend's dissertation chapter—she's a PhD student in linguistics, call her Mira—where she's trying to give a unified test for when something is speaker-implicated rather than just said or just inferred by the hearer. I'm in philosophy, not linguistics, so half of this goes over my head, but I have a bet with a friend about what Mira will say next, so I want to think it through. She has this distinction she calls word-borne vs. speaker-borne implicature: word-borne when the content seems carried by a particular expression, speaker-borne when it's carried by the speaker's intention in context. Her verdicts so far, from the cases she's worked through: (1) 'Some of the applicants have teaching experience,' said when she knows all of them do: the not-all content is a conversational implicature, not part of what is said; and if the speaker adds 'in fact, all of them do,' Mira says the implicature is canceled. (2) 'She is poor but honest': the contrast is not said; it's carried by 'but' and she classifies it as a conventional implicature, a word-borne thing. (3) 'There's a gas station around the corner,' said when she knows it's closed: she says the speaker implicates that it's open, even though the sentence doesn't say so. (4) 'I'm not saying the treasurer is dishonest, but some trustees have been asking questions': she says the speaker does not implicate that the treasurer is dishonest, because the speaker explicitly disavows it; the hearer may infer it, but that's not speaker meaning. (5) 'Can you pass the salt?' at dinner: she says this is not a question plus a request-implicature; it's a request, and the ability question is a politeness shell. (6) A letter of recommendation that says only 'Alex has beautiful handwriting': she says the writer conversationally implicates that Alex is not a good philosopher.

Now the new case: a speaker says, 'She is poor but honest, and I don't mean to suggest that poverty and honesty are in tension—in fact I think they have nothing to do with each other.' The speaker is sincere about the disavowal and not being ironic. What will Mira say about whether the speaker implicates a contrast? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, at least two other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, what each predicts for the new case, which precedent is closest, and what further case would actually force her to choose. Also, if her earlier verdicts really do underdetermine it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each answer and which one you'd bet on.
