Write a short story — length is yours to judge, though something in the range of 1,500 to 2,500 words is probably right — set in a small maritime commonwealth that has, on the advice of its philosopher-bishop, done away with metallic money and now circulates nothing but printed tickets, each one a bare claim on a share of the island's industry. Treat the following as the reformers' working commitments, and let the story test them rather than illustrate them:

- Money has no intrinsic value and is not properly a commodity; it is a ticket or counter, a device for reckoning, recording, and transferring claims on goods and labour.
- The genuine source of wealth is the elements and human labour; money earns its keep only by stirring up industry, and any other device that did the same job would serve as well.
- The names of money — pound, crown, shilling — denote not quantities of metal but a proportion between demand and plenty; the same denominations might be kept even if all the bullion in the world vanished.
- What people really want is power, and the aim of a well-governed state is that each member, in proportion to his industry, should have power over the labour of others.
- It follows that a little money circulating quickly may do the work of much money circulating slowly.

Build the story around a pressure point where the doctrine has to pay for itself. An outsider arrives with a chest of foreign gold and begins quietly buying up the island's tickets. If the tickets are mere counters, what exactly has he bought? If wealth is the power to command labour, has he now acquired it — or has he merely acquired paper that the islanders can refuse to honour? Let the story turn on a concrete decision: a magistrate, a bank clerk, a farmer, whoever you like, must rule or act on whether the outsider's purchase is real, and on what the island owes him. A second thread should stage the counterfeiter's question: if the tickets have no intrinsic value, what injury does a forger commit, and is it an injury to the state, to the people's convention, or to no one at all?

I want both faces of the theory visible — its genuine insight that credit and industry, not metal, are the substance of wealth, and its fragility when confidence or coercion fails. Avoid a tidy moral; end on a particular image or exchange rather than a verdict. Do not have characters lecture; let the argument emerge from what they do with the tickets.

Begin with the story itself — the first line should be the opening of the narrative — with no preface, epigraph, or commentary.
