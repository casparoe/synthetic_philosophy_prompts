I chair our animal care and use committee, and I've also taught Kant's ethics for twenty years, which is turning out to be a awkward combination. Next week we vote on a protocol from the pain lab: they want to induce a persistent inflammatory condition in mice and then test a new analgesic, so a lot of the animals sit in pain for days before anything is given, and their protocol says the humane endpoint only kicks in once the condition is 'severe.' Some of that I can live with, but whats bugging me is how the debate is going. Our vet keeps saying the mice aren't moral persons and never will be, so the only real question is whether the data justify the cost. And honestly, that sounds a lot like Kant to me — no duties to animals, only duties regarding them, and cruelty is off the table because it dulls the sympathy we owe humanity.

I want to write the memo that goes to the committee before the vote, and I want to write it as someone who actually takes that Kantian structure seriously rather than just waving the flag of animal rights at people who won't buy it. My tentative line is that the Kantian framework is the right one for a public institution like ours — we can't ground anything in animals having rights, but we can ground a lot in what cruelty does to us — except that the very same framework makes the mouse's suffering strictly instrumental, relevant only through its effect on human character. Which means if you could somehow be cruel without being hardened by it, Kant has nothing to say, and that seems like the wrong answer.

So work it out for me. Draft the memo, land on a concrete outcome for this protocol — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a deadline we can actually enforce, and give me the strongest case for the side I'm inclined against, since I don't want to walk in there having already decided. And tell me straight whether my line survives or whether I'm just using Kant to have it both ways.
