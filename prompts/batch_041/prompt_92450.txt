Last week a friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about a pattern I keep running into, and I want to know whether it has an established name or a literature attached to it, because he kept insisting it was simply the unilateralist's curse and I'm fairly sure that's wrong. The shape of it is this. A large number of people are each independently in a position to do some small thing: each could release a pair of ornamental fish into the same lake, or take a course of antibiotics they don't strictly need, or water their lawn through a drought. Taken one at a time, none of these acts is anything you could point to; the harm, if there is any, is invisible at that scale. But the acts aren't independent in their effects — they accumulate, and past some point the lake's ecology flips, or the resistant strain establishes itself, or the reservoir runs dry. Every one of the actors is well informed, perfectly well intentioned, and reasons correctly: my act makes no detectable difference, whatever harm comes is caused by the total, so I may as well. Almost everyone acts, and the bad outcome arrives. What nags at me is the combination of two features: no single act is either necessary or sufficient for the harm, and nobody is acting from ignorance or selfishness.

My friend says this is the unilateralist's curse. But when I read about that afterwards it seemed to be about a different thing — the case where the harm follows if even one person acts on a mistaken belief, so the trouble is that somebody is bound to, and the proposed remedy is a principle of restraint or conformity. That isn't what I'm after, because here the harm needs a crowd, and any one person's restraint changes nothing at all. It also isn't quite the tragedy of the commons as I've had it explained to me, since there the individuals are supposed to be tempted by private gain at the expense of a shared resource, whereas my people gain almost nothing and aren't thinking of themselves — they're just correctly noticing that their own contribution is imperceptible. I have a suspicion that what I'm describing is the same structure as the sorites paradox, and maybe the same thing as what ethicists and public-administration people call the problem of many hands, but I can't tell whether these are one thing or several things wearing the same coat.

So what I want is this. Is there an established concept for the pattern I've described? If there is, tell me exactly how it differs from the unilateralist's curse, from the tragedy of the commons, from the sorites/continuum problem, and from the problem of many hands, and which of these it actually is. If there isn't one, coin a term and define it properly, including the boundary cases: does it matter that the individual acts are imperceptible, or only that none of them is decisive on its own? Does it matter whether the actors could have coordinated, or whether the harm is reversible, or whether the resource is rivalrous? Give me two or three cases sitting near the edge and say which side your definition puts them on and why. I'm not looking for a bare label — I want the carving: what this thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
