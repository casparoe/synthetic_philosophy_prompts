At a long dinner last month I ended up as the audience for an argument between two friends of mine, Sofia and Ben, and I have been turning it over ever since. Ben had just come back from a year in a town where, as he kept putting it, 'nobody does anything the way we do,' and what began as a squabble about the tip screen on a card reader widened into queueing and jaywalking, so I'm giving you the whole thing rather than only the tipping part. I don't think I agree with either of them more than the other, and I suspect each of them is right about some moves and muddled about others, but I can't say which is which.

(1) Ben: 'Tipping is not a moral matter at all. The server's wage is owed by the employer under the contract; what I owe is the price on the menu. A tip is a gift, and a gift you are obliged to give is not a gift. Calling me a bad person for leaving nothing is etiquette dressed up as ethics.' (2) Sofia: 'You don't get out of it that way. If the menu price is set on the assumption that a tip will arrive — and where employers are permitted to count expected tips against the wage, that is exactly what the price is — then paying the price and leaving nothing means knowingly taking a subsidized meal while the person who served it absorbs the shortfall. There is a difference between asking whether a practice is a good one and asking what you owe someone within a practice you have knowingly entered. Nor is this only about tipping: a queue is a coordination convention that works because nearly everyone keeps to it, and whoever ignores it collects the benefit of everyone else's restraint.' (3) Ben: 'Both halves of that prove too much. The free-riding point only bites if the practice is decent enough to deserve sustaining; if the real function of tipping is to let an employer push labor costs onto customers and make a server's income depend on the mood of strangers, then the customer who declines is not free-riding on anything worth preserving, and the party with the complaint is the employer. And the queueing point evaporates where the norm is already dead — at a crossing where everyone jaywalks there is no equilibrium to free-ride on, and the only reasons left are the law and my own neck.' (4) Sofia: 'Then drop the practice and talk about the person. It isn't the fairness of the institution that grounds the obligation; it is the position of the particular server, who eats the loss tonight and did not design the system. And that is exactly where the visitor question gets sharp, which is why your year abroad matters. The "queue" outside your bakery was not a line at all — it was a matter of catching the baker's eye — so a visitor who stood quietly at the back was not being polite, he was invisible, and he made the regulars uneasy because they could not tell whether he was waiting or loitering. When a visitor's own convention is not merely useless but a foreign body, deference to local practice is the modest move: local customs encode knowledge he does not have.' (5) Ben: 'Deference is a fine heuristic until it collides with something that matters. "Follow local conventions" gives you no filter whatsoever: it tells you to shove onto the bus because shoving is the local way and to leave the tip because leaving is the local way, and it would equally tell a visitor to keep quiet about a custom he finds cruel. It hands the majority a veto over the outsider's judgment. And knowability cuts against you rather than for you: the bakery case shows that a visitor can be blameless and still fail a norm he had no way to see. If an obligation depends on my being able to know it, the eye-contact queue cannot obligate a newcomer — the regulars' irritation is real, but it is not a moral complaint.' (6) Sofia: '"No way to see it" is a very low bar. He could have asked, or watched for ten minutes. Conventions are learned by participation, not by inspection. Insist that only fully legible norms bind and you get a rule that looks even-handed while quietly privileging the visitor's habits and making the locals do all the accommodating.'

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point rather than announcing a winner: for each of the six moves, say what is right in it, what is wrong or overstated, and how much of it survives. I would also like your best attempt at the general rule that ought to govern what a visitor owes to local conventions — what, if anything, distinguishes the eye-contact bakery from the jaywalking crossing from the tip line, and whether the strength of the obligation tracks knowability, harm to particular people, the value of the coordination, or something else. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing that neither has raised, and then, if you have to, who is more nearly right overall.
