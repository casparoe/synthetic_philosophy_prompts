My friend Priya teaches philosophy and has spent basically her whole career on Strawson — resentment, indignation, the whole question of when someone is a fit target of the reactive attitudes and when you've quietly stopped relating to them as a person and started managing them like a problem. She has strong verdicts and she is definately, annoyingly consistent about them. Here's what I've collected from about ten years of arguing with her.

- A man has a first-ever seizure at the wheel and kills a cyclist. Priya: nobody's angry at anybody here, and not out of generosity — there's just nothing there to be angry at.
- A man gets drunk at a wedding, drives home, and hits a cyclist. Priya: he did it, full stop, and she is furious with him.
- A woman with an undiagnosed brain tumor goes into a delusional state and attacks her neighbor with a shovel. Priya: not responsible, and she wouldn't want an apology — she says it'd be like being mad at a tree that fell on your car.
- A man was raised from birth in a tiny end-times community that taught him outsiders are demons in human skin. At thirty-five he kills a lost hiker who wanders in. Priya: not responsible. Her line is that he never got the chance to become the kind of person who could see the hiker as a person.
- A man with lifelong psychopathy — reasons fine, has never once cared whether anyone suffers — talks an elderly couple out of their savings. Priya: not responsible. She says it's like being angry at the weather. She's all for locking him up; punishment is just the wrong category.
- An ordinary man, decent upbringing, at forty talks himself into a totalitarian movement and denounces a colleague to the authorities knowing it will destroy him. Priya: completely responsible, no mitigation. He talked himself into it, she says, so he could have talked himself out of it.

One more thing that took me years to even hear: with an ordinary careless screw-up — a guy leaves the door unlocked, the house gets burgled — she isn't resentful, but she still thinks he owes an apology and she'll hold him to it. With the tumor woman she says there's nothing to apologize for and an apology wouldn't mean anything anyway. So "I'm not angry at him" covers two completely different things in her head, and she insists they aren't the same thing.

Now the new case. Ray, fifty-two, drank hard for twenty years. Twice he ended up in the hospital with liver trouble, and twice the doctors sat him down and told him in plain English that if he kept drinking he'd do permanent damage to his brain, not just his liver. He kept drinking. Four years later it's happened: his neurologist says the frontal-lobe damage is structural and isn't going to improve. He genuinely can't stop himself once he's provoked, and — the neurologist's phrasing, not mine — he's no longer capable of the kind of reflection that would let him care what he does to other people. Last month a neighbor's kid dinged his truck and Ray put the kid in the hospital. He isn't sorry. He isn't anything.

So what does Priya say about Ray — he did this to himself, or look at him now? And which of her earlier cases is actually doing the work here? I've been wrong about her before, so I dont want to just guess.

1. "The rules that fit" — what her verdicts are actually tracking, and every rule I could read off them that isn't just gerrymandered to fit, about 200 words.
2. "What each rule says about Ray" — about 150 words.
3. "My call" — your prediction for Ray, which earlier case is the closest analog and why, and what principle she'd be taking on if she went the other way, about 150 words.
4. "The case that would settle it" — the further case that would tell the surviving rules apart, about 100 words.
5. "Confidence and the weak link" — how sure you are about all of this, and the single point you're least sure of. A few sentences.
