I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been reading about it on my own for some time, and I have become stuck on the idea that evidence underdetermines theory. A friend of mine who took a term or two of philosophy at university sent me his attempt at a summary, and I cannot tell how much of it to trust. He wrote:

"Underdetermination of theory by evidence is the thesis, usually credited to Duhem and Quine, that evidence can never decide between competing scientific theories. Because no hypothesis is ever tested on its own, but always together with a whole web of background assumptions, any theory can be made to fit any evidence by adjusting the web somewhere else. As Quine is usually read, any belief at all can be kept, provided you are willing to revise enough elsewhere, and no belief is safe from revision. This is not a practical limitation that more data would cure: two theories can be made to fit all possible evidence, so the choice between them is ultimately a matter of convenience or taste rather than reason. That is why Quine thought we should give up on the idea that our theories are, or are on their way to being, true, and why the sociology of science later took the point to show that what scientists accept is settled by social factors rather than by the evidence."

My own instinct is that the conclusion is right, and here is the thought that convinced me. Take any theory that scientists currently accept — say, a theory about how some disease works. I can invent a rival: it says everything the accepted theory says about anything anyone could ever observe, but adds that there is also an extra entity at work, one that by stipulation makes no observable difference. The two theories fit exactly the same evidence for ever, so there is nothing to choose between them but taste. If that is right, then the honest thing to say about all our science is that it is one option among many.

I would like you to audit my friend's summary sentence by sentence. Tell me which parts a philosopher of science would accept as fair, which are the sort of simplification they would wince at, and which are simply wrong, and say where a simplification has hardened into an error rather than just a compression. I would particularly like to know what distinctions I am missing — I suspect there are some — and whether they change anything about his conclusion. Tell me too whether my own example is a case of the kind the thesis is really about, or whether I have smuggled something in. Finally, please say whether the claim about social factors follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction. Please write in British English.
