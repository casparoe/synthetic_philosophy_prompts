Last term I took a seminar on twentieth-century conceptions of the meaning of life, and it caught me at a bad moment: I had just turned down a steady job to chase a career goal that is still several years off, and I had started to notice that my days feel less like a life than like a corridor to one. The essay that unsettled me was Moritz Schlick's 1927 piece on the meaning of life, whose central move, as I read it, is a distinction between work and play. Work, on his account, is activity undertaken for the sake of some future purpose and carrying no value in itself; play is activity that carries its own purpose and value within it, so that whatever effects it happens to have — even useful ones — are byproducts rather than the point. From this he draws the conclusion that the meaning of a life has to be found in the present, in play in the broadest sense, and not deferred to an end that is always just ahead of us. I found the diagnosis uncomfortably accurate and the conclusion attractive, which is precisely why an argument a friend in the seminar constructed, and then half-retracted, has been rattling around in my head. I cannot tell whether it is a genuine refutation of Schlick or a sleight of hand, and I would rather not abandon the essay if I don't have to.

Here is the argument as he set it out. (1) Schlick's criterion: an activity contributes to the meaning of a life exactly insofar as it is pursued for its own sake — valued as an end rather than merely as a means. Call any such activity play. (2) To pursue something for its own sake is to value it with no reference to anything further, so its value must be intrinsic rather than borrowed from some consequence it produces. (3) Now take any activity I would confidently call play — playing an instrument, a long walk, cooking a real meal, a late conversation with a friend. If I am honest, in each case I can name something further the activity produces: relief from anxiety, a feeling of being alive, becoming a more attentive person, being a better friend. (4) So in each case the activity is valued because of something it leads to. (5) If every candidate for play turns out to be valued for the sake of something beyond itself, then there is no play at all; everything is work. (6) By (1), a life containing no play contains no meaning. (7) Therefore every life is meaningless. (8) But that is absurd — I know perfectly well that some of my hours are meaningful. (9) So (1), Schlick's criterion itself, must be false.

I have a real stake in the outcome: if (1) survives, I have to read the essay as a serious indictment of how I am currently living, and if it does not, I can go back to grinding without a qualm. So tell me where this goes wrong, if it does — and do not simply say the conclusion is absurd, since I already know that; I need to see where the machinery slips, if it slips anywhere. For each premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked so plausible in the first place, what repairing it would cost elsewhere, and whether the part of Schlick's view I most want to keep — that meaning is realized in activity valued for its own sake rather than merely in results — can be preserved once the repair is made.
