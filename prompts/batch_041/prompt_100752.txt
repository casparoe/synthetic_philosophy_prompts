Question (10 points):
Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are caused by physical properties but do not themselves cause anything. Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment and assess whether it makes epiphenomenalism self-undermining. In your answer: (a) state the paradox as a causal argument, (b) say whether it afflicts only epiphenomenalism or also other views on which phenomenal properties lack causal powers, (c) give the epiphenomenalist's best reply and say how much it helps, and (d) briefly: does it matter for the argument whether the "judgments" in question are full-blown verbal reports or just dispositions to assent?

Student's answer:
Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but don't themselves cause anything — they're a by-product, like the whistle on a train. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is that this looks self-defeating. When I judge that I'm in pain, or that I'm seeing red, that judgment is itself a physical event in my brain. If epiphenomenalism is true, then the judgment is fully caused by prior physical events, and my pain plays no role in producing it. So the pain is not what makes me say "I'm in pain." But the epiphenomenalist's whole evidence for phenomenal properties is that we make these judgments — it's the only reason anyone believes in qualia in the first place. So the view undercuts the evidence for its own central claim. If the judgment would have occurred exactly the same whether or not the phenomenal state existed — a zombie twin would say all the same things — then the judgment gives us no reason at all to believe in the phenomenal state.

Really this is just the problem of other minds turned inward: I can't observe my own qualia doing any causal work, so I can't know they exist.

I think the epiphenomenalist has a decent reply though. Even if the phenomenal state doesn't cause the judgment, the physical state that causes the judgment is the same physical state that causes the phenomenal state. So there's a reliable correlation: whenever the judgment occurs, the phenomenal state occurs. A reliable indicator is all we need for evidence, so the judgment does give us evidence after all, and the paradox doesn't really land.

Also, the paradox is a problem for epiphenomenalism specifically. Any view that gives phenomenal properties causal powers — property dualism, for instance — escapes it completely, so it isn't a general problem for non-physicalist views about the mind.

(d) I don't think the verbal/dispositional distinction matters much, since either way it's a physical event that's doing the reporting.
