I retired from field ecology about six years ago — I spent most of my career doing mark-recapture on seabirds, which is really just standing in the rain with a clipboard — and since then I've been trying to make good on an old promise to myself to finally read some philosophy. Anyways, long story short, I'm in a small reading group that meets above a bike shop, and last month we got onto the handicap principle, the peacock's-tail thing. It completely derailed us. Deb, who did a PhD in economics before she quit to run a bakery, insists the job-market signaling model is the same idea with worse jokes, my neighbor won't stop explaining his watch to me, and now everything anybody does at the table is a costly signal. For our end-of-summer meeting we're each supposed to bring something funny, so I want to bring a parody and read it out loud.

What I'd like from you: a deadpan internal values-and-advancement handbook for a made-up firm — call it Plumage Partners, a mid-size management consultancy — that has sincerely reorganized its entire hiring and promotion process around the handicap principle. Write it as a numbered FAQ, the way a real HR department writes one: earnest, euphemistic, no winking, no narrator leaning in to tell us it's a joke, roughly 700 words. The whole thing should be in-universe, as if this handbook is genuinely in circulation at the firm. Here's the part I actually care about. The humor has to come from the signaling theory being half-understood by people who are very confident about it, so that an evolutionary biologist laughs instead of wincing. So the handbook should get some things right — it should grasp that a signal only stays honest if it's costly, and that the cost has to fall harder on the lower-quality type, otherwise it sorts nothing — and then muddle or botch the rest in ways a specialist will spot: they should confuse an index with a signal (treating a candidate's height, say, as an honest signal), they should assume any costly act is automatically informative, they should run a promotion arms race that is perfectly stable and also pure waste, they should maintain an Ordeal that literally everyone passes, and they should have a cheap-talk policy that happens to be fine for reasons they never work out (the interests line up and nobody notices). Somewhere in there let it slip, without anyone saying so, that the whole scheme might just be selecting for people who can afford to waste a year — and let the reader feel the real question at the end: whether an equilibrium can be honest and still not be worth what it costs. Don't quote anybody and don't cite anything; no references, no names, no studies, nothing to look up. Just the handbook.
