I have been reading rationalist and LessWrong-adjacent material for well over a decade, and I keep noticing that the community treats two quite different arguments as if they were two roads to the same destination. Both are supposed to establish that a rational agent's credences must be probabilities. The first is the betting argument: if your degrees of belief violate the probability calculus, someone can construct a book of bets that you will accept, each on your own terms, and that leaves you poorer however the world turns out; probabilistic coherence is thus the condition of invulnerability to a sure loss. The second is the accuracy argument: credences are estimates of the truth, and once you score estimates with a rule that is proper — one under which you maximize your expected score by reporting your actual credence — every non-probabilistic credence function is accuracy-dominated by some probabilistic one; coherence is thus a demand of aiming at the truth rather than of avoiding ruin.

I would like these two laid side by side and compared as competing justifications, not merely as two proofs of the same theorem. Specifically:

1. Identify the object each argument evaluates and the good each treats as fundamental: a disposition to accept bets and the avoidance of a sure loss, versus a credence function scored against the world and accuracy. State what each must assume for its conclusion to follow — for the betting argument, the willingness to bet at all, the linearity of utility in the stake, and the simultaneity of the book; for the accuracy argument, the availability of a proper scoring rule and the choice among the several that qualify.

2. Say how much each conclusion depends on those assumptions. Is the betting argument robust to an agent who will not bet, whose utility is non-linear in money, or who faces no adversary? Is the accuracy argument robust to the choice of scoring rule, given that both the Brier score and the log score qualify while producing different verdicts about particular credence functions?

3. Compare what each delivers beyond probabilism. Can either, on its own, justify updating by conditionalization rather than merely probabilistic coherence? Can either tie credences to evidence or to chance without importing further principles? Where one is silent and the other speaks, say so.

4. Here is a case I have been chewing on and have not seen discussed. Consider an agent with blatantly non-probabilistic credences who will never be offered a bet, holds no stake in any currency you could pay them, and whose utility in money is non-linear — call them the confident hermit. The betting argument has no purchase on the hermit, since there is no book to be made against someone who will not bet; the accuracy argument, if anything, applies to the hermit more cleanly, because accuracy dominance needs no adversary and no utility function. Does this settle the comparison in favor of the accuracy program, or does it instead show that the betting argument was never really about money in the first place?

5. I also want a distinction I have not seen drawn sharply. Call accuracy at the actual world worldly accuracy, and accuracy expected from the agent's own point of view self-endorsed accuracy. Which argument is committed to which? Note what looks like a degeneracy: if you merely instruct the agent to maximize self-endorsed accuracy under a proper rule, the instruction is empty, since propriety guarantees that every credence function already maximizes expected accuracy by its own lights. If that is right, the substantive work is done by dominance — a worldly, world-indexed notion — and not by any internal decision rule. Is that a fair diagnosis, and if so, which of the two justifications survives it better?

6. Finally, deliver a verdict. Which of the two should be treated as more fundamental, and can the other be recovered from it as a corollary — a Dutch book theorem falling out of accuracy dominance, or the reverse? If neither can be derived from the other, say what a unified justification would have to add.

A short table of correspondences, mapping the two frameworks onto each other point by point, would help me keep track. I care less about who said what than about which justification actually does the work.
