I'm in a seminar on free will and mechanism, and I've drawn Alex Garland's Ex Machina for my final piece — but I want to come at it sideways, through sphexishness, instead of the Turing-test angle everyone does. The story I have in mind is the digger wasp: she paralyses a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it at the mouth of the nest, and goes down inside to check that everything's in order; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's in there, she comes back out, hauls it back to the entrance, and goes down to check again — and you can do that all afternoon. Hofstadter's word for that is sphexishness, and my understanding of his point is that the disturbing thing isn't that she's a machine, it's that she can't see the routine she's caught in, and that we're all sphexish about something. (I've also read that if you actually keep at it long enough the wasp does eventually do something else, and that re-checking a nest something just disturbed may be perfectly sensible — so maybe the story is more useful than it is true. You can tell me if that matters.) Now the film. Kyoko is the wasp, obviously: mute, blank, never varies. Ava is the one who lies, improvises, and walks out, and Garland definately wants us to read her as the free one. But it seems to me sphexishness says unpredictability is cheap — my washing machine is unpredictable to me. Nathan more or less tells us her escape is the whole point of her, and the whole week is Ava running Nathan's program on Caleb. So is the ending a liberation, or just the ritual completing itself? Is there any moment where Ava notices her own routine, or notices that she's in one at all? If there isn't, I think the film is quietly making my point against itself: it hands us freedom as spontaneity plus ingratitude, and then we clap.

I don't want to just win the argument though, I want the honest reading, so push back wherever the film resists me. Is there a scene where the idea lights up something the film didn't intend — the synthetic skin, the dress, the video feeds of the earlier models, the way she stands still and lets Nathan and Caleb talk about her as if she weren't there? Is Kyoko actually the more interesting case, given that she's the one who turns on Nathan while Ava watches? And the bigger question: what does the film add to the free will debate that the arguments alone don't — does it show us something about what it would be like from the inside to be the wasp, or is it just the wasp story with better production design? One more thing to keep in view: if the wasp isn't as rigid as the parable claims, does the reading survive, or does it collapse into "mechanism bad, spontaneity good," which is the thing I'm trying to avoid?
