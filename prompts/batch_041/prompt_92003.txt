Two friends in my reading group argued for an evening about Nāgārjuna's four-cornered negation, and I came away unable to say who had the better of it.

Anna started from a hard case: a woman with advanced dementia once made a promise that she can no longer remember. Is she the same person who promised? Anna said the four corners — same, not same, both, neither — exhaust the possible answers, and that the fact that none of them can be asserted shows the question presupposes a fixed self-nature that nothing possesses. The point of the tetralemma, on her reading, is not to answer but to dissolve: it asserts nothing, and emptiness is not a thesis.

Ben made three objections. First, the fourth corner is a fudge — "neither" is just the ordinary gradualist answer (same in some respects, not in others) dressed up as transcendence, and the four corners look exhaustive only because the question was posed in yes/no form. Second, the method is a solvent that dissolves everything it touches: run it on the claim that fire burns, or on the claim that the tetralemma dissolves questions, and the same refutations become available, so it proves too much to prove anything. Third, it cannot state its own result: "all four corners fail" is itself a claim, and if it is true it is a fifth position, while if nothing is asserted the view is indistinguishable from nihilism — no small matter in a tradition built on karma and compassion.

Anna answered that a reductio aimed at an opponent's own commitments concludes nothing of its own, and that the two truths do the rest: conventionally, fire burns and promises bind; ultimately, nothing has a nature of its own, and even that last remark is not something to be grasped. Ben replied that this leaves a dilemma — either the ultimate is a kind of truth, in which case it is a thesis after all, or it is not, in which case the conventional is simply false and the nihilism charge returns. He added that a reductio that concludes nothing is merely ad hominem: it can embarrass someone who already shares the assumptions it exploits, but it cannot touch a realist untroubled by the contradictions it draws out.

Neither would budge. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move — the fourth corner, the universal-dissolution objection, the self-application objection, and the two-truths dilemma — saying for each whether it lands and what, if anything, both parties are missing, then say who is more right overall and whether the tetralemma can be stated at all without being caught in its own net.
