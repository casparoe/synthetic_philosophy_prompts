I chair the interpretation committee at our county museum — we sign off on gallery labels and on the text of the plaques the county puts up at historic sites, and we actually have to vote, so what I need is a memo I can circulate and read out on Tuesday, not a thinkpiece.

Here's what's in front of us. The Battle of the Ford society wants a plaque at the old river crossing reading that it's the site of the battle, 516, where the kings of the west turned back the invaders. The only source is a chronicle written about six hundred years after the fact, and it says itself that it's working from the old songs of the people. The society's case is that the story has been told at that crossing continuously for a thousand years, that everyone in the valley knows it, and that nobody can prove it didn't happen. Our field archaeologist says the only things anyone has ever turned up there are medieval potsherds and Victorian ginger-beer bottles. The tourism office is keen because it's the only thing in that end of the county anybody's heard of. My gut says we shouldn't be putting a date and a site-of on a county plaque on that basis, but I don't want to be the man who tells a village its story is fake, and I don't want to be the man who signs of on something the local paper makes us look stupid over in ten years.

Then there's the queue behind it. In September a group wants to rent our gallery for a traveling exhibit on the Exodus, and they want our label to say the events are historically confirmed — their words. In November the classical society wants us to co-sign something on whether there was a Trojan war at all, and their draft says the archaeology proves Homer. So it's one question three times and I'd rather the committee had a rule then three separate fights.

Two things I need from you. One, a recommendation on the plaque: approve, reject, or approve with the text changed — and if it's changed, give me the actual wording I can put in the motion, plus when we say we'll revisit it, because the society will ask and I want an answer ready. Two, the rule: what test do we apply, in plain enough language that a future committee with different politics applies the same one? I've been pushing a three-way split — keep separate (a) did something of that kind happen around then, (b) is this the place, (c) did it happen the way the story tells it — and I think that does most of the work, but tell me honestly whether it just lets us have it both ways, because one of my members keeps calling the whole thing heritage inflation and I genuinely can't tell if he's right.

Don't hand me the standard museum line about legends being valuable in their own right; we already have a panel in the lobby that says that. Tell me what to do, and tell me the strongest thing the society has going for it, because if I can't answer it in the room I'll lose the vote. Five of us: me, the archaeologist, two councillors and a retired schoolteacher who happens to be the society's secretary. Under two pages, no citations, I won't check them and the board won't either.
