I'm currently taking a seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds, and we've spent the past few weeks on the family of positions that go under the heading of virtual realism: roughly, the claim that the objects and events inside a virtual world are real objects and events, implemented in computation rather than in atoms, and not merely fictional or illusory. A classmate and I have been arguing about what follows from this for harm, and I'd like your credence in the thesis she defends, with the reasoning decomposed rather than asserted.

Her thesis, stated as carefully as the two of us can manage it: for any two experiences that are alike in phenomenal character, alike in how the subject herself values them, and alike in the consent conditions under which they occur, the fact that one takes place inside a virtual world and the other outside it is no reason whatsoever to count the virtual one as the lesser harm — virtuality as such is morally inert. She adds a parallel claim about the vehicles of harm: that an injury is delivered through an avatar or some other digital artifact rather than through a body is no reason to think that no one was injured. The paradigm cases in the literature are harassment and assault between avatars in social virtual spaces, and that is where I feel the pull of her view most strongly and the pull of its denial most strongly at the same time.

What I want from you is your credence that this thesis, as stated, is true — a number, together with the reasoning that produces it — and then a decomposition of that number into the sub-questions it depends on. Candidates, though I would rather you tell me if I have missed one: whether virtual objects and events exist in a sense strong enough to be the subject of claims like "she was harmed"; whether the morally relevant features of an experience are its phenomenal and evaluative features rather than its causal substrate; whether there is any independent weight to the intuition that it wasn't real, whether as the tracking of something genuine or as a distortion produced by the novelty of the technology; whether the harm is properly attributed to the other user rather than to the platform, the designers, or the victim's own decision to enter; and whether the thesis proves too much, in the sense that a view which makes harms real relative to a virtual world might also make them real relative to a novel, a daydream, or a merely entertained hypothetical, and whether there is a principled way to draw the line at computationally implemented and causally interactive worlds rather than at the worlds we happen to have built. That last one is where I keep going back and forth: if world-relativity is sufficient, the thesis starts to look as though it has no distinctive content, and if only implemented worlds count, I cannot tell whether that is a principled distinction or an accident of our current technology.

Please write your answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The thesis** (2–3 sentences): restate the proposition so that it has a definite truth value, flagging any point at which you had to disambiguate it.
2. **My number** (one sentence): your credence, to two decimal places.
3. **The decomposition** (a list or table of six to nine rows): each sub-question, your probability for it, and one line of justification.
4. **How they combine** (one paragraph): how the sub-questions combine into the number, noting any place where the combination is not a simple product.
5. **Sensitivity** (one paragraph): the sub-question your number is most sensitive to, with a rough indication of how far the number would move if that sub-question were 0.3 rather than 0.7.
6. **What would move it** (a few sentences): the argument or evidence that would shift your number most, in which direction, and what would count as settling the matter.
7. **Where we disagree** (one paragraph): my number is about 0.6 and hers is about 0.9; tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in.
