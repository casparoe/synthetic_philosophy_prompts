I am a second-year PhD student in linguistics, and the arrival of my daughter eight months ago has made the poverty-of-the-stimulus debate something I can no longer treat as a purely seminar-room question: I find myself narrating her input to her out loud and noticing, with a kind of vertigo, how thin it is compared to what she is plainly doing with it. I am also the teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on nativism and language acquisition, and the instructor has asked me to close the final session with something other than a summary. I would like you to write the poem for that occasion. Please take the role of a poet who is also a competent philosopher of language — someone who knows this debate from the inside and can write formal verse without fudging either the poetry or the argument — and work carefully rather than quickly. Before you draft, spend a few sentences thinking on the page about which side of the debate your chosen structure will commit you to and why, since I do not want the form to be mere decoration.

Then write a pantoum of five quatrains, following the standard rule that the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, with the final stanza closing the loop by taking the first stanza's first and third lines as its second and fourth — so that the poem's opening line comes back one line before the end, and the whole thing finishes having said nothing it did not begin with. The conceit I want you to build on is that the pantoum's recycled lines are the child's input and the lines that change from stanza to stanza are the learner's inferences. The recurring lines must therefore recur verbatim and must never be enriched — the data never grow — while the intervening lines should become progressively more articulated, as though grammar were being constructed out of material that was always already there. At least one of the recurring lines should be the sort of thing a parent actually says to an infant, ordinary and thin enough that a reader feels the poverty of it.

Let the standard structure-dependence case appear among the changing lines in paraphrase rather than quotation: a child who is never corrected, and never even encounters the ungrammatical version of a relative-clause question, nonetheless forms the right one, moving the auxiliary that belongs to the main clause rather than the one buried in the relative clause. I would like the closing loop to be double-voiced: when the opening line returns near the end, it should be readable both as the empiricist's evidence that the input was sufficient after all and as the nativist's evidence that nothing new ever came in, and you should not adjudicate between the two readings. The poem should leave a reader unable to say which reading the repetition supports, and the seminar should leave with the argument still open. Accuracy matters more than ornament here: a linguist ought to find the positions intact and correctly opposed, and a non-linguist ought still to find it a real poem. If you use rhyme, keep it consistent.
