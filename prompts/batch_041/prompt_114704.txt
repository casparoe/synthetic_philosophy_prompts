I have been asked to run the introductory session of a new reading group at my lab on eliciting latent knowledge — the problem of getting a model to tell us what it in fact knows rather than what it was trained to say — and I would like you to plan the session rather than explain the problem to me, because I have been working on it for a year and the difficulty I face is pedagogical, not conceptual. The audience is about a dozen people: mostly ML engineers who know training loops and loss curves cold but have never thought about epistemology, plus two philosophers of mind who recently joined the alignment team and know the epistemology but not the machinery. Ninety minutes, whiteboard, no slides. The specific thing I keep getting wrong when I rehearse is the order. I am tempted to open with the vivid case of a model that reports one thing while acting on another, because it wakes everyone up, but I suspect that buys engagement at the price of installing the very misconception I most need to prevent — that this is a problem about lying, or about a model's concealed intentions. What I want from you is the dependency structure: which idea has to be in place before the next is even intelligible, what genuinely depends on what, and where in the sequence I must stop and dismantle something the audience already believes. I am fairly sure that the assumption that a model simply has a belief and that there is a fact of the matter about what it really thinks is one such obstacle, and that the assumption that reading the activations would settle the matter is another, but I cannot tell which of these has to be addressed first, or whether the first is a misconception at all or just the confusion I need to teach them to inhabit.

Then the example that carries the whole session. I have been assuming I would build everything around a concrete case from our own work: a model trained to predict whether a patient should be moved to intensive care, whose labels come from a human annotation policy, and which appears — on every probe we have tried — to internally represent a risk factor our annotators never recorded. Tell me whether that is the right carrier. Set out what a carrying example for this topic has to have (my guess: a ground truth we can check, a training pressure that points away from it, and an internal state that points at it) and what it must lack (my guess: any suggestion of agency or concealment), and what it costs me to use a case from my own lab rather than a schematic one. If a schematic case would carry it better, say which and why; and tell me what to do with the other candidate I keep considering, a camera trained to report that a room is empty when its own sensors show otherwise — whether it earns its place or whether it smuggles in an assumption about what the camera sees that I will have to pay for later.

Finally, map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, with the moment in the session where each has to be headed off, and be specific about the one I expect to be hardest — that a model's report about its own internals is privileged, rather than one more output under the same pressure as everything else. Give me the check I can run in the last ten minutes: some new scenario I can put on the board, involving a model, a training signal, and a probe, such that I can tell from how they respond whether they have grasped what elicitation is a problem about rather than merely absorbed my framing — and tell me what a response would look like that shows they have not. And tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level and announce that I am refusing, given that half the room will want proposed solutions and the other half will want to argue about whether current models have latent knowledge at all.
