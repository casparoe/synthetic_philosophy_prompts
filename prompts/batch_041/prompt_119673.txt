I'm a novelist — two novels out, a third one stuck in the mud — and I've been going back through George Eliot's religious writing: the Feuerbach book she translated, some of the essays, and of course the novels themselves. Her position moves me, but whenever I try to say it out loud to my friends who actually read philosophy I end up sounding like I'm just describing how I write. Which gave me an idea.

Take her religious outlook — God as a projection of human feeling and need, what's often called her religion of humanity, the flat rejection of personal immortality, the idea that we go on living in our effects on other people rather than in any afterlife, sympathy as the real ground of moral life, duty and renunciation with no divine lawgiver standing behind them, suffering as something that deepens rather than redeems — and restate the whole thing, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in. I mean the craft terms: character, motivation, point of view, free indirect style, dramatic irony, the gap between what a character believes about themselves and what the book shows, scene versus summary, plot and causality, resolution or the refusal of it, revision, the implied author, authorial sympathy, the moral weather of a scene.

Then the part I really want: the residue report. What survived the trip intact, what got mangled on the way over, and what simply has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. A few things I'm already suspicious about, so push on these. Does obligation survive at all, or does it dissolve into something weaker like consistency of characterization? When her position says God is a projection, is anything left of that claim once I've said it in terms where a projection is just what a character is — have I accidentally made the claim true and toothless at once? And here's the one that bugs me most: my vocabulary is author-shaped. Everything I've got assumes somebody standing behind the people on the page. So am I smuggling in a God-sized hole in the grammar itself, and if so, is that a defect of my framework or does it show something her view can't quite say either?

Where the residue is a limit of my vocabulary, say so; where it's a sign her position was vaguer than it sounds, say that instead. And keep me honest about the state of the scholarship: separate what's actually settled in the texts from what's genuinely contested among people who know her — how much the Feuerbach material really drives the fiction, whether that religion-of-humanity label even fits her, whether her later work quietly shifts — and don't hand me one camp's reading as if everyone agrees. I'd rather have the argument flagged than a smooth answer.
