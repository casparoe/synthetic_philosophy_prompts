I am designing a unit for an undergraduate seminar on population ethics, and I want to use Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men as a case study. The film is set in a near-future Britain where humanity has become infertile, and the plot turns on the first pregnancy in roughly two decades; the film clearly treats this birth as a moment of hope and even redemption, and it treats the prospect of human extinction as an unmitigated catastrophe. But the philosophical literature on population size makes both of those attitudes less obvious than they look. Parfit's mere addition paradox and repugnant conclusion force us to ask whether adding lives can make a population better even when those lives are barely worth living; Benatar's asymmetry argument suggests that coming into existence is always a harm and that the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy that absence; and the debate between total and average views of population value makes the film's implicit arithmetic unclear. I have been calling the film's stance natalist redemption—the idea that the sheer fact of a birth can redeem a history of suffering and decline—and I want you to test whether that stance is philosophically coherent or whether it is a cinematic evasion.

Read the film through this lens. Does Children of Men presuppose a totalist or an average view of population value? Does its emotional architecture—the despair at infertility, the awe at Kee's child, the ambiguous ending on the boat—smuggle in a pronatalist premise that the film never argues for? How would Benatar read the film's ending: as a happy ending, a tragedy, or a sentimental refusal to confront the harm of coming into existence? Where does the film resist the population-ethics reading—for example, in its attention to refugees, political violence, and the instrumentalization of Kee's body, which are not primarily about the value of additional lives? I would like you to identify specific scenes and character choices (Theo's cynicism and his eventual protectiveness, the Human Project as an object of hope, the state's management of death, the moment when fighting stops at the sound of the baby) and say what each contributes to or complicates the philosophical question. End by saying what the film adds to the population-ethics debate that Parfit and Benatar do not: does it dramatize something about the value of a future population that the arguments miss, or does it simply beg the question?
