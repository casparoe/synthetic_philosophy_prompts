A seminar on early modern political philosophy is building a unit on counterfactual intellectual history, and I need an exercise that graduate students cannot answer by summarizing Spinoza's political works. The premise of the unit is that Spinoza's system is unusual among early modern theories because it makes right coextensive with power, so any verdict about an institution has to be reached by asking what actually increases the power and freedom of the multitude, not by asking what a sovereign is entitled to. The students have already read the standard material, so the exercise must move past the familiar claim that Spinoza treated democracy as the most natural form of state — a claim he never fully developed, since the Political Treatise is unfinished and stops just as its treatment of democracy begins. It should also engage the part of his work where he argued most directly for freedom of thought, speech, and worship, since that is where the stakes of the design below actually lie.

Here is the counterfactual. Spinoza is presented with a constitutional design that postdates him and that nobody in his century could have imagined in this form. It is called the Revision Chamber. Under this design, three hundred citizens are chosen by lot, stratified by neighborhood, from the adult population. Each member serves a single six-month term and can never serve again. Members are sequestered from lobbying and from contact with parties and organized interests. A permanent professional staff prepares the factual record but is barred from drafting the text of any decision. The Chamber has final authority over one narrow class of statutes — those that restrict speech, assembly, or association — and its decisions take effect unless overturned by a double supermajority in two successive general elections. Its deliberations are public, but individual votes are secret.

Write an entry of roughly 900 words that predicts Spinoza's assessment of the Revision Chamber, addressed to students who will be asked to attack it. Do not deliver a generic verdict that he would have favored more participation. Instead:

1. State, in the terms his system actually supplies, what the Chamber would have to accomplish for him to count it as increasing the freedom of the multitude rather than merely redistributing obedience. Be explicit about which of his commitments do the work here — for example the identification of right with power, his account of what holds a state together and what dissolves it, and his treatment of superstition as an effect of fear rather than as mere error.

2. Give the verdict, with the strongest reasons on each side, and say what would have to be true of the citizens under the design for the verdict to flip.

3. Identify at least two points at which the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by the surviving texts, and say for each what textual or structural feature of his system would settle it if we had it.

4. Close with the objection that a Spinoza loyalist would raise against your own prediction — for instance, that the secret ballot conflicts with his insistence that a state's strength lies in the coordinated power of the multitude rather than in the isolated choices of individuals, or that his exclusion of women from political authority sits badly with any reconstruction that treats the Chamber as a test of his democratic credentials.

The entry should be usable as a teaching text. It must name the specific Spinozistic premises each step depends on, and it must not attribute to him any position that rests on empirical facts he could not have had — where the verdict turns on such facts, mark the point as conditional and say what would have to be shown.
