Here's the problem, copied off the sheet:

"Two vials, A and B, sit on the bench. You run one assay. The printout does say which vial you used, but the label is smudged; all you can make out is that it is more likely to have been A, and you would now put 0.7 on A and 0.3 on B. You have not become certain of anything you were not already certain of. Let {A, B} be a partition of your sample space and let P be your prior credence function.

(a) Write down the rule you should use to update your credences in all your other propositions. State precisely what the rule requires of the partition, over and above the cells being exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and explain how the rule relates to ordinary conditionalization.

(b) Later you get a second smudged printout, this one bearing on a different question, whose answer is captured by another two-cell partition {C, ¬C}. You have to update on both readings. Does the order of the two updates matter? Justify your answer."

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) Let P′ be the new credence function. For any proposition X,

P′(X) = P′(A)·P(X|A) + P′(B)·P(X|B) = 0.7·P(X|A) + 0.3·P(X|B).

This is Jeffrey's rule (probability kinematics). It's a weighted average of my old conditional credences, with my new credences in the cells as the weights, so P′(X) always comes out somewhere between P(X|A) and P(X|B). The cells have to be exhaustive and mutually exclusive, otherwise the weights wouldn't be a genuine probability distribution; {A, B} plainly is, so the rule applies directly, and there's nothing further to check.

It generalizes ordinary conditionalization. If the smudge had gone the other way and I had become certain it was A, then P′(A) = 1 and P′(B) = 0, and the formula collapses to P′(X) = P(X|A), which is just conditioning on A. So strict conditionalization is the limiting case in which the new credence in one cell is 1. Note that I am not conditioning on A here — I never become certain of A, so I couldn't.

The rule holds the conditional credences P(X|A) and P(X|B) fixed (rigidity). This isn't really an extra assumption about the case, it's just what keeps the update from smuggling in information I don't have: any rule that shifted those conditionals around would be adding evidence rather than processing it.

(b) It doesn't matter. Two successive applications of the rule, like two successive conditionalizations, land you in the same place whichever order you take them in. That's part of what makes the rule convenient — you can process your evidence one item at a time as it comes in."
