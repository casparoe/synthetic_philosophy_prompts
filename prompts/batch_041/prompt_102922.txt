I write software for a living and have no formal training in philosophy or the social sciences, so if what follows turns out to be a familiar idea wearing a name I have never encountered, I would prefer to be told so directly. I have been reading about psychiatric diagnosis, and I keep noticing a pattern for which I cannot find a single word, and I want to know whether it has an established name, or whether it is really two patterns that have been bundled together.

The pattern runs roughly as follows. A category is introduced to describe a group of people — a diagnosis, a personality type, a label such as gifted or at risk. Once the category is in circulation, some of the people it picks out begin to understand themselves through it and to act in ways that fit it. Clinicians and researchers then observe that the people they are now seeing do not match the original description very well, and they revise the description. The revision changes how people understand themselves in turn, and the two keep adjusting to each other. There is no point at which the category can be said to have been simply correct about what was already there.

I think I can see this happening in two rather different ways, and I cannot tell whether they are the same thing. In the first, the people take up the label: they read about it, recognize themselves in it, join communities organized around it, and their conduct and self-description shift accordingly. In the second, the people never adopt the label at all; something else does the work. A school places a child in a separate track on the strength of a category, the placement changes what the child learns and how teachers treat her, and the resulting outcomes are then fed back into the data that justify the category. Here the loop appears to run entirely through institutions, without the labeled person's cooperation or even awareness.

Several comparisons have occurred to me, and none of them quite fits. This is not simply a self-fulfilling prophecy: a self-fulfilling prophecy makes a prediction come true, whereas here nothing becomes true so much as the description and the described drift together, and the drift need not stop. It is not people gaming a metric in the way Goodhart's law describes, because what changes is not only conduct but self-understanding, and in the second case above not even that. And it is not the observer effect of physics, because the things being observed can read the description of themselves and respond to it.

So: is there a name for this, and is there a body of work behind it? I would particularly like to know three things. First, whether the two directions I described — the category shaping the people, and the people reshaping the category — are usually treated as one phenomenon or as two. Second, what exactly makes a category capable of being affected by its own application, as opposed to categories such as having a fractured tibia or being allergic to penicillin, which seem immune to it. Third, whether the phenomenon is thought to belong specifically to human beings, or whether it extends to anything that can be described and then represent itself under the description. The third matters to me because I believe I see something similar in my own field, where categories such as technical debt or microservice seem to reshape the very practices they were coined to describe, and I want to know whether that is the same phenomenon or merely a loose analogy. If no single established term covers all of what I have described, I would rather you say so plainly, then propose a term, define it, and tell me which parts of what I have described fall outside its edges.
