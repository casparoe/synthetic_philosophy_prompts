My reading group friend has an arguement I'm 90% sure proves too much, but I've been staring at it for a week and I still can't say which joint is loose.

Setup: he's a Popper guy. The Poverty of Historicism is basically his scripture — no predictive science of history, historicism is bankrupt, all that. But he's stretched it into something a lot stronger and I don't trust the stretch.

Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) A genuine science of a domain has to be able to predict things in that domain. If a theory tells you nothing about what happens next, its not a theory of that domain, it's a description.
(2) The course of human history is strongly shaped by the growth of human knowledge.
(3) The growth of knowledge can't be predicted. To predict what we'll know tomorrow you'd have to know it today, and then it wouldn't be news — the prediction would already have done the thing it was predicting.
(4) So the course of human history can't be predicted.
(5) So there are no laws of history, no theoretical history, no science of historical development. (This is the bit he actually cares about.)

Then he adds what he calls the novelty-dependence point, and I'm fairly sure he made the name up:

(6) None of this is special to history. Take any domain whose later states depend on knowledge nobody has yet. You can't predict the content of the theorems that will get proved, or the technologies that will get invented, or the experimental result that kills the current theory.
(7) So by the same reasoning there's no theoretical biology (evolution keeps throwing up genuine novelty), no theoretical physics (the next theory is by definition unpredictable novelty), no theoretical economics (its later states hinge on inventions and discoveries).
(8) But that's obviously wrong — population biology, thermodynamics and price theory all make real predictions. So something in (1)–(5) has to go.

He says (3) is trivially true and (5) is the one he wants, so the guilty party must be (1) and he's happy to drop it. I'd sooner keep (1) and (3) than keep (5), which I think is the reverse of his ranking, and I can't tell if that's because he's wrong or because I'm squeamish about the conclusion. The upshot he draws is the usual one: no grand planning, only piecemeal reform, fix what's broken a bit at a time. Which bothers me, because piecemeal reform still means predicting what your reform is going to do — so if (4) holds, the piecemeal stuff should be dead too, and he doesn't seem to notice.

He also keeps adding that publishing a prediction changes what people do, so the prediction eats itself — he's got a name for it, Oedipus something — and says that makes (4) even safer.

Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I don't want to pay more than he does.
