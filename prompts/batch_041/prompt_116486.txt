I chair the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized contemporary art museum, and our next meeting has two proposals before it that have split the committee badly enough that I would rather circulate a written opinion beforehand than chair a fight. The first comes from an artist who spent four years training a generative model exclusively on her own archive of roughly thirty thousand drawings and now proposes to exhibit the model itself: a script samples from it once an hour and a plotter draws the sample on paper, so that over a three-month exhibition the wall accumulates drawings that no one, the artist included, has seen in advance. She would give us the trained weights, the script, a certificate, and the right to re-run the work whenever we reinstall it. The second is a suite of five large prints made by a curator who spent a weekend writing text prompts into a commercial image model, selected five outputs, and framed them. Our registrar needs the ordinary fields filled in for anything we take — medium, dimensions, edition, and a single object to which condition reports and insurance attach — and I have found that I cannot fill in those fields for either proposal without first deciding what we think the artwork is, and that no two members of the committee agree about that. Half of us want to settle the matter by asking whether the machine is creative or whether the artist is; my own suspicion is that this is the wrong question, and that the real work is ontological — what the object is, whether it is a type or a token, whether the plotter is part of it, whether the model is a medium or a collaborator — but I need the opinion to show that rather than assert it. We do already own conceptual works that consist of a written procedure plus a certificate and are re-executed each time they are shown, and I would like the memo to say whether that precedent actually settles cases like these or only appears to.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action on each proposal — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and generalize that into a policy with criteria our reviewers can apply to the next submission of this kind; and it should engage seriously rather than dismiss the two members most likely to dissent, namely the one who argues that we should simply acquire the individual drawings on paper, since that is what a visitor sees and what a wall can hold, and the one who argues that a work which can be regenerated at will has no fixed identity, so that our apparatus of provenance, condition, and edition has nothing to attach to. I want it to take and defend a position on whether the unpredictability of the output bears on authorship at all — the artist cannot foresee any particular drawing; does that make her less its author, or is it continuous with the procedures artists have long used to hand execution over to a process? — on whether the two proposals should come out the same way or differently, and why, and on what the medium and edition fields should actually say. Close by stating plainly what would have to be true for the recommendation to be wrong; I would rather the document be falsifiable than unanimous.
