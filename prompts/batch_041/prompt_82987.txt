Had a long argument with a friend last night about Benacerraf's dilemma and I want to see if my position survives contact with somebody who isn't him.

My position: the dilemma is genuine and can't be dissolved, only paid for. You can't have both the semantics you want (arithmetical sentences are true or false in virtue of how things stand with numbers, which are objects of some kind) and the epistemology you want (our beliefs about those objects are justified in some fairly normal way, causal or otherwise). Something has to give, and I've decided to give on the epistemology side: I'll take platonism and just accept that my access story is going to be a story about grasping structures and inferential patterns rather than about bumping into objects. I also think my friend's move is a cheat, or at least not a solution: saying that 'there are numbers' doesn't commit you to objects the way 'there are chairs' does. That feels like it does all the work with a hand-wave about how the little word 'are' behaves, and I dont buy it.

What would count against me? I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a platonist would themselves admit counts against them versus which they'd wave away. Which escape routes from the dilemma are real and which are just verbal? And specifically: what would show that my 'pick a horn and pay' framing is itself the cheat — e.g. if the dilemma only bites because we've assumed there's a single best semantics for arithmetic, or because we've assumed knowledge needs a causal or counterfactual link to its object, then it's an artifact of two optional assumptions and I should stop talking about it like a structural feature of the subject.

Two things I'd especially like pushed on. First, is the dilemma really one problem or two? There's the reference question (how our terms latch onto abstracta at all) and the reliability question (why our beliefs about them are any good), and I suspect most proposed solutions quietly fix one and leave the other dangling. Does splitting it help me, or does it just hand me two dilemmas? Relatedly — should any solution have to generalise, i.e. also work for logic, modality, and moral properties? Or does insisting on that make the dilemma artificially hard? Second, my friend's annoying point: a language model that learned arithmetic from text has no causal contact with numbers whatsoever, and we'd still say it knows that 7+5=12. Is that a counterexample to the epistemology horn, or is it exactly the case that shows causal contact was never the real issue? If it's the latter, tell me what the real issue is.

And say what would change your mind here, or where you think this reasoning is most likely to fall apart.
