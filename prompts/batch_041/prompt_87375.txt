I write about science and ideas, and for the past couple of years I have been reporting on the fertility industry. Along the way I have accumulated a set of gut verdicts about embryo selection that I cannot get to line up, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they express, and where I have to give ground.

Selecting an embryo free of a fatal childhood disease such as Tay-Sachs: not merely permitted but, it seems to me, what a responsible parent does.

Hearing parents choosing against hereditary deafness: fine.

Deaf parents choosing, through preimplantation testing, for a deaf child: the clinic should refuse.

A gene variant that carries a modestly elevated risk of depression but is also associated with unusual empathy and conscientiousness, which the parents want to screen out: I keep going back and forth.

Choosing among five embryos for the one with the highest polygenic score for educational attainment, where the score explains only a sliver of the variance and its causal effect is disputed: permitted, though not obligatory.

Choosing the lighter-skinned of two embryos in a society that discriminates against darker skin: wrong, and not merely for its side effects; the wrongness seems to lie in the capitulation.

Choosing the taller embryo because the parents believe tall people are happier: I am uneasy, and I think I would say no.

Creating a child who will be a lifelong tissue match for an existing sibling, where the new child will be loved and well cared for: permitted.

Selecting for sex when the parents already have two boys and want a girl: wrong, even though I cannot say the resulting child is worse off.

Choosing the embryo whose temperament points toward cheerfulness, even though I could not defend the claim that a cheerful life is objectively better than a gloomy one: permitted.

Choosing against an embryo that would merely be a carrier of a recessive condition, so that the child will not face the choice later: I lean toward permitting it, though the child is in no way worse off.

The literature has a name for the view that parents should choose the child with the best expected life. I am less interested in the name than in whether any version of it survives this list, and my own attempt does not. My tentative principle is this: selection is permissible when it removes a burden the child would otherwise carry, and impermissible when it adds a trait the parents want for their own sake, where a burden means something the child would experience as a limitation rather than something others would treat as one. That handles some of the list and mangles the rest.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to cases not on this list; for each clause, the case that forces it; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them I should abandon. Please also say what it predicts for a case I have not mentioned: screening against a condition whose badness is half biological and half stigma. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and identify the single step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
