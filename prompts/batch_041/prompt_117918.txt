I'm a retired field biologist — thirty-odd years on invertebrates, then a long stretch on seabirds — and since I hung it up I've been reading alot of philosophy for fun, which is how I ended up as the token scientist on the animal welfare committee at our regional aquarium. It's unpaid, four meetings a year, and I'm the only member who isn't a vet, a keeper, or the woman whose family paid for the shark tunnel. I need you to draft a written opinion for me, the kind of thing I circulate a few days before the vote. Plain continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. We send these around as plain text and half the board reads them on a phone.

The item is the capital budget. The big-ticket proposal is a rebuild of the octopus tank, and there are three ways it could go. One is a much larger, more changeable tank: puzzle feeders, dens we can rearrange, live prey on a schedule, water quality rigged so keepers can vary things without stressing the animal. Two is a cheaper tank that looks natural but is basically static — same rock, same feeding time, every day. Three is to get out of cephalopods entirely and put the money into the sea turtle rehab unit, which is where the same dollars would go otherwise. Staff are split on this and have gotten more invested than I expected, so whatever I write needs to be something they can all live with or at least respond to.

My problem is that I don't have a principled way to weigh the options, and I keep reaching for a scheme out of Daniel Dennett's book Kinds of Minds that I only half understand. He sorts creatures into four grades. Darwinian ones are just what natural selection made them, so getting the temperature and the substrate right is most of the story. Skinnerian ones learn by reinforcement, so what matters to them is whether the world is predictable and whether their actions get them anything. Popperian ones can test a hypothesis inside their own heads rather than out on the reef, which I take to mean that the cruel thing to do to them is to take away control — they need room to be wrong safely. And Gregorian ones, mostly us, use tools and borrow other minds' tools, so they need the tools and the company.

What I want out of this memo: tell me which grade the evidence actually licenses us to assume for an octopus, and be honest that the evidence is thin and that we may be stuck between two grades. Then say what each grade demands in concrete tank terms, so a keeper can read it and know what to build. Then give me a recommendation with a date attached — approve one of the options, approve one with conditions, or turn it down and say what happens to the animal. Handle the two objections I know are coming. One board member will say this is anthropomorphism dressed up as biology and that a mollusk doesn't care one way or the other. Another will say that if we're spending this kind of money on something that lives two years, we should spend it on the turtles. I'd rather have those answered properly than have you tell me what I want to hear, including the part where the objectors might be right. And one seperate thing I'd really like to get right in front of the board: if there's an observation the keepers could actually make — something the animal does with what we give it — that would tell us whether we're dealing with a learning machine or with something that can think ahead, tell me what that observation is.
