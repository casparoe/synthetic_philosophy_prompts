I'm running a one-off evening session for a local film club - about twenty people, they argue about movies constantly, and they all walk in already convinced that taste is subjective and that anyone who says otherwise is doing snobbery in a fancy accent. Topic is Hume on the standard of taste. Dont explain Hume to me, I've read the essay; plan how I should explain it in 75 minutes with a whiteboard and no handouts.

I want the order the ideas have to go in, so the puzzle lands before the solution does. My instinct is the whole thing collapses if I say there's a standard before they've felt the problem, but I'm not sure the problem they'll feel is the one Hume actually has. What has to come first, second, third? Where exactly does the move from beauty-is-a-sentiment to some-tastes-are-absurd have to sit, and what has to be true of them by then for that move not to look like a swindle?

The example. I keep reaching for the hogshead story Hume tells - two guys taste the wine, one says leather, one says iron, everyone laughs at them, then they empty the barrel and there's a leather strap and an iron key at the bottom. I like it, but I'm not sure it can carry the whole session, since it's really about delicacy of perception and my audience's problem is whether there's anything to be right or wrong about at all. If it can carry it, say what it has to be doing at each stage. If it cant, give me the one case that does the work, and tell me what it needs (a hidden fact? a third party who checks? something they already agree on?) and why the obvious candidates - I like vanilla, you like chocolate; the guy who thinks a famously bad movie is a masterpiece - will backfire.

Then the misconceptions in the order they'll actually arise, and where in my sequence to defuse each. I'm expecting: so Hume's a relativist after all; so he does think beauty is a real property and just hid it; the standard is a rule or a checklist, which is what my students always memorize as the five qualities of the good critic and then cant use; the true judge is just the academy, or the canon, or whoever has credentials; delicacy means liking difficult things; freedom from prejudice means having no preferences. And one I only half expect: that the true judge is supposed to actually exist, rather than being a construct Hume admits you'll basically never find. Tell me what I'm missing, especially misconceptions that form late, after the solution is already on the board.

I also want to know what to leave out and say I'm leaving out. Candidates: the whole realist/anti-realist fight over whether Hume is a projectivist, the comparison with Kant, the fact that his own examples are pretty Eurocentric and that this may be a problem for the standard he wants, and the Treatise background. Which can I skip without the session becoming a lie, and which do I need to flag as a complication I'm not resolving?

Finally the check for understanding. What do I ask at the end that shows they've got the shape of the view rather than just remembered that Hume has five criteria? I want something where the tempting answer is wrong.
