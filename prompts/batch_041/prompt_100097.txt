I run the research desk for a city council that hears roughly a dozen evidence-bearing claims per hearing — from agencies, consultants, advocacy groups, and members of the public — and before each vote I have to issue a one-page warrant sheet for every claim: what kind of warrant it rests on, how strong that warrant is, and what would defeat it. My council is impatient, my analysts are bright but not philosophers, and we are wrong in public when we get this wrong. The procedure has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by someone with a bachelor's degree and no training in Indian philosophy, and it has to be countersigned by me before it goes in the packet. I have been reading about classical Nyāya epistemology, and I think its account of the pramāṇas — the recognized means of valid cognition — fits this job better than the generic "is it peer-reviewed" heuristic we use now, precisely because it was built for a setting in which perception, inference, analogy, and testimony are weighed against one another instead of everything being collapsed into a study. Please turn it into a procedure: the steps in order, what counts as passing each step, and the exit points at which the sheet must say that no warrant exists or that the warrant is insufficient and the question goes to the council unresolved.

Be explicit about which parts of the Nyāya apparatus you are keeping and which you are dropping as unusable at our level. On perception: what, if anything, survives for a body that mostly reads documents rather than observes events. On inference: the conditions a good inferential mark has to satisfy — that it be present in the case under consideration, that it be found wherever the conclusion holds, and that it be absent wherever the conclusion fails — and what to do with a mark that is merely inconclusive because it also turns up in cases where the conclusion does not hold. On analogy: claims of the form "this worked in Portland, so it will work here," where the respect in which the two cases are supposed to be alike has to be stated rather than assumed. On testimony: the requirement that a speaker be competent on the matter and truth-directed in saying it, and what evidence of that we can actually demand from a witness in a hearing rather than from a treatise. Then handle two items that the tradition disputed rather than agreed on — postulation, where you posit an unobserved fact because it is the only way to account for what you do observe, and non-apprehension, where the absence of a finding is itself treated as a finding — and for each say whether we should admit it as a pramāṇa at all, under what conditions, and flag it on the sheet as a contested extension rather than settled doctrine, since I gather the schools disagreed about how many pramāṇas there are and I do not want my analysts thinking there is one official list.

Finally, build in the defeat conditions: what it takes for a claim that has cleared the earlier steps to be knocked down by something else, including the case where two warrants of ours point in opposite directions and we must say which one yields. If the tradition's treatment of what counts as losing an argument, as distinct from being wrong, bears on how we should word the sheet's verdict, fold that in too. And tell me honestly where the ordering of warrants is a value judgment dressed as a rule, because I will not have the sheet pretending to a precision it does not have. Then run the procedure on three claims from last month's hearing: a consultant's projection that a transit line will raise adjacent property values by a stated percentage, resting on a regression over other cities; an advocacy group's claim that a chemical in the water supply is safe because no study has shown harm at current levels; and a public-health official's claim, resting on her agency's own surveillance data, that a clinic closure will increase emergency admissions. I expect at least one of these to come out against the side my council would prefer, and I would rather see that than a procedure that flatters everyone.
