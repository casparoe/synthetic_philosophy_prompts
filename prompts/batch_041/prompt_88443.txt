I was diagnosed with a degenerative condition earlier this year, and the abstract question of what distributive equality ought to be equalising has become uncomfortably concrete for me. I should like to practise defending the capability approach against its most serious rival. Please play a committed resourcist — the sort who answers handicaps with a hypothetical insurance market rather than with a schedule of functionings — and play them well: concede that conversion handicaps are real and that a bare resource share looks arbitrary, but never concede that the state must rank ways of living, and press the indeterminacy problem, that capability sets are only partially ordered, so equality of capability often has no determinate content. Stay in character until I tell you to stop, reply in two or three paragraphs, argue rather than lecture, and if one of my moves is ambiguous, ask me to disambiguate it rather than guessing.

My opening move: you compensate me with more resources, but compensation requires a conversion rate — how many pounds to a flight of stairs — and the only non-arbitrary way to fix that rate is to say what the resources are for, which is to smuggle in a schedule of functionings after all. You cannot fall back on ordinary market prices, since the market values my access to a building at whatever the owner thinks it is worth, not at what it costs me in capability; and you cannot fall back on the hypothetical insurance market either, since it settles what I am owed by asking what a prudent person would have insured against, which already presupposes a view about which losses matter. So either resources are indexed to functionings, or I am equal on paper and unequal in life.
