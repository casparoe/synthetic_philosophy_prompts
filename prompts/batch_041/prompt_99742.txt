I run a small algorithmic-accountability unit inside a state human-services agency. Several times a year we are asked to sign off — or to refuse to sign off — on predictive models that allocate scarce public resources: which families are offered a preventive-services slot, which applicants are routed into a job-training program, which blocks are scheduled for a lead-abatement crew. The models arrive already built, with an accuracy figure attached and a vendor's assurance that the tool is fair. The decision is ours.

The difficulty is the impossibility results. A model that is calibrated within groups, that is balanced across groups in its average scores at a given risk level, and that has equal error rates across groups cannot in general have all three at once unless the base rates are equal or the model is perfect, and we can neither equalize base rates nor build a perfect model. So the parity properties are a menu, and every choice off that menu is a choice about which group bears which kind of error. The vendor's assurance is therefore not evidence of anything; it is a choice of criterion dressed up as a property of the tool.

Write me a decision procedure my analysts can actually run on a model-and-use-case package. It should take the package as input and produce a written determination that names, explicitly, which parity property we are treating as binding here, which we are treating as a target we will trade against, which we are knowingly sacrificing, and why. It has to force us to specify the real costs of false positives and false negatives for the particular decision at hand, and to say what we do when no defensible cost specification exists — because none of the standard parity criteria look at those costs, and a model that looks acceptable on any of them can still be the wrong thing to deploy. It has to cover the case where satisfying a parity property would require using the protected attribute, or a close proxy for it, at decision time, and the case where the relevant attribute is contested, self-reported, or simply unmeasured in our data. It has to make us state what label the model is predicting and why that label is the right object of concern, since a model can be impeccable with respect to a proxy that has nothing to do with the harm we are trying to prevent. And it has to mark, at each step, whether the step is a technical computation, a judgment call in technical clothing, or an outright value decision that must be escalated, naming who signs off.

Then run the procedure on two cases: a tool the agency badly wants and that will pass every check we can run, and a tool whose honest cost specification points toward refusing deployment altogether. Say plainly what the procedure cannot settle, where it will misfire, and where it will produce a defensible answer that nobody in the room will be happy to hear. We have three analysts, no lawyers on staff, about two weeks per review, and the final signature is mine.
