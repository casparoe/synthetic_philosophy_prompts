I have been reading about the familiar cases of simultaneous discovery in mathematics and biology, and I keep noticing a pattern that the usual vocabulary—multiple discovery, simultaneous invention, priority dispute—does not quite capture. It is not merely that two people hit on the same result at about the same time. It is that their results are not the same object: one may be a computational technique, the other a conceptual reorganisation; one may explain why a phenomenon occurs, the other why it persists; one may be framed in terms of a special case, the other in terms of a general principle. Later, textbooks and credit conventions treat them as one discovery, name it once, and award priority as if both had been aiming at that single thing. The distinctive commitments of each version are then sorted into anticipations, precursors, or mere alternative proofs, and the discovery that gets transmitted is a composite that neither person actually produced. The composite can be more powerful than either original, but it also makes the question of who discovered it look either obvious or meaningless, when the real problem is that the unit of attribution was assembled after the fact.

I would like to know whether there is an established name for this phenomenon, and if not, what it should be called. The nearest terms I can think of are multiple discovery, simultaneous invention, co-discovery, priority dispute, and Whig history, but each seems to miss something: multiple discovery presupposes that the same thing was discovered more than once; Whig history describes retrospective selection of what counts as important; priority dispute describes the social fight over credit. What I am after is the retrospective fusion of heterogeneous independent achievements into a single canonical discovery, and the way that fusion changes what credit, inevitability, and even the content of the discovery mean. I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, how it differs from each of those neighbours, where its boundaries lie—for example, when the fusion is legitimate synthesis rather than distortion, when the discoverers themselves do the fusing, and when one version is simply a special case of the other—and whether it has any bearing on whether discoveries are inevitable or on how priority should be assigned. Please be explicit about how confident you are in any terminological or historical claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
