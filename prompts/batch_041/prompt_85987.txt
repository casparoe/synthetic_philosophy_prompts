I am a PhD student working on the philosophy of self-binding commitments, and I keep returning to a puzzle that the legal and philosophical literature has not resolved for me. Consider a concrete case. A jurisdiction operates a centralized gambling self-exclusion scheme. A person who believes they have a gambling problem can register once, and from that moment every licensed operator in the jurisdiction must block them from opening or using an account; the registration cannot be undone for a fixed minimum period, often a year or more. Enforcement is not left to the registrant's willpower. Operators must run checks, and a registrant who manages to gamble anyway may be treated as trespassing, may have winnings confiscated, and may face other penalties. The scheme is justified in the language of protecting the vulnerable and supporting the person's own earlier choice, and it is often described as a way for a person to bind their future self.

Now place beside it a second practice. Psychiatric advance directives allow a person, while competent, to specify what treatment they want or refuse if they later become incapacitated. In many jurisdictions, a competent refusal of treatment in such a directive is legally binding on clinicians even when the patient, in a later episode, pleads for the treatment. But a directive that requests admission or treatment is often not enforceable in the same way: a clinician cannot admit a patient who is currently refusing simply because the patient's earlier competent self asked for it, unless the current legal criteria for involuntary commitment, typically some version of danger to self or others, are independently met. The past self can bind the present self to refuse, but cannot bind the present self to accept.

I want an essay that treats these two practices as a single object of analysis and asks what philosophical commitments they presuppose. Specifically:

1. What theory of the person, or of the will, does each practice presuppose? Does it require a distinction between an authentic or higher-order self and a merely present or lower-order self, and if so, how is the authentic self identified without circularity? The gambling scheme seems to treat the registering self as authoritative over the gambling self. The psychiatric directive seems to treat the refusing self as authoritative over the requesting self, but only in one direction.

2. Does the practice presuppose that the binding is a form of self-legislation, or is it better understood as the state or a third party enforcing a private contract against one of the parties? If the latter, what makes the contract valid when the enforcing party is not a neutral arbiter but the state, and when the breach is not a harm to a second party but a deviation from one's own plan?

3. What theory of rationality or of time-slice neutrality does the practice need? Must it hold that preferences are ranked by their origin (calm versus aroused, competent versus incapacitated) rather than by their content? Does that commit it to a controversial view about which mental states count as authoritative, and can that view be stated without begging the question against the present self?

4. Is the asymmetry between the two practices philosophically defensible, or is it an artifact of legal categories such as dangerousness, capacity, and the state's police power? If we tried to make the two practices consistent, which way should we go: make psychiatric requests enforceable, or make gambling registrations revocable on demand? What would each direction cost?

I also want the essay to consider whether the same structure appears in constitutional precommitment, where a past supermajority binds a future majority through entrenchment clauses or rights that cannot be amended by ordinary legislation. Is the implicit theory of the self in individual Ulysses contracts the same as the implicit theory of the collective self in constitutional entrenchment, or does the analogy break down because a polity has no single will to be authentic or inauthentic? If the analogy holds, what does that imply about the legitimacy of entrenchment; if it fails, what does that imply about the individual case?

I want the essay to reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as possible, then to distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. It should say where the practices are underdetermined, where several incompatible theories of the self would license the same institutional arrangement, and where they are genuinely committed. It should also consider whether the practices presuppose a contested view about the value of autonomy: for instance, whether autonomy is served by honoring a past choice or by honoring the present one, and whether that question can be answered without already assuming a theory of the self.

The essay should reach a conclusion. It should not merely survey the possible positions; it should say which philosophical commitments the practices actually need, which are optional, and whether the asymmetry can be justified. I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list. I want the argument.
