My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and I have written up the position I ended up defending so that I can get an outside view. I would like your comments on the draft below. I am most interested in three things: whether the formal claims are correct, whether the central idea is genuinely interesting or only a terminological variant of something standard, and where the argument is weakest. Please give me a committed response. Do not just lay out the options and leave the choice to me; tell me which repair you think is right, or whether the whole line should be abandoned, and give your reasons.

Draft:

The Kalai-Smorodinsky solution for two-person bargaining takes a convex feasible set S of utility pairs, a disagreement point d = (d1, d2), and the ideal point a = (a1, a2), where a1 is the largest u1 such that (u1, u2) is in S for some u2, and a2 is the largest u2 such that (u1, u2) is in S for some u1. It then draws the ray from d to a and selects the feasible point on that ray farthest from d. The result equalizes proportional gains: each bargainer receives the same fraction of the gain from disagreement to their own ideal.

My thesis is that this proportional-gain reading gives the solution a fairness rationale that the Nash solution lacks. Nash maximizes the product of the gains, which makes the division depend only on the local shape of the Pareto frontier near the solution. Kalai-Smorodinsky makes the division depend on how much each bargainer could have hoped for. If one party's ideal improves while the frontier near the Nash point is unchanged, Nash leaves the division alone and Kalai-Smorodinsky moves toward that party. I think that is the right response: a party who could do better in the feasible set has a stronger claim, and the solution should register it.

My friend's main objection was that this just rewards threat power. I replied that the ideal point is not the same as a threat: it is a feature of the feasible set, not a credible unilateral action. But I am not sure the reply is adequate. If a bargainer's ideal is high only because the feasible set contains outcomes that are terrible for the other party, it is not obvious that this should strengthen the first party's claim. That led me to a repair.

The repair is to replace the unrestricted ideal with a constrained ideal. Define a*1 as the largest u1 such that (u1, u2) is in S and u2 is at least d2, and a*2 as the largest u2 such that (u1, u2) is in S and u1 is at least d1. Then draw the Kalai-Smorodinsky ray from d to a* = (a*1, a*2) and take the farthest feasible point. Call this the constrained Kalai-Smorodinsky solution. The thought is that a bargainer should not get credit for an ideal that requires the other bargainer to fall below disagreement. Only outcomes that both could accept as individually rational should set the aspirations.

Here is my worry. In the simplest transferable-utility case, where S is the set of pairs with u1 + u2 at most M and both utilities at least their disagreement values, the constrained ideal is (M - d2, M - d1). The ray from d to that point crosses the frontier at its midpoint, and the resulting division is exactly the Nash division. So in the linear case the repair does not produce a distinct solution; it produces Nash. That makes me suspect that the constrained ideal is either the correct ideal and Kalai-Smorodinsky was never really distinct from Nash in the cases that matter, or that the unrestricted ideal is essential and the fairness rationale I gave is wrong. I cannot tell which.

I also have a formal worry about the unrestricted version. If the feasible set is not convex or not comprehensive, the ray from d to a may intersect the boundary more than once, and the farthest feasible point may not be Pareto optimal in any obvious sense. I have been assuming convexity and comprehensiveness, but I do not know how much the argument depends on those assumptions.

What I want from you is a verdict. Is the constrained ideal the right repair, or does it collapse the view into Nash? If it does collapse in the linear case, is there a non-transferable-utility or non-convex case where the two solutions still come apart in a way that vindicates the proportional-gain idea? If the unrestricted ideal is the right one, what is the best defense of using aspirations that the other party could not rationally accept? Please be specific about which claims in the draft are false or overstated, and end by telling me what you think I should conclude.
