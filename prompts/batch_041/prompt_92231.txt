My labmate Priya and I were at the pub with our colleague Dev, and the two of them got into it over whether Dev should publish something. Dev has worked out a trick that gets around a safety filter a lot of AI companies rely on — not a catastrophe by itself, but the kind of thing that, once it's out, makes the filter basically useless — and he wants to put it on arXiv with full details. Priya said he shouldn't. Dev said (1) it's just knowledge, and secrecy only means the good guys are the ones who don't know. (2) If it's a real flaw, publishing forces a fix; if it's not, no harm. Priya said (3) that's exactly the problem: he's judging the risk acceptable, but there might be fifty people in the same spot, and the release only needs to be judged acceptable by one of them, so the fact that *he* thinks it's fine is close to no evidence at all — anyone who'd release it thinks it's fine by definition. She said there's a name for this, some curse-of-the-unilateralist thing. Dev shot back (4) that this cuts his way: if someone else will publish something equivalent anyway, the outcome is the same whether or not he does it, so refusing only costs him the credit and hands the release to someone less careful. Priya said (5) that's like saying someone will steal the bike anyway so I may as well take it — another likely wrong doesn't license this one, and "someone less careful" is a story he's telling himself. Dev said (6) the difference is the bike has an owner and knowledge doesn't; and anyway (7) she's comparing a certainty to a maybe — she can't put a number on how likely the misuse is, so refusing to publish is just precaution with no stopping point, and precaution with no stopping point is its own kind of irrationality. I couldn't referee this at all. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right on each point — especially (3), (4) and (7) — and say what both of them seem to be missing?
