I'm writing a long piece on the Nordic model and keep running up against something I can't put into philosophical terms. The arrangement is familiar enough: tax rates among the highest in the world, welfare and public services correspondingly generous, and yet strikingly high voluntary compliance — people file honestly, and in some of these countries the declared incomes of private citizens are public record, open to any neighbour's curiosity. What philosophical commitments does this combination presuppose? Does it require a particular view of human motivation — that people are moved by reciprocity and fair play rather than by self-interest — or can a hard-headed story about enforcement, Hobbesian at root, explain the very same facts? Does the public disclosure of incomes presuppose that paying tax is a civic act rather than a private transaction, and if so, what conception of the state does that carry — contract, general will, civic friendship, or something else again? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival views would each license it. And finally: if the presupposed view were replaced — with a view of taxation as pure extraction, say — what in the practice would have to change, and would the change fall on the compliance, on the disclosure, or on both?
