I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a taxonomist, and I want her professional judgments to remain coherent even though I am not a biologist myself. Much of the plot turns on her deciding where one species ends and another begins, and I have already committed to four of her rulings in earlier chapters.

First: two populations of an alpine frog meet and interbreed freely along a ridge, and she can find no consistent difference between them in form or in genes; she counts them as one species.

Second: two populations of a moth share a meadow but breed in different seasons and differ in wing pattern and in genetics; she counts them as two species.

Third: two populations of a beetle are each other's closest relatives and look quite different, but they exchange genes across a narrow hybrid zone, and she judges that the gene flow keeps them together; she counts them as one species.

Fourth: a single butterfly population displays a continuous range of wing color, with every intermediate present and free interbreeding; she counts it as one species.

In the next chapter she must rule on a ring of populations encircling a desert basin. Each interbreeds with its neighbors the whole way around the ring, but where the two ends of the ring meet, on the far side of the basin, they live side by side without interbreeding. I would like to know which way she will rule, but more than that I would like to know the rule she has actually been following. My suspicion is that several different rules would fit the four rulings I have already written, that they come apart on the ring, and that I have been quietly smuggling in a criterion I never consciously chose. Tell me which rules fit, what each would predict about the ring, which of them you would bet on and why, and what single case I should write next in order to force the question.
