I am a novelist at work on a book about people who must commit themselves to a course of action under conditions they cannot truly calculate, and I have been reading Keynes's Treatise on Probability alongside the presidential commission's report on the loss of the shuttle Challenger. The explanations I keep meeting for that cold January morning in 1986 — groupthink, schedule pressure, the slow normalization of anomalous data — all seem to treat the engineers and the managers as though their error were simply an excess of confidence, a number set too low. Keynes gives me a sharper instrument. His claim, as I understand it, is that a probability judgment is only part of what a rational agent has to weigh: the same probability may rest on a great deal of relevant evidence or on almost none, and this second dimension — the weight of the argument, the sheer quantity of evidence standing behind the estimate — is an independent ground for caution that cannot be folded back into the probability itself. What I picture is a set of contractor engineers holding a very low estimate of O-ring failure that was nonetheless supported by a thin and nearly temperature-blind record, and a set of NASA managers overriding them not because they possessed better evidence but because the evidence everyone had was treated as sufficient.

I would like you to run the Challenger launch decision through this apparatus and then tell me honestly where it strains. Concretely: identify the agents and the judgments each of them actually held; say what Keynes's framework implies they should have done once the weight of the evidence, and not merely its probability, is taken into account; and bring in his later distinction between situations where we can reasonably attach probabilities and situations of genuine uncertainty, where the very terms of the problem are unstable and agents fall back on convention and on what he called animal spirits, to ask which kind of situation the launch decision really was. Then reverse the lens. What does the episode show about Keynes's own framework — does it vindicate the idea that weight is a genuine and separate dimension of evidential support, or does the case suggest that weight is only a second-order probability in disguise, or a proxy for something organizational that has no real place in a theory of rational belief? And be candid about what the theory cannot reach: the managerial and economic pressures, the burden-of-proof conventions that governed the discussion, and the uncomfortable fact that the engineers' caution may itself have rested on thin evidence. I care less about a verdict on who was culpable than about a careful mapping in both directions, theory onto case and case back onto theory, with the places where Keynes simply has nothing to say marked as such rather than forced to speak.
