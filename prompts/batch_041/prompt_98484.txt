I'm in a graduate seminar on interpretation, and I've gotten stuck on the old dispute about whether a text's meaning is fixed by its author's intention. What I can't shake is that the two sides seem to be answering different questions without noticing it. I want to distinguish (a) the intention to mean something by the words — the speaker's meaning, what the author was using the sentence to say — from (b) the intention to produce a particular effect in a reader, the aesthetic or rhetorical design behind the whole work. My half-formed view is that interpretation should track (a) and not (b), and that the familiar objections — the charge that appeal to intention is a fallacy, the possibility of meanings the author never entertained, the fact that authors are often unreliable about their own texts — all land on (b), or on a cartoon version of (a), and so leave a moderate actual-intentionalism standing.

Give me your credence that this view is right — that once the two intentions are separated, the author's intention in sense (a) is what determines the text's meaning — and decompose it rather than handing me a bare number. I want the sub-questions it turns on, each with a rough probability: whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about what an author meant in the hard cases; whether the audience's conventions, rather than any individual's intention, do the real work of fixing meaning; whether "meaning" in the interpretive sense is the kind of thing that can be correct or incorrect independently of what competent readers actually do with it; and whatever else you think the number depends on. Show me how the terms combine, tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or piece of evidence would move you most and in which direction. For what it's worth, I'd put myself at about 0.65; if you land far from there, I'd like to know which sub-question we most likely differ on.
