I run a monthly reading group at the software company where I work: twelve to sixteen people, mostly engineers, none of us with formal philosophy training beyond what we read together. The next session runs ninety minutes, and we have just finished a chapter on Lakatos's account of scientific research programmes. I want the session built around an activity rather than a discussion, and I want people to leave having felt in their hands the thing that is easy to nod along to and hard to believe: that there is no procedure for deciding, in real time, whether a line of work is progressive or degenerating, and that the distinction may only ever be available in hindsight.

Design that activity. Commit to one design — choose the hidden mechanism, the scoring numbers, the round structure and the moment of reveal, and justify your choices rather than handing me variants to pick between.

The rules should do the work, not my closing remarks. Set up two or three teams defending rival programmes, each with a stipulated core that is off-limits and a belt they may patch freely; have a hidden world generate observations round by round; let each team either accommodate the anomaly with an auxiliary hypothesis, stake points on a novel prediction, or abandon the programme for a lump sum. Arrange the payoffs so that accommodation is cheap and pays off immediately while the genuinely progressive move is costly and only pays later, and arrange the timing so that when the session ends the team that has been patching is not obviously losing. Include at least one round in which a team can score for a prediction that is novel in the letter and empty in substance, so that the group finds out for itself that the criterion can be gamed.

I need from you: the setting and materials, with no real money; roles, including whether to seat a two-person funding committee that allocates a budget each round; the hidden rule; the full payoff scheme; a round-by-round timeline with the reveal pinned to a definite minute; the debrief, mapping moments of play onto the hard core, the protective belt, the heuristics, and the progressive/degenerating distinction; the three or four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with what to do about each; and a frank paragraph on which part of Lakatos's position this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to state in my own words. Assume no philosophy background on my side — define terms where you use them, and write the rules as something I can read aloud.
