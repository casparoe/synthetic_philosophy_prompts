A lecturer of mine summarised Leibniz's ethics something like this, and I'm trying to work out how much of it to trust. "Leibniz's whole moral philosophy is captured in his formula that justice is the charity of the wise. What that means is that right action isn't a matter of obeying rules or commands; it's universal benevolence guided by knowledge of the good. Love, for Leibniz, is taking pleasure in the perfection and happiness of another, so the just person is someone who delights in the good of all and acts accordingly. He distinguishes three degrees of natural right: strict right, which is the bare requirement not to harm others; equity, which adds a duty of benevolence and fair dealing; and piety, which is the love of God and the highest form of justice — though he treats piety as a matter of private devotion rather than public law. These aren't three separate codes but a single ascending scale, with piety at the top. Because he grounds morality in reason rather than in God's commands, Leibniz is often seen as a forerunner of secular ethics: moral truths are eternal truths of reason, independent of any will, human or divine. He also thinks this is the best of all possible worlds, so while evil exists, it's always a necessary means to a greater good, and we should accept the order of the universe rather than trying to improve it. His critics say this makes him a complacent optimist, but his defenders reply that his view is really about the love of God as the highest good, which is why he thinks true justice requires piety."

I'm inclined to think the lecturer is basically right about the charity-of-the-wise formula but has probably got something wrong about the religion side of things, though I cant put my finger on it. Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what a Leibniz specialist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong? I'd especially like to know whether the bits about secular ethics and about accepting the world as it is follow from anything Leibniz actually held, or whether they're distortions of a more nuanced position. And does the closing criticism — that this makes him a complacent optimist — survive once the summary is corrected?
