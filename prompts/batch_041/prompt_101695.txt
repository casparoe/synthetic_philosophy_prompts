I'm slowly grinding through Aquinas's bit on war in the Summa (the "whether it is always sinful to wage war" question) and I've got the three conditions roughly down, but the third one is eating me alive. Here's the passage:

"Thirdly, it is necessary that the belligerents should have a rightful intention, so that they intend the advancement of good, or the avoidance of evil. [...] For it may happen that the war is declared by the legitimate authority, and for a just cause, and yet be rendered unlawful through a wicked intention. Hence Augustine says [...] 'The passion for inflicting harm, the cruel thirst for vengeance, an unpacific and relentless spirit, the fever of revolt, the lust of power, and such like things, all these are rightly condemned in war.'"

My seminar leader waved this around and claimed its the real reason you can't just say soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals -- that once you take "rightful intention" seriously, the whole moral-equality-of-combatants thing collapses. I'm pretty sure she's got it backwards but I can't say why. As far as I can tell the third condition is a condition on the war (or on the prince's intentions), not on what some conscript on the losing side is guilty of. And yet... if a war is "rendered unlawful through a wicked intention", unlawful for who exactly? The sovereign? The war as a whole? Every last soldier in it?

Can you pull the passage apart for me? I want to know who the "belligerent" whose intention matters actually is; whether the condition attaches to the act of war or to the agent; what exactly "rendered unlawful" is supposed to land on; how the intention condition relates to the other two, since it looks redundant untill you read the next sentence; and then, finally, what all of this does or doesn't imply about the moral equality of combatants. And if my seminar leader's reading is a live one, tell me what version of it would have to be true for this passage to support it -- lay the options out so I can go back to her with specifics instead of just mumbling.
