OK so heres a thing thats been bugging me. I work at a small shop that runs forecasting tournaments — we get people to put numbers on things like "will X happen by date Y", we score them with the Brier score, and we publish calibration curves: over a few thousand forecasts, the ones you gave 70% should come true about 70% of the time. We also tell people off for being wishy-washy, and the whole culture is that you have to commit to a number, that "well it isn't really a probability, it's just my judgment" is a cop-out. But the way we talk about the score is weirdly split. On one hand the score is supposed to reward you for being honest about what you actually believe — if you shade your number toward something you don't believe you do worse on average, and I gather theres a theorem that this is exactly what makes a scoring rule a good one, which sounds like a story about your degrees of belief and nothing else. On the other hand all the verification talk is frequency talk: "well calibrated" means the 70%s came true 70% of the time, like we're measuring a property of the events, not of the forecaster. And half our questions are one-offs — "will the incumbent win" — where theres no ensemble for it to be a frequency of, but people still say our model "got the probability wrong" when it said 80% and the thing didn't happen.

It gets worse on the consulting side, where we do catastrophe modeling for insurers: we generate a big synthetic catalog of simulated hurricane seasons, crank out an annual exceedance probability for one specific building, and hand the insurer "a 1-in-100 chance this year". Everybody in the room talks as if that is the risk, a fact about the building and the coastline, sitting there independent of our model and of anyone's opinion, and the insurers price on it as if it were as real as the square footage. Meanwhile the modelers keep solemnly splitting "aleatory" from "epistemic" uncertainty and then reporting a single number anyway.

So: what interpretation of probability does this whole practice presuppose? Is it coherent, or are we quietly running a subjectivist scoring rule, a frequentist verification story, and a propensity-flavored risk number side by side and hoping nobody notices? Which rival interpretations does the practice actually rule out, and which could it live with — is the scoring-rule bit committed to subjectivism, or could a frequentist or a propensitist use the same score for the same purposes? Where is the practice merely underdetermined, so that several readings of probability would license exactly what we do? And what would have to change — in what we report, what we punish, what we tell the insurers — if we went all in on one reading and dropped the others? Dont give me a reading list, just argue it out.
