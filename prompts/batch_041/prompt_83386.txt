I'm putting together materials for a graduate seminar on the problem of evil, and I keep running into the same frustration: students arrive already fluent in the cartoon Leibniz — the cheerful optimist for whom everything is for the best — and never engage the real position, which is a far more interesting and far more slippery piece of machinery. So I'd like a satire that only pays off for a reader who has taken that machinery seriously. Please take the role of a philosopher with a specialist's command of early modern metaphysics and of Leibniz's theodicy in particular: compossibility and the constraint that the best world must be a whole whose parts can coexist; the principle of sufficient reason and the principle of the best; the distinction between God's antecedent will, which wills the good of each creature taken singly, and the consequent will, which wills the whole together with the evils it permits; the claim that the world is optimal as a totality even though particular parts of it are plainly bad; and the argument that a world of free creatures who fall is better than a world of sinless automata. The satire should be a reductio constructed out of that machinery, not a mockery of it — the jokes have to be things the position itself supplies.

Concretely: write an annual letter to shareholders, about 700 words, from the fictional management of the best of all possible worlds. The voice is a chairman who is confident, numerate, and faintly bureaucratic, reporting on the year with the serenity of a man whose institution cannot lose. Give it the headings of a corporate report — a results section scoring the world on the dimensions a Leibnizian actually cares about (variety, order, the quantity of existence realized), perhaps with a small metrics table; an incident log in which earthquakes, plagues, and wars are entered one at a time and disposed of as necessary conditions of some greater good rather than as defects; a risk-factors section that the chairman intends as reassuring and that a careful reader will recognize as an admission that nothing could ever count against the claim; a brief dissent from a minority shareholder who objects that since nothing else could have occurred, calling this world the best is a category error, followed by the chairman's reply; and an outlook section explaining that no rebalancing is contemplated, since improving any part in isolation would alter the whole and therefore yield a worse world.

The comedy must come from the argument rather than from the chairman being a fool: he should be sharper than his critics, and a reader ought to feel the pull of his replies before recoiling from where they lead. Keep it deadpan — no nudging the reader, no asides, nobody in the piece ever remarking that any of this is absurd, and no winking section titles. Paraphrase the position in the chairman's own idiom rather than quoting Leibniz or any other real text, since you have no way to check a quotation and I would rather you didn't invent one; for the same reason, invent the shareholder rather than using a named living person. Before drafting, spend a few lines planning which standard objections you will smuggle into the incident log, what exactly the dissenting shareholder will say, and how the chairman will answer him — then write the letter itself.
