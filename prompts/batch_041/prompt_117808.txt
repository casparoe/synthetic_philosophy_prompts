I have been trying to reconstruct for myself the reasoning behind Pascal's remark that, being unable to make what is just strong, we have settled for making what is strong just, and I have ended up with an argument that appears to prove far more than anyone would want. (1) A rule that cannot be enforced is not a rule but a wish. (2) So if a dispute is genuinely to be governed by a rule, the rule must carry with it the means of settling that dispute. (3) Those means are held, as a matter of fact, by whoever is strongest — the crown, the landlord, the family that has collected the toll at the ford for nine generations. (4) What settles a dispute is whatever the parties to it are bound to treat as governing it. (5) Therefore the rule governing a dispute is the one enforced by the strongest party, and obedience to it is just. (6) There is consequently no such thing as an unjust law, only an unenforced one: the villagers who refuse the toll are not wronged but merely weak, and every successful usurper is retrospectively legitimate. I cannot accept (6); it looks to me like the flattery of power that the remark was meant to expose. I would sooner surrender (4) than (1) or (3), though I am not certain that (4) is the culprit. Tell me which step does the damage, why it looked innocent, and what I pay for giving it up — in particular, whether I can still hold that force and right come apart without reducing (1) to a platitude.
