I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'd like help adjudicating an argument I watched two of my teammates have during a design review. We're building an internal tool that ranks candidate rules for a long stream of small prediction problems; the ranking adds a penalty for longer encodings, so the tool prefers whichever candidate has the shorter description. My teammate M, who runs our ML infrastructure, says this is Occam's razor with a proof behind it, and that the proof is Solomonoff induction: shorter programs get exponentially more prior weight, and the resulting universal predictor is guaranteed to converge on anything produced by a computable process. My teammate C, who came out of compilers, says that's formalism as decoration. Here is how it went, more or less verbatim.

(1) M: The uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity is a red herring. An uncomputable optimum is still a specification of what you're approximating; "shorter is better" is no less meaningful because you can't compute the exact shortest program than chess strategy is meaningless because perfect play is uncomputable. And the invariance theorem settles the relativity worry: any two universal reference machines assign complexities that differ by at most an additive constant, so the ranking can be off by a constant and no more.

(2) C: A constant in bits is the whole ballgame. Our problems arrive with a few hundred bits of data at most. A correction that vanishes against unbounded data doesn't vanish here. And I can hand you a reference machine in which my preferred rule is one bit long and yours is enormous, so the ranking flips, and it flipped because I chose the machine.

(3) M: But that machine isn't free. Hardwiring my rule into a machine costs roughly what the rule itself costs, so you've paid for the simplicity in the machine rather than in the hypothesis. If the reference language is fixed once and reused across every problem we'll ever run, the trick doesn't systematically help you.

(4) C: "Fixed once and reused" is doing all the work in your reply. Nobody fixes a representation for all time and amortizes the constant across a lifetime of problems; we pick a representation per problem, after looking at the data, which is exactly the freedom that lets the trick work. When you say the tool prefers simpler hypotheses, what you mean is that it prefers hypotheses that are short in whatever notation the analyst happened to pick. That's a prior in formal dress.

(5) M: Then explain the convergence theorem. For data from any computable source, the universal mixture's total error is bounded by a quantity that depends only on the complexity of the true source, as measured in a fixed machine. That's a fact about the world, not a stylistic preference. It says the problem is learnable and that preferring short descriptions isn't a heuristic; it's what a prior over programs already implies.

(6) C: And the bound is stated in a machine-relative complexity and it's asymptotic. Ours are finite problems, and there are only a few hundred of them. A guarantee about the limit of an infinite sequence doesn't tell me which of two rules to ship on Thursday. What actually stops us from overfitting is the regularizer we added, and the razor is that regularizer with a story attached.

They asked me to referee and I had nothing. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what. Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating, and then say what both of them are missing that would dissolve part of the disagreement. Please also tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and identify the single point you are least sure about.
