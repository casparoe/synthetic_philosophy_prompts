Act as an economic historian with a second career in deadpan satire, and write the genre of document that has probably done more than any journal article to shape how the public understands the Great Divergence: the university press office's press release.

The occasion is a research team that has, at last, settled why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain and not in China. Write the press release announcing it — roughly 750 to 900 words, in the flat, relentlessly upbeat register of institutional communications: headline, subhead, dateline, a lead paragraph that delivers the finding, two or three paragraphs of "the research shows", at least one quoted passage from the study's lead author (invented), a paragraph on the implications for policy or for how we understand economic development, a "Related coverage" box listing the institution's own earlier definitive findings in the same area with their dates, and a "Notes to editors" boilerplate.

The satire is aimed not at any particular scholar but at a practice: answering a contrastive historical question with a single decisive cause, and treating that question as though it has one determinate answer waiting to be found. The humour has to come out of the substance, so every joke should be a point a specialist would recognise as fair rather than an invented absurdity. Without ever stating it, the release's own logic should keep dismantling the announcement from the inside. Among the things it should quietly put on display: that a factor nominated as decisive is very often one the comparison case also had, so the explanation keeps relocating to the next candidate; that the two sides of the comparison are not the same kind of unit, so a like-for-like comparison would have to set a region against a region rather than a continent against an empire; that the answer depends on when you say the divergence occurred, and that the dating is itself contested; that any explanation in terms of some durable feature of one civilisation must reckon with the laggards industrialising within a couple of generations once they began; and that the question, as usually posed, asks us to explain an event by contrast with a counterfactual history nobody can observe. The "Related coverage" box should make its point through the archive alone — the same institution, in previous years, having announced a different decisive cause with equal confidence — and must not attribute any of those earlier findings to a real scholar or work.

Constraints. Invent the research team, the university, the journal, the study's methods, and all earlier findings; do not cite, name, or allude to real books, articles, journals, or named researchers, living or dead. Do not use quotation marks except for the invented quotes, and do not attribute any historical claim to a real source. If a joke would require a specific fact or figure you are not confident of, cut the joke rather than guess at the number. Stay in register throughout: the release must never wink, never concede that it is being ironic, and never contain a sentence a real press office would not have written. A reader who knows the literature should be able to read it twice — once as an ordinary piece of university communications, once as a demonstration that the announcement cannot be made — and it should still work on the second reading; a reader who knows nothing about the debate should still follow it.

Begin your response with a few lines of planning: list the candidate causes you intend to cycle through and, for each, the methodological trap it springs. Then write the release. Aim for the version a specialist would find uncomfortably accurate rather than the version that is merely clever.

Finally, append a short note, clearly separated from the release and in your own voice, of about 200 words, saying which of the claims the piece trades on are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this — for instance, whether the bare fact of a large divergence in incomes and output by the nineteenth century is common ground, and whether the dating, the choice of units to compare, and the very well-posedness of the question are matters on which competent historians disagree. Do not present a contested position as consensus, and flag where the satire leans on a reading of the debate that is itself one side of it.
