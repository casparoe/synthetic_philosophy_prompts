My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about probability in the many-worlds interpretation, and I want to turn the argument into a short story. She holds that the Born rule is not an extra postulate at all — that once you ask what a rational agent should expect before a measurement, the squared amplitudes are simply the right weights; I think that move either assumes what it sets out to prove or quietly imports a principle of indifference over branches, and that nothing in the theory by itself tells me which of my successors I have reason to care about being. I don't want the story to settle this by fiat. I want it to make the pressure of the dispute felt by people who have to live inside it.

So write me a short story, roughly 1,200 words, built on this conceit: a small lab runs a long series of trials in which the outcome that keeps the volunteer alive and reporting is the one with a tiny amplitude, and because only survivors are around to file reports, the survivors' logs show an unbroken, miraculous-looking streak of improbable survivals. Some of the volunteers have concluded that they cannot die, and have begun to act on it. A visiting statistician has been hired to audit the records and to show that the streak is unremarkable — but she is not allowed to help herself to any probability rule she cannot justify from the theory alone, which is exactly the thing in dispute. Let the story stay close to her audit: the logs, the interviews, the volunteer who has stopped flinching. Let the ideas live in the work and the arguments rather than in exposition, and don't end with a lecture that resolves the question.

Follow the story with one section titled "Where I've taken liberties", about 250 words, noting which parts lean on a contested reading of many-worlds or on a probability principle that is itself under dispute, stating how confident you are in the claims you've made, and singling out the one claim you are least sure of.
