I'm a freelance journo who writes a column on science and ideas, and I'm midway through a piece about the whole "who gets to decide?" thing — you know, when a referendum or a consultation happens and half the complaints afterwards turn out to be about who was allowed to vote rather than about how they voted. Before I file it I want to run my own position past someone, because I've got a horrible feeling it dosn't hang together and I'd rather find that out now than in the letters page.

So here's roughly where I've landed. (1) The all-affected principle: if a decision materially affects you, you ought to have a voice in making it — not a veto, just a voice. (2) There's no pre-political people: any statement of who "the people" are is itself a political decision, so the question of who votes isn't prior to politics, it's the most political thing there is. (3) I'm a proceduralist about democratic legitimacy — what makes an outcome legitimate is that it came out of the right kind of process (equal votes, genuine contestation, a live possibility of challenge or exit), not whether the outcome is substantively just. (4) On the concrete case I keep coming back to: a county council wants to site a nuclear waste facility in one district, and the neighbouring districts, which share the water table and the only motorway, are furious that they don't get a vote on it. I think the neighbours are wrong — it's the host district's burden and the host district's call, and their recourse is compensation and legal challenge, not the ballot box. (5) And I think that if a region ever wants to secede, the vote should be held among the whole existing electorate of the state rather than just the region, and should require a supermajority.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture on democratic theory. Tell me which of these pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look like they're in tension and actually dissolve on inspection (I suspect at least one does, but I can't see which), and what the cheapest way out is for each real conflict and what it costs me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad, and be blunt about which commitment I can't keep if I want to keep the others. I'm not a philosopher, so keep the vocabulary tight — but don't water down the substance, I can take it. Please give it to me as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "tensions", an array where each entry has "pair" (like "1 and 3"), "genuine" (true or false), "severity" (1 to 5, 5 being worst), "what_conflicts" (a sentence or two), "cheapest_fix" (what to give up or reword), and "cost_of_fix" (what that concession loses me); "dissolved", an array where each entry has "pair" and "why_it_dissolves"; "load_bearing", the one commitment doing the most work and least able to be dropped, with a one-line reason; "single_revision", an object with "change", "pairs_fixed" (a list) and "cost"; and "bottom_line", a single sentence telling me whether the package survives as is.
