We've been on Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments in my ethics seminar and I thought I had the shape of it, but the impartial spectator is doing my head in. I get the mechanism: sympathy is me imagining myself into your situation and feeling something like what you feel, and the impartial spectator is how I check whether my reaction is proper instead of just self-serving. What I cant get past is the word "impartial" — I keep hearing it as "disinterested", a neutral observer with no skin in the game. But that seems wrong for Smith, since the spectator has to be sympathetic, has to actually feel with the person, and being unmoved would be a defect rather than the ideal. So what work is "impartial" actually doing here? Is it just fully-informed-and-not-a-party, or is it a claim about the kind of feeling the spectator has — properly proportioned sympathy — rather than the absence of feeling? And heres the follow-on I think is really the same question: if the spectator is something I build in my own head, what stops me from building a flattering one? Smith goes on about self-deceit somewhere and I assume thats the worry. So is the impartial spectator best read as a procedure I actually run, or as a standard my actual judgments have to match — and does that choice matter for whether the device can do the normative work he wants it to?
