The debate over whether an ought can be derived from an is has been running in the rationalist blogosphere for as long as I have been reading it, and I have come to suspect that both sides are arguing about the wrong gap. I have a modest one-semester independent-study stipend to justify, and I need a short research proposal (roughly 1500–2000 words) built around the reframing below.

My half-formed idea. The is/ought gap is nearly always defended by an argument that would prove far too much. State the underlying principle at full generality — no conclusion committal about F follows from premises wholly silent about F — and it holds for any F whatsoever: being charged, being alive, being red, being legally required, being a legal chess move. If that is the whole of it, then the is/ought gap is not a special fact about normativity at all but the most mundane instance of a completely general feature of deductive consequence, and the reason it strikes people as deep is only that we care about the ought side. That deflationary line would explain the gap without any of the apparatus usually wheeled in for it — non-natural properties, the autonomy of practical reason, expressivism — and it predicts that every gap argument in the history of philosophy is one and the same argument in different costumes. But the opposite reading is also live. Perhaps the general principle is a triviality about vocabulary, while what people actually care about is a different claim: that no body of descriptive information can rationally compel a normative conclusion, in the sense that an agent might accept every descriptive premise, understand them fully, and reject the conclusion with no logical or conceptual error. That compulsion claim is not the vocabulary claim, and it is not obviously entailed by it; conflating the two would explain a great deal of the mutual incomprehension in the debate. Two small observations the reframing has to absorb: the inference from the claim that someone ought to do something to the claim that they can do it runs from a normative premise to a descriptive conclusion, so a symmetric version of the gap would forbid it; and the claim that someone ought to do something is itself a descriptive fact about them, which makes the vocabulary version of the gap look like a point about bookkeeping rather than about normativity.

So the proposal should take up this question: is the is/ought gap a domain-general triviality about which predicates occur in which premises, or is there a residue — an epistemic or rational-compulsion gap — that is specific to the normative case, and if there is such a residue, which of the standard packages (non-naturalism, non-cognitivism, or a quietism that denies there is any single gap) can carry it at the least cost?

State the question so that it could come out either way and I would recognize the outcome. Argue, rather than assert, that the standard positions as usually stated leave it open — my suspicion is that they equivocate between the vocabulary reading and the compulsion reading, but the proposal should make that case. Say why this is a conceptual and formal question rather than one that more empirical work on moral psychology could settle. Describe the method concretely: I am willing to do some formal work (toy languages with a distinguished vocabulary, checking which candidate principles survive as theorems and which turn out to be artifacts of how the language was set up), alongside ordinary conceptual work, so the proposal should say what the formal part buys and where it is likely to beg the question. Say what success looks like, and be candid about the two or three ways the project could collapse — into triviality, or into an unmotivated stipulation about what counts as a descriptive premise, or into a merely verbal dispute that I have not earned the right to call verbal. Give me a realistic scope for one semester of part-time work, and name the deliverable: I am thinking one long paper plus a shorter explainer written for readers of the blogs rather than for philosophers.

No bibliography and no citations; I will supply those myself. Write it so the argument stands on its own rather than on appeals to named literature, and assume the reader is a funder who is intelligent but not a philosopher.
