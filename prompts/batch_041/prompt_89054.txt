I've been asked to run a ninety-minute session — whiteboard, no slides — on the naturalism-versus-normativism debate about health and disease, in a medical humanities elective that's open to the whole university. The room is about a third clinical students and two-thirds humanities students, almost none of whom have read any philosophy of medicine, and they arrive holding opposite dogmas: the clinicians think a disease is a fact about a body that a pathologist could in principle settle, the humanists think calling something a disease is obviously a social judgment, and each side reads the other as either naive or sentimental. I don't want the explanation itself; I want a plan for teaching it. What has to be in place before the question even looks like a real question rather than a fight about words? Which pieces depend on which — specifically, when the notion of a species-typical function has to arrive, whether the evaluative element in harm can be introduced before the naturalist position has been stated at full strength or only after it has, and where the hybrid positions (the ones that keep a biological dysfunction condition and add a harm condition) belong in the arc: before the students have felt the pull of either pure position, or after, and what each ordering buys me? And what should I openly refuse to cover at this level, saying so out loud?

For the case that carries the session, my sister, who is a GP, has given me one, and I need you to tell me honestly whether it can bear the weight. Two men in their early fifties come in during the same week; both had a scan for an unrelated complaint, and both turn out to have the same incidental structural anomaly in the heart muscle; both are asymptomatic, both sail through an exercise test, both have the same numbers on every measure of how their bodies currently perform. The only difference is that one of them has a relative who dropped dead at thirty, and the other does not. My sister says the two men leave with the same sentence in their charts and utterly different standing as patients: one is now a cardiac patient for life, the other is a man with an interesting variant. Nothing in either body distinguishes them. Is this the right carrying case — what does it have to have, and what must it conspicuously lack, for the group to feel the naturalist's discomfort and the normativist's temptation without my having rigged it? Does it smuggle in the verdict by calling the finding an anomaly in the first place? And what second case should I pair it with, so that the naturalist isn't left looking like a straw man by the end of the hour?

I expect at least these misconceptions and want to know which are genuine misconceptions rather than defensible readings: that normativism means any condition a society dislikes is thereby a disease; that naturalism is the safe view because it merely reports the biology and leaves values out; that value-laden means arbitrary; and that disease and illness are interchangeable. Tell me where in the sequence each will form, which one I should let stand for a while rather than correct on the spot, and which will be manufactured by my own framing rather than by the students. I also want to hand the room a two-question test to apply to any candidate disease — is some bodily process going wrong by the standards of how bodies of this kind work, and is that going wrong a misfortune for the person living this life — and I suspect that scaffold quietly leans normativist; tell me whether it begs the question against the naturalist by assuming the two questions can come apart, and if it does, what to replace it with. Finish with the question I should put to the room in the last ten minutes that would reveal whether they've grasped the structure of the dispute rather than memorized a side, and say what a good answer to it would and would not contain. No readings, please — I want the teaching plan.
