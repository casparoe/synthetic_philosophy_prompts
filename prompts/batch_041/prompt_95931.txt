I am closing a graduate seminar on moral change and would rather end with a villanelle than with a summary, so I want you to write that poem. Its subject is how a practice that was once a point of honor — dueling, foot binding, the Atlantic slave trade are the stock cases — becomes, within a generation or two, a source of shame. The poem should take a definite view of why that happens, but it must never state the view. No philosophers' names, no labels, no thesis sentence, nothing that reads as moral progress laid out in argument. The claim has to live entirely in the machinery of the form.

The machinery I have in mind works like this. Give the villanelle two refrains, and let them stand for the two explanations that are always in play: the moral argument against the practice, which was available in recognizable form for generations before anything actually changed, and the line of honor by which practitioners justified what they did. The argument refrain must be word-for-word identical each time it appears and must mean exactly the same thing each time, and the poem must make a reader feel that it accomplishes nothing — that it is true, and worn smooth, and inert. The honor refrain must also be word-for-word identical each time, but its sense must reverse across the poem: what is a boast in the first tercet has to have become a confession, or an indictment, by the last. Crucially, the reversal must be caused by a change in who is watching — the audience whose esteem the practitioner actually tracks — and not by any fresh reason the poem supplies. The form's promise of symmetry should end up quietly broken: the two refrains arrive at the same place, and one of them has been hollowed out along the way.

Two further demands. First, the reversal should be readable without strain in either of two ways — as the practice ceasing to be honorable and as the practice ceasing to be worth it — so that a reader cannot decide whether the poem is telling the honor story or the interest story, and neither reading is foreclosed. Second, let the speaker be someone who did the thing under the old code and is still alive under the new one, so that the same words are spoken by the same person on both sides of the change.

A reader who knows this literature should be able to say which refrain is which, and should see that the poem's causal claim is not the naive one; a reader who knows nothing of it should simply find a good poem. Do not resolve the tension in the closing quatrain. The envoi may bring the two refrains into the same breath, but the point should be that sharing a breath is all the argument ever gets.
