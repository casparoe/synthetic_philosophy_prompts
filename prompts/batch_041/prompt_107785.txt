I am assembling the final examination for a graduate seminar on formal decision theory and its applications in ethics, and I want a single essay item on expected value fanaticism that a student cannot answer by reciting the standard puzzle. I have therefore built the following case, and I would like you to construct the item around it.

A welfare agency must select exactly one of three one-shot portfolios:

- Safe: 999 units of welfare with certainty.
- Gamble A: 10^12 units of welfare with probability 10^-9, and zero otherwise.
- Gamble B: 10^12 units of welfare with probability 10^-9 − 10^-15, and zero otherwise.

Their expected values are 999, 1000, and 999.999 units respectively.

The question should require the student to do four things. First, state exactly what expected value maximization prescribes for this case and articulate what is supposed to be objectionable about that prescription. Second, examine the most obvious repair — discarding outcomes whose probability falls below a fixed cutoff — and say what that repair costs, including whether a rule of this kind can still respect first-order stochastic dominance, given that Gamble A stochastically dominates Gamble B. Third, assess the following proposed alternative, which I want the question to state explicitly: that the cutoff should not be a fixed constant but should instead be indexed to the number of comparable one-shot decisions the agent will ever face, so that an agent facing many such choices across its lifetime is licensed to ignore smaller probabilities than an agent that will face only one. Fourth, say whether the difference between Gamble A and Gamble B is the kind of difference a theory of rational choice ought to be able to register at all, and defend the answer.

Please produce three things: the examination question itself, as a single essay prompt suitable for a 90-minute written answer; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme worth 25 marks in total, broken into must-hit points with the marks each carries. For every must-hit point, the marking scheme should also name the characteristic error a weaker answer makes in its place, so that the scheme visibly separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one.

Constraints on the item: it must be answerable from the case as stated together with general decision-theoretic reasoning; it must not require students to name, date, or cite any author, paper, or doctrine; and it must not depend on any specific published example. Any principle the model answer relies on must be stated and defended within the answer rather than attributed to someone. Write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme in American English, and keep the register formal enough that the question could be handed to students as it stands.
