I am a high school junior, and this spring our ethics elective ran a debate unit in which each pair argued a resolution rather than a policy. The resolution assigned to the last round was, in substance, that guilt is a better moral emotion than shame, and that a community can deliberately choose which of the two to cultivate. I was the designated flow-sheet keeper for that round, so I wrote down as much of the exchange as I could and have reconstructed the rest carefully from my notes. Our teacher's only feedback was that both sides had made good points, which is exactly the kind of comment that leaves a person with nothing, so I would like an actual adjudication rather than a scorecard. I have tried in what follows not to tilt the retelling toward either debater, Marisol (for the resolution) or Dev (against it).

Marisol opened by saying that guilt aims at something a person did rather than at who the person is, and that this is precisely what makes it useful: if I feel guilty for having lied, there is an object to repair, an apology to make, a resolution to keep, whereas shame attaches to the self and can only be discharged by hiding, by being humiliated, or by turning on someone weaker. Dev answered that she was describing shame at its worst. On his account, shame is the feeling of falling short of a standard one shares with other people, which is why it draws a person back into the community rather than out of it, and why it lifts only when those who matter can see that the standard has been met again. Guilt, he said, is private bookkeeping: a person can feel terrible, settle the account internally, and change nothing, so guilt is the comfortable emotion that lets you hold the community's norms at arm's length. Marisol replied that if shame only lifts when others certify your return, then your standing sits in their hands, and shame is therefore exactly as good as the community's opinion; she asked him to imagine a community with monstrous standards, in which case shame would punish the one person who resists and reward the ones who comply, whereas guilt needs no audience and can be felt by the only person in the room who thinks something is wrong. Dev granted that this cuts both ways, since a guilt-ridden person can be entirely mistaken and can flagellate himself over breaking a rule that deserved to be broken, and he then made what I took to be his strongest move: guilt, he said, is not a different emotion from shame at all but shame that has been internalized to the point where it no longer needs witnesses, since one can only feel guilty about things one was taught were wrong, and one was taught by a community. Marisol responded that if that is right, the internalization is the improvement, because the standard has been detached from whoever happens to be watching, and she added that shame attaches as readily to traits as to acts, so that children are shamed for being poor or slow or awkward, none of which can be amended, while guilt at least stays where amendment is possible. Dev's last point was that some things genuinely are about the person and not merely about a single act, that someone who betrays a friend once is thereafter someone who betrays friends, and that a morality which cannot say this has become shallower rather than kinder; he closed by claiming that cultures already choose, and that ours chose confidentiality and therapy over public confession. Marisol's closing claim was that a culture cannot choose an emotion the way it chooses a policy, since what it can actually do is design procedures that reward and punish, after which the feelings follow on their own, so the honest formulation is that we choose the procedures and hope for the emotions. Throughout, both kept returning to a case from our own school last year, in which a student was caught using a forged permission form at a competition and the honor council had to choose between a public apology at assembly and a private process of restitution; they never agreed on which arrangement their positions recommended.

Please go through the exchange and adjudicate it point by point, saying for each of the major claims whether it is true, false, or true only under a qualification that one of them failed to state, and identifying the places where a debater's argument quietly depends on a premise the other never granted. I am especially interested in three things: whether Dev's claim that guilt is just internalized shame is a substantive thesis or a redescription that does no work; what the correct answer is to the question about whether a community can choose its moral emotion, since I suspect both of them are half right and I cannot see how to state the half each one has; and how the honor council case should come out under the best version of each position, since that seems like the fairest tiebreaker available to us. Finish by saying who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing.
