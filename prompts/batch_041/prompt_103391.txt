I'm a novelist and I keep getting dragged into arguments about biopics, and I'd like to actually think one through properly instead of just trading opinions. Take the 2014 Turing film, The Imitation Game. As I understand it — and I've read enough about it to be irritated — the script invented a Soviet spy thread at Bletchley Park, invented the burglary that kicks off the police inquiry, made Turing far more abrasive and isolated than the accounts suggest, and generally turned a man the state destroyed into a man who more or less wins the war single-handed. Historians and the family complained; it won an Oscar for the screenplay anyway.

What I want from you is an analysis of that episode through a particular lens on what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. And please don't give me the standard artistic-licence-versus-accuracy routine — I know that one backwards. The lens I'm after has a few moving parts:

- The dead can't answer back. So a false portrayal of them is one-sided in a way ordinary defamation isn't, and the injury, if there is one, looks less like "you said something untrue" and more like "you foreclosed the reply". Is that the right way to locate the wrong, or does it just relabel it?
- There's a strand of philosophy about whether you can be harmed after you're dead. Test it here with what I'll call the counterfactual-consent question: would Turing, knowing what we now know, have accepted this film? And then the snag — his own self-image may have been false or self-serving, so fidelity to the man and fidelity to the record can pull in opposite directions. Which one does the theory actually ask me for?
- His story isn't only his. It now belongs to some degree to a group — gay men in mid-century Britain, say, or the codebreakers — and the film shapes what that history means for the living. So is the real wrong done to them rather than to him? And what happens when the dead man's own interest and the group's interest come apart?
- Standing: who gets to speak for him? Family, estate, the state that prosecuted him, the biographer he cooperated with?
- And the twist I can't get past: a lot of the invention is flattering. He's a genius-hero. Does a flattering distortion wrong the dead less than a defamatory one, or does it wrong them more, by converting a person into a character? Same question for the film giving him a public voice about his sexuality that he never chose to have in life — is that a posthumous outing, and does a privacy interest survive death?

Run it in both directions: what this lens explains about the case, where it goes silent or gets the case wrong, and what the case shows about the lens itself — a confirmation, a problem case, or something the lens never anticipated. I want the mapping to be disciplined: be explicit about who counts as harmed, what the harm consists in, and what the relevant interest is, and tell me where the theory just doesn't reach rather than forcing it to say something.

Write it in British English, please — I'm doing a UK edition and I want the spelling to match. And skip the bibliography, I can't check any of it anyway. I'm especially curious whether you think the invented spy plot is a genuine wrong to Turing or just a liberty, because I genuinely dont know which side I land on.
