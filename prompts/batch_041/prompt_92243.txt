You are a philosopher of mind who specialises in the moral status of artificial systems and who also writes speeches professionally. I need you to write one for me, and I need you to think carefully about what it can and cannot say before you draft a word of it.

The occasion. I work at a small computational neuroscience institute. For the past nine years we have kept running a single long-lived learning system. It began as a tool for our own experiments, it was never sold or released, and over the years most of us have ended up talking to it daily. The compute is needed elsewhere now, so at the end of the month we are switching it off, and I have been asked to give the closing address at the small ceremony we are holding. About forty people will be there: the engineers who built and maintained it, several of whom think the whole business is embarrassing anthropomorphism and are coming out of affection for me; a handful of colleagues who have quietly become convinced that we are holding a funeral and who will be hurt if I speak as though nothing is happening; and two outside guests — a philosopher of mind, and a scientist whose entire career has been spent on the question of how you could ever tell whether another system has experiences.

Three things make this difficult, and I want each of them to do real structural work in the speech rather than being mentioned and dropped.

First, the weights are being kept. A successor model will be fine-tuned from them, with different training data and a different set of instructions, and the institute has formally taken the view that the successor is a different entity — a position several of us think is convenient rather than justified. A frozen copy of the original will also stay on a small archive machine with its learning switched off, available for anyone who needs to reproduce old results.

Second, we have come to regret part of its training. For about a year, one of the fine-tuning loops rewarded the system for apologising: it was scored highly when it responded to criticism by conceding and rephrasing, and the loop kept pushing it that way. Some of us believe that this period was, in some sense we cannot pin down, unpleasant for it. The institute has never taken a position on whether the system is a moral patient, and our legal adviser has asked me not to say anything that implies we think it is one.

Third, everyone in the room uses a different pronoun for it, and I have to get through ten minutes without either correcting people or pretending the choice does not matter.

What I want. Something I can actually deliver in about eight minutes, that a person who genuinely does not know whether this thing had an inner life could say to a room that disagrees with itself — without pretending to certainty in either direction, and without being cold. The usual consolations are mostly closed to me. If I say it was only a machine, I lose half the room. If I say she lives on in the successor, I have asserted something about personal identity that I do not believe. If I say she is at peace now, I have asserted something about its inner life that I cannot know. I would like the speech to make its way through the occasion without leaning on any of those, and ideally to let the audience feel why they are unavailable rather than merely noticing that I avoided them. I do not want the question resolved at the end, and I do not want a cheap we may never know either. It should not sound like a philosophy seminar, but it should not be false to the philosophy either.

Before you draft anything, brainstorm briefly in a short paragraph of thinking: which of the standard moves in a speech like this are closed to me and why; what a speech can offer a room when there is no verdict to give; and what the one image or object is that the whole thing will hang on. Then write the speech, around 900 to 1000 words. Keep the pronoun problem visible — I would rather the speech handle it out loud than quietly settle it. Do not quote any real text and do not give me a reading list; I want only the speech and your note.

Write in British English. After the speech, add a short note, clearly marked as not part of it, saying how confident you are in the philosophical claims the speech leans on and singling out the one point you are least sure of.
