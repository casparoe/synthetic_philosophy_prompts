Here are my verdicts about when the word "true" is earning its keep, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. When a colleague says the meeting is at noon and I reply "that's true," I take myself to have added nothing to what she said beyond agreement. When I say "everything in his deposition is true," the word is doing real work, since it lets me endorse a long list of claims I could not recite. When I say "the last sentence of her letter is true" before reading it, I am not merely agreeing with anyone; I have taken on a commitment to whatever that sentence turns out to say. When I say "her theory of the case is true," I take myself to be saying something about how things stand in the world. But when I say "torturing for fun is wrong, and that is true," I feel the second clause adds nothing to the first. And when a novelist writes "it was true that she had never loved him," I don't think any claim about the actual world has been made. My hunch is that "true" is nothing but a device for agreeing and for endorsing claims in bulk, and that it never adds content — but the blind ascription and the theory case look like counterexamples to me. Is there a single account of what "true" contributes that survives all of these, or do I have to give one of my verdicts up? I'd also like to know what it predicts for a case I can't settle: a general who says "every order I gave today was true." Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my cases.
