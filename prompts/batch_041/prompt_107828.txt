I am a novelist preparing for a public conversation with a philosopher whose work bears directly on my current book, and I want the exchange to test her position rather than let her restate it. Her view, in brief, is that the transformative-experience debate has overemphasized the solitary decision-maker who cannot know what a future self will be like; she argues that many transformative choices are proleptically co-authored, in that the agent borrows narrative scripts, rituals, and evaluative stances from others in order to rehearse a self that does not yet exist. She further distinguishes thin transformative experiences, which are socially scripted enough for such rehearsal to preserve rational decidability, from thick ones, which are not. She also holds that an after self's ratification of the choice can make the choice rational even when the before self could not compare the values involved, and she treats the ignorance in transformative choice as a variable produced by the availability of scripts rather than as a fixed epistemic wall. Please give me exactly five numbered interview questions, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Each should include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and together they should press on the following: the difference between narrative rehearsal and ordinary testimony; the thin/thick distinction and whether it collapses for the cases the literature cares about; the objection that phenomenal knowledge of a transformed self cannot be anticipated from the outside; the worry that after-self ratification is too cheap; and what a novelist should learn from her account about the temptation to make transformative choice legible. Begin directly with the first question and end with the fifth.
