I am in a term-long reading group on Keynes's Treatise on Probability, jointly run by the economics and philosophy faculties, and we have spent most of the term on the two features that make the book unlike anything else in the subject: first, that probability is a logical relation of partial entailment between a hypothesis and the evidence for it, so that a degree of belief is rational exactly to the extent that it answers to the strength of that relation; second, that this relation is not always measurable on a numerical scale, and not always comparable, so that there are pairs of hypotheses whose probabilities the evidence neither equalises nor ranks. We keep returning, too, to the distinction between the probability of an argument and its weight, and to the umbrella case in the chapter on measurement, where Keynes is content to say that when the barometer and the clouds pull in opposite directions it may be rational to let caprice decide and to waste no time on the debate. A friend in the group, an economist by training who has spent the term trying to make the Treatise earn its keep in his own discipline, has produced an argument that he says shows the whole package collapses under its own weight. I cannot accept his conclusion, and I am reasonably sure one of his steps is doing something illegitimate, but I cannot say which, and every way I have tried to state the objection either denies something Keynes plainly holds or turns into a distinction without a difference. His argument runs as follows. (1) On Keynes's account, a probability is a logical relation of partial entailment between a hypothesis and the evidence bearing on it, and a degree of belief is rational exactly when it matches the strength of that relation. (2) Not every pair of probabilities is comparable: for some hypotheses the evidence determines neither that their probabilities are equal nor that either exceeds the other. (3) Many probabilities cannot be assigned any numerical value at all. (4) To choose rationally between two courses of action one must be able to compare the probabilities of the outcomes that distinguish them; on the standard account, one must be able to compute and compare their expected values. (5) Where probabilities are non-numerical, expected values are undefined; where they are non-comparable, the comparison that (4) requires cannot be carried out. (6) So in any case where the probabilities that distinguish two options are non-comparable, neither option can be shown to be rationally preferable to the other. (7) Such cases are not marginal but are the ordinary condition of practical deliberation — an investment, a policy, a career, a marriage — as Keynes himself allows when he says that in such matters there is no basis for a calculable probability. (8) Hence on Keynes's own account rational choice is impossible in the ordinary case; where it remains possible, it is only because the probabilities happen to be measurable and comparable, which is precisely the calculus he spent the Treatise denying was generally available. (9) A theory of probability which entails that rational action is impossible in the ordinary case, and which leaves the field clear only where the classical calculus works, proves too much and refutes itself.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a defence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would far rather keep (2) and (3) than accept (9) — the non-comparability claim is the main reason I find the book worth the effort — so if blocking the argument requires a substantial concession about what the theory can do for practice, I want that concession stated exactly, and I want to know whether Keynes can afford it. Rank the standard replies by what each has to give up: the subjectivist line running from Ramsey, which treats degrees of belief as the whole of probability; the imprecise or interval-probability approach, which accepts non-comparability but denies that it leaves us with nothing to say; decision rules that stop short of expected-value maximisation, such as dominance or maximin; and Keynes's own appeal to the weight of argument and to the conventions by which investors and statesmen actually decide. For each, say what it surrenders and which of them a committed Keynesian could live with. Two suspicions I have not been able to settle, and would like you either to confirm or to dismiss with reasons: that (4) is smuggling in a decision rule Keynes rejects, and that the argument slides between the evidence failing to rank two hypotheses and our failing to know how they rank. Please be concrete about which numbered step you are attacking, explain why that step looked innocent, and write in British English.
