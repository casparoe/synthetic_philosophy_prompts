I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate writing the central section of a term paper for a seminar on Dennett and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. The part I'm stuck on is his argument that systems built to pass as people are counterfeit people — artifacts he compares to counterfeit currency, on the grounds that both corrode the trust that money and communication alike depend on, so that making them or passing them along should be outlawed. What I can't extract from the argument as stated is whether the wrong it identifies is a wrong done to deceived individuals or a wrong done to a practice; the money analogy suggests the latter, but the rhetoric suggests the former. I'd like a designed case that forces the issue rather than a summary of the debate.

Please build me a matched pair of scenarios. They should share one mundane setting, one system, and one population of interlocutors, and should differ in exactly one variable: how hard it is to tell the system from a person. In both scenarios nobody is ever in fact deceived — every counterfeit is caught before it forms a belief in anyone. In the first, detection is free, instantaneous, and certain. In the second, detection is possible but only by deliberate probing, of the sort Dennett himself imagines friends and family subjecting one another to online. Write each scenario as a single paragraph, and keep the setting ordinary rather than speculative — a workplace or a service line, not a laboratory.

Then do the following. First, state what Dennett's rationale says about each scenario on its own terms and why, not what he would say if he were hedging. Second, name the single feature that makes the difference and say whether the two scenarios come apart because of deception, because of detection cost, or because of something else; if they do not come apart at all under his rationale, say so and explain what that shows. Third, test the pair by removing the feature — make detection free in the second scenario, or add real deception to the first — and report whether the verdict moves. Fourth, tell me how a defender of Dennett should attack the pair. My suspicion is that a defender will say the relevant harm is a practice-level good that can be damaged with no deceived party and no detection cost, and I want to know whether the pair leaves room for that reply and whether it can be tightened to close it. Fifth, add one further probe: a genuine human instructed to pass as an AI system. Say whether Dennett's rationale condemns that, and what the answer implies about whether the argument is really about deception at all.

Constraints: no science fiction, no exotic technology, nothing that turns on whether the system is conscious or really understands — I don't want the case settled by that question — and the whole thing no longer than a page. I can't search for anything, and I don't need citations or further reading; I need the case itself and the demonstration that it does what I've asked.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the step you are least sure of.
