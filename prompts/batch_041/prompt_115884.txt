I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we've spent the last few weeks on computationalism -- the strong version of the claim, that the mind literally *is* a computer, that thinking just is the execution of computations over representations, as opposed to the weak "the brain is kind of like a computer" analogy that everyone nods along to and nobody defends. For my final paper I want to write about Memento, which I've always loved, because I have a hunch it's a much better test case than the usual androids-and-robots material. Leonard can't lay down new memories, so he offloads the whole operation onto the world -- the tattoos, the Polaroids, the little running system of notes and habits -- and then he runs himself like a program: read the instruction, execute, update the record, repeat. In a strange way he makes the machinery visible, which is exactly what the computationalist says is always humming along under the hood of a normal brain. So does the film dramatize computationalism or quietly refute it? I want a reading that's honest in both directions. Treat the tattoo-and-Polaroid setup as a candidate implementation of a cognitive system and ask whether it really is one, and at what level the interesting story lives -- is it the level of what the system is *for* (the task it's solving), or the level of the algorithm he's actually running, or the level of the ink and the paper and the guy reading it? And take seriously the objection that any physical object can be described as implementing any computation you like as long as you get to pick the mapping, because Leonard is a person whose entire mind is literally written on his skin, and I suspect that makes the objection bite much harder than it does for a brain.

Then there's the twist, which I think is the real reason to use this film: he's the one who wrote the instructions, and by the end he's deliberately falsifying his own records so that his future self will go off and do something. The program, the programmer, and the thing being computed are all the same man. Where does that leave the syntax-versus-semantics line that people always bring up against strong computationalism? Leonard handles the symbols flawlessly -- the question is whether there's any interpretation on which they actually mean what he takes them to mean, or whether he's a very sophisticated symbol-pusher with nobody home. I'd like you to finish by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that a straight paper on the Chinese room or on the implementation problem doesn't already cover, and if the honest answer is "nothing, it's just a vivid illustration," please say that too. I don't want a reading list or a bunch of quotes from the film, I just want the argument, and I'd rather you commit to a view than hedge everything.
