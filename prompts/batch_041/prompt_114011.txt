I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep getting stuck on the idea of free-floating rationales — reasons that nobody has, but that still seem to explain why a trait is there. A friend in our evo-devo reading group threw this at me last week and I can't tell if its a reductio or a genuine problem. It goes:

(1) A trait has a free-floating rationale when its presence is explained by the fact that it solved a problem for ancestors, even though no ancestor represented the problem or the solution.
(2) Natural selection is exactly the kind of process that can generate such traits: variation, differential retention, no mind required.
(3) The same abstract structure — variation, selection, retention — shows up in lots of non-mental systems: the immune system's antibody repertoire, market prices, the distribution of sand grains on a dune, maybe even the way a river carves its bed.
(4) If free-floating rationales are real, then any system with that structure has free-floating rationales.
(5) But sand dunes and rivers don't solve problems; they just happen. A dune's profile isn't for anything, and the river doesn't have a reason to reach the sea.
(6) So either free-floating rationales don't require solving a problem in the representational sense, in which case they collapse into ordinary causal functions and the idea loses its critical edge, or they do require it, in which case natural selection can't have them either.
(7) Therefore the notion of a free-floating rationale is either trivial or empty.

I want to keep (2) — I think natural selection really does explain traits in a way that's reason-like without a reasoner — and I'd rather not give up (5) either, because pan-teleology makes me queasy. But I can't find the move. Is one of the steps equivocating? Is the sand dune case doing illegitimate work? And if I have to give up one of the premises, which one is cheapest, and what does the rest of the picture look like after I pay? I'm especially worried that the answer will turn on what 'problem' and 'representation' mean, so if that's the crux, spell out the options rather than just telling me to disambiguate. For context, I'm comfortable with the etiological theory of function and the selection-for/selection-of distinction; what I want is the diagnosis, not a reading list.
