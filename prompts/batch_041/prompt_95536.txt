I teach a graduate seminar in contractualist moral theory, and I am drafting the essay section of this year's final examination. I would like you to act as an experienced examiner in normative ethics — someone who knows the contractualist literature well and who has graded enough scripts to know exactly where a student's grasp of the view collapses into recitation. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming on your own: lay out the strongest moves available to a contractualist in the case below, then the best replies to each, and only then compose the deliverables.

The case (my own construction, so students cannot look it up):

Bayview is a town of 200,000 adults. A public health authority must choose, once and for all, between two twenty-year programs. Under the Universal program, every resident of Bayview bears a real but moderate burden: each loses roughly six months of healthy functioning, experienced as a chronic but manageable condition. No one dies early. Under the Selective program, no one bears that moderate burden; instead, a single resident, chosen by a fair lottery from the same 200,000, loses about twenty years of healthy life and dies roughly twenty years earlier than she otherwise would have. The other 199,999 residents bear nothing at all. All residents are equally situated and equally deserving; there are no side effects, no costs to third parties, and no question of the lottery's winner consenting — the authority simply picks one program.

What I want the question to turn on is a distinction the standard treatment blurs: between aggregating complaints across persons, which the individualist restriction forbids, and aggregating the many small losses that make up a single person's complaint, which the restriction must permit on pain of incoherence — for if the selected resident's twenty lost years are not somehow the sum of thousands of lost days, the view owes an account of what makes her complaint a single complaint at all. The question must force students to say whether that asymmetry is principled or ad hoc, and to state what follows for the verdict in Bayview. A student who merely rehearses the familiar charge that contractualism cannot handle numbers should not be able to pass.

Deliverables:

1. The examination question itself, as it would appear on the paper. It may have two or three parts, but it must be answerable by a well-prepared student in ninety minutes and in roughly 1,200 words.
2. A model answer of about 800 words. It must take a definite position rather than concluding that both sides have a point; where more than one verdict is defensible, it should commit to one and say what it costs.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each (twenty total), plus a one-line note for each point on the mistake that most commonly costs candidates that mark.
4. A brief note, no more than three sentences, on what separates an A answer from a B answer.

Constraints: the students will have no internet access and may consult nothing, so the question and the model answer must not depend on citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or page references, and neither should quote any text. Assume the students know the primary text and the assigned secondary literature, but do not build the question around any particular interpretive claim about what the author says in a given chapter; the case is doing the work. Write in American English.
