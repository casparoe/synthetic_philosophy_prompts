I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, spending this year as an ethics fellow with the enforcement division of a state health-professions board, and I've been asked to turn the moral luck literature into something the board can actually run.

Here is the setting. The board disciplines licensed physicians and nurses: about fifty contested cases a year, fifteen or so involving a patient death or serious injury, sanctions from reprimand to revocation, imposed in public hearings. Eleven members, mostly clinicians and public members, none with philosophical training. Reviewers receive the file ten days before the hearing and I need a procedure they can complete in under an hour, producing a one-page worksheet. The general counsel and the board chair have to sign off before it goes into the hearing manual, so anything that runs against the governing statute must be flagged rather than quietly worked around. That statute requires sanctions to be proportional to the seriousness of the violation and directs the board to consider the degree of harm to patients, so I cannot tell them simply to ignore outcomes, and I don't want a memo that pretends the statute isn't there.

The problem I'm trying to solve: the current worksheet asks for a single seriousness score, and harm does double duty in it, both as an aggravator in its own right and as evidence about what the practitioner actually risked. Reviewers see the outcome before they rate the conduct, and I suspect they reverse-engineer the ex ante risk from the result.

What I want is a decision procedure. It should (a) fix the order of operations so that conduct is rated before outcome information is admitted, and state precisely what the outcome may and may not be used for, including the hard case where the outcome is the only evidence of what the practitioner knew or should have known; (b) sort the ways luck enters a case, namely the outcome that happened to be bad, the situation the practitioner happened to be in (the short-staffed shift, the patient who arrived at 3 a.m.), and the training and temperament they happened to arrive with, and say which of these may bear on culpability and which must be handled some other way; (c) say how to satisfy the statutory harm factor without letting it bleed into the culpability finding; and (d) say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it must hand the question to the board as a value judgment rather than a finding.

Then run it on three cases: two nurses who made the same error in a potassium infusion, one caught before any harm and one where the patient died; a physician who followed the standard of care and lost a patient to a rare reaction; and a practitioner who took an unjustified shortcut for two years, harmed no one, and never reached our docket until a colleague reported it. I expect the procedure to produce at least one result I won't like, so say so plainly rather than softening it.

Finally, tell me where the procedure is just the control principle, that blameworthiness reaches only as far as what was within the agent's control, dressed up in procedural clothing; where I am smuggling outcome-sensitivity back in under another name; and what the procedure cannot do, including any question that is really about what the board is for rather than about luck.
