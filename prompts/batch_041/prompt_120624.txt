I have been turning over an idea that surfaced in a graduate seminar on political morality, and I would like to know how much exposure it has. The idea, which I have been calling the exchange view of moral compromise, is this: when two people or two communities hold different value systems, they can often both be made better off by trading rather than by fighting or by one side simply conceding. A holds her own cause dear and is nearly indifferent to B's; B is the mirror image. If A does a little for B's cause and B does a little for A's, each gives up something she ranks low and receives something she ranks high, and the result is not a mere truce but a genuine surplus. On the exchange view we therefore have at least a pro tanto reason to seek such trades, and perhaps a duty to look for them before resorting to coercion or to an outright contest of strength. I have added a wrinkle that I have not seen stated cleanly, and that I think is the heart of the matter: the surplus can arise even between two people who agree on the ranking of goods and disagree only about their weights. Both may rank liberty above equality above prosperity and still have reason to trade, because one will surrender a great deal of prosperity for a little more liberty while the other will not. If that is right, then the domain of profitable compromise is far larger than the people who talk only about clashing values suppose, and the exchange view has an advantage over the usual defenses of compromise: it need not claim that either party is mistaken, that the truth lies in the middle, or that moderation is a virtue in itself.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true for the exchange view to be false, or for it to be true but idle? I am interested in defeaters of every kind — conceptual, moral, and practical — and in which of them are decisive rather than merely awkward. A few have occurred to me and I cannot dispose of them: that values may be incommensurable, so that there is no fact of the matter about whether a trade yielded a surplus; that goods their holders treat as sacred or protected may be untradeable by their very nature; that the market analogy smuggles in assumptions moral life does not supply, such as enforceable entitlements, a common currency of value, and the absence of externalities; and that a person who trades away part of a commitment may erode the very thing she meant to honor, so that what looked like a surplus at the moment of exchange is a loss over time. I am confident I am missing the objections that would actually do damage, and I am not sure I have ranked even the ones I have.

Please sort the objections from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them a committed defender of the exchange view would concede on his own terms rather than merely have pressed upon him, and say honestly whether the view can be framed so loosely that it survives every conceivable finding — and if it can, whether that is a defect of the view or simply the shape any framework of this sort must have. The objection I personally find hardest to answer is the integrity one, that there is something corrupt about a person who is forever willing to price her commitments; but I would rather be told which objection should trouble me most than have the one I already fear confirmed. I would also like to know what the exchange view predicts that its rivals do not — what we should expect to see in actual negotiations and moral arguments if it is true.
