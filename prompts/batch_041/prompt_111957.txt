I am a sixth-form student, and I present a fortnightly interview segment for my school's philosophy society podcast. Our next guest is a visiting researcher who has developed a distinctive position on pure time preference, and I want my questions to press on the substance of her view rather than allow her simply to restate it.

Her position, as I understand it, is as follows. The ordinary debate treats pure time preference — discounting a future benefit merely because it lies in the future — either as unjustified or as justified only by uncertainty, by opportunity cost, or by the chance that the benefit may never be enjoyed. She accepts none of these. She holds that a legitimate pure discount does exist, but that it is not a constant rate applied to elapsed time; it is indexed to personal continuity. On her account, the discount one may apply to a future experience should track how much of one's present evaluative outlook — one's cares, projects and commitments — survives into the self who will undergo that experience. Where the outlook is wholly preserved, the discount is zero; where it has wholly lapsed, indifference is permitted. She treats this as continuous with the familiar puzzle about personal identity over time, and she claims to have a measurable proxy for the relevant continuity, drawn from the rate at which people's stated cares change across their lives.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. I want questions that turn on the actual content of the position, not on how she arrived at it, and no generic openers of the "what first drew you to this topic" variety. At least one question must address each of the following three points:

- the intergenerational case, in which the affected parties are not the same person at all, so that her index of continuity appears to license indifference towards distant future people;
- the temporal–spatial asymmetry — why she permits discounting along the temporal axis but presumably not along the spatial one, and what she would say about a present-day stranger whose cares diverge from mine as sharply as a future self's might;
- whether her measurable proxy is circular, since the present cares from which she reads off the discount rate may themselves already be shaped by the very rate she is trying to derive.

For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising if she takes the expected line. Do not include requests for reading lists, bibliographies or citations, and do not refer to sources I would have to look up. Write in British English.

End your reply with a single line of the form \answer{...}, containing, in no more than fifteen words, the one question from your list that she is least likely to be able to answer.
