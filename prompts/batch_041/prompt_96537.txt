I'm helping teach a phil of language unit on definite descriptions, and I want to stress-test how far the classic Russellian unpacking actually goes -- you know the move where 'the F is G' gets cashed out as 'there's exactly one F and it's G'. Instead of the same three or four textbook examples everyone reuses, I've got a pile of sentences I want to run through it, and I'd like you to sort them into teirs by how much trouble each one gives that analysis. Tier 1 = handled cleanly, no changes needed. Tier 2 = needs some kind of patch, but the core idea survives. Tier 3 = the analysis is genuinely in trouble here and something has to give. One line of justification per placement, please, not an essay on each.

Here's the list:

- the cat is on the mat
- the current emperor of Brazil is tall
- the last digit of pi is 7
- the average voter is 45 years old
- the dragon in my novel is lonely
- the round square is round
- Maya thinks the person at the door is her brother, but the person at the door is the postman
- the tallest building in the city is the building my dentist works in
- the table is covered in books (there are four tables in the room)
- a man walked in, and the man was wearing a hat
- the number of provinces in this country could have been larger

A few things I also want out of this, since I'll be putting it on a slide:

1. Does the ranking shift if you let 'the F' pick up a contextually restricted domain instead of ranging over the whole world? Which items move, and in which direction?
2. Seperate out any of these that are really doing a different job altogether -- ones where the Russellian unpacking isn't even the right tool and arguing about whether it "works" is a bit of a category mistake. Say what the right description of those is instead.
3. Flag any case where you think two reasonable people would land it in different tiers, and tell me what the disagreement actually turns on.
