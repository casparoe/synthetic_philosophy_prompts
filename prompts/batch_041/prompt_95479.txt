I'm putting together a grad seminar that reads Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" alongside the two-dimensional semantics apparatus -- Kaplan's character/content distinction, Chalmers's primary vs secondary intension, Stalnaker's diagonal proposition -- and I keep flip-flopping on what the story is actually a case of, so I'd like you to work it out with me.

Quick reminder of the story in case it's gone stale: it's a fake critical notice, written by a fussy admirer, cataloguing the publications of a recently dead French writer, Pierre Menard, whose real masterpiece was invisible -- two chapters of the first part of Don Quixote, word for word identical with Cervantes, arrived at not by copying but by Menard trying to live his way back into writing them himself. The narrator thinks Menard's version is vastly richer than Cervantes's, precisely because of everything the world learned in between.

Here's my muddle. Is Menard's text the same character with a different content, or the same content with a different character, or neither? My instinct says: same words, so same character, and the differing context of utterance does all the work. But then the narrator's actual claim -- that the second text is richer, more deliberate, something close to impossible rather than inevitable -- doesn't seem to live anywhere in the two-dimensional picture, since that picture tells you about reference and truth across worlds, not about significance. And I half suspect the story is a Twin Earth thought experiment before its time: Menard is a man on a twin planet trying to mean what Cervantes meant by running the same mental process, and the story's verdict is that this fails, meaning ain't in the head. But if that's the right reading, then it's an externalist story, not a 2D story, and the 2D machinery is just anachronistic dressing I'm bringing to it.

So which reading survives scrutiny? Where does the framework genuinely earn its keep on this text, and where does the story push back? And tell me straight whether I'm confusing what a reader has to know -- pragmatics, recognizing the diction as deliberate archaism because she knows who wrote it -- with what the sentence means, since I suspect thats the whole muddle. Also, I've heard people say the Menard case shows the same sentence type can have different contents while others say it shows the same content can be read differently; those can't both be the interesting claim, so say which one is. Finish with what the story contributes to the character/content debate that a philosophy paper on it can't.
