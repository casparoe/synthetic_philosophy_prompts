I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the boundary problem — the difficulty of specifying who belongs to the demos without presupposing a demos to do the specifying. What I need from you is a single constructed case, not a review of the literature. Do not supply readings, citations, or sources; the case has to stand on its own for a reader who cannot look anything up.

Fix two principles in the forms I want used, and use them throughout in exactly those forms.

The all-subjected principle: a person belongs to the demos for a decision if and only if she is subject to the coercive authority under which the decision is taken — that is, if the body deciding claims and exercises the right to enforce its rules against her.

The all-affected principle, in a deliberately narrow form: a person belongs to the demos for a decision if and only if the decision can significantly set back her welfare interests — her health, livelihood, property, and physical security — where being subject to coercion does not by itself count as setting back a welfare interest.

Specification. Construct one case in which these two principles, applied to the same decision, deliver demoi that are not nested: each principle must admit at least one person whom the other excludes. In particular I want the direction that is supposed to be the difficult one — at least one group that the all-subjected principle includes and the all-affected principle excludes. The standard complaint is that the all-affected principle is the expansive and indeterminate one, threatening to make the demos coextensive with humanity; I want a case that reverses that on at least one comparison.

Constraints. (i) Contemporary and mundane — a municipality, a province, a regulator, or a firm, not an imaginary world, and no science-fictional devices. (ii) The group that is subjected but not affected must be excluded because its welfare is genuinely protected, not because its interests are trivial; it should be a substantial group, and the protection must come from a credible external guarantee that the deciding authority cannot revoke. (iii) The group that is affected but not subjected must not be a member or co-national of the polity, and must have no vote, no exit, and no other institutional channel. (iv) No border or immigration cases, no global climate change, no future generations, no non-human animals — I want a fresh setting rather than the canonical illustrations. (v) The case itself in one paragraph; the analysis may run longer.

Then demonstrate that the case does what I asked, in this order. (1) For each group, state the verdict each principle returns, and show that the verdict follows from the principle's own terms rather than from a stipulation in the setup. (2) Identify the single feature that makes the two principles come apart, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed. (3) Give the strongest objection a defender of the all-affected principle would raise — in particular, whether counting subjection to coercion as itself an effect on a person's interests collapses the gap, and whether the case survives that move. (4) Say whether subjection, being a standing relation to an authority, and affectedness, being a relation to a particular decision, can be made commensurable here, and whether the case depends on their not being commensurable. (5) Tell me plainly whether the specification is jointly satisfiable or whether the case works only by smuggling in a premise a critic could reasonably reject; if the latter, say which constraint has to be relaxed. Do not rescue the case by describing the guarantor as infallible.

What the case is for: I want to display that the all-subjected principle is not simply the more inclusive cousin of the all-affected principle, and that the direction of the disagreement between them depends on how narrowly affectedness is read.
