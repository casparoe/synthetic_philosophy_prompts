I am a retired geophysicist — thirty years spent on plate tectonics and seismic tomography — who has drifted, in the last several years, into philosophy and the history of science, and I have hit a wall in Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), which I am working through in a library translation. What I want help with is not the historical circumstances but the internal logic of a stretch of argument in the middle of the letter. Reconstructed in my own words — and I flag that it is a reconstruction, since I have no wish to put words in the man's mouth — it runs roughly as follows. God is the author of both Scripture and nature, and since the same author cannot contradict himself, the two books cannot genuinely disagree; where they appear to disagree, the fault lies in our reading of one or the other. Nature, however, is inexorable and indifferent to human wishes: it never deviates from its course in order to accommodate our understanding. Scripture, by contrast, was given for the sake of our salvation and is therefore accommodated to the capacities of ordinary, unlearned readers, speaking in their idiom and often in figurative or phenomenal language — as when it attributes hands to God, or describes natural things in the language of everyday appearance rather than in the language of the schools. From this asymmetry Galileo concludes that when a literal reading of a scriptural passage conflicts with a conclusion about nature that has been demonstrated by sense and necessary proof, the passage must be reinterpreted rather than the demonstration abandoned. He reinforces the point with a line he borrows from Cardinal Baronio — that the Holy Spirit's intention was to teach us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go — and with a string of patristic authorities, Augustine above all, to the effect that Scripture belongs to everyone and must not be pressed into the service of specialists' disputes.

I can follow each step, and yet the whole leaves me uneasy, and I cannot tell whether my unease is philosophical or merely the residual positivism of a laboratory life. So I would like a patient reconstruction and critique of the argument as an argument. In particular: what exactly is the accommodation premise doing, and does it do the work Galileo needs, or does it merely relocate the interpretive burden? Is there a circularity in the procedure — Scripture is reinterpreted to fit nature, but only where the natural conclusion has been demonstrated, and the criterion of demonstration seems to be doing all the load-bearing work while remaining unstated? How does the claim that Scripture and nature cannot contradict each other square with the practice of reading Scripture non-literally precisely wherever they do? And is the appeal to Augustine and the Fathers a genuine argument from authority within his own tradition, or a rhetorical flanking maneuver that a modern reader should discount? Finally, tell me where the argument is stronger than its nineteenth-century caricature as a simple separation of science from religion, and where the caricature has a point.
