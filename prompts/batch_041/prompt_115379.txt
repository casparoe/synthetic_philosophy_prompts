I'm in the final weeks of a seminar on infinity and paradox, and the last session is on supertasks. We spent most of it on Thomson's lamp: the lamp is switched on half a minute before noon, off a quarter of a minute before noon, on an eighth of a minute before noon, and so on, so that by noon infinitely many switchings have been completed and yet there is no last switching to consult. The room split cleanly in two. Half of us hold that something has gone wrong and that the lamp cannot be in any state at noon, since it must be either on or off and neither answer survives scrutiny. The other half hold that the schedule simply does not say what happens at noon, so there is no contradiction to be had, only a gap in a description into which we are smuggling a demand. Someone also raised the version in which the lamp's brightness is halved at each step instead of flipped between on and off, so that the states approach a limit and noon is just that limit, which appears to make the puzzle evaporate; our instructor suggested that this may be less a solution than a change of subject. I would like to close the session with a poem, and I want the poem to do the work of the puzzle rather than explain it.

Here is the constraint I would like you to build around: fifteen lines, arranged in stanzas of eight, four, two and one, so that the poem's own sections halve as they go and a further stanza would have to be zero lines long. The flicking should live in the eight-line stanza, with the states alternating and the intervals between them shrinking; the four-line stanza should compress toward the limit; the two-line stanza should hold the last two moments a reader can actually point to; and the final one-line stanza is the moment at noon. I do not want that last line to name a state. It should be a line that cannot be assigned to either side of the alternation, and I would like each of the two camps in my seminar to be able to read it as its own. No rhyme scheme and no meter are required, but a reader who counts the lines should be able to see the halving without being told about it. Please do not use the words supertask or paradox anywhere in the poem, no epigraph and no footnote, and do not let the poem buy its way out by dimming the lamp into a continuum at the end, since a half-lit limit is exactly the change of subject I want the poem to stay stuck short of. It has to stand as a poem.

After the poem, in a short note of a few sentences, tell me which verdict the ending leans on, that there is no state at noon or that there is one and the description merely fails to give it to us, and say honestly where that leaning is weakest: what a defender of the other reading would put to you, and which consideration would actually make you rewrite the last line. I would rather have a poem that knows what it is doing than one that hedges, so do not pretend the ending is neutral if it is not. Write the note as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, numbered lists or tables anywhere in the response.
