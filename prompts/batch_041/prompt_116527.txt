I am revising a paper on whose values an aligned system should serve, and I keep running into a move that I want to think through carefully before I answer it in print. The move, made by a colleague in our department's ethics-of-technology reading group and repeated by two graduate students in their written comments on my draft, is this: an aligned artificial agent ought to be like a referee at a game, not a player in it. It should see to it that the rules are applied evenly, that no party is permitted to impose its own conception of the good on the others, and that the various pursuits of the participants are left to unfold as they will. On this picture the system's business is procedural — keep the field level, adjudicate disputes, decline to take sides — and the temptation to build it instead as a maximizer of aggregate welfare, or as an instrument for some particular vision of flourishing, is a mistake of the same order as a referee who begins scoring goals for the team he prefers.

I have two things I would like you to sort out. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: in the referee picture, what plays the role of the rulebook, of the players, of the spectators, of the league that employs the referee, of the field and its boundaries, and of the goal that defines the game? Then tell me where the mapping fails, distinguishing the failures that matter for the conclusion my colleague wants from the ones that are merely picturesque. My suspicion — which I would like either confirmed by argument or dissolved — is that the analogy imports, without argument, something the political case conspicuously lacks: a single uncontested constitutive aim. A referee need not adjudicate whether the point of the game is to win, because the rules settle that in advance; the disputants in the political case disagree precisely about what the game is for, and the rules that would level the field are themselves the contested thing. I also want to know what to make of the fact that referees do not write the rules but enforce a book handed down by someone above them, that their authority is borrowed from the parties' antecedent consent to play, and whether the referee's procedural neutrality — which nonetheless determines the outcome on every close call — is the kind of neutrality the alignment case needs, or whether the distinction between neutrality of justification and neutrality of outcome collapses here.

Second, say what follows for the aggregation half of the question. The referee picture is usually offered as an alternative to thinking of the system as an aggregator — a device that sums, averages, or otherwise combines many people's preferences into a single policy. If the referee picture cannot bear the weight my colleague places on it, does anything remain of the anti-aggregation conclusion, or does that conclusion survive on independent grounds, such as the objection that any determinate aggregation rule silently privileges some rankings over others? And is there a better analogy available — a landlord renting the same field to incompatible games, a translator among parties who share no language, a butler with several principals whose instructions conflict — or does every candidate import an uncontested aim just as the referee does?
