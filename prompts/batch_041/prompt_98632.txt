I am a high school junior and the captain of my school's debate team, and our league has begun rewarding a move I do not know how to answer: a debater claims that because she can coherently imagine a scenario — a mind without a brain, a person who survives having her neurons replaced one at a time, a duplicate of water that is not H2O, a past that can be changed — that scenario is possible, and then argues from that possibility to a conclusion about the actual world. I want to hand my teammates a written procedure they can run in the three minutes of preparation they get before a rebuttal, so it has to be short, it cannot assume they have read any philosophy, and it has to tell them what to say out loud. Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a verdict that the argument fails, and the point at which it hands the call to the judge because the dispute is about what a term means rather than about what is possible. Build it around the difference between my teammates being unable to imagine something and that thing being contradictory, make it say what to do with cases where we later discover that two things we had taken to be distinct are in fact the same thing, and make it handle the trick where the imagined scenario has the conclusion smuggled into its own description. Run it on two or three of the examples above, including at least one where it tells my team that the argument survives and that they must concede that much, and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure and about what the procedure cannot decide. Please write in American English.
