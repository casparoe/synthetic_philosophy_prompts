I'm in a small seminar on the Scottish Enlightenment, and next week it's my turn to circulate a short position paper on Adam Smith and the American colonies; the assigned reading was the colonies chapter of the Wealth of Nations, which I've now been through twice. My draft is below and I'd like comments on it before I send it round to the group. A friend in the seminar keeps calling Smith the first anti-imperialist, which is what set this off: I think that label and the "apologist for empire" label are both wrong, but I keep worrying that the way I say so is either trivial or quietly inconsistent with itself. One procedural note — the draft was written from notes and memory, so I'm paraphrasing Smith throughout and have deliberately cited nothing. Please don't answer with page references, editions, or further reading; I couldn't check any of it and it wouldn't help me. What I want is the substance.

The draft, such as it is: *Thesis: Smith's case against the colonial system is not a case against empire. It is a case against two institutions — the exclusive trade of the mother country, and the practice of paying for the colonies out of British revenue — and it is argued throughout in terms of the welfare of the people of Britain. Smith never argues that the empire is illegitimate because it rules people who never consented to be ruled. (1) The exclusive trade is a transfer, not a gain. Smith treats the monopoly of colonial commerce as a machine for raising the profits of a particular set of merchants and manufacturers at the expense of everybody else: the British consumer pays more for what he consumes, and capital is drawn into the colonial trade beyond the share it would otherwise take, so that the country's stock is employed less productively than it would be under free trade. This is where he is at his most indignant, and the indignation is distributive rather than humanitarian — the exclusive trade, he says, is "a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind" — someone is being robbed, and the someone is the British consumer. (2) The colonies' prosperity is not the monopoly's doing. Smith thinks the American colonies grew unusually fast because land was abundant and cheap and because the settlers were free to dispose of their own produce and their own labour; the monopoly, far from producing that prosperity, held it back. This fits his more general claim that a colony planted on thinly peopled land follows the natural order of things — agriculture first, then manufactures and foreign commerce — which is precisely the order that Europe, for its own bad reasons, had inverted. So the mercantile system takes credit for a prosperity it obstructed. That is a nice rhetorical position, but I think it is also an uncomfortable one: colonies are the least "natural" of settlements, since they exist by charter and by force. (3) The fiscal argument is the load-bearing one. Smith's real complaint is that the empire is a bad bargain for Britain — the cost of the civil and military establishment of the colonies, together with the wars the colonial trade occasioned, exceeded anything the monopoly brought in, while the colonies themselves contributed next to nothing to the common expense of the empire that protected them. (4) The remedy shows what he is objecting to. Smith proposes either a full union, with the colonies represented in Parliament in proportion to what they contribute to the public revenue, or, failing that, a voluntary separation; he doubts the colonists would accept the first, and he thinks the second is better than coercion. I read him as conceding that the colonists are not represented at Westminster and that their objection on that score is not unreasonable, though he immediately converts the point into a prediction about what they will and will not pay for — which is where I begin to suspect I'm reading my own views into him. (5) So the argument is contingent, and that is my thesis. Everything in 1-4 runs on an accounting unit: the "great body of the people" of Britain. It therefore implies nothing about the wrongness of dispossession, and Smith's scattered remarks about the ruin of the peoples already living in America, and about slavery, sit outside the argument like decoration. If the monopoly were repealed and the colonies paid for their own defence, Smith's objections would dissolve. That is why I say he is anti-mercantilist and anti-coercion rather than anti-imperialist, and why I think the "first anti-imperialist" line is a category error.*
