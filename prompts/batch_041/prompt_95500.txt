I spent 35 years as an industrial chemist and since I retired I've been reading a lot of philosophy on my own, badly and happily. I've written a short essay about the Anthropocene for a general-interest blog (they asked for 1500 words, mine is about 1200) and I'd like you to tell me what's wrong with it. Here it is.

**Nobody Discovered the Anthropocene**

The stratigraphers spent about fifteen years on this and then voted it down. The Anthropocene Working Group proposed a site — Crawford Lake, in Ontario — as what they call a golden spike, the physical point in the rock that would mark the start of a new epoch, and they put that start in the mid-twentieth century, in the layer of plutonium fallout from above-ground nuclear tests. Then the relevant subcommission rejected the proposal by a wide margin, and the international bodies above it confirmed the rejection. The stated reasons were that the sediment record is shallow and the proposed start date is far too recent.

My claim is that they were answering the wrong question. A golden spike is supposed to be a discovery of where a boundary already is: you go out and find the rock that records the change. But there's nothing here to find. Nobody is going to dig up the moment when humans became a planetary force. That isn't a fact sitting in the sediment waiting for a geologist — it's a decision about what we want the word to do. The plutonium layer is real, and it's sharp, and that's precisely why it's a bad reason to pick it: it's the cleanest signal, not the most important boundary.

Think about the prime meridian. Greenwich won in 1884 not because that's where east turns into west, but because somebody had to pick something and the pick had to work for shippers and mapmakers. Same for the metre, the kilogram, the zero on a thermometer. These are conventions with real consequences, and the fact that they're stipulated doesn't make them arbitrary or unimportant.

So the real question isn't which marker is stratigraphically best. It's who the Anthropocene is for, and which convention lets those people coordinate. If it's for historians, lawyers and people writing policy, a boundary drawn at the start of industrialisation — around 1780, when the coal economy starts moving the atmosphere — is far more useful than one drawn in 1950, even though 1950 has the better geochemical fingerpint. And if it's only for stratigraphers, then it stays informal, which is exactly what happened.

Here's the part I find philosophically funny. The decision about when the age of humans began is made by humans, in a committee, in the middle of the phenomenon being dated. We aren't outside the rock looking in; we're inside it, and we're the ones holding the pen. That's why when did it start and who decides are the same question rather than two questions. The 2024 vote wasn't a failure of the evidence. It was a disagreemant about authority, argued in the vocabulary of evidence. And if the boundary really is a convention, it ought to be revisable when the users change — which is the opposite of how the geological time scale is supposed to work, where once a unit is ratified it's meant to be permanent.

I know this is one step from saying it's all socially constructed, which I don't want to say. The bomb plutonium really is in that lake sediment. The carbon dioxide really is in the ice cores. What's stipulated is the line, not the changes.
