I'm a graduate TA and next month I have to run a single ninety-minute session on origin essentialism for a mixed room: second- and third-year undergraduates who have had exactly one lecture on rigid designation and one on possible worlds, plus a handful of biology students who signed up because the title mentioned cloning. The question on the table is the sperm-and-egg one — whether you could have come from a different sperm and a different egg — and I should admit that I taught it once before and it went badly: the discussion turned into an argument about determinism and about whether I secretly believed in souls, and the modal claim itself never got stated in a form anyone could examine. So please do not explain origin essentialism to me; plan how I should explain it. I need the order in which the components have to be introduced, and for each step why it cannot come earlier — in particular, where the notion of a de re necessity claim has to sit relative to the intuition pump, whether the audience can be given the transworld-identification worry before they have accepted that there is anything to worry about, and at what point the difference between a necessary truth and a truth that merely could not have been otherwise for causal reasons has to be nailed down, since I think that is the hinge the whole session turns on.

The plan also needs a carrying example: one case that does all the work for the whole session, and I want you to argue for your choice against the obvious alternatives — whether the gamete case itself is the right thing to build on or whether it should be held back until the end, what a good carrying example has to have and must lack if it is going to survive the objections the biology students will raise, and whether the artifact analogue (a table, a ship, a statue) actually helps or quietly imports an assumption about how far the analogy to persons is supposed to run. Then the misconception map, in the order this particular room will form them: I expect at least the determinism confusion, the thought that the thesis is an empirical claim about genomes, the thought that it is about souls or some spark of life, and the thought that the baby from the other egg would simply have been someone else — which I suspect is not a misconception at all but the real objection, and so needs a different kind of treatment from the other three. Tell me where in the sequence each should be headed off and roughly what to say, and give me one closing question that separates a student who has grasped the modal claim from one who has merely memorized the conclusion.

Finally, I want the session anchored in something they cannot have read in the assigned material, because otherwise the whole thing becomes a recitation of a famous argument. I am considering mitochondrial replacement, the so-called three-parent procedure, where the nuclear DNA is the parents' but the egg — and with it the mitochondrial DNA — is a donor's: if you are essentially the product of this sperm and this egg, what does that make of the donor, and does the thesis have to be stated over gametes, over zygotes, or over something finer still? The alternative candidates I have in mind are human cloning, where the origin story is a somatic cell rather than a gamete, and the fact that a single zygote can split into two people, which seems to make this sperm and this egg too coarse a unit to be anyone's origin. Pick whichever of these can actually carry the weight, say what it does to the thesis, and if one of them trivializes or refutes origin essentialism rather than illustrating it, say so plainly and keep it out of the session. And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes — counterparts, the parallel with species essentialism, the extension of origin essentialism to artifacts — and what I should say when I refuse.
