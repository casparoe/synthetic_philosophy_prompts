I am a high-school debater preparing for a national tournament, and the motion I have drawn is that a group is more likely to reach the correct answer than any of its individual members. I would like to meet it with a constructed case rather than an abstract objection, and I would like the case built to my specification rather than to whatever example first comes to mind.

The specification. A small committee — an odd number of members, no more than seven — votes on questions that have a determinate right answer, and the majority verdict is less likely to be correct than the verdict of the single least accurate member of that committee. Everything else in the case should be as friendly to the majority as I can make it. Every member must be right more often than not on these questions. No member may be insincere, irrational, bribed, or under any instruction to vote with another. The members must not deliberate together before voting. The majority must still be right more often than not, so that I have a competent group that is nevertheless worse than its weakest member, rather than a group that is simply incompetent. The dependence among their verdicts, which I understand cannot be absent, must arise from something you describe in ordinary language — a shared source, a shared temptation, a common teacher, a habit of mind — and not from an assumption announced as a bare stipulation. Nor do I want the case to work by contrariety, engineered so that one member is reliably wrong whenever another is right; if any association between two members' verdicts is present, it should be the ordinary positive kind.

Describe the situation in a paragraph or two of plain prose. Then give me the numbers in a table: the possible patterns of correct and incorrect verdicts with a probability for each, and from that table each member's chance of being right and the chance that the majority is right, set out so that I can check the arithmetic myself.

After the case, four things. First, confirm that each item in the specification is met, and show the comparison that makes the majority worse than the least accurate member. Second, name the feature that is doing the work: say what would have to be true for the majority to become at least as reliable as the best member, and what becomes of the numbers if the members' errors are made independent of one another. Third, say how someone defending the usual claim — the one I know as Condorcet's jury theorem, that a majority is more likely to be right than any individual when each voter is better than a coin flip and the votes are independent — would respond to my case, and whether that response rescues the claim or concedes that its assumptions are rarely satisfied in practice. Fourth, tell me whether the case is an artifact of the small committee: if the same structure were used with nine or fifteen members, would the majority still be worse than the least accurate member, and if not, what breaks. If some part of the specification turns out to be unsatisfiable, I would rather be told which part and why than have it quietly relaxed.
