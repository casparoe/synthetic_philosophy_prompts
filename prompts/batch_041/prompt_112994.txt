I'm a novelist, maybe two-thirds through a book whose main character is a philosophy grad student who's drifted into the anti-realist camp — she thinks truth is constitutively tied to what inquiry could in principle reach, so she's signed on to something like "every truth is knowable." I want her to tell a joke at a party in chapter 19, and I want it to actually land for someone who knows the terrain rather than being a "philosophers, amirite" bit. I'm not going to explain the paradox to you as if you've never seen it, because the whole point is that the punchline should turn on the real machinery: grant that every truth is knowable, help yourself to the two boring thoughts that knowledge is factive (if you know it, it's true) and that knowing a conjunction means knowing both halves, and then the truth "p, and nobody knows that p" — take any true p that happens to be unkown — turns out to be unknowable. Which means the principle that sounded like modesty about our place in the universe quietly collapses into "there's nothing left to find out," i.e. everything true is already known. The party's a department end-of-year thing in someone's cramped apartment, there's a guy in the corner explaining Gödel badly, and my character's been nursing one drink for an hour working up the nerve — so the joke needs to stand on its own out loud, setup and punchline, no citations, no "as Fitch showed," she's telling it at a party, not giving a talk. If it helps, you can build it around the idea of a guest who can never be introduced, or the one thing you can't know being the fact that nobody knows it; those are just seeds, do whatever's funniest.

After the joke, I'd like a short note — a few sentences, not an essay — on what it's taking for granted and where people who actually work on this split. Please don't sell me one camp's line as the consensus: tell me what's genuinely settled (that the derivation goes through once you grant the principle and the two platitudes about knowledge) versus what's really contested (whether the fix is to restrict the knowability principle so it doesn't apply to propositions that themselves mention knowledge, or to ditch the unrestricted version, or to say the real lesson is about the idealization baked into "in principle," or whether the whole thing even counts as a refutation of anti-realism as opposed to a naive slogan). If the joke leans on a reading a working philosopher would push back on, just say so — I'd rather know than have it read as a cheap shot.
