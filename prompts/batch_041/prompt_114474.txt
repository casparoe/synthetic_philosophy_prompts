I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a rehabilitation hospital. Our index case is a woman with a right insular lesion who reports that her left arm hurts, rates it 7 out of 10, and localises it precisely, yet shows no distress, does not withdraw from examination, and declines opioids because they make her drowsy. We must now settle a standing policy for pain assessment in patients like her. The nursing director wants to keep the existing rule that a verbal rating above threshold triggers mandatory analgesia; the palliative care lead argues that in these patients the rating tracks a discriminative signal rather than suffering, and that we should titrate instead to observable distress, sparing them sedation and opioid exposure. Draft the committee's written decision memo: give a concrete recommendation with a review date, and state the criterion our assessors should actually apply at the bedside. Engage the strongest version of the position you reject. In particular, if we stop treating the rating as the harm, we owe an account of what is bad about pain in the first place, and of whether that account transfers to the asymbolic patient's pain or silently assumes that the same thing must be bad for her as for everyone else. Say how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
