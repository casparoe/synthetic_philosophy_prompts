I lead instrument development at a clinical research group. We build and validate patient-reported outcome measures: questionnaires whose items ask people to report states only they have access to — pain, fatigue, breathlessness, tingling, mental clouding — and whose numbers end up as endpoints in trials, where a regulator will eventually decide whether a drug worked partly on the strength of them.

Roughly twice a month my team brings me a proposed item or a proposed revision and I have to rule on whether it may be admitted into a scored instrument. A sponsor is always pushing to include it, a clinician wants to know what to do with the answers, a patient community wants its experience recorded, and the research nurses who administer the instrument have no training in philosophy. My methods committee signs off at the end of each quarter, so anything I adopt has to be stateable as a rule and defensible in a meeting.

Wittgenstein's private language argument is the most serious thing I know of that bears on what we are doing, and I want it turned into something I can run. Give me a procedure: an ordered set of tests for deciding whether a term for an inner state is determinate enough to be scored, what counts as passing each test, the order in which the tests are applied, where the procedure stops and returns "this cannot be measured as proposed", and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than of meaning. Keep the argument's actual content: what fixes the meaning of a sensation term; what a purely private sample could and could not do; why sincerity and consistency are not enough to fix what a term means; why a subject's agreement with his own past applications is not the same as his being right; what follows from the point that, if a sensation term is construed on the model of a name and the object it names, that object plays no part in fixing what the term means; and the difference between a term's being unusable in a language-game and the experience's being unreal. Simplify where you must, but tell me what you dropped and what breaks if it is dropped. If what you produce turns out to be ordinary psychometric advice with philosophical decoration, say so; if it departs from ordinary psychometric advice, say exactly where and why.

Two fights the procedure has to settle. First, our statistician's proposal to admit items on the strength of reliability alone: if a patient applies the term consistently to his own sensations over time, and everyone in the sample does the same, he says the term has earned its place. In his vocabulary, say what is missing from that and what evidence would supply it. Second, proxies: for patients who cannot report — advanced dementia, minimally conscious states — the team proposes to have caregivers complete the same instrument. Say what the procedure does with that, and whether the answer changes when the item asks about pain behaviour rather than pain.

Then run the procedure on three items and show the working: (a) "brain fog", proposed as a scored item in a post-viral fatigue trial; (b) a novel bodily sensation in a rare metabolic disorder that patients describe as a fizzing under the skin and that the sponsor wants named and scored; (c) an item we have used for twenty years, pain intensity over the past twenty-four hours, which the team treats as beyond dispute. I expect (c) to be the uncomfortable one.

Finish with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the items it will wrongly admit and the ones it will wrongly reject; the case in which following it will oblige me to tell a patient community that their experience cannot be measured, when what they will hear is that it is not real; and which of its steps are the contested moves in the argument rather than the uncontested ones, so that I know what a hostile reviewer will attack. No reading list and no citations — I want the procedure, and the reasoning that makes each step do its work.
