I'm putting together a session for a reading group on ethics and technology, and I want to use A Clockwork Orange as the case study — the novel mainly, though I'll probably show the Ludovico scenes from Kubrick's film too, so feel free to move between them where it helps.

The lens I want is the hedonistic imperative: the view that suffering is the one thing that's bad in itself, and that we should use biotechnology not just to cure diseases but to engineer pain, and the nastier reaches of the emotional range, out of sentient life altogether. The people who push it are usually pretty cheerful about it and treat the main objection as a sentimental attachment to suffering as character-building.

Alex is the obvious test case. The Ludovico technique is a bit of biotechnology aimed at abolishing a certain kind of harm — it makes him incapable of violence, so his victims don't get hurt. So is it a crude early version of the programme, a parody of it, or its opposite? My instinct is that the novel's objection isn't the one the abolitionists actually have to answer. The chaplain's complaint is about free will — that goodness which isn't chosen isn't goodness — and the imperative lot can just shrug and say fine, we aren't proposing to torture anyone into virtue, we're proposing that no one wants to hurt anyone and no one can be hurt. Those are different projects.

So what I want from you:

1. Does the novel put any pressure on the project itself, or only on the state's version of it? Be straight with me if it's only the latter.
2. The thing I keep coming back to is the collateral. The treatment doesn't only take out his appetite for violence, it takes out Beethoven. I've started calling this the adjacent-pleasure problem — you cant pull out one wire without pulling out the ones beside it, because the same machinery seems to run cruelty and ecstasy, aggression and art. Is that a technical claim about how affect is wired, or a moral one about what a life is worth once you've pruned it? Does the novel think the trade is worth it? Does it even think there is a trade, or is it just showing a clumsy state?
3. Where does the novel resist the reading, and where do I have to admit it? Alex is a seductive narrator, and a lot of the reader's pleasure in the book is pleasure in exactly the energies an abolitionist would want gone. I suspect the novel is making an aesthetic case for the wickedness it also condemns, and that's an argument the philosophy papers don't really make. If so, say what it adds to the debate that argument alone can't.
4. The ending. The film leaves us with the treatment reversed and Alex gleeful again. The book has one more chapter — the one the American edition cut and which Kubrick therefore never filmed, unless I've got that wrong — where Alex is a bit older and the violence has simply gone off him on its own. If I've got that right, what does it do to the argument? Nature cures him, so the state's intervention was needless; or does it let the abolitionist off the hook, since the appetite faded without anyone engineering it away?

Write it up in British English please — it's going to a UK group and I dont want to re-spell everything. And dont bother with a reading list, I just want the reading.
