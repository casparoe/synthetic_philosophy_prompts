Every June, a small graduate program in contemplative studies holds a commencement for a class of about forty students, and this year they have asked you to give the address. The difficulty is that you are a tranquilist. You hold that the only thing that is intrinsically good is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering — that pleasure, accomplishment, love, curiosity, and every other positively valenced experience are at best instrumentally useful and at worst fresh occasions for clinging, and that no state of affairs is made intrinsically better merely by being enjoyed. You are not a gloomy person and you are not out to spoil the day; you simply cannot say, in good faith, that the graduates' future happiness is a good thing in itself, and you think the ordinary commencement vocabulary of dreams, fulfillment, and joy mostly names the machinery of craving rather than its end.

Write the address. It should be a real commencement address — spoken aloud, addressed to the graduates directly, warm enough to hold a room that has come to celebrate, and short enough to be delivered without notes. The audience is mixed: a handful of fellow tranquilists near the front who will notice every infelicity, a larger block of families and friends who have never heard the word and who came expecting uplift, and a few faculty who invited you precisely because they want the class to hear something it will not hear from anyone else.

The view must do structural work rather than decorate the speech. That means the shape of the address — what it is allowed to praise, what it must quietly refuse, where it turns, and what it finally asks of the graduates — should be dictated by tranquilism, and a listener who knows the view should be able to see it operating at every joint. In particular, the standard commencement consolations (that the graduates' joy is itself a good, that their suffering will be redeemed by their successes, that they should go and maximize their happiness) cannot survive unrevised; decide what, if anything, you can honestly put in their place. You may invent a small concrete example or image to carry the argument, and you may speak in the first person about your own practice, but do not cite, quote, or gesture at any texts, authors, or traditions — the audience wants a speech, not a reading list, and any specific source you name would be a guess. Aim for something that would comfort a grieving parent in the third row and still be defensible to the tranquilists in the first.
