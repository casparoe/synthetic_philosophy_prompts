Some friends and I have been arguing for months about a three-way distinction I keep running into in essays and podcasts, and I have noticed that we never actually settle anything because we never agree on what a good answer would even look like. The distinction is this. In the first sort of community, a person's standing lives in other people's eyes, so an insult that goes unanswered is a real loss, and the person is expected to answer it himself, sometimes with violence, and everyone treats that as ordinary. In the second sort, a person's worth is thought to be something insults cannot reach, so slights are to be ignored or else handed to impartial authorities to sort out, and the one who retaliates is the one who has behaved badly. In the third sort, slights are treated as injuries that matter very much, and the person who suffers one is expected to make it public, appeal to the judgment of an audience, and sometimes compete to be recognized as the one who has been wronged most. What started the argument was a friend who grew up in the first kind of place saying that letting an insult pass would have cost him more than a fight would have, and my being unable to say why I still felt he had described a worse moral world than mine, when all I really had behind the feeling was the standards of the second kind of place, which I had never noticed I was assuming.

Before I hear one more verdict about which of the three is morally superior, I want the specification first: what would any account that ranks these three have to get right in order to count as adequate? Go through the requirements one at a time, in separate sentences rather than as a list, and for each one give me the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. I mean the things the account has to explain, the things it must not contradict, including what is actually known about where these patterns tend to arise and about how the same code can be a burden to the very people it governs; the distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse, such as the one between a code and the people living under it, or between the standing of a practice and the standing of a person, or between what a culture demands and what its members manage to do; and the points at which it must avoid simply assuming the standards of one of the three and calling that the answer. I am asking about which is morally better, not which is more pleasant to live in, and I do not want a verdict about my own society smuggled in as though it were a neutral standard. Tell me which of the requirements I have asked for cannot all be satisfied at once, because I suspect the honest answer to my original question is that the list itself rules something out, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in narrowing the field. Then, briefly, say how the three ways of ranking fare against the list.

Please write this out in continuous prose, with no headings, no bulleted or numbered points, no bold text, and no tables, the way you would write a long letter to someone who has been thinking about this on his own and wants to work it through with you.
