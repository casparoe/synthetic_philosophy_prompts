For a seminar handout on how archaeologists move from objects to minds, write a decision tree that takes a student from a bare description of a burial assemblage to a named position about what may legitimately be inferred concerning the dead community's beliefs. The case is one I am inventing for the handout, so no real site, date, or literature is needed: a small later prehistoric cemetery in which most graves hold a single pierced animal-tooth pendant at the throat, while one grave holds an intact aurochs skull laid face-down across the chest of an adult. Build the branches out of the inferential licences first and place the positions at the leaves afterwards. Each branch node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — for instance, whether the repetition of the pendant across graves licenses a norm or only a common cause; whether a shared practice entails a shared propositional belief or can be sustained without one; whether the inverted skull is deliberate inversion or disturbance and re-deposition; whether an ethnographic parallel is doing the work of generating a hypothesis or of warranting a conclusion; and whether anything mental has to be posited at all. Each leaf should carry the name of a position — processual middle-range inference, contextual or interpretive archaeology, practice-theoretic accounts that treat ritual as constitutive rather than expressive, cognitive-universalist reconstruction, taphonomic minimalism, and whatever else the tree requires — followed by one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment; write "unnamed" where no one occupies a leaf, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Where a combination of answers goes unoccupied, say briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every question in terms of the objects rather than the labels, and do not worry about attributing positions to particular scholars: I want the inferential moves named, not a reading list.
