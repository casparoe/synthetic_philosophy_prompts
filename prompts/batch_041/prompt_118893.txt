I've been reading a lot of archaeology lately, mostly site reports and the interpretive bits at the end, and I've noticed I have pretty firm gut feelings about which inferences from objects to past beliefs or institutions are allowed and which are just wishful thinking dressed up. I'd like to know what principle, if any, is actually behind my verdicts — and whether I have to give one of them up.

Here's the pattern I keep finding.

A pit at the edge of a settlement, dozens of pots, all smashed the same way and piled in. I'm happy to say people deliberately smashed them and put them there. I'm not happy to say it was 'a sacrifice' — that word seems to add nothing except making the scene sound religious.

A burial with a body, a whole ox, and a wagon. I think we can say the funeral cost somebody a great deal and the dead person mattered. That one feels solid.

Bones with cut marks and the breakage pattern you'd get from getting at the marrow, in a pile alongside ordinary food waste. I'll say the flesh was removed and the bones processed. I won't say cannibalism — funerary defleshing explains the same marks just as well.

Thousands of near-identical clay female figurines spread over hundreds of miles. I'll say there was a shared way of doing things. I won't say they shared a religion or worshipped a single goddess.

A big building with a raised platform and burnt deposits on it, in the middle of a town of ordinary houses. Here I'm fine calling it a temple, or at least a communal ritual building.

Rooms in a settlement where the small finds differ house to house, and one has a lot of imported pots and beads. I'm fine saying that household had wider connections or more stuff than the others. Fine.

Now the thing that nags me: the smashed pots and the burnt platform are both non-utilitarian deposits, and I'll call one ritual and refuse the other. And I'll read a shared institution off a building but not a shared beleif off a figurine, even though both are just me reading minds off things. Those two pairs look like they should get the same answer and I give them different ones.

The rule I've half-formulated for myself is that an inference is OK when I can say concretely what people were doing, step by step, that would leave exactly this behind — some sort of 'could an ordinary person have done this on purpose?' test. Smashing pots and burying them: yes. Worshipping a goddess: I can't fill in the steps, so no. But then a temple is also hard to fill in step by step, and a big funeral is easy, so maybe that isn't the rule either.

Is there one account that gets all six, or close? If it forces me to drop a verdict, which one, and why that one rather than another? I also want to know what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a hoard of coins buried in a field, no settlement for miles, no container, just coins sitting in a hollow in the ground.

Say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict — and where in all this your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
