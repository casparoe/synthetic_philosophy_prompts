I sit on the ethics review committee at a mid-sized neuroscience institute — we sign off on maybe fifteen or twenty protocols a term, and over the last two years the pile has gotten weird. Cortical organoids grown past the point where they spike in organised bursts, mice with human glial grafts, a lab doing conditioned-avoidance work in octopuses, and now a couple of groups who want to run large RL agents through something they're calling a 'pain-like' training curriculum just to see what happens. We have an animal care committee and an IRB and neither of them knows what to do with any of this, so it all lands on us, and our current practice is basically that whoever cares most in the room wins. I'd like an actual procedure we can run live, in under an hour, on any proposal, that leaves a written record of why we landed where we landed. Assume nobody on the committee is a philosopher of mind (I'm the closest thing, which is not saying much) and that we are never going to reach consensus on the metaphysics of consciousness, so the procedure has to route around that rather than settle it.

The angle I want to try, and tell me if it's a dead end: don't try to decide whether the system is conscious, try to decide whether the system is *attributing* anything to itself — whether there's a state the system actually uses, as opposed to a state that we the observers find it convenient to describe it as having. So the first filter is something like: is the candidate state read out by other parts of the system, does it change downstream behaviour in a way that's sensitive to its content rather than just its presence, is it reportable or reportable-equivalent, and can it come apart from the stimulus that produced it. Then a second filter on whether the thing being attributed is the kind of thing that could be good or bad for anyone — a scalar that the policy gradient just eats seems different from a state the system represents as being about its own condition. I know this maps onto the usual suspects (global workspace, higher-order theories, recurrent processing, predictive-processing accounts of affect) but I don't want a survey of those, I want the operational version. And I want it honest about the seams — which steps are real tests we can run and which are us dressing up a judgment call as a test.

Then run it on three things and don't be polite about the results: (1) the organoid line, where the PI will be furious if we slow them down and where I suspect the procedure will tell us something inconvenient; (2) the octopus avoidance study, where my gut says approve and I want to know whether the procedure agrees or whether my gut is doing the work; and (3) a big pretraining-plus-RLHF run on a simulated pain-avoidance task, where the grad student will argue that if we flag this we have to flag every training run ever. For each one give me the verdict, the step that did the deciding, and what would have to be true for the verdict to flip. Also tell me the cases where the procedure shouldn't be allowed to make the call at all and a human has to own it, and what to do about the fact that the procedure will let through some things that are bad and block some things that are fine — if we have to pick a direction of error, which one, and why.
