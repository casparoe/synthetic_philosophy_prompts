I am a doctoral candidate in comparative philosophy, and the seminar I am presenting in requires me to defend a single thesis rather than survey a literature, so I need a verdict and not a menu. The comparison I have been assigned is between two responses to human mortality that are often lumped together as consolations but seem to me to rest on quite different foundations: the Epicurean claim that death is nothing to the one who dies, since good and evil consist in sensation and death is the absence of sensation, and the Zhuangzian attitude in which dying is one phase in the ceaseless transformation of things, to be met with equanimity — the attitude behind the familiar stories of his drumming and singing after his wife's death, and of his indifference to whether his own corpse is eaten by crows above ground or by ants below.

I want you to compare these two positions on the specific question of whether death is an evil for the person who dies, and then to commit to a verdict about which position is more defensible and where the other one fails. Please treat the following as pressure points you have to work through rather than merely gesture at, and do not simply rehearse what each thinker is standardly said to hold.

First, keep apart two claims that the popular summaries run together: that death is not a harm at all, and that death may be a harm but equanimity toward it is nonetheless warranted. Decide which of the two positions actually asserts which, and say whether the Zhuangzian talk of transformation is best read as a metaphysical claim about what death is or as a therapeutic stance toward it, and what follows for the comparison either way.

Second, take seriously a case neither doctrine was built for: a person dies leaving a body of work she cared about above all else, and that work is afterwards misrepresented or destroyed. Does the Epicurean view leave any room to call this a misfortune? Does the Zhuangzian? Use the divergence, if there is one, to say which view better accommodates our ordinary conviction that the dead can be wronged.

Third, address grief and mourning directly. Epicureanism seems addressed chiefly to the fear of death and to say little about why we mourn, whereas the Zhuangzian stories are precisely about mourning and about the disposal of the body. Say whether this asymmetry marks a real advantage for one view or only a difference in what each was trying to do.

Finally, give me your verdict: which of the two you would defend, what you take its strongest version to be, and the single objection to it you find hardest to answer. State your position plainly and give your reasons, and if the comparison forces you to side with one view on one issue while conceding the other elsewhere, say so explicitly rather than balancing the two into a draw.
