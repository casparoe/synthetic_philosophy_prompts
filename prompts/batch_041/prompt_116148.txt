I write a science-and-ideas column and host a small interview podcast on the side, and I am preparing for a long conversation with a philosopher of mind whose work I want to take seriously and also test hard. Her central claim, as I understand it, is that the unpleasantness of pain is not a sensory ingredient of the experience — not a special bad flavour added on top of the stinging or the burning — but a motivational state. Pain hurts, on her account, roughly the way a command presses on you: it demands that you protect the injured part, and its badness lies in that demand rather than in any intrinsic quality of the sensation. Her principal evidence is pain asymbolia, the rare condition in which a person has pain without being bothered by it. My understanding of the clinical picture is that it is seen after damage to the insula and neighbouring cortex, and also after cingulotomy and in patients on morphine; that such patients can locate the stimulus and rate its intensity while saying, in effect, that it does not trouble them; and that the older descriptions stress how little the usual defensive reaction is in evidence. She reads the syndrome as showing that the affective dimension of pain can be peeled away from the sensory one, and therefore that the badness of pain is separable from the sensation itself.

I would like eight to ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am never left holding a thread she has already dropped. Please give the sequence roughly the following weight, in roughly this order:

1. The deflationary reading. Press on whether asymbolia might show only that patients describe pain differently — that they have found a new way of talking about a sensation they no longer bother to complain about, or that the word 'bothered' is carrying weight the phenomenology cannot support. Ask what would distinguish her reading from the claim that the sensation is still unpleasant and only the report of it has changed, and what she would say if the patients turn out to be poor witnesses to their own experience.

2. What is left in the experience. Ask her to be specific: is the asymbolic patient feeling something bad but not motivating, something motivating but not felt as bad, or nothing that deserves the name of pain at all? Then press on whether her own view can accommodate the first of those without collapsing into the position she rejects.

3. The 'ow' problem. If badness is a demand rather than a quality, why does ordinary pain hurt rather than merely instruct? Ask her to account for the felt urgency of pain without quietly reintroducing the sensory ingredient — and if she says the demand is itself felt, whether that is not simply intrinsic badness under another name.

4. The evidential base. Ask how much a lesion syndrome can tell us about the nature of ordinary pain: whether a conclusion about the structure of pain can rest on a small number of patients with brain damage whose reports may be shaped by impairment, medication, or the testing situation, and whether the cingulotomy and morphine cases are convergent evidence or a different phenomenon altogether. Follow up by asking what result would cut against her.

5. The two-component picture. Ask whether her account is a version of the familiar division between the sensory-discriminative and the affective-motivational dimensions of pain, or a rival to it — and if it is a version, how it explains the fact that the two normally travel together so tightly, and why we are not constantly awash in raw sensations whose significance we then have to decide upon.

6. Axiology. Ask what follows if pain's badness is extrinsic or conative: is anything bad in itself, and what becomes of the longstanding treatment of pain as the paradigm case of something intrinsically bad? If pain is bad because it commands, does its badness depend on our desires in a way that makes it merely instrumental — and does she accept that consequence or resist it?

7. Animals and the non-verbal. Ask what her view says about pain in creatures we cannot ask about the minding, and whether a rat that licks a burnt paw is suffering or merely being moved. Follow up on whether her account makes suffering, rather than pain, the thing that matters morally, and what that does to where we draw the line of moral concern.

8. The clinical upshot. Ask what changes if suffering is the conative element: whether the aim of analgesia is better described as removing the demand than as removing the sensation, whether that reframes how we should treat persistent pain that comes without proportional tissue damage, and whether she thinks the relief of pain and the relief of suffering can come apart in ways clinicians should care about.

Close with a question that forces her to state what evidence would count against the view, and one that asks her to draw the line between pain and suffering in a single sentence. I would also like two or three conditional branches — if she answers one way at question 2, the follow-up at question 3 should change accordingly; if she concedes something at question 6, I want a question ready that presses on it. Please keep everything on the substance of the position: nothing about how she came to the topic, nothing about what I should read next.
