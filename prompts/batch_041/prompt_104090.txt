Next month I'm presenting to my department's moral responsibility reading group a paper arguing that karma is a real desert theory and not just a supernatural ledger — that the good and bad a person undergoes can be genuinely deserved by their own past actions. The twist is that I don't want to lean on a soul or on memory continuity: I treat the desert as attaching to a causal stream rather than to a persisting self, roughly the way you can say something is good for a person's later self without there being one enduring subject who owns it. The room is mostly free-will people — a couple of compatibilists, a libertarian or two, one hard determinist who thinks desert is a fiction across the board — plus a Buddhist studies scholar who already suspects I'm laundering a Western retributive framework through a tradition that dosen't have one, and a social psychologist who studies belief in a just world and will hear the whole thing as that.

Prep me for the questions. Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come and how much damage each one does, phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks.
