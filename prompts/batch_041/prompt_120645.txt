So here's a game I want to play. Take Kant — the real one, working in the 1780s and 90s, full toolkit, nothing added — and drop him into the present day with complete knowledge of everything we've learned since. Not "what would he tweet." I mean: given his own commitments, what would his considered position be on questions that simply weren't live in his lifetime?

Animals is where I think the interesting action is. Everyone knows the shape of the recieved view: animals aren't ends in themselves, we have only indirect duties concerning them, cruelty is wrong because of what it does to us, and using them as means is fine so long as you aren't brutalizing yourself in the process. That's the boring summary and any model can spit it out. I want the stress test.

So put him in front of (a) industrial animal agriculture, where nobody is doing the beating and the whole thing is a supply chain; (b) cultivated meat, which involves no animal suffering at all but does involve taking cells and "using" animals in some sense; (c) a legal regime that gives animals standing and enforceable rights while explicitly denying them any rational or autonomous nature — rights without dignity, basically; and (d) the last few decades of animal cognition work, which makes the "they have no reason" premise look considerably softer than it did in his day.

Now the injected bit. Suppose a colleague of mine proposes what she calls the lost-reason test. Take any treatment of an animal and ask whether you could do the same thing to a human being who has permanently lost the capacity for reason — someone in a deep vegetative state, or a person with profound cognitive disability. If the answer is "no, obviously not, that would be monstrous," then reason can't be what's doing the moral work, since that human doesn't have it either. If the answer is "yes, actually," you've bitten a bullet almost nobody will bite. She thinks this shows the indirect-duty view is either unstable or covertly leaning on species membership.

Would Kant agree with her? If not, what's the move that saves him — reason present only in germ, the noumenal self, the fact that the individual belongs to a kind whose normal members are rational? And is that move principled, or is it a patch? Then: does any of it change what he says about (a) through (d)?
