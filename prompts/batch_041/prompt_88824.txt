I write software for a living — streaming data systems, mostly — and a design review last month turned into an argument I still haven't settled. The question was whether the unbounded sequences our library exposes are genuinely infinite or merely a convenient fiction, and I found that I could not state the difference between the two in any terms I actually trust. So here is what I would like from you: take the philosophical debate about actual infinity and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I think in.

The debate, as I understand it from the outside, has three parts. First, the claim that completed infinite totalities are legitimate objects of mathematics, along with the arguments meant to make them tractable — Hilbert's hotel being the standard illustration that an infinite collection can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself. Second, the diagonal argument that some infinite totalities outrun any enumeration of them, so that not all infinities are of the same size. Third, the finitist and constructivist objections: that only what is finite, or only what can be constructed, ought to be admitted, and that classical mathematics traffics in infinities it cannot produce.

The vocabulary I want it in is that of programming and computation: lazy evaluation and generators; the distinction between a value and a procedure for producing values; bijection as isomorphism of representations; enumeration as an algorithm together with its resource cost; decidability and the halting problem; asymptotic density; and the everyday difference between unbounded and infinite. I am a competent working programmer with an undergraduate mathematics minor — comfortable with computability, big-O, and the rudiments of type theory — but I have never studied set theory formally and I know no category theory, so do not soften the mappings in order to protect me.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about most.

- Which claims in the debate carry over into statements about programs and representations without loss, which carry over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all?
- Where a claim fails to carry over, is that a defect of my vocabulary — something real that computation cannot express — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it appeared to?
- My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed: that everything Hilbert's hotel is used to demonstrate becomes banal in my vocabulary (shifting the indices of an infinite sequence plainly does not add anything to it), and that the diagonal argument survives only as a statement about effective enumeration — that is, as the halting problem — while shedding its claim to be about size. If that is right, tell me exactly what is lost when uncountable becomes not effectively enumerable, and whether anything is lost at all.
- And in the other direction: is there a finitism native to my vocabulary, something like the view that only what can be constructed within a resource bound exists, and does that view retain any of the force of the original, or does its force come from something the resource-bound reading leaves out?

Finally, the bottom line: if I were to tell my colleague that from where I sit the whole dispute is either obvious or empty, what precisely would I be getting wrong?
