I am setting the final examination for an upper-level module on rational choice and practical rationality (third-year undergraduates, with a handful of master's students), and I need one essay question on money pumps and diachronic rationality. It will be worth 20 marks; candidates get 75 minutes and a word limit of 900. Please write the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme.

Three constraints govern everything you write. First, the question must be self-contained: candidates sit it with no notes and no internet, so it must not require recall of a reading, an author, or a named position, and the model answer should make its points without leaning on citations. Second, the question must not be answerable by reciting the standard story that a money pump refutes intransitive preferences; the difficulty should lie in locating what the argument actually establishes and what it presupposes. Third, the case below is invented for this paper, so no candidate can have met it; keep its details exactly as given and make the question turn on them.

The case to build the question around. Priya spends one week in a co-working space that rents her one of three desks per day: the Window desk (W), the Standing desk (S) and the Quiet desk (Q). Her preferences are cyclic: she prefers S to Q (her back), Q to W (the glare on the screen), and W to S (standing all day tires her legs). For each of the three possible swaps she would pay up to $3; the space charges $2 per swap, and she must confirm every swap herself. The manager makes one offer per day, Monday through Wednesday: each offer is the swap from the desk she currently holds to the next desk in the cycle (W→S→Q→W), and an offer she declines is simply repeated the following day. Before Monday's first offer, and only then, she may buy a lock for $4 that blocks all swaps for the week. She knows all of this in advance, knows her own preferences perfectly, and has no other commitment device.

The question must require candidates to do all five of the following, and the marking scheme should be built on the five corresponding moves:

1. Reconstruct the pump for Priya's case, using the numbers, and state exactly what the sure loss is.
2. Set out the assumptions the reconstruction needs — about how desks and money combine in her preferences, about her willingness to take a strictly preferred swap whenever one is offered, and about what commitment is available — and say which of them a critic could reasonably reject.
3. Work through four ways she might spend the week: taking every offer; declining every offer from the start; buying the lock in advance; and taking exactly one swap and then declining every later offer. Say which leaves her best off, and whether that last path is open to her, given that at each later moment the swap she declines is one she prefers.
4. Say what the case establishes about her — that she is exploitable, that she is sure to suffer a loss, or that she is irrational — and defend the distinction rather than running the three together.
5. Take a stand on whether the argument is synchronic or diachronic: whether it can support transitivity as a requirement on a preference ranking at a time, or whether it presupposes a norm governing a sequence of choices; and say what someone who holds that rationality is entirely synchronic should conclude about Priya. As a diagnostic, the question should also ask why the same three-day set-up would pose no problem at all for an agent whose preferences over the three desks are transitive.

The model answer should resolve the case rather than survey it: it should use the numbers, reach a verdict on Priya, and be candid about which premise the verdict rests on and what would have to be true for the opposite verdict. Do not let it drift into the general literature on money pumps; the value of the item is that it forces the candidate to say what the argument assumes. The marking scheme should list five must-hit points, the marks each carries (totalling 20), and, for each point, one line naming the mistake candidates typically make; end with a single line distinguishing an A answer from a B answer.
