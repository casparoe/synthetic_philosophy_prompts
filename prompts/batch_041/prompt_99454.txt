Write a deadpan op-ed of about 600 words for an invented quarterly of strategic studies, by an invented official who heads a 'commitment office' in the defense ministry of a nuclear-armed state. His argument: our deterrent is not believed because everyone knows we would never carry it out, and the remedy is less control, not more. He proposes to put execution genuinely beyond human hands and then to tune the residual probability of an accidental launch upward until the threat is credible again — all in the register of sober cost-benefit analysis, complete with a rival school he accuses of insufficient nerve.

He must get the underlying logic exactly right, so that a reader who knows the deterrence literature laughs rather than winces, while a reader who does not still follows him: a threat is believed only if executing it would still be in the threatener's interest afterwards; the two standard escapes are to look irrational and to delegate the decision to something that cannot reconsider; brinkmanship is the deliberate manufacture of a shared risk that neither side fully controls, so a small chance of catastrophe is an asset rather than a liability; hence safety devices, hotlines and defenses are a drag on deterrence; and mutual assured destruction made small wars safe and therefore likelier, which pushes brinkmanship down the ladder. Never let him wink. The reader should see the reductio — that his argument, taken seriously, recommends maximizing the chance of an accident — without the author ever conceding it.

Then add a note of about 150 words, set off from the piece and in your own voice, separating what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested (whether the credibility problem is really as he describes it, whether such commitments have ever worked, whether the historical record bears out the paradoxes he invokes), and flag where the op-ed takes liberties with the ideas it uses.
