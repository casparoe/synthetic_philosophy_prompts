I'm a junior at a suburban high school and I run our philosophy club, which meets Tuesdays after school for about an hour with roughly sixteen members, mostly sophomores and juniors who have never read any philosophy. Last week my teacher gave us a handout with a case she called Parfit's hitchhiker: you're stranded in a desert, a stranger offers you a ride into town on the condition that you pay them once you arrive, and the trouble is that once you're standing in town with your wallet, paying has nothing to recommend it — the driver is gone, you'll never see them again, no one will know. So a driver who reasons it through will never stop for you at all, and the person who "wins" the reasoning loses the ride. I follow the case itself, but I cannot get my head around the claim that being the sort of person who would pay — or being constitutionally unable to talk yourself out of paying — might be the rational thing to be, and I'd much rather my club feel that tension than hear me explain it. So I'd like you to design the whole 55-minute session as an activity we play, not a discussion: the rules and the payoffs should do the teaching, and I should be able to run it without lecturing up front. Please give me the setup, the materials (I'd rather use index cards and a bowl of candy or slips of paper as the currency than real money, and I want the numbers written out so I can copy them onto a whiteboard), the roles, the exact sequence and timings, and a debrief that walks back through what happened and names the ideas.

Here's the structure I was imagining, though I'd like you to fix it or replace it if something better serves the point. I want at least two rounds and a third phase. In the first round everyone draws a secret card making them either a Driver or a Traveler, Drivers choose first whether to stop, and only Travelers whose Driver stopped then choose privately whether to pay — the arithmetic should be tuned so that a Driver who thinks Travelers will defect doesn't stop, and so that the group's actual outcome is mostly no rides for anybody, which should annoy them. In the second round I want a phase where, before anyone knows whether they'll be a Driver or a Traveler, everyone votes on a rule they'll be bound by for the rest of the session, and one of the options should cost something real in candy to adopt — a commitment device, a publicly worn badge, a signed card in an envelope — so that some people pay to make themselves the sort who can't back out. The interesting moment, I think, is when the people who paid the cost start getting picked up and the people who kept their options open don't, and I want the rules arranged so that this happens because of what they chose earlier, not because I tell them it should. What I want members to walk away having felt, without me saying it, is that the choice between being a certain kind of person and choosing well on the day may not be two separate questions, and that a theory of rationality which only looks at what pays off at the moment of action may have no room for the move that saves you. If you think the whole two-round structure buries the point, say so and give me the version you'd run instead.

In the debrief I want you to map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the idea — where the Driver's reasoning is the crux, where the commitment purchase is doing the work, where the one-shot anonymity of my setup is doing the work and might be smuggling in the answer — and then to tell me honestly what the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words: I suspect a real disposition is not something you can buy at a table, and that once people can see each other's badges the case stops being the case, but I'd like your list rather than my guess. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my members are good-natured and I expect at least two of them to pay out of sheer niceness, which would wreck the arithmetic, and someone will probably announce that it's all just about reputation, which I think is a different point — and what I should do when it happens. One last thing, and I mean this seriously: in the debrief notes, keep separate the parts of this that people who study it actually agree on, like the shape of the case and the fact that it's a commitment problem, from the parts that are genuinely disputed, like what the case shows about rationality, whether "rational irrationality" is even a coherent idea, or whether the right conclusion is about how you should decide rather than about what you should want. If a reading of it is contested, please label it as contested instead of writing as though everyone who knows the topic signs off on it. Write everything in American English, and I don't need a reading list — just the activity.
