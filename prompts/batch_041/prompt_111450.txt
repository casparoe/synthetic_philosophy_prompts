I sit on the diagnostic quality committee at a teaching hospital, and I have to circulate a written opinion before the committee votes at the end of the month. The question is what to do about prior clinical information reaching our radiologists before they read an image.

Under our current protocol, the reading radiologist sees the referral note, the relevant history, and — on second reads — the first radiologist's report. A subgroup wants blinded first reads with independent second reads. Our imaging director objects on three grounds: that context is clinically useful; that a blinded service would cost roughly a third more radiologist hours; and that the case for change rests on a claim he rejects, namely that knowing what to look for can change what a radiologist literally sees, and not merely what she is willing to call abnormal. If the effect is confined to judgment and reporting, he argues, blinding is the wrong instrument and calibration and audit are the right ones. What we actually have is an audit showing that the same two hundred prior images, re-read with the referral note visible, yielded about a fifth more incidental findings than when read without it — a result each side cites in its own favour. I do not think the audit settles anything, and I would like the opinion to say why or why not.

We also commissioned an external review that proposes a three-way distinction: registration (the prior changes what is perceptually taken in), threshold (the percept is unchanged but the bar for calling something abnormal moves), and formulation (percept and judgment unchanged, but the report reads differently). The review claims these can be pulled apart by comparing readings under prior-clamped, prior-known, and prior-revealed conditions. Two members think the taxonomy is unfalsifiable and that we should decide on error rates alone.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should give a clear recommendation, with a time frame and any conditions attached; state plainly whether the three-way distinction is usable for our purposes, and whether it makes any difference to what we should do; say what would have to be true of perception for the imaging director to be right; treat his position at its strongest, including what we should do if he turns out to be correct; and specify what evidence would make us reverse course. Two committee members are lay representatives, so it has to be readable without jargon. No citations are needed — argue the case rather than surveying a literature.
