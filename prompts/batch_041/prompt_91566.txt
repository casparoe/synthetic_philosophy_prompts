I'm a PhD student working on the philosophy of mind in the Arabic tradition, and I'm drafting a chapter on whether Avicenna's flying man is the sort of argument a case could ever confirm or embarrass. I take the thought experiment as Avicenna gives it: suppose one of us were created at a stroke, fully formed and perfect, suspended in the air, unable to perceive anything external and with his limbs held apart so that they cannot feel one another; he would still affirm his own existence while affirming nothing of his limbs, his organs, or his brain, and would not ascribe any magnitude to the self he affirms. Please treat the following as given in your answer. This is not a cogito: there is no inference, and self-awareness is not an act the soul performs but something it possesses. The target is not the mind's dependence on the brain — Avicenna is content to let the rational soul use the body as an instrument for ordinary cognition — but the narrower claim that self-awareness is acquired through the senses and through the body's awareness of itself. And the argument has been read two ways: as a conceivability argument for the soul's immateriality and substantiality, and as a first-person argument about the givenness of self-awareness, aimed at a psychology on which the soul is only intelligible as the form of a living body. Note also that Avicenna stipulates his subject is created fully formed and perfect, which is not an incidental detail.

Now the case. Read the last three decades of work on disorders of consciousness through the argument, and the argument through the case: patients who meet the criteria for the vegetative state and yet follow commands by imagining actions; the locked-in patient who dictated a memoir one blink at a time; and above all the total locked-in patient, in whom even the eyes are gone and every channel of testimony is closed, so that the diagnostic categories themselves become contested. Which of the argument's premises does this material actually bear on, and which does the stipulation of a fully formed subject put out of reach? Does Avicenna's account predict what these patients report — a self that survives the loss of the sensorimotor body — or does it fail to anticipate how much of that self remains embodied: located, affective, dependent on a body image it cannot shed? Be honest about the epistemological knot: the argument's datum is first-personal and non-inferential, whereas every piece of evidence for the patient's awareness is third-personal, a blink or a hemodynamic response, so the case can reach the argument only if we take testimony on trust — and testimony is itself bodily behavior. Tell me which reading of the argument the case favors, where the argument is simply silent rather than vindicated, and whether the episode counts as evidence for Avicenna's conclusion, against it, or as a change of subject. No reading list or citations, please; I want the analysis.
