I've been lurking on rationalist blogs for way too long and I keep getting sucked back into the identity of indiscernibles. Leibniz's principle says no two things can differ in no respect; Max Black's two-spheres case is the classic alleged counterexample — a universe containing exactly two perfectly similar iron spheres arranged symmetrically, so there's supposedly nothing in the world to make them two rather than one. Leibniz would just say that universe is impossible.

What I actualy want from you: suppose we could plonk Leibniz down in front of everything that happened after him. Black's spheres, sure, but more interestingly the modern physics of identical particles — two electrons, say, treated as genuinely indistinguishable, where swapping them isn't a different physical situation at all, and where this is baked into the formalism rather than being mere ignorance, and where people now argue over whether such particles are still discernible in some weaker relational sense, since each bears a relation to the other that it doesn't bear to itself.

Tell me what Leibniz would actually say. Does the quantum case vindicate his principle, or would he say the physicists are conflating indiscernibility with numerical identity, or would he retreat and say the principle only ranges over complete individual concepts and not over physical objects? Be specific about which of his own commitments push him each way, and flag where you're genuinely guessing versus where his principles force your hand. And if the whole question is a category error — if modern identity-of-indiscernibles debates aren't even about the same thing as Leibniz's principle — then just say that, don't humor me.
