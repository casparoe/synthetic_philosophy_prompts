Three weeks ago a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about Lakatos, and we did not settle it, which has become a problem: in a month I am to give the closing address at the annual banquet of our field's professional society, and she will be sitting at the second table.

The room will hold about a hundred and fifty people. Perhaps thirty of them are historians or philosophers of our field and will follow every step of a Lakatosian argument; thirty more are graduate students who know the vocabulary but have never had to use it; the remainder are working researchers, a few practitioners, the society's staff, several donors, and my wife's family. The address is expected to do the traditional thing — to take stock of where the field stands — and I would like it to do that honestly rather than as a courtesy.

Here is what we could not settle. On the ordinary reading, a research programme is progressive when it keeps generating predictions of novel facts and some of those predictions are borne out, and degenerating when it survives mainly by accommodating anomalies after the fact — patching the protective belt rather than extending the reach of the hard core. My friend thinks that criterion is too coarse to be applied to anything real, and she proposes a refinement I have not been able to dismiss. She wants to separate the novelty of a prediction from its subject matter. A programme, she says, can go on producing genuinely novel predictions that are genuinely corroborated while the content of those predictions migrates steadily away from claims about the world and toward claims about the programme's own machinery: the behaviour of its instruments, the values of its internal parameters, the outputs of its own simulations, the performance of its own estimators and protocols. On the standard criterion such work looks like the picture of health — novel predictions, successful tests, no ad hoc rescue. She says it is the signature of a programme that has begun to feed on itself and that we need a third category for it, or at least a warning label. Her evidence, in our case, is the last decade of the field's flagship journal, which now publishes mostly results about how our models behave under perturbation, how competing formalisms compare, and how to estimate things better — and comparatively little about the systems the models were built to describe. I say that if the predictions are novel and corroborated, their subject matter cannot by itself be disqualifying, and that she is smuggling in a taste for metaphysics over measurement; she says that I am calling a programme healthy because I cannot imagine what its death would look like.

I would like the address to take a position on this without becoming a seminar. First, state her refinement as precisely as it can be stated: what exactly would count as the content of a prediction migrating inward, and how one would distinguish that from the ordinary, healthy division of labour in which instrument work, calibration, and methodological housekeeping are simply what a mature programme does. Then say whether the refinement is a genuine third category, a useful diagnostic short of a category, or a re-description of the ordinary criterion. Then apply it to our field's dominant research programme — the one everyone in the room is actually waiting to hear about — and commit to a verdict: progressive, degenerating, or her third thing, with the evidence you would rest it on.

The verdict will determine what the speech can be. If the programme is healthy, the usual arc is available — we have come far, the young people will take it further — and I can close on the founder's students, who will be in the front rows, without flattering them. If it is degenerating, or feeding on itself, then that arc is dishonest, and I have to find some other way to be truthful in a banquet hall at ten o'clock at night without delivering a funeral oration for a living enterprise or using a festive occasion to settle scores. The founder is ninety-one and will be there; his two most senior students are the people who would hear a degenerating verdict as a verdict on them. So the speech has to be one a person could actually give in that room.

Write the address. It should run about twelve minutes spoken. It should be intelligible, and even enjoyable, for the practitioners, donors, and relatives present — no unexplained jargon, no recitation of anyone's results — while the philosophers at the second table should be able to see that the position taken is a real position and not a gesture. Do not cite literature or offer a reading list; the audience wants a verdict and an argument, not a bibliography. Tell me plainly what you are claiming, what it would take to show you wrong, and what I should say to my friend when we walk out.
