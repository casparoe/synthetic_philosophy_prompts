Owen, who works on our distributed scheduler and reads alot of this stuff on the side, said the interesting part is that the twenty can't coordinate. Nadia, who's on the auctions team and is very into mechanism design, said the interesting part is that it's a solved problem. To make it less trivial they bolted an extra rule onto it: each claim has to come with a $1,000 fee, so if you claim and somebody else also claims, you're out a grand and get nothing. My retelling of the setup is probably mangled, so correct me if I've got it wrong, and I've almost certainly botched the arithmetic below, so please redo it rather than taking my numbers on faith.

Here's how it went. (1) Nadia said: with the fee, there's a mixed equilibrium where each of the twenty claims with probability p such that the expected value of claiming is exactly zero, which works out to p around 0.30. In that profile everyone's expected payoff is zero, and it's the only profile where nobody can do better by changing their own behavior alone. So that's the answer, and "superrationality" is a distraction. (2) Owen said that's a terrible answer, because the profile where all twenty claim with probability 0.05 gives each of them something like $19k in expectation, and it requires no communication at all. He says the "nobody can improve unilaterally" test is the wrong test here, since the twenty are stipulated to be identical reasoners — if a move is right for one of them it's right for all of them, so there isn't really any such thing as "one of them deviating." He says the right question is what rule you'd want all twenty to follow, and the equilibrium sure isn't it. (3) Nadia said the unilateral-improvement test isn't a technicality, it's the whole thing. If everyone else is at 0.05, then any single participant who claims for sure makes around $375k in expectation, so the profile is one where everybody has a massive reason to jump ship. And 0.05 is a knife-edge: if you think the others claim less than roughly 30% of the time, claiming for sure is better; if you think they claim more than that, never claiming is better; 0.05 is only right for you if you're certain the others are sitting at exactly 0.05 and thinking exactly what you're thinking. That's not a recommendation, it's a coordination assumption in disguise. (4) Owen said the equilibrium is just as knife-edged and its knife-edge is worse, because it's the point where everyone is exactly indifferent between claiming and not — i.e., where everyone gets nothing. If a decision theory's answer to "what should twenty identical reasoners do" is "randomize so precisely that all the surplus is destroyed and everyone ends up with zero," while they could each have expected $19k, then the theory has a coordination-shaped hole in it. He'd rather describe the twenty as failing to solve a coordination problem than as each of them acting correctly, and he adds that when you've literally got the same code running in all twenty, the "deviation" move isn't available, because the code is what it is in all of them. (5) Nadia said: then point to the coordination device. Communication is forbidden and you don't know who the others are, so any rule you can actually apply is a function of your own private information, which is the same kind of thing for all twenty of you — so at best you get all-claim or all-refrain, both worth nothing, and the good outcome (exactly one claim) needs an asymmetry nobody is in a position to produce. The failure is real, she says, but it's a fact about the missing mechanism, not a gap in decision theory; and if you're allowed to write one policy for all twenty copies then you've changed the problem, which is like saying the two generals problem is easy if both generals are the same general.
