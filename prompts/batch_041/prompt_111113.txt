In a Westminster-style constitutional monarchy such as the United Kingdom's, the head of state is at once the best-informed and the least powerful official in the state, and the arrangement looks deliberate rather than accidental. She assents to every statute, yet assent is by convention never refused, and a refusal would precipitate a constitutional crisis. She appoints the prime minister, yet must appoint whoever can command the legislature. She is briefed on the government's most sensitive business and meets the prime minister privately and regularly, and no minutes are taken. Every legally significant act she performs is done on ministerial advice, and responsibility for it falls on the minister rather than on her, so that in law she can neither do wrong nor do anything on her own account. Yet the same order that denies her discretion assigns her a personal function — to question the government, to counsel it, and to warn it — and in a hung parliament, or with a prime minister who will not resign after losing the confidence of the legislature, it leaves her a genuine discretion that no written rule resolves.

What theory of political authority does this pattern presuppose? Does it commit its defenders to a determinate view about where sovereignty resides, or could several incompatible views underwrite it equally well? Is the rule that ministers, not the monarch, answer for her acts an application of a general thesis — that authority is legitimate only when some identifiable person is accountable for it — so that the monarch's unaccountable discretion is tolerable precisely on condition that it is never exercised? I am especially interested in what I have been calling constitutional latency: an authority whose entire effect is produced by the standing possibility of its exercise, so that actually using it would extinguish it. Is that a coherent species of authority or an unstable notion, and can a power be genuine if its exercise destroys it? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, identify the rival theories it quietly rules out, and say where the practice is underdetermined, so that a quite different account of legitimacy would license exactly these institutions. Then say what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by the view that the monarch is a ceremonial placeholder whose functions require no discretion whatever.
