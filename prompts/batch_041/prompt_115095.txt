Compare two kinds of self-binding: the Ulysses contract, in which an agent who foresees that he will later want to do what he now wants not to do arranges in advance that he cannot do it, and constitutional precommitment, in which a political community entrenches rules — rights, supermajority requirements, an independent judiciary — so that later majorities cannot do what they might otherwise want. The comparison is often credited to Jon Elster; I am less interested in the pedigree than in whether the analogy survives pressure, and I would like you to test it rather than assume it.

The pressure comes from four places.

First, agency. Odysseus's ropes bind one person, and the self that ties the knot and the self that strains against it are the same person at different times; the binding is intelligible as a way of managing one's own predictable inconsistency. In the political case it is unclear who the agent is. If a founding generation binds its successors, the successors are governed by preferences they had no part in forming, and the objection that the living are ruled by the dead has real force. If instead we say that a people binds itself, we need an account of what makes the later people the same people — the problem of personal identity transposed to a collective, and harder, because a people may be partly constituted by the very document that is supposed to bind it.

Second, the model of the act. A Ulysses contract is a self-administered restraint. A constitution is at least as much like a promise: it is public, it creates expectations in others, and it gives third parties — citizens, courts, other states — standing to hold the promisor to it. Work out what follows if the promise model is the better one. Does it make the constraint more robust or less? Does it locate the binding force somewhere other than the agent's own will, and does that help or hurt the case for entrenchment?

Third, calibration. A rope one cannot slip and a rope one can slip at will are equally useless; the device must be tight enough to bind and loose enough to remain the agent's own. Identify the constitutional analogue of that calibration, and say whether the fact that a determined majority can always override a constitution at some cost is a defect of constitutionalism or the very feature that makes it tolerable.

Fourth, the release problem. Advance directives are Ulysses contracts in the clinic: a person while competent refuses treatment in advance, and later, incompetent but apparently content, refuses it again. Say who should win and why, then carry the answer back to politics — under what conditions, if any, may a people release itself from its constitution?

Close by saying which parts of the analogy you would keep and which you would drop, and what constitutional precommitment looks like if the Ulysses model is abandoned altogether.
