Two philosophers of mind have spent a year watching the same harvester-ant colony, and they have come to disagree about whether it has a mind. They have taken to calling the colony Aunt Hillary.

The colony consists of roughly half a million ants, no ant having more than a few hundred thousand neurons, and no ant occupying any vantage point on the colony as a whole. Yet at the colony level it behaves in ways that are hard not to describe as deliberate. It settles on the richer of two sugar feeders after a stretch of apparent hesitation, and sometimes reverses itself. It reroutes a foraging column around a new barrier within a day. It returns to a feeder it has not visited in a fortnight. It emigrates to a new nest site only once a quorum of scouts has converged on one.

One of the two holds that Aunt Hillary has a mind: that she believes and desires things, and is at least a serious candidate for phenomenal consciousness, because what makes something a mind is its functional organisation, and the colony realises the right kind. On this view the ants stand to Aunt Hillary roughly as neurons stand to us.

The other holds that she has no mind at all: that there is no one home, that talk of the colony's beliefs and desires is at best a compact summary of what half a million small agents are each doing on their own account, and that the impression of a single deliberating subject is an artefact of the observer rather than a fact about the colony.

Write the dialogue between them. Neither is a fool; each must answer the other's actual argument, concede what should be conceded, and press where pressing helps. The dispute is to be conducted on the merits of the case, not by appeal to who has said what, and not by quietly stipulating a definition of mind that decides the question in advance.

Two further cases must figure in the dialogue, and the two speakers must disagree about what they show.

The first is the slow substitution. One ant at a time, with no interruption of colony behaviour, every ant is replaced by a micro-machine that does nothing but follow pheromone gradients and lay pheromone by fixed rules. At the end of the process no component of the colony has anything resembling a nervous system, and yet the colony behaves exactly as it did before. Did Aunt Hillary survive the substitution, die in it, or was she never there?

The second is the fission. A wall is driven through the nest and the colony is divided into two halves that can no longer reach each other. Each half reorganises within days into a functioning colony with its own foragers, its own nest, and, within a month, open hostility toward the other half. The two halves now have nothing to do with one another. Was there one mind before and two after, two before and one after, or was there only ever a convenient way of speaking?

Let the dialogue end where the argument actually ends: with the two of them agreeing, if they can, on what observation would settle the question, and on why it may be a question that no amount of watching ants can settle.
