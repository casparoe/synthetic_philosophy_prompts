I've been working through the case for what is sometimes called geometric rationality: the thesis that a rational agent facing a sequence of bets should maximize the expected logarithm of her wealth — equivalently, the expected growth rate of her capital — rather than expected wealth, and, more ambitiously, that this is not merely practical advice about how to avoid ruin but a genuine requirement of rationality that should govern how we assess any decision under risk. What convinces me is the combination of the ergodic argument, according to which over long horizons the time-average growth rate is what an agent actually experiences and maximizing it is what almost surely separates the survivor from the ruined, and the further thought that an agent who bets in a way that assigns positive probability to total ruin has made an error that no amount of expected value can redeem. I would like you to take this position seriously and tell me what would count against it.

What I am after is a map of the view's exposure: which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical patterns should move someone who holds it, sorted by how decisive they are; what the view predicts that rival accounts of rational choice do not; and whether it has been quietly qualified into something that risks nothing. In particular I would like you to address (i) the classic objection that maximizing expected log wealth is simply maximizing expected utility for a particular and arguably arbitrarily concave utility function, so that the view collapses into standard expected utility theory and inherits all of its problems — does this reduce its ambition or refute it? (ii) the objection that log-optimality is not in general compatible with accepting every favorable bet, nor with the value of information, nor with the way an agent should treat bets whose stakes are a large fraction of her wealth — which of these is a genuine defeat and which a misunderstanding? (iii) the case of a single, non-repeatable decision, where there is no sequence over which to average and the ergodic argument seems to lose its grip; and (iv) a modification a friend proposed that I find hard to assess, namely that geometric rationality should be applied to the whole trajectory of a life rather than to each bet separately, with wealth redefined to include non-monetary goods and the agent's concern for others, so that what is maximized is the expected log of a much richer quantity. Would that generalization strengthen the view or render it unfalsifiable, and what would count against the generalized version that would not count against the narrow one? I would especially like to know which of these objections a committed geometric rationalist would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as resting on a confusion — and, at the end, whether the view's insulation from counterexample is a defect or simply what any correct normative theory looks like.
