I recently moved from a biology background into a cognitive science programme, and the collision between the two has left me preoccupied with the standing of evolutionary psychology. Over the past few months I have settled into a position I did not expect to hold, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it. The position is roughly this: the familiar charge that adaptationist hypotheses about the mind are unfalsifiable just-so stories is overstated, because such hypotheses can be made to carry genuine empirical risk. Developed properly, a hypothesis about a cognitive adaptation commits itself to more than a redescription of known behaviour. It predicts a specific computational structure — a signature of special design, in the sense of economy, precision, and specificity that a domain-general learning mechanism would be unlikely to produce — and that structure can be probed by dissociations, by cross-species comparison, and by whether the hypothesis outperforms a byproduct-and-plasticity null model on some independently measurable dimension. On this view, evolutionary psychology is a normal science with a demanding but satisfiable evidential standard, and its critics have mistaken the difficulty of meeting that standard for the impossibility of doing so.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed that position really is. Which observations, arguments, or formal results would count against it, and which of those would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Test the position against concrete cases if that helps — the proposal that disgust functions as a behavioural immune system, the proposed cheater-detection module, the claim that low mood is an adaptation for analytical rumination — and tell me where the evidence cuts against the view rather than for it. I am especially interested in the gap between the position as an ideal and the field as it is actually practised: what would it take to show that the standard is satisfiable in principle but almost never met in fact, and would that be a problem for the position or only for the field? Tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not — say, the view that most of these traits are byproducts, or the view that they are better explained by developmental plasticity and cultural transmission — and identify the divergent bets that have already been run and what they came to. I would also like to know which of the standard escape hatches are legitimate qualifications and which amount to insulating the view from refutation: retreating to testability in principle, quietly widening the ancestral environment until it explains anything, or allowing the adaptation to be whatever mechanism happens to solve the problem on average. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, say which ones a committed defender would themselves concede rather than only what critics allege, and finish by telling me whether the position, as I have stated it, still risks anything at all — and if it does not, whether that is a defect in the position or simply the price of a mature research programme.
