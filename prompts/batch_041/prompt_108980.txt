You are a philosopher with expertise in practical reasoning, institutional ethics, and the theory of intention, and you are used to advising boards whose members have no philosophical training. I serve on the board of trustees of a small public community college. I am a retired hospital administrator, not an academic, but I have been turning this over on my own and I need a written opinion I can circulate before our next meeting.

The case: a wealthy alumnus has offered the college $1.5 million, paid immediately, if the board passes a resolution stating that it intends to eliminate our developmental math program in eighteen months. He is explicit that the resolution is not a contract; there is no legal or financial penalty if we change our minds and keep the program. He says he is not trying to buy the outcome. He wants to know whether an institution can form a sincere intention now that its future self may have no reason to carry out. To make the point sharper, he has added that if we pass the resolution and later reverse course after a public meeting, he will give another $150,000 to the scholarship fund—not as a penalty, he says, but because he wants to study what he calls institutional backsliding. The program serves about 300 students a year, many of them first-generation; eliminating it would save roughly the amount of the gift over three years, while the gift would fund scholarships and a tutoring center that many of the same students use. Our lawyer says we can pass the resolution without legal exposure. The president wants to accept. Several trustees say it would be dishonest to resolve to do something we may not do, and that accepting would make the board look cynical. Five of our nine trustees, including the president, will still be in office in eighteen months.

This strikes me as a version of the puzzle where a billionaire offers you money today if you intend to drink a painful but harmless toxin tomorrow, knowing that once you have the money you will have no reason to drink it. I want to know what the right decision is for a public body, not just for an individual. Can the board form the intention sincerely? Does sincerity matter, or may we treat the resolution as a statement of current priorities rather than a prediction about future action? If we accept, what should we do to make our later decision legitimate—should we commit now to a review process, or does that just pretend the puzzle away? If we reject, what do we owe the students and the donor?

Please write the memo I will circulate to the board. It should open with your recommendation in one or two sentences, then give the reasoning. Recommend a concrete course of action—accept, reject, or accept with specified conditions—and give reasons the other trustees could actually engage with. Include a realistic implementation and time frame: what we should do at the meeting, what we should say publicly, and what review we should schedule. Give the strongest argument for the side you do not recommend its full due, not a straw man. Keep the tone appropriate for a public college board: plain, serious, and free of academic jargon. Explain any technical idea in ordinary language, and do not assume I know the philosophical literature.

Before writing the memo, think carefully and briefly brainstorm the competing considerations in a few bullet points. Then write the memo itself. End with a single final line in exactly this machine-readable form: \answer{...} — inside the braces put your recommendation in no more than eight words, for example \answer{accept with a public review condition}.
