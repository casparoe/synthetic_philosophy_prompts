I've spent years reading the units-of-selection literature through the lens of the rationalist blogs — the gene's-eye view of Dawkins and Williams, the Sober-and-Wilson revival of group selection, and the recurring suggestion that the whole controversy is verbal — and I'd like a consistency audit of where I've landed, because I suspect my position set is unstable.

Here is what I hold:

(1) The gene's-eye view is the fundamental account of adaptation: a trait is an adaptation insofar as selection built it for its effects on the replication of alleles, and organisms are vehicles rather than the ultimate beneficiaries of selection.

(2) Group selection is a real and sometimes important evolutionary force, not a mere redescription; some traits are best explained as adaptations at the level of the group.

(3) Inclusive-fitness theory and multilevel-selection theory are formally equivalent: any model in one framework can be translated into the other without loss, so the choice between them is a matter of notational convenience rather than biological fact.

(4) The units-of-selection debate is nonetheless a substantive dispute with real stakes; it changes what counts as an explanation of altruism and cooperation, and it bears on how we understand the major evolutionary transitions.

(5) I am a causalist about explanation: to explain a trait is to identify the selection process that actually produced it, not merely to display a model under which its spread can be represented.

Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be, and — since I assume something has to go — which single commitment I should abandon and what abandoning it costs me. Order the tensions by severity, and give me a definite verdict rather than a menu of options: name the revision you would actually make and defend it. Cap the response at 600 words.
