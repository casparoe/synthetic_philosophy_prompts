I am assembling materials for a graduate workshop on the axiology of suffering, and I want a self-scoring instrument that sorts participants by the structure of their actual commitments about pleasure and pain, rather than by the labels they would volunteer. Please write a quiz of nine or ten items, each a concrete choice situation with two or three answer options, together with a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions.

Constraints on the items. No item may ask directly whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, whether pain is lexically prior to pleasure, or which view the respondent holds. Every item must present a case, and the discriminating work must be done by what respondents say about the case. Keep the item stems free of technical vocabulary, so that someone who has never opened the literature can answer using nothing more than ordinary comparisons of better, worse, and equally bad; do not name philosophers or works in the items. The following cases should anchor the instrument, since they are the ones I want it to turn on:

- a staircase case, in which a long series of individually trivial worsenings of an experience, each accompanied by an independently attractive reward, jointly deliver an outcome nobody would accept at the outset;
- a plain exchange-rate case, asking how much mild pleasure would justify a fixed quantity of intense but bearable pain;
- a threshold case, asking whether there is a level of pain that no amount of pleasure, however large, could offset, and a lower level at which ordinary tradeoffs are unobjectionable;
- a boundedness case, in which the lesser good is increased without limit, so that the respondent must say whether the priority survives an arbitrarily large quantity of it;
- a scope case, presenting the identical tradeoff three times: within a single life, divided across two lives, and divided across two beings of very different experiential capacities, so that the answers reveal whether the priority is meant to hold intrapersonally, interpersonally, across species, or in some combination;
- a forced-choice case, in which no option is neutral, so that the answers reveal whether the respondent's priority is a claim about which outcome is better or instead a constraint on what may be done;
- a parity case, constructed so that the two experiences are plainly not equally good and yet neither seems better than the other, with options that distinguish a hidden difference in value from a comparison that is on a par, from one that is indeterminate, and from one that has no truth value at all;
- a case involving an unbounded quantity of one of the two, to see whether the respondent treats the question as answerable or as ill-formed.

The scoring key should sort respondents into at least the following positions: full commensurability on a single scale, with no special role for pain; commensurability with an extreme but finite exchange rate; unbounded lexical priority of pain over pleasure; a bounded lexical threshold, in which the priority holds only up to some quantity of the lesser good or below some level of welfare, with ordinary tradeoffs resuming beyond it — the position I would call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism; that same threshold view restricted in scope to one life or extended to many; parity, on which the ordering of pleasure and pain is incomplete; indeterminacy, on which the ordering is complete but some comparisons lack a determinate verdict; and a constraint-based view, on which the values are commensurable but the tradeoff is forbidden all the same. Include the mirror-image position, lexical priority of pleasure over pain, as a leaf even if you judge that nobody holds it, and say what an adherent would have to say about the staircase case.

Then do three further things. First, identify which pairs of positions the quiz cannot separate, and for each pair say whether a further item could separate them or whether no item could, because the difference between the positions is not one any case can make salient. Second, list the answer patterns that correspond to no position in your key, and diagnose each as incoherent, merely unattractive, or merely unexplored. Third, examine the staircase case for consistency with the key: say whether a respondent who answers it in the natural way can still be sorted into the bounded-threshold position, and if not, say which item you would replace and with what.
