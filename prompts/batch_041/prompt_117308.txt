I'm a third-year PhD student in moral philosophy, and for the past few weeks I've been trying to turn a disagreement from our ethics reading group into a workable paper. The disagreement began with the familiar debate about whether the numbers count, often traced to Taurek, in which it is argued that the mere fact that there are five people rather than one does not by itself give us a moral reason to save the five. What has been bothering me is that most of the discussion seems to assume a single episode in which all the affected parties are present, identified, and equally saveable. I keep running into cases where that assumption fails: a fixed rescue budget must be spent either on saving one identified person who is trapped now or on a programme that will save five people who are not yet in danger and whose identities are unknown; a policy can save five lives but only by imposing a small, uncompensated risk of death on one person who is not among the five; or you can save five now, but doing so will leave you unable to save one different person later, whose claim is equally urgent. I am also interested in cases where the five can be saved only by a lottery that gives each of six people an equal chance of being saved, while the one can be saved for certain, and in the difference between aggregating lives and aggregating chances.

Suppose I have about a year and one chapter to work on this. Which open questions in this area should I prioritise, and why? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: rank the questions, say which are upstream of which, how tractable each is with the tools of analytic moral philosophy, and what would move a question up or down the ranking. For each, explain what makes it worth a year of my life, whether because it would resolve a live disagreement, because other questions depend on it, or because it would change how we think about actual triage and rescue policy. I am especially interested in whether numbers can serve as a tie-breaker when individual claims are equally strong, whether the ex ante and ex post perspectives can be reconciled without collapsing into a version of aggregation that Taurek's challenge was meant to resist, and whether the separateness of persons is best understood as a constraint on aggregation or as a constraint on the kinds of claims that can be weighed at all. Please also tell me which much-discussed questions, such as fairness and lotteries, the moral relevance of causal proximity, and the distinction between saving and letting die, are overrated or mis-framed for someone with my constraints, and which neglected questions deserve more attention.

Please write your answer in British English. I don't need citations, a reading list, or a bibliography; I need the reasoning and the ordering. Where you rely on a distinction or a case that is not already well known, please explain it in enough detail that I can see how it does the work, rather than simply naming it. I would also like you to flag any place where the ranking depends on a controversial metaethical or normative assumption, and to say how the ranking would change if that assumption were rejected.
