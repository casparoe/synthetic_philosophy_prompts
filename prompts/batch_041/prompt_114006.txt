I have been reading arguments about Nozick's tracking theory for years, in blog posts and forum threads and the occasional paper, and the way the scorecard on it gets kept has always struck me as skewed. The defect everyone leads with is the failure of closure: I can track the proposition that I have hands, the story goes, without tracking the proposition that I am not a handless brain in a vat, even though the first entails the second, so the theory either denies that knowledge is closed under known entailment or else lands me in the conjunction of knowing I have hands while not knowing I am not a handless brain in a vat. That conjunction is treated as the fatal cost of the view, the thing that shows the whole approach is a non-starter. I want you to argue that the ranking is backwards: that the closure failure is the more tolerable of the theory's problems, and that a different defect — one that gets far less attention — is the one that should sink the analysis.

The defect I have in mind concerns what the tracking conditions do with beliefs whose truth is not a contingent, empirical matter: necessary truths, arithmetical and logical truths, and whatever else we are said to know a priori. The sensitivity condition is a counterfactual — roughly, that if the proposition were not true, the subject would not believe it. But there is no possible situation in which a necessary truth fails, so for any such proposition the condition is satisfied vacuously by anyone who happens to believe it: the careful mathematician and the person who guesses a theorem correctly and believes it on no good grounds come out the same. I want you to argue that this vacuity is not a minor leak to be patched but a structural failure of the counterfactual approach, and that it is worse than the closure failure for a reason you should develop and defend rather than simply assert.

Please make the case in full. Anticipate the obvious replies and answer them: that necessary truths are known by a separate, a priori route and so are simply none of the tracking theory's business; that the second, adherence-style condition — that if the proposition were true the subject would believe it — does the work the sensitivity condition cannot; and that swapping sensitivity for a safety-style condition, on which a belief is safe when it could not easily have been false, repairs the problem. On this last point I want you to be especially careful: work out whether safety is any less vacuous than sensitivity for necessary truths, and say what your answer shows about the whole family of modal analyses of knowledge rather than just about Nozick's particular version of it. Close by saying what a defender of the tracking theory would have to give up in order to accommodate mathematical and a priori knowledge, and whether that concession leaves anything distinctively tracking-theoretic still standing. I am not after a survey or a reading list; I want the argument itself.
