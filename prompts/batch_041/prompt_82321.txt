I'm in a formal epistemology reading group and one of the regulars — call her R — is writing a dissertation that's basically one long defense of Dutch book arguments. Her line is that almost every epistemic norm worth having can be justified by showing that violating it leaves you open to a sure loss, and she's spent two years fighting off the people (including her own advisor, who thinks the project is a dead end) who say Dutch books are a parlor trick that only ever tells you something about betting odds and nothing about rationality. Last week over coffee she ran through her verdicts on the standard cases and I wrote them down, because I wanted to see whether she's actually running one principle or just improvising case by case. Her list: the synchronic book against credences that break the probability axioms — negative values, values above one, failures of finite additivity — works, and anyone who says otherwise is confused. The book against countable additivity does not work; her reason is that however you string the bets together there's no stage at which you're actually down anything, the loss only shows up at the limit, and nobody pays out at the limit. The diachronic book for conditionalization works — the one where you bet her now, at her own current odds, on what she'll think tomorrow, and then hand tomorrow's self the bet that self will cheerfully take, leaving her down whatever happens. The book for the reflection principle works too, same style of reasoning. And the book against someone who gives probability zero to a proposition she'd admit is possible — the regularity case — does not work: she says you simply can't assemble a finite package of bets that loses her money in every state of the world. She won't even discuss the money pump against intransitive preferences, on the grounds that it's about desire rather than credence and it's a distraction from what she's doing.

Now here's the case she hasn't ruled on, which is what I actually need help with. It's the imprecise credence one. The agent doesn't have a single credence in P, she has a range — say 0.3 to 0.7 — and she'll take either side of a bet at any odds inside that range. The argument, which R knows about and hasn't made up her mind on, is that you can still Dutch-book her: you assemble a finite package of bets, each of which some credence function inside her range would rate as fair or better, arranged so that the package as a whole loses her money in every possible state of the world.

So what's she going to say, and — much more useful to me — what rule is she actually running? Give me the principle that best fits all five of her verdicts, at least one other principle that fits them just as well, what each of those two predicts about the imprecise credence case, which one you'd bet she's really holding and why, and the single further case I should put to her that would actually seperate them. And if her five verdicts genuinely don't settle the sixth, say that straight, and tell me what she'd be signing up for either way.
