I edit a small press that does religious-studies textbooks and study editions, and I have a problem I need help with. Every manuscript we take in is full of sentences like "Jesus said X", "Jesus did Y", "the historical Jesus was a Z" — and I have to decide, claim by claim, what our footnote says. Right now my two freelance copy-editors (neither has a PhD, both have a decent undergrad background) basically vibe it out: if it sounds like something they've heard a lot they write "widely regarded as authentic," and otherwise they write nothing. An academic reviewer tore us apart over this last month, fairly.

So: take the role of a biblical scholar with one foot in the philosophy of history, think this through carefully, and build me a procedure — a checklist to be run in order — for deciding what to say about a single claim of the form "Jesus did/said/was ___". Start by brainstorming what such a procedure has to be able to handle, then write the procedure itself, then do the worked cases.

The procedure should:

- fix the target. I care about three different things and I suspect my copy-editors keep sliding between them: whether the claim reports something the man actually said or did (authenticity), whether it belongs in the best reconstruction of what he was up to (historicity), and what it tells us about the community that preserved it. Say how a verdict on one is supposed to constrain the others, and where it must not.

- say which standard criteria to apply, in what order and with what weight — embarrassment, dissimilarity/discontinuity, multiple attestation, coherence, fit with the Jewish Palestinian setting, the fact of the execution — and for each: what counts as it firing, what doesn't (I've watched them count four documents as four witnesses when two of them are the same story retold), and the particular way it tends to go wrong or beg the question. I want a circularity check built in: for every criterion that fires, the practitioner has to write down whether it already assumed the conclusion it's supposed to support.

- be honest about the thing I keep reading, that these criteria are widely thought to be broken in some deep way. Don't just wave at that — say which uses survive and which don't, and build the procedure so it can't fake precision it doesn't have.

- output a verdict from a small fixed set of labels, a confidence marker, and a one-line rationale that can go into a footnote without embarrassing us.

- run in twenty minutes, by someone with no Greek or Hebrew and no library.

- say where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a human — me, or the outside consultant we can afford maybe twice a year — and which of those handoffs are factual disputes versus just our own editorial/religious position.

Then run it end to end on three claims: (1) Jesus was baptized by John; (2) Jesus taught something along the lines of loving your enemies; (3) the tomb was found empty. At least one verdict should be one I won't like — if the procedure says the ethical teachings come out weaker than something I'd rather not print, say so and show me the steps.

Last thing, a calibration test: give my copy-editors a control case, a claim about a figure from a totally different tradition where we have independent reason to think the attribution is late, and tell them what the procedure should return on it, as a check that this isn't just a machine for saying yes.

Dont give me citations or a reading list — describe positions in plain English, and if a criterion's name is contested just describe what it does. Be upfront at the end about what this procedure can't do.
