MacCallum's central claim is that freedom is always a triadic relation: an agent is free from some constraint to do or become something. Once you see this, the gulf between negative and positive liberty largely disappears, because both camps are answering the same question and just filling in the variables differently. Berlin's negative theorist cares about the first two slots, who the agent is and what counts as an obstacle, while the positive theorist shifts attention to the third, asking what the agent is really aiming at. So MacCallum's conclusion is that the great debate is basically verbal: there is one concept of freedom and the two sides are using different words for it.

For Berlin, negative liberty is non-interference: you're free to the extent that other people don't stop you doing what you want. Positive liberty is self-mastery: you're free to the extent that you're the author of your own life rather than the plaything of your passions or of other people's wills. Textbooks often put this as freedom from versus freedom to, which makes positive liberty look like negative liberty plus the resources and capabilities you need to actually use your options. On that reading Rousseau and Hegel are best understood as just adding means to liberty, noticing that a door you can't reach isn't much of a door.

Now the slave. He isn't interfered with, since the master is benevolent and the slave has no wish to escape. So he's free in the negative sense, and Berlin himself leans on this sort of case when he wants to show how hollow non-interference can be. He's unfree in the positive sense, since he isn't self-determining. What Pettit and Skinner add is that he's unfree in a third way as well: he's dominated, because the master has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily, and it's the standing capacity rather than its exercise that makes him unfree. That's why republicans say liberty isn't the same as non-interference, and it's also why they can accept that law interferes with us without thereby dominating us, since what matters is whether the interference is arbitrary or answerable to those subject to it. So the slave case shows that non-interference is neither necessary nor sufficient for freedom.

For (c), I think MacCallum's scheme can absorb non-domination, but only by putting the master's power in the constraint slot: the slave is unfree from his master's arbitrary power to live as he chooses. That seems right, and it shows that the republican conception is really just a more careful version of negative liberty, since what the republican objects to in the end is a certain kind of obstacle in the slave's path. The positive theorist, by contrast, is making a different sort of claim altogether, because self-mastery isn't about obstacles at all, it's about who's doing the choosing.
