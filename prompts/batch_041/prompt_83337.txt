Ok so I've been reading about how regulators in the US put a dollar figure on saving lives, and it's been bugging me. When the EPA or the Department of Transportation runs a cost-benefit analysis on some new rule, they use a number for the value of a statistical life -- a few million dollars, sometimes close to ten -- and that number mostly comes out of studies of how much extra pay workers demand to take riskier jobs, or surveys where people say what they'd pay to knock a hair off their chance of dying. Then they multiply it by the deaths the rule is expected to prevent and stack that up against what the rule costs.

What gets me is what the whole practice has to assume. It seems to assume that a life has a price, that my life and your life are worth the same amount, and that the way to find out what something is worth to a person is to look at what they'd trade for it. But the same government won't let me sell my own kidney, and juries in wrongful-death suits come up with numbers that have nothing to do with the ten million, and nobody ever asks the workers who took those risky jobs whether they'd actually want the money spent on the rule that protects them. Also a friend told me there's a real difference between what people choose and what they end up experiencing -- like the guy taking the risky job is deciding based on what he imagines the risk will be like, and the value of the thing itself could come apart from what his choice reveals, especially if he's bad at judging small probabilities or if he just adapts to the danger over time.

So here's what I want. What philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of utility and value that it only makes sense under. Is it preference satisfaction, is it hedonic, does it need utility to be cardinal and comparable across people, does it have to assume people know their own interests? Then tell me which rival theories it quietly rules out, and which ones it's merely compatible with -- my suspicion is that a lot of it is underdetermined and several theories would license the same numbers, but I want to know where the practice is actually doing philosophical work and where it isn't. And tell me whether the parts hang together: the value of a statistical life in a rulemaking docket versus the refusal to let people trade in their own organs versus whatever juries do in tort. Last thing: if you swapped out the underlying view for the other one my friend mentioned, where what counts is experienced well-being rather than what people would choose, what would concretely have to change about how regulaters do this? I don't want citations or a reading list, just the argument.
