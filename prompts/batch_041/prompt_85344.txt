Hey, I'm in this reading group on epistemic logic and we keep going in circles about logical omniscience — you know, the fact that in the standard possible-worlds setup an agent believes all the validities, believes every logical consequence of what they believe, can't tell logically equivalent propositions apart, and if they believe a contradiction they believe absolutely everything. Our group has running joke that every proposed fix is either (a) technically clean but quietly solves a different problem, or (b) actually grapples with the phenomenon but gives up all the nice properties. So I want to put together a tier list and I'd like your help. Could you sort the main families of responses into tiers (S through D is fine, or just top/middle/bottom) by how much they genuinely *dissolve* the problem rather than just relabel it — that is, how much they actually capture the epistemic situation of a bounded reasoner, instead of just relocating the omniscience somewhere else in the formalism. For each one, a sentence or two on why it lands where it does and what it's still getting wrong.

The families I want on the list: impossible-worlds semantics (worlds where classical logic breaks down); awareness-style models where the agent is only aware of some formulas and you distinguish explicit from implicit belief; the older syntactic "beliefs are just a set of sentences sitting in a database" approach; resource-bounded and procedural models that build the agent's reasoning steps or proof search into the semantics; relevance and paraconsistent logics that block explosion and irrelevant entailments; and the deflationary option where you just say omniscience is a harmless idealization, like frictionless planes in physics. Rank those against each other, not just in the abstract.

Does that earn a spot above the syntactic approaches, or is it basically a syntactic approach in a trench coat? Be honest with me, I can take it.
