I teach a 200-level rational choice seminar — 15 students, 75 minutes, a mix of econ and philosophy people who've all seen expected utility written on a board but never had it actually pushed on them. I want to spend a whole session on ambiguity: the difference between a bet where you know the odds and one where you don't, and why being willing to pay less for the second one definately feels like it should be fine but maybe isn't. I don't want to stand up and lecture it. I want them to feel the pull and then find themselves stuck inside it.

So: design me a runnable in-class game. Constraints, roughly:

- No real money. Points, candy, bragging rights, whatever, as long as the payoffs are legible and people actually care a little.
- The asymmetry has to show up in what they do, not in what I say afterward. Ideally there's a moment where each of them has committed to some choices and then looks at their own answers and sees that the two halves can't both be right.
- Don't say the word ambiguity, or the name of the paradox, until the debrief. Let them build the thing first.
- If you can manage a second beat, I'd love one: after they've displayed the preference, something that makes clear it's the unknown-ness they're avoiding and not any actual risk — since the unknown urn is just sitting there with some definite composition, whatever it is.

Give me: materials and setup, the rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal distinction (risk vs. uncertainty, the sure-thing style reasoning the pattern seems to violate, and the kind of theory you'd need to rationalize the aversion). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about what the activity can't do — I suspect the game can get them to feel the aversion and see their own inconsistency, but it can't by itself show that the inconsistent pattern is irrational; that argument is on me, and I want to know exactly where the activity hands off to me.

Keep the whole thing under 1100 words.
