I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and I get pulled in maybe two or three times a month for the same knotty situation: someone on the care team wants to tell a patient or a family member something that isn't true, usually with decent intentions, and nobody's sure weather it's allowed. I've been reading Augustine lately -- I'm not religious, I just got curious after a chaplain mentioned him -- and from what I can tell his position is unusually strict: he thinks every lie is a sin, full stop, no exceptions, not even to save a life or spare someone terrible pain, because the wrong is in the act itself and in what it does to the person who does it. My understanding is he does allow you to stay silent, or decline to answer, or leave something out, but never to assert something you beleive false. I'd like you to turn that view into a decision procedure my team can actually run in the fifteen minutes before a family meeting.

So: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a flat no; and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a judgment about values rather than a fact the procedure can settle. Then run it on three cases. (1) A woman in early-stage dementia asks several times a day where her husband is; he died two years ago and each time she's told she relives the grief from scratch; her daughter is begging us to just say he's on his way and will be here soon. (2) A man with pancreatic cancer asks his oncologist point blank whether the new regimen will cure him; the oncologist thinks it won't, might buy a few months, and the family wants him told something encouraging. (3) A nurse wants to give a saline injection as a placebo for pain, telling the patient it's a strong painkiller -- technically she never utters a false sentence, but the patient will draw a false conclusion. For each, say what the procedure spits out and why.

I also want you to be honest about the edges. Where does his view actually leave room to maneuver -- is the silence-or-concealment option doing real work here, or is it a loophole he'd close the moment he saw these cases? And where does the procedure just run out: situations that hinge on whether something counts as asserting versus merely implying, or on whether withholding a truth is itself a kind of deception. Flag the case where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like -- I suspect there's one hiding in those three -- and say plainly what the whole exercise can't deliver, so I don't oversell it to the committee.
