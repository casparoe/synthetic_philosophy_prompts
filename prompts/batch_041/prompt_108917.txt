I sit on the evaluation committee of a mid-sized nonprofit that funds maternal-health clinics, and we are redesigning how we score clinic directors for annual bonuses. Our current scorecard has four domains: patient follow-up rates, average wait times, staff retention, and patient-reported trust. We have already seen directors game the easy ones—shortening visits, cherry-picking which patients get follow-up calls, and timing the waiting-room clock—so the committee is under pressure to change the system. A colleague who has been reading about Goodhart's law proposes what she calls a blind scorecard: we would still compute all four domains, but we would keep secret which domain is weighted for bonuses, rotate the secret weighting every quarter, and publish a decoy weighting for everyone to see. Her reasoning is that Goodhart's law proves that any measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure, so the only way to keep a measure honest is to prevent people from knowing it is the target. I am sympathetic, but something feels wrong. If the directors do not know the real target, how can they be gaming it? And if they cannot game it, have we not solved the problem? I would like to know whether Goodhart's law actually supports this design, or whether my colleague and I are both misreading what the law says. I also recall a taxonomy that distinguishes regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial forms of Goodharting, and I wonder whether the blind scorecard addresses all four or only one.

More broadly, I am trying to understand whether Goodhart's law is a proven theorem about all metrics or a family of mechanisms that depends on context. If it is the latter, what does it predict about a system where the true target is hidden but the fact of being scored is public? Would the directors' uncertainty push them toward gaming whatever remains visible, toward defensive documentation, or toward the decoy? Is there a principled difference between using metrics for internal learning and using them for high-stakes rewards? And how does this relate to what I have seen called specification gaming in artificial intelligence, where a system satisfies the letter of an objective while violating its spirit—is that the same phenomenon, a special case, or a different problem altogether? Please answer as if advising our committee: tell me where my colleague's inference is sound, where it rests on a false presupposition, and what a better design would look like. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Please write in American English.
