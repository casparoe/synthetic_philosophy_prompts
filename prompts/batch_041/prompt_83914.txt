I've been reading Braudel for a seminar on historical method and I think I've talked myself into a stronger version of the longue durée position than I suspect Braudel himself would actually sign off on. Roughly: the real causal action in history lives in the slow stuff -- geography, climate, disease regimes, the deep structures of how people feed themselves and move goods around -- and political events and individual choices are mostly surface. The event is epiphenomenal; the great man is a weather vane. I should be honest that I keep sliding between two seperate claims: (a) events are caused by structures and can't be understood apart from them, and (b) events are causally idle, so that the structural story is the whole story. I hold (a) pretty firmly and I'm definately tempted by (b). I'd like you to treat those as different positions and tell me what counts against each, since I think most of the interesting objections land on (b) and I want to know if any of them reach (a).

What I'm after is a map of the position's exposure. What observations, arguments, or bits of historiography would count against it -- and ranked, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. A friend of mine who does statistics thinks the whole framework is unfalsifiable and has a name for the mechanism: the hindsight ratchet. Anything that happens can be redescribed after the fact as the expression of some structure you'd never have specified in advance, and since the redescription is always available, the thesis never risks anything. She says the real test would be whether the framework ever generates a short-term prediction that a naive event-level historian gets wrong. I'm not sure that's the right test, but I don't have a better one, so tell me what a fair test would look like and whether the view could pass it. I'd also like to know what the strongest empirical support actually is -- the climate-history stuff where harvest failures and cold spells get tied to revolts and state breakdowns seems like the best case, and I want to know how much of it is real and how much is just re-description -- and whether that evidence, if it holds up, supports (a) or (b). And there's a modern wrinkle I can't resolve: is something like the printing press, or the internet, an event or a structure? If the answer is "it's a structure that only looks like an event in retrospect," I worry the view is doing exactly what my friend says it does.

Finally, I want the defenders' side of this, not just the critics'. Which of these objections would a committed Braudelian concede as genuine costs, and which would they wave off as a misunderstanding of what the framework is for? Does the view predict anything its rivals don't -- something that should be true of history if structures dominate and false if events and individuals matter -- and if so what? And if it turns out to be well insulated against everything I've listed, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or is there a legitimate sense in which a framework for interpretation isn't supposed to be falsifiable in the way a hypothesis is? I'd genuinely like a straight answer on that last one rather than a both-sides shrug.
