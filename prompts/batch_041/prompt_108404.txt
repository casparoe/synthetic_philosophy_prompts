Our philosophy club spent this term on Daniel Dennett's four kinds of minds, and I've been asked to close our last meeting with a poem. The framework, as I understand it, is a ladder of increasingly cheap ways of coping with the world. A Darwinian creature's competence is installed by natural selection rather than learned: it arrives already knowing what to do, and the only way it can be corrected is by dying. A Skinnerian creature can improve, but only by acting and then living with the consequences of what it did. A Popperian creature can run the trial and error inside its own head, so its mistakes cost it nothing — it can try a hypothesis and throw it away without paying in flesh. A Gregorian creature thinks with tools, words, and other people's minds, so that a great deal of what it knows is not inside it at all but in its environment; the fourth label comes from the psychologist Richard Gregory, who argued that even ordinary perception is a matter of testing hypotheses against the world. Dennett's point, as our club read it, is that each rung keeps the rung below it: the Popperian still has reflexes and habits, and the Gregorian still runs on a Popperian brain.

What I would like is a poem of roughly sixteen to twenty-four lines in four sections, one per creature, in that order, for me to read aloud at the final meeting. The form should do the work the creatures do. I would rather the poem never use the words Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, or Gregorian: a club member should be able to tell which section is which from the shape of the lines alone, and someone who has never heard any of this should still find it a good poem rather than a lecture. It also shouldn't be four separate miniatures. Each section should be audibly built on the machinery of the ones before it, the way the creatures are.

Some suggestions for how the sections might behave, though you may solve it another way as long as the underlying logic holds. The first section could be written as though it had been composed before the creature existed and nothing the creature does can revise it: locked lines, repetition that cannot develop, no line that reaches past the one before it. The second could let something from the first section come back changed, and make clear that the change is a response to what happened in between — improvement earned by acting, not chosen. The third could raise a claim and take it back inside the same line, or set up a rhyme and refuse it, so that the error is made and discarded without cost; I want the reader to feel that the mistake was free. The fourth should be assembled largely out of phrases lifted from the first three sections and recombined, and it should contain at least one line that could not have come from anywhere earlier in the poem, because it came from outside the poem — a tool, a book, somebody else's voice — and it should end by turning outward, toward whoever is listening. If it can be done without straining anything, I'd like the whole thing to be recognizably the kind of object a Gregorian creature would use: something borrowed from other minds and handed on to others.

A title is welcome, as long as it doesn't give the scheme away. I don't need any references, dates, or attributions in the poem itself — I'd rather it stand on its own — and please don't strain for a moral at the end. If a reader who knows the framework finds the ladder right, and a reader who doesn't finds it a good poem, that's what I'm after.
