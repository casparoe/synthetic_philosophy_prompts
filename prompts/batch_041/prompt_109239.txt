I am preparing a seminar that pairs the neuroscience of free will with dystopian fiction, and I would like a reading of Anthony Burgess's novel A Clockwork Orange and Stanley Kubrick's film adaptation through the lens of Libet's experiments and the debates they generated. My students will have encountered the basic Libet result—the readiness potential precedes the reported conscious intention to move by a few hundred milliseconds—and also the now-influential reinterpretation according to which that potential is better understood as the stochastic accumulation of neural noise crossing a threshold rather than as a decision signal that causes the movement. They will also have read about Libet's own attempt to rescue a role for consciousness in the veto, the capacity to cancel an action once it is underway. I want a reading that takes both the fiction and the science seriously, not one that uses the novel as a mere illustration of a predetermined conclusion. I have been arguing with a colleague who teaches the film as a straightforward parable about behaviorism, and I think that reading misses what the neuroscience does and does not show.

I want you to distinguish three senses of freedom that the novel and film may run together: freedom as the ability to do otherwise, freedom as acting from one's own desires without external constraint, and freedom as self-authorship, where one endorses or identifies with the desires that move one. Which of these does the Ludovico technique actually remove from Alex? Does it target the readiness potential, the conscious intention, or the veto? And if the readiness potential is not itself the decision, does the state's intervention even do what it claims to do? I am especially interested in the scene where the prison chaplain objects that Alex has been robbed of free will, and in the way the film turns Beethoven's music into an instrument of aversion. In the novel's final chapter, which some widely circulated editions omit, Alex begins to find violence boring and imagines a domestic future; does that change the philosophical verdict, or does it only change the mood? Kubrick's ending, in which Alex is restored to his old appetites, is often read as a dark joke about the state's failure; I want to know whether it is better read as a claim about the resilience of the will or as a demonstration that the will was never the thing at issue. I would also like you to consider whether the Ludovico technique is better modeled as an attack on the veto or as the implantation of a new and overpowering desire, and whether that distinction matters for how we judge Alex's later violence.

Finally, tell me what the novel and film dramatize that the Libet debate tends to miss—about the difference between a cause and a reason, about the role of aesthetic experience in agency, or about the social conditions under which a capacity for self-authorship can develop at all. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument. And please say what would change your mind: which consideration or counterargument would lead you to revise your reading, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
