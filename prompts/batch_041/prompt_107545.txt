Most discussions of the norm of assertion take for granted that a single individual both holds the relevant epistemic state and performs the speech act: the question is framed as what that one speaker must know, believe, or have warrant for, in order for the assertion to be licensed. I would like a careful explanation of a conceptual issue that this individualistic framing tends to hide.

Take what might be called delegated or proxy assertion. A spokesperson, press officer, or ministerial representative publicly asserts a proposition on behalf of an institution. The institution's information is distributed across many people, files, and procedures, so that the institution may count as knowing the proposition in a recognizable epistemic sense, while the person at the podium neither knows nor believes it — she has been instructed to say it and has reason to trust the internal process that produced it. The reverse can also occur: the individual may have strong evidence for the proposition while the institution's official line is the opposite.

Explain what is genuinely at stake in deciding whether the familiar candidate norms — knowledge, truth, belief, justification — attach to the individual speaker or to the asserting entity, and why the choice is not merely a relabeling. In doing so, address three things. First, whether a knowledge norm even has a determinate application once the knowing subject and the asserting subject come apart, and what would fix the relevant subject. Second, what proxy assertion reveals about whether such norms evaluate the act or the agent — the utterance or the utterer — and whether that distinction is stable. Third, how the phenomenon bears on the common assumption that assertion is governed by one unified norm, rather than by several norms that may be satisfied by different bearers. Reason from the structure of the cases rather than from any particular author's position, and do not spend the answer cataloguing who has defended which view.
