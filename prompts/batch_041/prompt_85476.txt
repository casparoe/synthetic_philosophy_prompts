I've somehow been volun-told into running the next session of our reading group, and the topic that won is the paradox of tolerance — the Popper note in The Open Society where he says a tolerant order keeps the right to suppress the intolerant, which everyone quotes and nobody reads closely. Watching us argue about it, I've realised we're all answering different questions and then getting annoyed that the other guy's answer doesn't fit: some people are asking what the word 'tolerance' even covers, some are asking who's allowed to draw the line, some are just asking whether the nazis will win. So instead of a discussion I want a diagnostic instrument: a 'which view about the limits of tolerance do you actually hold?' quiz. You're a political philosopher who's spent too long on toleration, pluralism and the limits of liberal order — before you write anything, brainstorm the axes the disagreement actually runs along, and build the quiz out of those axes rather than out of a list of famous names, because if the questions are recognisable as the standard positions people will just answer the label they like.

The distinctions I actually care about, roughly in order: (a) whether toleration requires disapproval at all — if you're indifferent to the thing, or you fully endorse it, you aren't tolerating it, so 'just be less judgemental' can't be an answer to anything here, and I want cases that force people to notice this; (b) whether the limits of tolerance are internal to the norm itself (there's a point where extending tolerance stops being tolerance and becomes something else) or external and merely prudential (tolerance has no built-in limits, we just can't afford to lose); (c) who adjudicates, and the thing that actually makes the paradox bite — that the norm has to be self-applying, so any exception you carve out is also available to the people you'd rather it didn't apply to; (d) whether we're talking about the state's coercive power or about any norm-governed practice whatsoever, since a Discord server, a seminar and a knitting circle aren't obviously the same problem; and (e) the one everybody fumbles, including me — whether the claim is that a tolerant order is *permitted* to shut the intolerant down, *required* to, or *forbidden* to. A permission and an obligation are completely different views and I want them as seperate leaves, not squashed into one 'Popperian' bucket. Somewhere in there I also want the conceptual-versus-empirical trigger: is the threshold crossed when the position is incoherent to tolerate, or only when they're genuinely about to win? I'd keep those apart too.

Ten to twelve questions, each one a small concrete case with two or three options, phrased so a person can answer it without knowing where it leads — no question may just ask 'do you believe in free speech?', and none may name a doctrine. Keep the cases specific and a bit petty rather than grand: a knitting group bleeding members because one woman keeps insisting knitting is for women; a maths seminar where a guy keeps presenting his argument that group differences in test scores are largely genetic and the seminar is losing its women; a city that has to decide whether to let a party campaign on abolishing the city's own elections; a Discord mod wondering whether banning the loudest guy is itself an exercise of the norm rather than an exception to it; a hiring committee, a newspaper, a family dinner. Include at least one case about the tolerant person's own state of mind, since that's the only place the disapproval point really shows up. Then give me a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions — real names where they fit, 'unnamed' where nobody defends it — and make sure it can reach at least these: constitutional self-defence / militant democracy, the deflationary 'there was never a paradox, tolerance was always bounded, the only question is where you draw the line' view, the modus vivendi view on which the limits are just power and negotiation, the neutralist view that a state can't disapprove of anything and so official toleration either collapses into indifference or is incoherent, the open-society view where the limits track whatever destroys the practice of giving and taking reasons, the asymmetric view where tolerance is a demand on whoever holds power so the same act counts differently depending on who does it, and the view that the paradox applies to the state but not to voluntary associations (or the reverse, I've heard both defended and I can't tell if they're the same person). Flag any two of those your instrument genuinely can't seperate instead of pretending it can, and tell me which answer-combinations nobody occupies — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Write the key in prose I can read out loud, not as a table. Think it through properly before you commit to the axes, this is definately going to get argued with.
