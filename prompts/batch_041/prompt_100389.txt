I'm a policy grad student and a luck egalitarian, and I'm trying to defend a workfare program that requires claimants to log five job applications a week and show up to a weekly meeting; miss twice and you loose benefits for three months. I built this argument for a seminar and now it looks like it proves too much. (1) The state may sanction a claimant for missing the requirement only if she is blameworthy for missing it. (2) She is blameworthy only if she could have met it. (3) A claimant who misses because of depression, no childcare, or a local job market with no openings could not have met it. (4) So the state may not sanction her. (5) But the same reasoning applies to any failure caused by factors outside her control. (6) Upbringing, genes, and social conditions are always outside anyone's control, so no one is ever blameworthy for anything. (7) So no one may ever be sanctioned for anything. (8) But a state that can't sanction anyone can't enforce any law, which is absurd. (9) So something in this chain has to go. I'd like to keep (1) and (3). Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'm especially interested in whether the fix is a compatibilist reading of "could have" or a distinction between a sanction and a punishment -- and whether either fix leaves workfare looking like a price rather than a penalty.
