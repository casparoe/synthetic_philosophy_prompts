I am a novelist at work on a book about a predictive bureaucracy, and I have been reading Popper's The Poverty of Historicism alongside Asimov's Foundation novels. I would like a critical reading of the Foundation series through Popper's critique of historicism -- not a plot summary, and not a verdict on whether psychohistory could ever be real, but an argument about what the novels dramatize, complicate, and get wrong about the possibility of laws of history.

Popper's case against historicism turns on a set of distinctions the novels seem to stage without naming. There is the difference between a trend and a law: a trend is a statistical regularity that holds for a time and place, while a law is universal and supports counterfactuals. There is the Oedipus effect, in which a prediction can become a cause of the predicted event, so that the growth of knowledge -- including knowledge of the prediction -- makes the future course of history unpredictable. There is the charge that historicism slides into a conspiracy theory of society, explaining events by hidden designs rather than by the unintended consequences of action. And there is Popper's own alternative: not prophecy but situational logic, the reconstruction of an agent's situation so that the action becomes intelligible without appeal to historical laws.

Read the Foundation novels against that grid. Where does psychohistory look like exactly the target of Popper's argument, and where does it look like a reply to it? Hari Seldon's plan is a statistical prediction that works only because it is secret and because individuals are unpredictable; does that secrecy make the plan a dramatization of the Oedipus effect or an evasion of it? The Mule is usually taken as the refutation of psychohistory, but is he a refutation of the possibility of historical laws or only a demonstration of their scope conditions -- masses yes, great individuals no? The Second Foundation's hidden mentalics may be the novels' most Popperian moment, since it turns historical explanation into the very conspiracy theory Popper warns against. And the Foundation's recurring crises are often solved not by prediction but by situational logic: the traders and mayors infer the necessary move from the constraints of their position. Does that vindicate Popper's alternative or merely relocate the problem?

I am less interested in whether Asimov was a historicist than in what the books reveal about the literary form of historical prediction. What does the serial, generational structure of the novels do to the idea of a law of history? What does it mean that the plan is a text -- a set of recorded instructions -- and that the characters live inside a prediction they cannot fully read? Which scenes should I study as a novelist who wants to stage the collision between individual agency and statistical necessity without turning either into a lecture? And finally, where does the work resist the Popperian reading, so that the honest critic has to say that the novels are doing something the philosophy cannot? End by saying what Foundation adds to the debate over laws of history that argument alone does not.
