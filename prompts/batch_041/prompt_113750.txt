I'm a backend software engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever (one ethics elective in college, and I mostly remember the vending machine in the hallway), but I've gotten weirdly into decision theory on my commute. There's this thing I keep running into called risk-weighted expected utility, usually abbreviated REU, and I want a story that makes it actually click for someone like me. You're going to be my expert here, so put on both hats: professional philosopher of decision theory, and short story writer.

First, here's the idea as I understand it, so we're on the same page. Please correct me if I've got it wrong, and then use the corrected version.

In ordinary expected utility theory you take each possible outcome of an action, multiply its utility by its probability, add it all up, and pick the highest total. Your attitude toward risk ends up smuggled inside the shape of your utility curve, so "I'm a cautious person" and "I just care less about money in general" can look identical on paper. REU pulls those apart. You sort the outcomes of an action from worst to best, and the weight an outcome gets isnt its own probability but a transformed version of the chance that you end up at least that well off. The transformation is a "risk function" you apply to the cumulative probabilities, and it's the same function across every decision you face. So a risk-averse person isn't a person with a bent utility curve, they're a person who systematically gives extra weight to the bad tail and discounts the good tail regardless of stakes. Weird upshot: someone can be extremely cautious about anything with a catastrophic downside and still happily take stupid long-shot gambles where the worst case is trivial, and that's one coherent attitude rather than two contradicting ones.
