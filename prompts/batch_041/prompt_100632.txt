I have been arguing about dialetheism in a reading group, and I have put together an argument for it that I distrust in proportion to how smoothly it runs. I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs, because I cannot tell whether the argument has a genuine flaw or whether it is valid and I am merely reluctant to pay the price. For what it is worth, I came to this through the online rationalist scene, so my instinct is that a contradiction is a bug rather than a feature, but I am trying not to let that instinct do the work.

Let L be a sentence that says of itself that it is not true.

(1) By the disquotational schema for truth, L is true if and only if it is not true.
(2) By excluded middle, either L is true or L is not true.
(3) Suppose L is true. Then by (1), L is not true. So L is true and not true.
(4) Suppose L is not true. Then by (1), L is true. So L is true and not true.
(5) So in either case L is true and not true.
(6) A sentence that is not true is false. So L is both true and false.
(7) Hence at least one contradiction is true.
(8) So the law of non-contradiction is not a law, and dialetheism is correct.

What makes me suspicious is that the argument proves too much, in two ways. First, the reasoning is thoroughly classical — disjunction elimination at (5), excluded middle at (2), the schema at (1) applied inside both branches — and yet the conclusion is that classical logic's central constraint is false. If true contradictions are acceptable, on what grounds do I keep trusting the very inferences that delivered them? Second, if I accept (7) then I accept that L is true and also that L is not true; and if a contradiction licenses any conclusion whatever, then dialetheism's own negation follows as well, and the position is both true and false in a way that gives me no reason to hold it rather than its denial. The only way to stop that seems to be to give up the inference from a contradiction to everything — but then I have quietly swapped the logic in the middle of the argument.

Someone in the group says the real mistake is to read (8) as a claim about the world when (7) is at most a claim about our concept of truth, and that the slide from the one to the other is where it breaks; I cannot tell whether that is the flaw or a dodge.

I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) if I can, and I am rather attached to (6). So: which step do I reject, and what do I owe once I do? I want the diagnosis to be specific — if there is an equivocation or a smuggled premise, name it and say exactly where it bites — and I want the price list: for each step one might abandon, what must one accept instead, and which of the available escapes (restricting the truth schema, denying that not-true means false, going paraconsistent, or something else) is cheapest and which is most honest?
