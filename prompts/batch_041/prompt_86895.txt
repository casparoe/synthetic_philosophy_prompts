I am taking a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and our unit on epistemic logic has left me with a question I would like to work out in an essay. In the standard framework an agent's knowledge is represented by a set of possible worlds, and knowledge comes out closed under entailment: if the agent knows P, and P entails Q, then the agent knows Q. The consequence our seminar spent two sessions on is that any agent who knows the axioms of propositional logic thereby knows every theorem of it, however long or unobvious the proof, and indeed knows every logical truth, since what the agent knows is fixed by the worlds compatible with what the agent knows. The framework therefore appears to make agents logically omniscient. Three responses were canvassed, and I could not get the seminar to choose among them. The first holds that this is a harmless idealisation, in the way that a frictionless plane is a harmless idealisation: the semantics is a theory of the structure of knowledge, not of the psychology of reasoning, and no one should have expected otherwise. The second holds that the semantics must be revised, either by adding a component that records what the agent is aware of, or by admitting impossible worlds, or by treating knowledge as a relation to sentences or proofs rather than to propositions. The third holds that the difficulty shows knowledge is not a relation to propositions at all but something closer to a skill or a standing condition of an agent, so that principles of closure do not so much as get a grip.

What I would like the essay to do is adjudicate among these. To give it a focus, here is a proposal from section, together with the objection to it, neither of which I have been able to settle. A classmate has proposed what she calls the cost-of-inference test: an adequate semantics for knowledge must be able to represent two agents who are identical in evidence, information, and cognitive capacity, one of whom has carried out a longer chain of inference than the other, as differing in what they know. She takes this to be a datum of ordinary usage — we say that the mathematician does not yet know the theorem she has not proved — and concludes that no closure-entailing semantics can be a semantics for knowledge at all. A second classmate objects that the test proves too much. By the same standard, a semantics for perception would have to represent two agents identical in visual information and capacity but differing in where they happen to be looking as differing in what they see; yet we do not conclude that no semantics for perception is a semantics for seeing. We say instead that the semantics describes what is available to the agent, not what the agent has taken up. My suspicion is that the two of them are talking past each other, but I cannot locate the seam.

Please write the essay. It should reach a verdict on whether the problem of logical omniscience is a genuine defect that a semantics of knowledge must repair, or an artefact of conflating two things that ought to be kept apart — and, if the latter, which two, and what work each is doing. It should say what the cost-of-inference test is really testing, and whether the perception analogy defeats the test or only relocates the difficulty. I would like it to test its verdict against a case I keep returning to: an agent who knows the axioms, is aware of them, and would assent to the entailment if asked, but who would in fact make an error were she to attempt the derivation. Does she know the theorem? And does the answer change if she once carried out the derivation correctly and has since forgotten the proof while retaining the conclusion? Finally, the essay should state and answer the strongest objection to its own conclusion, and close with an explicit account of what a semantics of knowledge ought to be able to represent, and whether the problem of logical omniscience, so understood, survives.
