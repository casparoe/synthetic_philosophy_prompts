Took a philosophy of economics seminar this term and the prof keeps saying 'preference is just choice' — or sometimes 'we only ever know preferences through choices' — usually to shut down anyone who asks whether utility numbers measure anything real. I've got a term paper coming up and I want to actualy understand what that slogan is doing instead of just nodding along.

So: what are the different precise claims 'preference is just choice' could be making? Lay them out as a menu, not a lecture. I can already see a few lurking in there — (a) an operationalist/behaviorist thing where 'preference' is defined as choice, so any talk of preference beyond choice is meaningless; (b) a purely evidential claim that choices are the only evidence we have, while preferences themselves could outrun them; (c) a claim about rationality or consistency, where a preference just is a pattern of choice that satisfies some axioms; (d) the idea that utility is only a representation of a choice ranking, so it's ordinal and not a measure of anything; (e) the everyday thought that you can choose against your own preference or just choose badly. Which of these are trivial, which are substantive and actually defensible, and which are false? And which reading is the prof most likely leaning on when he says the utility function doesn't 'measure' anything — does that reading really get him there, or does he need a stronger one?

I'm especially stuck on the mistake case. If I can genuinely choose against my preference, the identity reading looks dead to me, but maybe there's a way to keep it alive by fiddling with what counts as the relevant choice or the relevant preference. Tell me where you come down and, more importantly, what would change your mind — what argument or example about mistakes, or about preferences being incomplete, or about how to individuate choices, would flip your verdict on whether the slogan is even coherent?
