I am assembling the specification for a theory of dreaming before I look at any particular theory, and I want the criteria on the table first. The theory in question must take a stand on whether dreams are experiences — on whether there is something it is like to dream, and if so, whether that something happens during sleep or only in the act of waking recollection. Please give me the desiderata such a theory must satisfy. This is not a request for a survey of positions; it is a request for the checklist against which positions are to be measured. State each requirement together with the case or consideration that motivates it.

Organise the list under four headings: (i) the cases the theory must get right; (ii) the distinctions it must not collapse; (iii) the empirical findings it must not contradict; and (iv) the explanatory and methodological demands on it — among which I count, for instance, that it not presuppose the very answer it is meant to deliver, and that it respect the evidential asymmetry that our only access to anyone's dreams runs through waking reports.

For (i), I am especially interested in how the theory handles the following cases, which I have collected myself and which I do not take to be standard in the literature. Treat them as data points the theory must classify, not as illustrations to be waved at.

- The silent narrator: over a stretch of the night a sleeper produces fluent, coherent speech describing a scene and events, but addresses no one, and on waking recalls nothing at all. Was an experience occurring during the night?
- The bare verdict: a sleeper wakes with no experiential residue whatsoever yet finds himself simply disposed to assert that he dreamed such-and-such; contrast him with a sleeper who wakes with a vivid memory of having undergone the dream and asserts the same sentence.
- The inserted dream: in a laboratory, a subject is woken, told a dream-like narrative, and within minutes comes to remember it as something he himself dreamed. Should the theory count this as a dream?
- Real-time signalling: a sleeper in rapid-eye-movement sleep uses rehearsed eye movements to signal, as it happens, that he is aware he is dreaming and can follow instructions. Does responsiveness in real time establish that experience is occurring during sleep?
- The replay: a device reconstructs the neural activity of a sleeper's dream episode and plays it into the brain of a waking subject, who then undergoes a sequence of experiences matching the sleeper's later report. Where, if anywhere, is the dream?

For (ii), I take the following distinctions to be independently plausible, and I want to know whether the theory preserves each one or, if it rejects one, at what cost: the distinction between there having been an experience and there being a memory or report of one; between the dream's occurring during sleep and the dream's being a construction of the waking mind; between dreaming as a phenomenon and the dream report as the datum; between being a subject of experience and being a system that merely processes information; and between a dream's content and its phenomenal character — whether content can survive the loss of the latter.

For (iii), the theory must accommodate at least: that dream reports are far more frequent on waking from rapid-eye-movement sleep than from other stages, though reports from other stages occur; that recall decays sharply within minutes of waking; that external stimuli delivered during sleep are sometimes incorporated into later reports; that lucid dreamers can signal in real time; and that report length and coherence vary with sleep stage. Correct me if any of these is stated too crudely, but do not simply drop them.

Then, once the list is complete: mark each requirement as negotiable or non-negotiable, and say who would dispute the ones you call non-negotiable. Where you believe two requirements cannot both be met, say so explicitly and give the argument; a list that yields at least one impossibility result is worth more to me than one that does not. Name the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories, and explain the exclusion. Close with no more than a sentence each on how the Malcolm-style view (that dreaming is not a kind of experience at all, the dream being constituted by the waking impression rather than undergone during sleep), the Dennett-style challenge (which does not assume that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep, and treats the answer as turning at least partly on how we deploy the term experience), and one further contender of your choosing fare against the list.

End with a single final line beginning Verdict: that names, in that order, the one requirement that excludes the most otherwise attractive theories and the pair of requirements you claim cannot be jointly satisfied.
