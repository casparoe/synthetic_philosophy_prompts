In my seminar on decision theory and cooperation we have been working through Hofstadter's notion of superrationality: the idea that in a symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma, an agent who takes it that any other superrational reasoner facing the same problem will reach the same conclusion should cooperate, since defection can only be justified by a difference between the reasoners rather than by a difference in their situations. I would like to test that against a historical episode rather than a toy payoff matrix: the informal truces between British and German units on the Western Front in the winter of 1914–15, the wider pattern of tacit restraint soldiers described as live and let live, and the erosion of that restraint in 1915 under orders from above, the rotation of units, and the mounting bitterness of the fighting. Please run the analysis in both directions.

First, the mapping. Who are the players — the men in the forward trenches, their junior officers, the higher commands — what were their options, and what did the payoffs look like, including the risk that a unit which held its fire while its neighbours did not would be destroyed? Then, the condition the theory actually needs and whether the episode meets it. Superrationality requires more than symmetry of situation: it requires that each party's reasoning be a replica of the other's, and that this be common knowledge. Say where the trenches depart from that idealization — the interaction was sequential and observable rather than simultaneous and one-shot, the units were similar but not identical, information was partial, bad weather sometimes forced cooperation on both sides, and a third party with both the motive and the means to suppress cooperation sat above the players.

Second, the discriminating question. Superrationality is supposed to explain cooperation without repetition, communication, or enforcement, yet the trenches supplied all three, so an account in terms of reciprocity — or a correlated-equilibrium account in which the shared environment supplies the correlation device — may already do the explanatory work. Tell me what, if anything, superrationality adds, and whether the record could in principle distinguish these accounts; if it cannot, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a test.

Third, the theory under pressure. Superrationality is fragile in a specific way: assign the other party even a small probability of not being your replica and the recommendation can flip back to defection. Ask whether the shape of the episode — truces forming locally and tacitly at platoon level, surviving in quiet sectors, and dying when units rotated or when raids and barrages were ordered — bears on that fragility, and what it implies about the higher commands, who were themselves in something like a symmetric mutual-restraint situation and yet chose to escalate and to police fraternization.

Finally, the verdict on the theory: what the episode shows about superrationality as a decision rule — whether it is a limit case holding only under an idealization rarely met, whether it can be applied by one party alone, and how it compares with universalizability as a test of one's maxim. I would rather have a disciplined account of where the theory is silent than a forced fit.
