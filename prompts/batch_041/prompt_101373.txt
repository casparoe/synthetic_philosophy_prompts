Last week a colleague in my reading group closed our discussion of Machiavelli's chapter on fortune with the one-liner everyone seems to know: 'fortune is a woman.' His point was that the chapter's lesson is that deliberation and preparation are for the timid — that when luck turns against you the winner is whoever moves first and hardest, and that to plan against bad outcomes is already to have lost. I work in flood-risk engineering, and the argument has been nagging at me, because the very same chapter contains Machiavelli's own figure of fortune as a river in flood: 'I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place,' the moral of which is that one should raise defences and barriers while the weather is fair. A little above that he writes that fortune is 'the arbiter of one-half of our actions,' leaving us 'to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less,' and near the end he confesses a preference for the adventurous over the cautious, since fortune 'allows herself to be mastered by the adventurous rather than by those who go to work more coldly.' The line my colleague quoted is continuous with that last passage, and in full it adds that 'if you wish to keep her under it is necessary to beat and ill-use her.' So the chapter appears to counsel both preparation and aggression, and the slogan seems to be recruited for the second. I want to know what the slogan actually commits you to.

Treat 'fortune is a woman' as a piece of language rather than a settled doctrine, and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making — as a menu, not as a single preferred reading. Include at least: readings on which it is close to empty (that luck is changeable, that fortune is fickle, that fortune is not to be relied upon); readings on which it is a substantive thesis about how fortune is to be handled (that boldness succeeds where caution fails, that fortune rewards those who seize her, that fortune must be coerced rather than courted); readings on which it concerns the limits of virtù (that human skill accounts for only half of what happens, or less); and the reading that takes the figure literally, as a claim about mastery and about who counts as an agent. For each, say whether it is trivial, contentious, or false, and whether the chapter's own text — the river, the 'one-half of our actions' claim, the closing preference for the adventurous — actually supports it. Then say which reading, if any, would license the use my colleague put the slogan to, namely aggression over preparation, and whether that use survives contact with the rest of the chapter.

Please give the answer in four named sections:

1. 'The menu' (the bulk of the response): the distinct readings, each with a short name and a couple of sentences spelling out exactly what it commits you to.

2. 'Verdicts': for each reading, a short paragraph saying whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, with the textual evidence for or against.

3. 'What the slogan was doing': one paragraph on which reading the aggression-over-preparation argument actually needs, and whether Machiavelli's text supports it.

4. 'Bottom line': two or three sentences.
