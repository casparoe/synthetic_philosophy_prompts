I'm putting together a handout for the first meeting of a graduate seminar that sits between welfare economics and normative ethics, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than a list of positions, because the labels in this area are nearly useless: students arrive convinced that they are either card-carrying ordinalists or defenders of cardinal utility, and it turns out they cannot say whether the thing they are comparing is the level of a person's well-being or the rate at which additional goods improve it, or whether the comparison is supposed to be discovered or stipulated. The tree should begin from commitments a reader can assess without knowing any of the names, and it should be built from the logical space outward: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space. Please make every internal node a question that genuinely splits the positions rather than a restatement of one of them, and make sure the early nodes cover at least these four issues — whether a sentence that compares two people's well-being can be true or false at all; whether what is being compared is the level of well-being (who is worse off) or the unit (how much a given increment of some good is worth to each of them), since a view can coherently accept one and reject the other; whether the comparison must be grounded in a common measure such as hedonic states, resources, preferences, or an objective list, or is irreducibly a normative stipulation; and whether full comparability is required or partial comparability will do, so that some pairs of people are comparable and others are not.

For the leaves, I want each one named, or marked unnamed if nobody actually defends it, and I want these positions to appear somewhere in the tree: full cardinal comparability on a common scale; ordinal level-comparability of the kind that underwrites a priority for the worst off; the asymmetrical views that license comparisons of units but not of levels, and the reverse; the view that comparisons are meaningful only once a conception of the good has been fixed, so that there is no fact of the matter across conceptions; the view that such comparisons are not truth-apt at all but are ethical stipulations we impose; and a resourcist or capability-based proxy that abandons the comparison in favor of something measurable. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers with no occupant, tell me so and diagnose it — is that corner empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it? Then please flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, rather than forcing a distinction the questions do not support, and tell me where the tree has to make a choice that the underlying debate does not settle.

To keep it concrete, anchor the abstract nodes in a few cases the reader has to have an opinion about: a person in chronic pain versus a person who is merely bored but whose preferences are equally satisfied; a person who has adapted to a deprived condition and reports being content versus a person who is objectively better off and reports misery; and two people whose preference-satisfaction scores are identical but whose felt lives are plainly unequal. Keep the whole instrument short enough to work through in about fifteen minutes — no more than roughly a dozen nodes and six to eight leaves — and close with a short scoring note explaining which leaf a reader lands on given each pattern of answers, plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment that leaf carries.
