I teach in a philosophy department and have been asked to run a forty-five-minute lunchtime session on the Church–Turing thesis for an interdisciplinary group — mostly neuroscientists and economists, none of them with any logic. I don't want the thesis explained to me; I want the session planned. Tell me the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; the single example or case that should carry the whole thing, and what that case must have and must lack (it has to leave the leap from "no one has ever found a counterexample" to "this is the correct analysis" feeling like an open question rather than a foregone conclusion); the misconceptions I should expect from this audience and exactly where in the sequence each should be defused — I am already braced for "so the brain is a Turing machine", "it's a theorem, so it's provable", "it's only a definition, so there's nothing to argue about", and "it's about what computers can do, so it's about speed and memory". I keep getting tangled between two readings of the thesis, one on which it stipulates what "computable" shall mean and one on which it is a substantive claim about the reach of physical processes, and I want the plan to make clear which reading the usual evidence — the convergence of the several independent formalisms, and the failure of decades of attempts at counterexamples — actually supports. Finish with a single question I can put to the room that would show me whether they have understood what kind of claim this is, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level.
