I write about intellectual history and the afterlife of ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am giving the closing plenary at a genocide-studies symposium. The room will not be friendly, and I would like to walk in knowing exactly what is coming. Prepare me.

The audience divides into four camps. There are archival historians of the Third Reich, who regard Arendt as a brilliant amateur whose book is riddled with errors and who will want to litigate what the later documentary record shows about Eichmann's own antisemitism. There are moral philosophers working on complicity and role morality, who suspect the banality of evil does no philosophical work at all, and who will press the difference between a description of a perpetrator's mind and a verdict on his guilt. There are people from memorial institutions and survivor foundations, for whom the phrase has become a public instrument and who find it exculpatory and evasive. And there are a few committed Arendtians, who will think my reading saves the phrase by draining it of its scandal.

My argument is this. The banality of evil is not a claim about Eichmann's inner life, and the evidence of his fervent antisemitism therefore does not refute it. What Arendt identified was a failure of judgment with a determinate structure: the substitution of role-conscientiousness for the question of what one is actually doing. I call the moment of substitution the remit shift — the point at which an agent stops deliberating about the object of his action and begins deliberating about its warrant, so that the question of whether he is permitted to act crowds out the question of what the act is. On my account banality is a property of a reasoning procedure rather than of a personality, which is why a passionate, intelligent, fully informed agent can be banal in the relevant sense, and why the phrase has nothing to do with stupidity or dullness. I close with a contemporary case: a mid-level administrator who privately dislikes the policy she is implementing but treats its legality as having settled the matter for her.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet of no more than 1,500 words. Give me the five or six objections that will actually do damage in this room — not the ones I would get from a graduate seminar — ordered by how much damage they would do to the argument, each phrased the way the faction that will raise it would phrase it. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede in my opening remarks before anyone has asked, and what to hold. And tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and why it is the one that should worry me. No citations or reading lists — I know the literature; I need the argument.
