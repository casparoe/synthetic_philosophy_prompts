OK, background first, because I think it matters: I spent about thirty years as a field biologist — mostly wetland survey work, nothing glamorous — and since I retired a couple of years ago I've been sitting in on undergrad philosophy lectures at the university in town. I never had time to read any of this properly when I was working, and now I do, and it's mostly wonderful and occasionally it makes me want to throw the book across the room, which I'm told is the normal experience.

Here's what's got me stuck. Somebody in my little study group handed us this argument about fiction and I wrote it out with numbers, because numbers are the only way I can hold onto anything anymore:

(1) A genuine emotion — real fear, real grief, real delight — requires you to believe that its object is real, or is really the case. Otherwise you're only going through the motions.
(2) When I'm deep in a novel and the character I've come to care about dies, I do not believe any of it actually happened. I know perfectly well it's invented.
(3) So what I feel when she dies is not genuine grief. At best it's a rehearsal, or a shadow of the real thing.
(4) Fine, say I swallow that. Now push (1) a little further. I still grieve my brother, who died eleven years ago. But I don't believe, right now, that he is dead — not in the sense that it is presently the case; I believe he died, which is a fact about the past. If (1) means the object of the emotion has to be real right now, then grieving someone long dead has the same shape as grieving a made-up character: in both cases the person is not, at this moment, here.
(5) Same with dread of a future I can't prevent, and with the sickness I feel reading about a famine that ended before I was born. In neither case do I believe the bad thing is obtaining now.
(6) So if (1) is right, nearly all of my emotional life is counterfeit — and so is everybody else's.
(7) That can't be right. So (1) is wrong, or at least very badly stated.

The trouble is I can't tell where it breaks, and neither can the study group. One of them thinks the flaw is in (2), that I secretly do believe in the character while I'm reading, and I don't buy that for a second — I'm not confused about what a novel is. But I can't put my finger on the real problem either. Either (1) is simply too strong as written and steps (4) and (5) are exposing that, or (4) and (5) are cheating somewhere, smuggling in something that (1) doesn't actually say.

And there's a nastier case I keep circling back to, which is probly why I can't let this go. A friend of mine years ago wrote to a pen pal overseas for a long time — years of letters — and then found out the whole thing, the person, the letters, everything, had been made up by someone closer to home. She had grieved this pen pal when the letters stopped. Grieved him for real. And then found out he'd never existed. Was the grief genuine? If I say yes, then believing the object is real wasn't necessary after all and (1) is dead. If I say no, then I don't see how any of us can ever be sure our feelings are the real thing, and I don't believe that either. Same knot with things I know are impossible rather than merely invented — I get a genuine chill from a story that runs on time travel that I'm quite sure can't work.

So: which step do I give up, and what do I have to pay for giving it up? If (1) has to go, what's the least bad replacement — and do the going alternatives actually handle the pen pal and the impossible story, or does each of them buy its way out of one case by making another one worse? I'd rather keep (1) if there's any way to, but I'll pay the price if I have to. Just tell me what the price is.
