For the past several months I have been caught up in a running disagreement with a close friend about a contested matter of public policy, and in the course of trying to keep the argument civil a third party — someone with a background in decision theory — told us both, more or less as a conversation-stopper, that rational people cannot agree to disagree. I have since met the same line in three further settings: in a reading group on the epistemology of peer disagreement, where it was offered as a one-sentence summary of Aumann's 1976 result; in a departmental argument about whether persistent political disagreement is itself evidence that one side is arguing in bad faith; and in a discussion of a mathematical proof, where it was used to suggest that anyone still unconvinced had simply failed to understand it. I am now fairly convinced that these uses cannot all be licensing the same claim, and I should like a careful inventory of what that one sentence could actually be asserting.

Please set out the distinct precise theses the sentence might express, arranged roughly from the trivial to the contentious, and attach a verdict to each: which are true but nearly empty; which are genuine results with substantive preconditions; which hold only under idealisations that never obtain in the disagreements we actually have; and which are simply false as stated. The words doing the work are 'rational', 'agree', 'disagree' and 'can', so I should like each reading to say explicitly what it takes them to mean — whether the claim concerns posterior probabilities, preferences, or actions; whether it presupposes a shared prior, common knowledge of the two agents' opinions, common knowledge of their rationality, or a partitional structure on what each knows; and how it behaves once those assumptions are relaxed, for instance by allowing heterogeneous priors, by allowing each agent to be uncertain about the other's reasoning rather than merely ignorant of the other's evidence, by considering a dialogue in which the two take turns announcing their credences, or by moving from a single proposition to a disagreement about values. I am also curious about the converse direction: I have heard it said that the impossibility of agreeing to disagree is at least a necessary condition for the existence of a common prior, and that a stronger condition — the impossibility of agreeing on the profitability of a bet — is necessary and sufficient for it. I should like to know whether claims of that shape are claims about rational agents or merely constraints on a mathematical model, since the two seem to me to be run together constantly. I am not asking for pointers into the literature; I want the distinctions.

Finally, please say which reading, if any, would actually license the conversational move that was made against me — the inference from a disagreement's persistence to somebody's unreasonableness or bad faith — and which readings would support the stronger suggestion that a rational person ought to be moved by the theorem itself. Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts attached rather than choosing one and expounding it, because what I want is to be able to say, at each point in the exchange, exactly which claim is being invoked and whether it will bear the weight.
