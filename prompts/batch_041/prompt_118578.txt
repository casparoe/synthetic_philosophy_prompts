Write a dialogue between two epistemologists arguing over a juror who's just heard a forensic analyst say, in effect, "I'm about 70% confident the sample matches." One of them thinks the juror should handle this by Jeffrey conditionalization -- treat the analysts report as uncertain evidence and update by probability kinematics, so her credence in "match" becomes a weighted average of her credence conditional on match and on no-match. The other thinks thats a pointless detour: the juror's only *certain* evidence is the plain fact that the analyst said what he said, and ordinary conditionalization on that fact does all the work, no special machinery needed. Let them actually go at it. The second should press hard that "uncertain evidence" is just certain evidence about somebody's mental state, and the first needs a real comeback -- something about the case where the uncertainty lives in your own perception, or where you couldn't state the likelihoods if your life depended on it. Then toss them this twist to chew on: the juror later learns the analyst's confidence ratings are miscalibrated, he's overconfident precisely on the hard borderline samples, the ones where the answer is genuinely unclear. Does that sink the "just condition on his report" move? Does it sink the Jeffrey move too -- after all, which partition is she conditioning over, and why that one and not another? Or does it just show both sides need something extra bolted on? Neither side should be a strawman and neither should talk past the other, every reply has to pick up the actual point just made. You can leave them unresolved, but make the disagreement as sharp as you can.
