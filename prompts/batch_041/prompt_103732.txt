Next month I'm presenting at our joint stats/philosophy reading group and I want a Q&A prep sheet. Here's my line: the old-evidence problem for Bayesian confirmation is real, and both of the standard fixes fail. The Garber-style counterfactual move -- ask what your credence in E would have been if you hadn't already known it -- is busted, because there's no determinate fact about that counterfactual credence and it makes confirmation hostage to a psychological accident. The other fix, just redefining confirmation as a likelihood ratio or Bayes factor and dropping the conditionalization requirement, is busted too, because it throws out exactly the incremental, time-sensitive notion we cared about. My positive proposal is that confirmation should be knowledge-relative rather than credence-relative: what matters is P(E | H & background knowledge), not the agent's subjective prior, so old evidence can confirm perfectly well even when the agent already assigns it probabilty 1.

The room: two committed subjective Bayesians (personalist, de Finetti types) who'll say the whole thing is a pseudo-problem, a phil-sci person who knows the Glymour literatue cold and thinks Bayesians are dodging, and a statistician who lives in model selection and will go after whether I've just reinvented the Bayes factor or quietly violated the likelihood principle. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll hit, phrased the way each of them would actually put it, sorted by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
