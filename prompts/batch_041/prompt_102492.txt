So I've been chewing on Dennett's "Quining Qualia" for the better part of a term now — I'm a second-year PhD student and my seminar keeps circling back to it — and I've talked myself into a stronger version of the claim than I think Dennett himself quite commits to. It's not just that qualia are hard to measure, or that we're bad at introspecting them. My version is that the term "qualia," as it's traditionally used (intrinsic, private, ineffable, directly apprehensible features of experience), doesn't come with a criterion of identity attached to it at all: there's no fact of the matter, even in principle, about whether two experiences have the same quale or different ones, and a posit with no identity conditions isn't a determinate posit — it's a place-holder, not a property. The Chase-and-Sanborn case is what definately sold me: the two coffee tasters' reports just don't let us decide whether the coffee's taste-qualia changed or their liking for it changed, and my line is that this isn't an epistemic gap we could close with better instruments — the two "hypotheses" aren't even distinct, because nothing in the concept of a quale tells you which of the subject's internal changes would count as a change of quale.

What I want from you is the honest defeater map for this specific version of the view, not the generic Dennett-vs-Jackson stuff. What would actually count against it? I want the evidence, arguements, cases, and formal results that should move someone who holds it, ranked from what would be decisive to what would be merely awkward — and sorted by which ones a defender of my view would themselves have to concede as counting against them versus which ones they'd be entitled to wave off as question-begging. A few things I'm already chewing on, so you can tell me if I'm wrong to fixate on them: (a) whether the whole thing collapses if someone can give a functionalist or relational account of qualia that smuggles in the identity conditions I say are missing — does that rescue the concept, or just change the subject to something I'd insist isn't qualia anymore; (b) the transparency-of-experience point, that when you introspect you only ever find properties of the objects you're experiencing and never intrinsic properties of the experience itself, which a friend tells me is the real linchpin of my argument and which I suspect makes my view rest on a contestable thesis about introspection; and (c) whether the charge proves too much, since the same "no criteria of identity" move might apply to plenty of respectable posits (beliefs, moods, even pains-as-states), and if so that's either a reductio of my argument or a sign I've mis-stated the charge. Also: does my view risk being unfalsifiable in the way people complain about — is there anything a qualia realist could produce that I wouldn't just redescribe as "well, that's not qualia"? If it turns out I can't lose, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's actually a problem for a claim of this kind or just what it means for the claim to be about a concept rather than about the world.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — just the verdict on how exposed my view is and the single most serious defeater — then give the reasoning and the ranked map, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
