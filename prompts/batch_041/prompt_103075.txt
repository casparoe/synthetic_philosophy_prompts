I am preparing a seminar on default assumptions in language, and I have been rereading Hofstadter's Person Paper satire, in which the standard defence of the so-called generic masculine — the defence that runs through the familiar cases of chairman, mankind and the man in the street — is rewritten with race substituted for sex, so that the marked and unmarked roles are reassigned and a conventional arrangement that ordinarily passes unnoticed is made to sound absurd. I intend to use it, but a colleague in the department objects that the whole device is a cheap trick: that race and sex are not alike enough for the substitution to establish anything, and that the shock it produces is doing all the work. I cannot decide whether he is right, and I would like you to audit the substitution as an analogy rather than merely as a joke. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the unmarked term, what plays the role of the marked term, who plays the reformer and who the defender of tradition, and what plays the part of the reader's sense that something has gone badly wrong — and then say which of these correspondences are load-bearing for the essay's argument and which are merely decorative. It may also be that the device is better read as a reductio of the traditionalist's position than as an argument from analogy at all; if so, tell me what difference that makes to the audit.

My difficulty is that the two systems of markedness are not obviously isomorphic. In English racial discourse, white has long been the unmarked or invisible term — the one that does not need saying — whereas in the satire whiteness is made to carry the markedness that womanhood carries in gender discourse. If that is an inversion of the actual arrangement rather than a preservation of it, does the satire trade on a false parallel, or does the inversion in fact sharpen its point? I should also like you to separate two conclusions the essay might be thought to establish: first, that the masculine generic is arbitrary in the way any convention is arbitrary, so that a proposed replacement is no more artificial than what it displaces; and second, that the masculine generic is not neutral at all, in that it imports male imagery even when it is being used generically. Which of these does the race-substitution genuinely support, and does it support one only by borrowing force from the other? Finally, say what the device cannot establish — in particular, whether anything follows from it about whether we ought to alter our usage, as opposed to about what our usage is already doing, and whether the satire can tell us anything about cases where the two systems of markedness come apart, as in languages whose gender systems are not organised as English is.
