I'm a high school student writing a philosophy-of-knowledge essay, and I'd value detailed comments on it. The essay begins from a distinction I find genuinely useful: an epistemic bubble is an environment in which you simply never encounter the strongest opposing views, because your sources never put them in front of you; an echo chamber is an environment in which you do encounter them but have been led to distrust everyone who holds them. My central claim is that this two-way split overlooks a third phenomenon, which I provisionally call a closed frame. In a closed frame, opposing views are neither hidden nor smeared; instead, the questions that would generate them are never asked, because the vocabulary and background assumptions in circulation do not contain them. My running example is a group that debates only whether a proposed rule is efficient or inefficient, so that the question whether efficiency should be the goal at all is not suppressed but never forms. I argue that the three cases require three different remedies — exposure, rebuilding trust, and something closer to importing new vocabulary — and that mistaking a closed frame for a mere bubble leads people to prescribe more exposure, which accomplishes nothing.

Could you comment on whether the third category is genuinely distinct from the other two, or whether it collapses into one of them or into ordinary framing effects? Please also flag any place where the essay overreaches, and tell me the single strongest objection to the whole three-way scheme.
