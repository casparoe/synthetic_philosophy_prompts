For a graduate seminar I run on thought experiments that resist being stated as arguments, I've been trying to build a writing exercise out of a puzzle in formal epistemology that I keep meeting in the abstract and have never once seen dramatized convincingly. The puzzle, stripped down: two communities share a deep aquifer, and each has its own word for the water — I've been calling them the vein and the sheet, though a better writer would find better terms — and each word is fully disciplined by that community's practices, its wells, its allocation customs, its stories about who owes what to whom. Neither word translates into the other's vocabulary without loss; rendered into the other tongue, each must be split into several words or else collapsed together with something its users would insist is a different thing entirely. Whether the two words pick out the same water, overlapping water, or water that merely touches is a genuine question with a genuine answer. But the answer turns on facts about the rock — connectivity, residence time, the shape of a buried channel — that no one has ever observed, and the one procedure that could settle it, a trace run, would introduce a dye into the aquifer that permanently alters the thing it was meant to describe. Meanwhile the allocation has to be made: a mediator must issue a single finding in a single vocabulary, and any vocabulary she chooses will count some water twice and some water not at all. A colleague at the law school who teaches water rights insists the exercise is rigged, that I've built a puzzle with no honest exit; I'd like the story to be my answer to her.

Please write a short story of roughly 1,800 words that makes this concrete. Tell it in the third person, limited to the mediator — a hydrogeologist with a legal mandate and no stake in either community — and keep the reader inside the texture of her work: the well logs, the pump meters, the hearing room, the smell of the water, the arguments she is not permitted to record. Invent the place and both communities outright, including their words for water, and let those words do real work in the sentences rather than sitting in the story as curiosities; you may keep my placeholder terms or replace them. Do not use the words ontology, concept, or inaccessible anywhere in the story, and do not let any character deliver a lecture about reference, translation, or the limits of evidence. If the puzzle is legible at all, it should be legible from what the mediator can and cannot write down. Her own technical vocabulary — recharge, unit, basin, yield — is itself a third way of carving the water, and the story should let a reader feel that without ever having the narrator remark on it.

Two things the story must not do. It must not resolve the ambiguity by revealing the underlying geology after all — no late discovery that vindicates one community or reconciles the two — and it must not end with the mediator concluding that both sides were right in their own way, which is the ending the seminar has seen too many times. The ending I want is a signed document: the finding she issues, in the terms she is permitted to use, correct as far as it goes. The final page should let a careful reader see exactly what that document cannot say, and what each community has therefore given up in the act of being assigned a share.
