I am a final-year secondary-school student, and for a physics-and-philosophy project I have been reading popular accounts of Maxwell's demon — the ratchet-and-pawl discussion in the Feynman Lectures, Szilárd's single-molecule engine, Landauer's principle about the minimum cost of erasing a bit, and the standard resolution of the paradox that says the second law is saved by the demon's own bookkeeping rather than by anything about the gas itself. I have also been arguing about all this with two friends, one of whom insists the demon is a long-solved pseudo-problem and the other of whom insists the demon wins and the second law is merely a rule of thumb. I am not confident enough in my own grasp to referee them, so rather than ask you to settle their dispute I would like you to audit my own position, which I have written out below as carefully as I can. Please treat it as a serious set of commitments I actually hold, not as a list of things I might cheerfully abandon if that made the set tidier.

Here is what I believe. (1) The second law is statistical rather than absolute: it says entropy almost certainly increases, not that it must, so a single lucky run against it is not impossible, only astronomically unlikely. (2) Even so, no demon can beat the second law as a matter of principle, and the reason is informational rather than a matter of luck. (3) The reason is that obtaining the information costs something: to learn which molecules are fast, the demon has to interact with them, and that interaction raises entropy somewhere else. (4) The reason is instead that the demon's memory is finite and must eventually be wiped, and wiping a bit costs at least an amount proportional to the temperature, so the demon loses even if the measurement itself were free. (5) Information is not itself a physical substance; it is a bookkeeping device for correlations, and the slogan that information is physical is a metaphor that has been taken far too literally. (6) A demon that never erases anything — a fully reversible machine that keeps all its records and returns to its starting configuration by reversible steps — is at least conceivable, and I do not see what in physics forbids it.

Please go through this set and tell me where it fails to hang together. I want the tensions ordered by how severe they are rather than listed as though everything were equally bad; for each one, say precisely what is pulling against what, and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me. I suspect some of what looks like conflict is not, and I would much rather you tell me which apparent clashes dissolve, and why, than hedge by treating them all as real. Then commit: name the one commitment you think I should give up and defend that verdict, and tell me which single change would do the most to make the remainder of the set defensible, even if it is not the one I would most enjoy surrendering. I would also like you to be candid about which parts of this are genuinely contested among people who understand the physics and which parts are settled, since from what I have read I cannot always tell the difference, and please do not soften your answer into a survey of views — I am asking you to take a position and give your reasons for it.
