I recently took over the rule by which our unit decides what to fund and what to work on: a fixed pot of money, a limited pool of reviewer hours, and more proposals worth doing than either can absorb. I had assumed that the right description of what we do is satisficing — we set a bar, fund whatever clears it, and adjust the bar when too much or too little clears it — and I said as much to a colleague who works on decision theory. He pushed back hard. On his view, satisficing is not a genuine alternative to optimizing at all: once you write the costs of searching, deliberating, and reviewing into the objective, a threshold rule simply is the optimal policy for agents in our position, and describing it as satisficing adds nothing. I would like a careful comparison rather than a verdict by slogan, because I will have to defend our procedure to people who ask exactly this question.

Compare Herbert Simon's satisficing with the resource-rational (or computational-rationality) program, which treats a bounded agent's policy as the solution to a meta-level optimization problem whose objective includes the cost of the computation itself. Set out precisely what each claims — on one side the search process, the acceptability threshold or aspiration level, and its adaptation to what has already been found; on the other, the object-level/meta-level split and something like the expected value of computation — and then work through the following.

First, is the subsumption claim true? Is satisficing simply what optimality looks like once search and deliberation are costly, or does something in it resist being written into a single objective? Second, if an aspiration level is fixed by an agent's history, its identity, and its comparisons with others rather than derived from an objective, does satisficing become a different kind of theory rather than a special case? Third, does the meta-level optimization regress — optimizing how much to optimize, and then how much to optimize that — tell against the resource-rational program, or is it a harmless feature of it? Fourth, what kind of theory is each: a description of how agents actually choose, an explanation of why their choices look as they do, or a normative standard for how they should choose? And finally, what would each say about a case like mine, in which a threshold is chosen deliberately, revised in the light of what turns up, and constrained mainly by the scarcity of review attention?

End with a judgment: which framework should I use to describe and defend our selection rule, and what does the other one capture that I would lose by abandoning it.
