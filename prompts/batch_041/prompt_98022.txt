I am preparing a session for a graduate reading group in metaphysics that has recently turned to the philosophy of mathematics, and I need a teaching plan rather than an explanation. The topic is Skolem's paradox. The audience has all completed one solid course in first-order logic: they know what a structure is, they can state and use the completeness and compactness theorems, and they are comfortable with the distinction between a theory and its models. None of them has studied set theory beyond the naive level, and none has met the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems. Two of them have just read a popular essay arguing that the paradox establishes that there are no uncountable sets at all, and they arrive already committed to that conclusion; my aim is to have them see precisely what is and is not being claimed, not merely to be told they are wrong.

Please plan how I should teach it. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each step say what it depends on and why it cannot come earlier — in particular, where the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem has to sit relative to the notion of satisfaction in a model and relative to the point that countability is expressed by a formula, and whether the paradox can be stated at all before the students see that one and the same sentence can be true in one model and false in another. Give me the single carrying example around which the whole session should be built, and make it a concrete case in which one and the same object is uncountable according to the model yet countable from the outside; tell me what that example must contain and what it must leave out, so that it does not quietly presuppose the resolution. Organize the technical heart of the session around the asymmetry between the notions that are Σ1 and those that are Π1 — why a set the model calls countable really is countable, while a set it calls uncountable need not be — and tell me at what point in the sequence that asymmetry should be introduced so that it lands as a clarification rather than as a patch applied to a contradiction.

Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, with a note on exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. I expect at least these: that the model's failure to contain a bijection between the set and ω means that no bijection exists; that the paradox therefore shows set theory to be inconsistent, or the real numbers to be countable; that the countable model's ω is the genuine ω; and that the whole difficulty is merely an artefact of first-order logic being too weak to pin down its intended subject matter. Include any further misconception you think this audience will form, and identify the one I should most fear.

Close with a single question I can put to the group at the end that will separate a student who has grasped the relativity of countability from one who has merely concluded that set theory is broken. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so to the group.
