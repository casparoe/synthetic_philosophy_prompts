I spent thirty years as a chemist before I got bored and started reading philosophy on my own, so forgive me if I mangle a term. I keep tripping over Pascal's mugging — the setup where a stranger says he'll torture some astronomically large number of people unless you hand over your wallet, and the expected-value math says you hand it over. I want to laugh at it, but I want the laugh to be erned, not just "haha decision theory dumb."

So write me a parody agony-aunt adivce column — call it "Dear Expected Utility" or whatever you like — where the columnist is a committed expected-utility maximizer and the readers keep sending in fresh versions of the mugging. Three reader letters, each twisting the setup: one plain mugging; one where the mugger says he only bothered to threaten you because he knew you'd pay; and one where the whole neighborhood is crawling with muggers and paying them all would ruin you. The columnist answers each one deadpan and consistently — no winking, no "obviously this is absurd," no quietly going soft. The funny has to come from the logic being *right*: the expected-value calculation, the absurdly tiny probabilities, the problem of many muggings, and whether the threat's probability even depends on your answer. A decision theorist should read it and nod, not wince.

Structure it like this: "Editor's Note," about 50 words setting up the column; "The Letters," the three short reader letters; "The Replies," the columnist's answers, the bulk of it, so the whole column runs maybe 600 words; and then a final section called "Notes," about 150 words, where you drop the character and tell me plainly what you were parodying and which objections you folded in, so I can check the decision theory underneath was real.

American English, please. Keep the mugger's numbers suitably absurd — that's the whole point.
