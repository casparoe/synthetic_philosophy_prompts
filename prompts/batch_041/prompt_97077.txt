I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar in philosophy of law and social ontology, with fourteen students, and I want to run a constitutional-convention game that makes the paradoxes of legal self-reference something they run into rather than something I announce. I have in mind the family of self-amending rule games sometimes called Nomic, but I do not want a reproduction of the standard published rule set; I want a fresh, self-contained activity whose initial constitution is simple enough to be read aloud and whose rules are arranged so that the group's own attempts to amend them generate the trouble. The students should leave having felt at least three distinct pressures: the pressure of entrenchment (a rule that tries to protect itself from amendment), the pressure of procedural self-application (a rule that governs how rules are changed being used to change itself), and the pressure of interpretive finality (whoever resolves conflicts becoming the thing that needs resolving). No lecture before the game; the vocabulary of self-amendment, validity, and basic norms should arrive only in the debrief.

Please design the activity in enough detail that I can run it without improvising. Give me the initial rule set—perhaps six to ten numbered rules—including at least one rule that makes all rules amendable by simple majority, one that gives the amendment procedure a higher threshold, one that says the rules are to be read according to the plain meaning of their words, one that assigns final interpretive authority to a designated participant, and one that specifies the win condition. Specify the roles (delegates, a registrar, an interpreter, perhaps a facilitator's silent observer), the materials (index cards, tokens, a whiteboard, a printed constitution), the voting and scoring scheme, and a round structure that fits 90 minutes, with three or four rounds of play and a final debrief. The scoring should create real incentives to pass amendments and to protect one's own rules, but it must not require money or anything beyond the classroom. I would like the game to be winnable, and I would like one of the available moves to be an attempt to change the win condition itself. Build in at least one seeded controversy—for example, a proposed amendment that would make a previously passed rule unamendable, or a later rule that says later rules prevail when an earlier rule says earlier rules prevail—so that the group cannot simply play a tidy procedural game.

Then give me the facilitator's apparatus: a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the concepts of self-amendment, entrenchment, the hierarchy of norms, the difference between a rule's validity and its enforceability, and the difference between a legal system's basic norm and a particular statute; a list of the predictable failure modes the first time I run it (students ignoring the paradox and playing for points, one delegate dominating, the interpreter becoming a tyrant, the group never reaching a self-referential amendment, or the whole thing collapsing into shouting) with practical fixes for each; and an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply in the debrief. I am especially interested in the last point: I want to know which feature of the paradox of legal self-reference is lost when the game has a facilitator who can always reset the board, and what I should say to make sure the students do not mistake the game's artificial closure for a solution. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to the literature; the activity should stand on its own.
