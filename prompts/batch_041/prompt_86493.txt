I'm testing whether the Athenian assembly punished rebellious cities by any consistent rule, and I'd rather have you test my track record than extend it. As I read it: Mytilene, 427 — the assembly decrees death for every adult male and enslavement for the rest, reverses itself the next day, and executes rather more than a thousand men identified as the authors of the revolt, razing the walls and taking the fleet but leaving the remaining population on the land, which is divided into allotments for Athenian owners who draw rent from it. Scione, which had revolted to Brasidas — the same decree, carried out when the city falls, its land handed to the Plataean refugees. Melos, 416 — a city that was never a tributary, had kept out of the war, and refused to join either side; its adult men are killed, its women and children sold, and five hundred Athenian colonists settled in their place. Two verdicts on individuals belong in the same record: the generals who won at Arginusae in 406, condemned in a single vote without separate trials and executed; and Socrates, condemned in 399. Now suppose that in 413, with Athens still reeling from Sicily, a city that has paid tribute for decades revolts — not under a Spartan army at its gates, but because a faction opens them while Athens looks weak. It has a large fleet and pays a large tribute, the garrison withdraws, it surrenders without a fight, its population is willing to be reintegrated, the authors of the revolt are few and known, and Athens has no settlers on hand to replace anyone. What verdict should I predict, and which rule actually survives my record — retribution for voluntary revolt, the utility of the place to Athens, the guilt of the few, or nothing but the mood of the day? Tell me what each predicts here, which I should prefer and why, whether the two individual verdicts are the same rule or a second one, and the further case that would force the choice.
