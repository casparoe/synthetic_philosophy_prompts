Having spent thirty-five years as an experimental geophysicist and the last four reading moral and legal philosophy, I keep stumbling over a pattern in the law of interrogation that I cannot make coherent, and I would like you to help me see what it presupposes. In the liberal legal orders I know anything about, torture is not merely prohibited but prohibited without exception: the treaties and statutes are drafted so that no emergency, however catastrophic and however many lives hang on it, opens a lawful door to it. Yet those same orders permit a police marksman to kill the man who is about to murder hostages, permit interrogators to deceive and pressure a suspect, and generally recognize a defence of necessity when someone breaks the law to avert a greater harm. And the confession a torturer produces is thrown out, even when there is every reason to think it true and that acting on it would save lives, which suggests the rule is doing something other than filtering for reliability. My puzzle is not whether the ban is right; it is what theory of the morality of torture the ban, and the surrounding permissions, actually commit us to.

So: does the absolute ban only make sense on a deontological view that treats torture as an absolutely forbidden act, an exceptionless constraint of a kind that killing apparently does not enjoy, or is it equally well explained by a rule-consequentialist argument (that any exception would be stretched and abused), or by a straightforward empirical bet (that torture yields unreliable information, so the ticking-bomb case is a fiction that never arises)? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the practice we have.

Two details especially bother me. First, the asymmetry between killing and torturing: if the marksman may end the hostage-taker's life to save the hostages, on what view is it impermissible to inflict pain on the same man for the same end; does the practice presuppose that death is not an evil of the same order, or that torture degrades the torturer in a way that killing does not, or merely that we trust ourselves less with pain than with bullets? Second, the inadmissibility of the coerced confession: if it is excluded because torture produces unreliable testimony, the doctrine rests on an epistemic claim, but if it is excluded because the state must not profit from its own wrongdoing, it rests on a deontic one, and the two would come apart in a case where the testimony is independently corroborated and the stakes are enormous. Tell me which commitment the practice actually makes. Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a plain act-consequentialism: which parts of the present arrangement would have to change, which would survive untouched, and would anything be left of the absolute ban? Please lead with a two- or three-sentence statement of your overall verdict, then give the supporting analysis, in no more than 750 words.
