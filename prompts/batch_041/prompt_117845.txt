I'm in a philosophy of action seminar this term and we spent two weeks on Kavka's toxin thing — the eccentric billionaire, the vial that makes you miserably ill for a day, the million dollars that's already sitting in your account before the drinking is even due, all you have to do is intend at midnight to drink it the next afternoon and then you're free to change your mind. I got into a stupidly long argument about it with my flatmate (who does econ and thinks the whole thing is just a word game about "intend") and I came out of it with a pile of verdicts that I can't get to line up. I want someone to tell me what principle they add up to, and I'd rather you not just hand me the two standard lines from the literature — I've read those, and neither of them matches what I actually think.

Here's what I actually think. (i) Original setup, million in the account overnight, one day of nausea: it's rational to try to form the intention, I'd pay real money for a pill that made me intend it, and then the next day I would not drink. I dont see why intending to do something has to mean predicting you'll do it — changing your mind is a normal thing people do, and once the money's mine, drinking is pure loss. (ii) Same deal but the wire only goes through after I drink: no puzzle at all, I drink, I don't need any pill. Which bothers me, because the only difference between (i) and (ii) is the timing of a bank transfer, and it feels wrong that rationality should hinge on that. (iii) Now make the toxin lethal — I die, family gets the million, same overnight payment. I couldn't intend that one, pill or no pill, and I'd refuse the pill if it were offered. But I can't see how that's consistent with (i): if I can intend what I'm certain I won't do, I could intend the lethal one and simply not drink it either. (iv) Instead of intending, the money is for *believing* at midnight that I'll drink it. Can't do it, and I wouldn't take a pill for it — it feels slimy in a way the intention pill doesn't, even though both are just fiddling with my own head for cash. (v) Harmless version, the vial is water: the whole thing evaporates, I intend it instantly, no pill, no tension. (vi) Promise version: instead of intending, I give the billionaire my word, paid upfront, unenforceable. I'd do it and then not drink, but I'd feel worse about it than about (i), and I can't say why the promise is worse when neither one actually binds me to drink anything.

So: what principle, or small set of competing principles, gets these — stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is earning its keep in which case — and what does it predict for cases I haven't listed? I'd be curious about things like intending to drink it twice, or intending it for a stranger's benefit rather than mine, or being offered the pill before I know how much the money is. And please be straight with me about which verdicts the principle can't save. I suspect (i) is the one that has to go — if it does, say so and say what should replace it, and tell me whether the resulting view still lets the billionaire's offer be a genuine offer or turns it into a trick. No citations or reading list, this is just for me and my notes.
