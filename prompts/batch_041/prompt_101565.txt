I have been trying to find a name, if one exists, for a pattern I keep running into whenever people argue about how strictly a rule ought to be enforced, and I want more than a label handed back to me.

The pattern is this. A rule is in place, and a case arises that nearly everyone agrees is a bad fit for it: a commuter delayed by a shutdown who arrives fifteen minutes late, a student who steps out to take a call about a sick parent. Someone proposes to let the case pass. The reply is that the exception is not the issue, because the case itself is harmless and everyone in the room agrees it is harmless; what is at stake is that granting it teaches everyone watching that the rule is negotiable, so the rule's grip on all the ordinary cases is loosened by a decision taken about an extraordinary one. What puzzles me is that the harm is said to lie entirely in the expectation the decision fixes, and to hold even if no second exception is ever granted and no one actually exploits the slack. The objection is to the standing the decision confers, not to any later case it might cause.

Two things make me doubt that the existing labels cover it. First, the structure seems to run in both directions. I have seen the same move used to defend leniency, where a single act of mercy is said to commit the community to mercy and so to convert a rule meant to be strict into one understood as discretionary. That suggests the pattern is not about harshness or leniency but about a decision's effect on shared expectations, whichever way it points. Second, it does not require any claim about downstream consequences. The familiar objection that one thing leads to another does require such a claim, and it is answered by pointing out that the predicted chain is speculative; the thing I am describing seems to survive that answer, because it never claimed a chain in the first place.

Is there an established name for this, for the idea that the cost of a one-off enforcement decision lies in the expectations it fixes rather than in the case decided, and that this cost is real even with no downstream case? I suspect it is near a few things I only half-know, and I would like to be told which is nearest, exactly where each falls short, and whether what I am describing is one of them or something that needs its own name and definition. In particular I want its edges drawn: what distinguishes it from the ordinary worry that an exception will multiply, from the view that a rule is valuable simply because it is a rule, and from a purely predictive story about deterrence.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
