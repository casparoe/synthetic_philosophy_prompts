I am a high school junior and the secretary of my school's philosophy club. For our next meeting the club has been assigned a question that I assumed would be easy and now find genuinely difficult: whether it is legitimate to say that an AI system — a large language model, say — believes anything at all. Our faculty sponsor opened the discussion by recalling Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, the strategy of predicting and explaining what something does by treating it as a rational agent that acts on the beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, and then asked us whether adopting that stance toward a chatbot is a useful fiction, a genuine discovery, or something awkwardly in between. I have to bring a written dialogue to the meeting, and I want mine to be considerably better than the shallow version of the debate my classmates keep having, in which one side insists that of course the model believes things because it says them, and the other insists that of course it does not because it is only doing arithmetic.

Please write that dialogue. Its two speakers should be equally informed and equally sharp, and neither should function as a stand-in for the obviously correct answer; each should at some point grant the other a real point, and each should press the other exactly where the pressure hurts. I would like the conversation to work through, at a minimum, the following matters: whether the intentional stance was ever intended as a claim about what exists or only as a method for predicting behaviour, and what actually turns on the difference; whether a system's pronouncements are revisable in the light of reasons and evidence, and whether something that cannot be revised that way deserves to be called a belief at all (a friend of mine argues that beliefs come in interconnected webs, so that altering one ought to alter others, and she thinks a model has no such web); whether attributing a belief to a model is better understood as attributing it to the character the model is simulating, much as we might say a narrator believes something the author does not; and whether the case is clarified or merely muddied by the fact that we already talk this way about corporations, teams, and thermostats.

I would rather the dialogue not end with both speakers shaking hands and announcing that it all depends on what you mean by belief. If they cannot settle the matter, I want it to end with the precise question on which they have discovered they disagree, phrased so that it is clear what evidence or argument would move each of them. Also, because I will be reading this aloud, please make the exchange feel like a real conversation — interruptions, concessions, replies to the other speaker's actual words — rather than two monologues sitting in alternating paragraphs.
