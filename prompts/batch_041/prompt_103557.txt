I have no philosophical training, and the argument below is entirely my own construction, so I may be misusing words; please read it for the thought rather than the vocabulary. It comes out of a year I spent living in a town where I repeatedly and unknowingly broke small local conventions — a matter of who goes first through a doorway, and of when one speaks to a neighbour in the street — and out of the argument I have had with myself ever since about whether my neighbours had a genuine complaint against me or were merely annoyed. Each step below strikes me as plausible on its own, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept.

(1) An action is wrong only if it wrongs someone — that is, only if it gives someone grounds for complaint.
(2) Many everyday matters — which side of an escalator you stand on, whether you tip, whether you cross against the light on an empty street, whether you take your turn by waiting in line or by pressing forward — have no right answer in themselves. The only answer is the one the people in a place have settled on.
(3) Since there is no answer except the settled one, the only thing that can make a visitor's breach of such a convention wrong is the injury it does to the people whose settlement it is.
(4) That injury is rarely material. It is that the breach treats their arrangement as beneath notice — it says that the way they have ordered their common life does not count for much.
(5) Being treated as beneath notice, by someone who could easily have done otherwise, is a real wrong and not merely a discourtesy.
(6) Therefore a visitor is bound by the local conventions of a place whenever complying would cost them little.
(7) Nothing in (4) or (5), however, makes cheapness part of the wrong. Cheapness only limits how much may be demanded of a person before the demand becomes unreasonable; it does not change what the wrong is.
(8) Therefore a visitor is bound by local conventions unless compliance would be unreasonably costly.
(9) But in some places the settled convention is that a guest must eat whatever is put in front of them, or must not address certain members of the household, or must surrender a seat to any local who wants it. A visitor who complies with that is servile, and I do not think a visitor is required to be servile.
(10) Since (9) is unacceptable, one of (1) through (8) has to go.

I would most like to keep (1) and (9). I cannot tell whether the trouble lies in the move from (2) to (3), in what the word "injury" is doing at (4), or in the move from (6) to (7), and I may be looking in the wrong place altogether. Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs me — in particular, whether I can keep the thought that my neighbours had a real complaint, or whether keeping it commits me to something I will not want to accept. If the whole shape of the argument is a mistake — if "convention" is doing no work here and I am really just asking when causing annoyance is wrong — say so plainly rather than answering as posed. I would rather be told the question is misconceived than be handed a tidy answer to a bad question.
