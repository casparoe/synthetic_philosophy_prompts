I am helping a colleague assemble materials for a graduate methods seminar on archaeological inference, and I would like a defensible ranking to put in front of the students. Her framing of the central difficulty is this: material remains are the residue of behaviour, but the questions we are usually asked to answer concern beliefs, and beliefs are systematically underdetermined by the remains they leave behind. As the seminar's touchstone she has proposed a thought experiment she calls the twin-site test. Imagine two communities whose excavated remains are, layer for layer, indistinguishable, but whose members hold different beliefs — about what becomes of the dead, about whether the natural world is populated by intentional agents, about whether certain substances are pure or polluting. An evidence type earns a high place in the ranking to the degree that it would betray such a difference; it falls toward the bottom to the degree that the very same remains would be left behind under either set of beliefs. (She is alert to the converse failure as well, and would want it noted where an evidence type could plausibly be produced by a single shared belief operating under different material constraints.)

Please sort the following classes of evidence into tiers according to how well each performs on the twin-site test, understood specifically as evidence for belief rather than for behaviour, function, or subsistence:

1. Votive or hoard deposits buried with no apparent intention of later recovery;
2. Grave goods placed with the dead;
3. Standardised tool forms replicated with little variation across a wide region;
4. Monumental architecture with astronomical alignments;
5. Refuse and discard assemblages accumulated without evident selection;
6. Figurines showing no use-wear and no clear functional role;
7. Objects deliberately broken, bent, or otherwise "killed" before deposition;
8. Structures in which an earlier feature was carefully preserved or built around rather than demolished.

For each item, give a sentence or two explaining why it lands in its tier, and make explicit which items you regard as strong evidence for practice but weak evidence for belief, since the seminar is partly about that gap. Where two items seem to you to belong in the same tier but for quite different reasons, say so. Then give your overall verdict on the ranking as a whole: how confident you are in it, and which single argument or consideration, if it turned out to hold, would most likely force you to move an item across a tier boundary or to abandon the twin-site test as the organising criterion — in other words, where your reasoning here is most likely to be failing.
