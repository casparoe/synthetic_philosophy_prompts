I've spent the better part of a year arguing with a friend who is finishing a dissertation on the problem of universals, and we keep talking past each other, because I'm a programmer — I work on the type checker for a statically typed language and have also put in years with dynamically typed and prototype-based ones — and every time he tells me that the universal is present in the particular, or that resemblance is primitive and needs no further ground, I hear a claim I could state more precisely in my own terms. So I'd like the translation done systematically rather than rhetorically. Take the main positions — transcendent realism, immanent realism, the several nominalisms (resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, and the view that simply refuses to posit universals at all), conceptualism, and trope theory, on which properties are unrepeatable particulars rather than anything shared — and restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of type systems: classes and instances, interfaces and implementations, nominal versus structural typing, duck typing and prototype chains, generics and monomorphization, type erasure and what is left at runtime, reflection, metaclasses, and the stratification of universes in dependently typed languages that lets you have a type of types without paradox. Treat each position as a design for how the world sorts things into kinds, and say what each design commits its holder to.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each position: what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am especially curious about three cases where I suspect my framework is quietly doing the philosophers' work for them — self-predication and the Third Man regress, which look to me like the ordinary business of a class being an instance of a class and the regress you run into before you stratify your universes; the uninstantiated universal, which in my world is just an interface nobody implements; and the question whether a universal explains the resemblance among its instances or merely is that resemblance, which looks like the difference between a shared supertype and a structural check performed at the point of use. Do not let the type-system picture smuggle in a designer: a compiler has types only because somebody wrote them down, and if the translation makes every universal depend on being declared, I want to know whether that is a discovery about the original debate or an artifact of the target vocabulary. End by telling me whether the residue points to something the type-system picture genuinely cannot express, or whether it shows that one or more of the classical positions was never more than an implementation choice dressed up as a metaphysics — and if the latter, which ones. I'm fluent in everything I listed above, so don't define subtyping for me, but I have no philosophical training beyond one undergraduate course, so don't leave gaps in the translation for me to fill. Organize the answer position by position so I can check each against my friend's version, and keep the whole thing under 900 words.
