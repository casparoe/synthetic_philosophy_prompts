ok so I'm putting together a session for a group of people who work on voting reform -- campaigners, a couple of local councillors, some policy folks -- and the whole reason we need it is that everyone in the room says they want to 'expand the franchise' and then agrees about nothing. Some want 16 year olds voting, some want non-citizen residents voting, some want compulsory voting, and they keep assuming those three go together or don't, without ever saying what they think the vote is actually *for*.

What I want from you is a decision tree / taxonomy that sorts the positions properly. Build it from the ground up: the first split should be over what grounds someone's claim to the vote -- not 'are you a democrat' but the real dividing questions. Things like: is the vote owed to whoever is *bound* by the law, or to whoever's *interests* are at stake, or to whoever is *competent* to use it, or is it a mark of *membership* in a people, or is it just whoever has skin in the game / reciprocal standing. Each of those should be a branch you reach by answering a question about a concrete case, not by picking a label. And put in a node for the circularity problem, since the people deciding who gets in are the ones already in -- I want to see which leaves have a story about that and which just quietly assume the boundary.

Then the three issues I care about -- compulsory voting, lowering the age, votes for non-citizens -- should show up as the diagnostic nodes further down, because thats where the views actually come apart and where my group is hand-waving.

Here's the angle I really need, and its the part I can't get from any textbook: I want the tree to show whether a position's take on *compulsory* voting is genuinely entailed by its ground for the franchise, or whether it's just bolted on. Like -- if you think the vote is a competence you exercise, forcing people to exercise it kind of makes sense; if you think it's a right you hold because your interests are at stake, forcing you to pursue your own interests looks weird; and if it's a duty you owe to the people who won it for you, compulsion follows almost trivially. So some cells in your tree are going to be combinations where the franchise-ground and the compulsion-stance don't fit together. Don't smooth those over -- mark them and say whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to defend it.

Please make sure these leaves turn up somewhere: the view that the franchise should go to everyone affected by the decision (and yes, follow that where it goes -- foreigners, future people, the lot); the view that it goes to everyone subjected to the law's coercion; the competence-graded view; the bounded-people / membership view; the stakeholder or reciprocity view; and one deflationary leaf for 'the franchise is just whatever a stable constitution happens to settle on, there's no deep ground.'

Use these as the branch questions, or at least make sure your branches can handle them: a 16 year old who works and pays tax; a non-citizen who's lived here twenty years, pays tax, and can be deported; a citizen who's lived abroad for a decade; someone with advanced dementia; a prisoner; and someone in another country whose whole livelihood will be wrecked by our climate policy.

Also -- if any two leaves give the same answer on every one of those cases, say so, don't pretend they're seperate. I'd rather have a smaller tree that actually splits things than a big one that just lists labels.

Be commited about this: don't hand me five options and shrug. Cut the tree where you think it should be cut, tell me which empty cells are empty for a reason and which are just unexplored, and at the end say which leaf you actually think is right and why. Aim for somewhere between six and ten leaves.
