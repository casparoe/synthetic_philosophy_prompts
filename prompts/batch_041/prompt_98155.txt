I'm a TA for a fourth-year seminar on the ethics of automated decision-making, and I've got a stack of homework to grade this weekend. The question I set was about responsibility gaps, and before students answered it I spent a whole lecture building a three-way distinction that I wanted them to actually use. Roughly: (1) the tracing gap — the harm is real but there's no culpable human act to trace it back to, either because the behaviour emerged from learning rather than design, or because contribution was so diffuse that nobody's contribution was necessary; (2) the foresight gap — there is a human act in the causal chain, but the harm wasn't reasonably foreseeable given what the agent knew, so blame doesn't attach; and (3) the standing gap — the people with the knowledge to catch the problem have no authority to act, and the people with the authority dont have the knowledge, so even when you can locate a responsible party they were structurally unable to discharge the duty. The question also gave them a scenario. Meridian Health deploys an adaptive dosing model across its hospital network; it's retrained nightly on pooled data from every site; a patient in a rare subpopulation gets a fatal dose. At Meridian, the floor nurse who notices the anomalous recommendation can't override it without pharmacist sign-off, the pharmacist who approves it never sees the model's inputs, and the vendor owns the weights and won't hand them over. The question was: which of the three readings is doing the real explanatory work in this case, what do the other two get wrong about it, and does the standard human-in-the-loop remedy address the gap you picked?

Here's one student's answer, typed up as written:

"The Meridian case is a tracing-gap case. The fatal dose wasn't produced by any decision anyone made — it fell out of the interaction between the adaptive model and a subpopulation that barely appeared in the pooled training data, and the model's behaviour at that point was an emergent property of the whole fleet's nightly updates rather than anything a designer wrote down. So the first thing to say is that the causal chain runs out before it reaches a culpable act.

The foresight gap is a red herring here. As we said in lecture, the foresight gap only gets off the ground when there's a duty to have known, and Meridian's engineers had no such duty toward a subpopulation nobody had any reason to expect the model to mishandle. Since the harm wasn't reasonably foreseeable, the foresight reading has nothing to say about this case.

That leaves the human-in-the-loop remedy, and this is where it gets interesting. If you put a pharmacist in the loop, the causal chain now runs through the pharmacist, and a chain that runs through a person is exactly what tracing requires. So the remedy works — but only if the pharmacist has real veto power, which is the same as saying it works only if you also fix the diffusion problem. Meridian's actual failure was that the nurse who noticed the anomaly had no authority and the pharmacist who had the authority never saw the inputs. That's just diffusion again, seen from the other end.

Conclusion: the deep problem with autonomous systems isn't that they're unpredictable, it's that contribution gets smeared across too many hands, and the fix is to give somebody both the knowledge and the authority."

I'm marking out of 10. Give me the grade plus the specific comments that justify it — point by point, saying what's right, what's wrong, and what the student should have said instead where they went astray. I haven't decided on a number yet, so don't assume a verdict and work backwards.
