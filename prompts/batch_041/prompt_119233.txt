I have no academic training, and for some time I have been trying to think my way through a claim I keep meeting in popular writing about the mind.

The claim is that consciousness should be understood as a kind of interface. On this view, the felt, vivid, inward character of experience is not a window onto what the brain is really doing; it is more like a computer desktop, which presents tidy icons and folders while concealing the machinery beneath. Taken seriously, the suggestion is that the sense of being a subject to whom things appear — a point of view that belongs to someone — is itself part of the display, a convenient self-model rather than a report of any real inner glow.

What puzzles me is a circularity I cannot pin down. An illusion is ordinarily something that appears to a subject to be a certain way when it is not. Here, though, the thing said to be illusory is the very appearing-to-a-subject. If there is no experiencer, in what sense is anything appearing at all? The claim seems to need the very thing it sets out to deny.

I can imagine two ways of rescuing it. The first says that the illusion is not an illusion for anyone; it is simply a system representing itself as having an inner glow, and that representation happens to be false. The second says that the term is being used loosely, and that the real content of the view is only that we should not treat the felt qualities of experience as fundamental. The first leaves me unsure what makes the self-representation false rather than merely strange, and the second seems to give up whatever was interesting.

It also reminds me of the claim that there are no truths at all, which appears to cancel itself out. But I suspect the case of consciousness is not quite the same shape as that, and I cannot say precisely how it differs.

Please explain the conceptual issue here. I would like to understand what the interface view denies and what it leaves untouched; whether the appearance of self-undermining is a genuine problem or a verbal trap; how the view differs from the far more modest claim that our introspective reports about our own experience are often unreliable; whether it is self-refuting in the way the denial of all truth is, or merely self-effacing in some weaker sense; and what sort of consideration could count for or against it. Please work at the level of the concepts, and do not assume I know any of the technical vocabulary.
