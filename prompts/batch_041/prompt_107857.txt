You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of how institutions shape people's choices — the morality of influence, the difference between helping someone and steering them, and the conditions under which a person's agreement to something should be taken at face value. Think carefully, and do not reach for a verdict before you have laid out what is actually at stake on both sides.

I manage benefits for a company of about four hundred people, and I have been asked to sign off on a change to our health program. At present the health-risk questionnaire is something employees can fill out if they want to; participation runs around thirty percent, and the insurer's actuaries estimate that if we made it the default — the form arrives already checked, and you have to go in and uncheck it — participation would rise to eighty or ninety percent. The wrinkle is what our vendor calls the renewal default. Anyone who declines is not simply left alone: the form comes back every quarter, again already checked, and you have to decline again, indefinitely, unless you ask to be taken off the list for good — an option that exists, that the vendor says almost nobody uses, and that sits two menus deep. There is money in it as well. The insurer gives a premium discount to employees who complete the questionnaire and recovers the cost through a surcharge on those who do not, so the people who keep declining end up paying more than the ones who comply. The vendor's pitch is that nothing is mandatory, every screen is honest about what is happening, and a pre-filled form is no more coercive than a pre-filled address field. A colleague of mine says it is a machine for getting people to do what we want while letting us tell ourselves they chose it. My own worry is subtler: I think most of these people would in fact be better off filling the thing out, and I do not think we are deceiving anyone, so I cannot locate what bothers me — and I would like to know whether the bother is real or just squeamishness. (I have been reading popular behavioral-economics books in my spare time, which is how this landed on my desk.)

Give me your considered advice on whether we should adopt this, and if you think some version of it is defensible, tell me what you would change. I care more about the reasoning than the verdict: what distinguishes a permissible way of arranging a choice from an impermissible one when the people arranging it are also among the beneficiaries, and where the line falls here. Please begin by stating the situation back to me as you understand it and listing the considerations you think matter, before committing to a recommendation, and tell me if I have framed anything misleadingly. At the end, say how confident you are in your overall conclusion and identify the single point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
