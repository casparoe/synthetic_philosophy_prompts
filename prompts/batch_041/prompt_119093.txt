Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The theory I want it to test is an indirect one: what an agent has reason to do is what a suitably improved counterpart of her would endorse. Two specifications of the improvement are in play, and I want a case that drives them apart.

Under the remedial specification, the counterpart differs from the agent only where the agent has gone wrong: she holds no false beliefs, draws no invalid inferences, and overlooks no consequences of her own commitments. Her desires are otherwise the agent's own. Under the constitutive specification, the counterpart is simply stipulated to be fully informed and fully coherent, and whatever desires she ends up with are the ones that count, whether or not they are the agent's.

Build a single case — realistic, a decision an ordinary adult could face this year, no science fiction, no omniscience, no infinite computational power — in which the two specifications give different answers to the question of what the agent has reason to do. An answer may take the form of a verdict of indeterminacy, provided the indeterminacy is derived rather than stipulated.

Constraints. First, the divergence must not arise because the remedial counterpart holds a false belief or reasons badly; if the case works only when the agent is mistaken about something, it is not a case about idealization. Second, the agent must have a preference whose content is partly about her own epistemic state — something she wants or does not want to know, or to be the sort of person who does or does not know it — so that improving her epistemically can alter the object of the preference rather than merely correct an error in it. Third, the case must not turn on the agent being irrational, weak-willed, or self-deceived; the divergence has to come from the structure of the idealization, not from a defect in the agent. Fourth, the divergence must not be an artifact of a thin idealization: it should persist when the counterpart is described as more informed and more coherent, as long as the two specifications are kept distinct. Fifth, keep the scenario itself to a paragraph; the analysis may run longer.

Then do the following, in order. (i) Set out the agent's initial state, the two counterparts, and the verdict each yields, deriving each verdict from its specification rather than asserting it. (ii) Name the feature of the case that produces the divergence, and say what happens if you remove it — if the two specifications then converge, say what that shows about which feature was doing the work. (iii) Give the best reply available to a defender of the constitutive specification: in particular, whether she can maintain that the remedial counterpart is merely an under-described constitutive one, or whether she must concede that the two procedures can come to rest in different places, and if so, what she can say about which resting place is authoritative. (iv) Add the one-line concession that a defender of the remedial specification would have to make in return.

The case is for a seminar on whether indirect theories of this shape are well posed; one that stipulates the two verdicts instead of generating them from the specifications is of no use for that purpose. Close with a verdict of your own: is this one theory with two readings, or two theories that share a slogan?
