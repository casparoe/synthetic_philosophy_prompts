I have been teaching the Stoic doctrine that only what lies within our own agency — our judgments, our impulses, our acts of assent — is properly ours to answer for, while the body, reputation, and outcomes belong to the class of things that are not up to us; and each term I find that both my students' verdicts and my own outrun the tidy dichotomy. So I have taken to keeping a ledger of cases and recording, as honestly as I can, where my sense of responsibility actually lands. Losing my temper at a driver who cut me off: answerable. The flare of anger before I have judged anything: not. Failing to prepare for a talk I then gave badly: answerable. The audience's verdict on the talk: not. Forgetting a close friend's birthday: answerable. My friend's feeling hurt by it: not. Coming, on evidence I never troubled to check, to believe a colleague untrustworthy: answerable. The impression of suspicion that arrived before I endorsed it: not. And then two I cannot settle: the fear that rises in me walking home late, which comes whether I will it or not, and the week of despair that descends after a rejection, which likewise seems to arrive without my consent, yet which I somehow feel I ought to be able to set down.

I would like to know what single principle, if there is one, is generating these verdicts, and whether it is the classical Stoic criterion of agency or a modern repair of it. My students report that the current revival divides the field three ways — what is wholly up to us, what is partly up to us, and what is not up to us at all — and they assure me this is an advance, but I cannot tell whether the middle category marks a genuine joint in nature or is merely the old dichotomy with a hedge installed in the center. Please state the principle as precisely as you can, tell me which of my verdicts it compels me to surrender, and say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: whether I am answerable for a belief I acquired by trusting my parents when I was a child, and whether I am answerable for the person I have become after twenty years of small choices. If the verdicts cannot be saved by a single principle, I would rather know which one to abandon than be told that my intuitions are simply inconsistent.
