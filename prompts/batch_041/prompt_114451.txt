I retired from a chemistry department a few years back (combustion and reaction kinetics, mostly) and I've been filling the time with philospohy, which is far harder than I expected and definately far more fun. Here's the thing I keep chewing on.

For thirty years I taught that a candle flame is a self-sustaining thing: it takes in wax and air, gives off heat, water and CO2, it holds its own shape (that blue zone, the little cone of unburnt vapour inside it), it leans away when you open a door, it dies when the wax runs out, and it can't be scaled up much before it starts to flicker and smoke. Half in jest I used to tell my students that a flame is the nearest thing in the lab to a living organism, and that if you'd never seen either one you couldn't easily say which of the two was the alive one. I'm told this isn't just my joke -- that people have reached for fire as the model of life since antiquity, and still do when they argue about whether viruses count, usually to conclude that the question is a verbal one: a virus is alive in the sense a flame is, i.e. it isn't, and we should all stop fussing. A friend with a PhD in philosophy of biology says the analogy is doing all the work in those arguments and shouldn't be trusted an inch.

So audit it for me. What maps onto what -- I'd like the correspondence list (metabolism, boundary, reproduction, response, death, whatever else you think the flame offers) -- and then where the mapping breaks. Which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion above, and which are just scenery? Does the conclusion survive once the breaks are on the table, and if not, is there a better analogy to hand, or does the whole analogy-first approach to the question deserve the bin? And tell me where my flame picture is wrong about real organisms. I suspect it's the reproduction bit -- my candle only makes another candle if I hold a taper to it, and even then nothing of the first flame goes across -- but I'd like to know whether that's the killer objection or whether I'm overrating it. It's the prions that really confuse me, if I'm honest: no metabolism at all, and yet they seem to breed true.
