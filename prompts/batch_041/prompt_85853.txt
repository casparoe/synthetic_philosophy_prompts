I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and in three weeks I'm giving a ninety-minute talk at our joint logic-and-philosophy-of-mathematics seminar on a paper I've been circulating under the title "Closure and Capture: Why Skolem's Paradox Is Relocated Rather Than Dissolved." I know the room fairly well. Most of the audience are model theorists and set theorists from the math department, who regard the paradox as a pedagogical non-issue: they will say that countability was never absolute in the first place, that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem is a theorem about first-order formalizations, and that anyone who finds a countable model of set theory disturbing has confused a model with the world. Two or three are philosophers of mathematics who think the standard dissolution is too quick but who will be equally suspicious of any argument that leans on an intuitive notion of the intended interpretation. One is a logician who works on second-order logic and will raise quasi-categoricity at the earliest opportunity, and there is a historian of the field who will want to know what is actually new here. My thesis is that the standard dissolution conflates two things a theory might be asked to do — constrain which objects the quantifiers range over, which I call domain-fixing, and determine the extension of predicates such as "is countable," which I call extension-fixing — and that our pre-theoretic notion of the uncountable is a domain-fixing notion, whereas any first-order theory can at best extension-fix relative to a model. On my view the paradox is therefore not a confusion to be cleared up but a genuine circularity in the way we fix the interpretation of "all sets," and the familiar appeal to an intended interpretation does not remove the circularity so much as relocate it into the question of what makes a model intended rather than merely correct.
