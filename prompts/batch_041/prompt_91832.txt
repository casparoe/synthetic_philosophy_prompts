I am a junior at a large public high school, and our small philosophy club meets after debate practice; last Thursday I spent most of the meeting watching two of the older members argue about vagueness while the rest of us ate vending-machine pretzels and stayed out of it. The fight started over something much smaller: our school's charter rule says a club needs "a sufficient number of interested students" to be officially recognized, and a friend's film club was turned down with eleven members. One member — call him R — said there is a fact of the matter about whether eleven is sufficient, that the administration's answer is either correct or incorrect and we simply cannot know which, in the same way we cannot know exactly how many hairs a person may have before "bald" stops applying. His position, as I understood it, is that vague words have perfectly sharp boundaries fixed by the messy totality of how our community uses them; that the sorites argument therefore contains a false premise (there really is a last bald man, a last sufficient number of members); and that the premise looks innocent only because we can never discover where the boundary falls. That, he said, is why we should keep classical logic and bivalence and treat vagueness as a kind of ignorance. The other member — call her S — said this is backwards, and that at the borderline there is no fact of the matter at all: "bald" and "sufficient" admit many equally admissible ways of being made precise, a sentence is true only if it comes out true under all of them and false only if false under all of them, and where the cases run out we should say it is neither true nor false.

Then they went back and forth, roughly as follows. (1) S said R's view makes the fate of a single hair a hard fact about the world, which is absurd, and makes all of us systematically mistaken whenever we say someone is neither clearly bald nor clearly not, which would mean we do not understand our own words. (2) R answered that the absurdity is a fact about our psychology rather than about the world, and that S's view has a worse problem: S must say the sorites premise — for every n, if n hairs is bald then n+1 is — is false, yet S cannot point to any particular n at which it fails, so S has a false universal with no false instance; and worse, since at every one of S's admissible sharpenings some cutoff exists, the sentence "there is some number of hairs at which baldness stops" comes out true under all of them, so S is committed to a sharp boundary too, just one that cannot be located. (3) S replied that a boundary at each sharpening is not a boundary simpliciter, that the existential is true only in the thin sense of true-on-every-sharpening, and that super-truth of that kind yields no fact about which number it is, so R's point is verbal; S added that R owes an account of how facts about our usage could fix an exact number — if they do, then someone who surveyed them all could in principle know it, so it is not unknowable, and if they do not, then R has made the cutoff a feature of the world rather than of our language, which is worse. (4) R said there is no reason to think every fact is knowable, and that S's central notion is itself vague: there is no sharp line between a sharpening that respects how we use a word and one that does not, so S's theory of vagueness cannot say of its own key term whether that term has a determinate extension. (5) S said that is merely higher-order vagueness, a genuine phenomenon her theory ought to accommodate, and that R faces the mirror-image problem, since there is no sharp line between a cutoff's being knowable and its being unknowable, so R cannot even state his own thesis sharply.

I would like this sorted out claim by claim rather than settled with a winner. For each numbered move, tell me whether it is right, half-right, or mistaken, and why, being explicit about which parts are claims about logic, which about language, and which about metaphysics. Then tell me which side, if either, has the better of the exchange, what neither of them said that both are missing, and whether they were even answering the same question or were talking past each other. Finally, tell me what the right thing to say actually is about the film club's eleven members.
