Ok I need help getting the Gödel analogy in GEB straight, and I dont want the bedtime-story version.

The bedtime version: Gödel found a way for a formal system to encode statements about itself, so it can crank out a sentence that effectively says "I'm not provable in this system"; if the system could prove it, it'd be wrong, so the sentence is true but unprovable. Hofstadter then says minds pull the same trick — they loop back on themselves — and thats the kernel of self-awareness. Fine, I know that part.

What I want is the actual conceptual hinge, explained gut-level. Here's what I suspect is going on: the analogy quietly needs two different things that keep getting conflated. (a) a system having a fixed point that *mentions* itself — which is all the diagonal construction really buys you — versus (b) a system having a *model of itself* that it can then act on and revise. A Gödel sentence is a frozen fixed point: it makes one claim about its own provability and that's the whole of its life; it can't turn around and edit the axiom set that produced it. So my question is whether the GEB analogy only ever trades on (a), while the thing that would actually matter for a self-aware mind is (b).

Please explain: what the diagonal / fixed-point machinery actually gives you, and why that isnt the same as self-modeling; whether the analogy can be rescued by moving to a system that can revise its own axioms, and what goes wrong when you try (does the incompleteness just reappear one level up, in the meta-system that's doing the revising?); and where the real disagreement sits between people who think the analogy is deep and people who think it's a category error, once we throw out the lazy "minds aren't formal systems" slogan.

Keep it concrete — a toy example of a self-revising system would help a lot, and I'd rather see the actual moving parts than a survey.
