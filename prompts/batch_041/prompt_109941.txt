Bit of an odd ask. I'm a retired chemist (polymers, nothing glamorous) who's spent the last few years reading philosophy, and I've somehow let myself get talked into giving an hour-long session at a meetup of working engineers who build scoring models for insurers and lenders. The topic I picked is the fairness impossibility results: the claim that you can't have a score that's well calibrated in every group and also has the same false positive rate in every group and also the same false negative rate, unless the base rates are equal or the score is perfect.

Don't explain the result to me, I can follow the algebra and I know its right. What I need is a plan for how I explain it, because I've done enough teaching to know the theorem is the easy part. These people will walk in with the vocabulary already (ROC curves, false positives, probably a fairness toolkit they've poked at once) and no structure underneath it, which I think is the worst combination, and I want to know in advance where that goes wrong.

So, what I need from you:

The order. My instinct is that everything hinges on one thing that has to land before the theorem even makes sense, namely that the base rate is a fact about the world while the error rates are facts about the model, and that all the confusion comes from mashing those two together. Tell me if that's really the load-bearing idea or if something else has to come first, then give me the sequence with the dependencies spelled out, why each step can't come before the one it leans on. And at some point I want them to see that the three criteria are the same kind of claim about each other rather than three unrelated rules someone made up, so tell me when that unifying move goes in.

The example. I want a medical one, not the recidivism thing, and I want numbers on the whiteboard that they have to work out themselves. Here's what I've built: a model that flags patients for a follow-up test. At the threshold we choose, in both patient groups it catches 80 percent of the people who actually have the condition and flags 10 percent of the people who don't. Identical error rates. But the condition is four times as common in group A as in group B, 20 percent against 5 percent, a thousand patients in each group, and that difference is real rather than a labeling artifact (say one group is older on average). By my arithmetic, of the people flagged in group B about 3 in 10 actually have it, and in group A about 2 in 3. Check that, and if it holds, tell me the order I should have them compute things in so the punchline arrives as their own discovery rather than my announcement. Also tell me what the example must not look like. My worry is that they take the moral to be that the model is broken or the data is dirty, and then the whole hour is wasted.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. I'm expecting, roughly: so the model is biased, clean the data. Just don't feed it the group label. Then use a different threshold per group, which is true as far as it goes and which I suspect they'll treat as a free lunch. And someone proved which definition of fairness is the correct one and the rest are just wrong. Underneath all of them, the general sense that this is a technical problem with a technical fix. Add what I'm missing, and pay particular attention to what they'll get wrong about calibration specifically, since I keep suspecting that's where the real damage is.

Related, and I want to keep these seperate: I keep tripping over whether the thing my example breaks is calibration proper or only the at-the-threshold version of it, and whether that distinction is worth thirty seconds of the hour or is a distraction I should skip out loud. Advise me.

The closing question. One question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether the structure landed, not whether they memorized a slogan. Something whose answer can't be a definition, and where a wrong answer would actually be informative to me about where they are.

What I say I'm not covering. I'd rather announce the omissions than bluff my way through a question. And I don't want to name theorems or authors anywhere in the hour: if it has a name they'll file it and think they own it, and they'll remember the name wrong anyway.

No slides, whiteboard only, about twenty-five people. Thanks.
