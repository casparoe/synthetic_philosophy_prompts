I'm a staff software engineer, and I run the measurement-and-incentives module in my company's internal data-science bootcamp. The cohort is experienced engineers, analysts, and ML practitioners; almost none of them have any formal philosophy training, and the assessment is closed-book, no internet. I need one final written exercise for the module, and I want it built around an idea I have been floating to the team that I am no longer sure is actually sound.

The idea — I have been calling it the three-role classification — is this. Any metric an organisation optimises stands in one of three relations to the thing the organisation actually cares about. First, the metric can be an estimate of the goal: the goal is real and independently measurable, the metric is a noisy, partial indicator of it, and the failure mode under pressure is statistical, because you select for the top of the metric's range, where the metric-to-goal relationship is weakest, so the average goal value in the selected population falls even though nothing in the world has changed. Second, the metric can be a cause of the goal: response latency is not evidence about whether users are satisfied, it is one of the things that makes them satisfied, so optimising it genuinely produces the goal, and the failure mode is different — you drive it past the range in which the causal link holds, or you buy the metric by spending some other, unmeasured cause. Third, the metric can be a constituent of the goal rather than a sign or a source of it: the number of completed transactions on a marketplace is not evidence of liquidity, it partly is liquidity, and there is no independent instrument that could reveal the metric drifting away from the goal, because there is nothing for it to drift away from. In the first two cases divergence is at least in principle detectable by measuring the goal separately. In the third it is not, and the honest description of the failure is not that the metric stopped tracking the goal but that the metric became the goal.

What I want the exercise to test is whether that classification does any work, or whether it is a distinction I invented that collapses under pressure. I want the question to force two worries out into the open. The first: is the third category genuinely distinct, or is it just what the first category looks like to an analyst who has not yet found the independent measurement — and if it is distinct, what follows about which countermeasures are available (independent audits, dashboards of several metrics, holding the metric fixed while varying the incentive, randomisation)? The second: where do the familiar horror stories fit? The support bot that closes tickets as resolved without resolving anything, the team that meets its coverage target with assertion-free tests, the bounty that produces more rats — some of these look like a metric that was a bad estimate, some like a metric that was a real cause and got overdriven, and some like agents responding strategically to the fact that they are being measured. I suspect that last one is not a fourth category but a cross-cutting thing that can happen to any of the three, and I want the question to make students commit on that.

Anchor the question in concrete cases rather than abstractions. Use two, chosen so that they land in different categories of the classification: an AI support assistant whose success is measured by ticket deflection rate, and a two-sided marketplace whose health is measured by completed transactions per week. Students should have to commit to a category for each case, say what that category predicts about how the metric will fail as optimisation pressure increases, and then say which countermeasure the classification licenses in each case and why the two answers differ. The second half of the question should make them turn on the classification itself.

Constraints on what you write. No citations, no author names, no paper titles — the students cannot look anything up, and the question should not need them to. The question must not be answerable by reciting the well-known slogan about measures ceasing to be good measures once they become targets, and it must not be answerable by listing the standard four-way taxonomy from the ML-safety literature (statistical selection, extreme tails, causal decoupling, adversarial gaming). If a student could get full marks by reproducing either of those, the question has failed; assume they already know both. You do not need to tell me whether my classification already has a name in the literature. Format: one essay question, 90 minutes, 25 marks, no credit for mentioning any particular author or result. The scoring should reward students who can locate where the classification breaks, not students who can restate it.

Please give me: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so that a strong student has to take a position on the classification rather than summarise it; (2) a model answer of roughly 700–800 words that would earn full marks, written as a student would write it rather than as a lecture; (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a top answer must make, assigns marks to each, states explicitly what separates an A from a B answer, and gives a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs marks at each point.
