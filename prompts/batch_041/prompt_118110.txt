For a graduate seminar on teleology in the philosophy of biology, I want a specification before we begin comparing accounts. Please write the desiderata for a theory of free-floating rationales — the rationales that, on Dennett's picture, can attach to a trait or behaviour without being represented by any mind that holds them. Work from these cases: the cuckoo chick that evicts its foster siblings; the killdeer's broken-wing display; the ventilation logic of a termite mound; the sphex wasp that re-drags a cricket it has already positioned, running its routine as a rigid script; the hoverfly whose wasp-like bands buy it protection from predators that have learned to avoid wasps.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I especially want the distinctions the theory must not collapse: between a rationale and a mere cause; between a rationale for the type and for the token; between selection-based rationales and ones underwritten by learning or computation; between teleonomy and full-blooded Aristotelian teleology; and between a rationale that is normative in the robust sense and one that is merely statistical or functional. Then the results it must be consistent with: that selection is non-purposive, that a free-floating rationale can be defeated or obsolete (the wasp again), and that not every beneficial causal regularity earns a rationale, on pain of the notion becoming vacuous.

Flag any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. My suspicion is that the demand for genuine normativity sits badly with the demand that no subject be required for whom the rationale is a reason, and I want to know whether that tension is fatal or simply the theory's central problem. Finally, name the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise attractive positions.
