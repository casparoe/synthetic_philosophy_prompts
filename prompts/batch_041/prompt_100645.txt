I teach a seminar on democratic backsliding and want to close the term with Sinclair Lewis's 1935 novel It Can't Happen Here as a literary case. I have been using the standard scholarly vocabulary — backsliding as distinct from breakdown, executive aggrandizement, competitive authoritarianism, constitutional retrogression — and I want a reading of the novel that takes both the fiction and that vocabulary seriously, rather than treating the book as a period piece about European fascism. Please write the following sections, in this order, in British English, and do not quote the novel verbatim or supply citations; work from what is commonly known about its plot.

The concept (about 300 words). State, as tightly as you can, what the contemporary concept of democratic backsliding actually commits us to: how it is distinguished from democratic breakdown and from a classical coup, what its characteristic mechanisms are, and where the concept is genuinely contested — in particular the worry that it amounts to a threshold on an index, or a description of actors' intentions, or something closer to a partisan label.

The novel as a case (about 500 words). Read Windrip's rise and rule against that concept. Which features of the book's trajectory fit the diagnosis — an elected demagogue, mass acquiescence, the sidelining of Congress, the corporatist capture of the state — and which do not: the speed of the transformation, the open illegality, the armed paramilitary "Minute Men", the self-coup? Be concrete about which scenes or episodes carry the weight, and say where the American setting matters.

Where the novel resists the concept (about 350 words). Where does the book sit awkwardly with a theory that has taught us to look for gradualism and legalistic erosion? Is it, on the scholarship's own terms, a story of breakdown rather than backsliding — and if so, does that mean the novel is simply the wrong case for the concept, or does it expose a blind spot in a framework that may have overcorrected against the coup? If it helps, say briefly how the book's trajectory would be classified alongside the real cases I also teach (Hungary, Poland, Turkey, the United States).

What the novel adds (about 250 words). Finally, say what the fiction can show about the phenomenon that the scholarship, with its indices and typologies, cannot — the texture of complicity, the ordinary reasons people go along, the way a regime feels from inside — and whether that should change how I teach the concept.

Keep the whole thing analytic rather than appreciative. I want to hand it to the seminar as a model of reading a text and a concept against each other, and I would rather it end by naming what the novel gets right that the theory misses than by hedging.
