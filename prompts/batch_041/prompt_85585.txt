I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've got a thing that started as a pub argument and is now refusing to leave me alone. Every day a few billion of us perform the same tiny ritual — tick the box, pick out the traffic lights, or just sit there while the page decides about us without asking anything at all — and Google's own help page for the box cheerfully calls it "a turing test to tell human and bots apart," adding that if you get the green checkmark you've "passed our robot test (yes, it's that easy)." Alongside that there's the newer wave of proof-of-personhood outfits — the crypto project whose Orb photographs your iris so it can hand you a credential as a unique human, and the ones now sniffing around face scans and passports instead — which seem to have given up on behaviour altogether and gone looking for the human in the body. My brother-in-law, who does fraud prevention for a bank, insists all of this is just security engineering and has nothing to do with philosophy, which is precisely the claim I want tested, because I'm definately writing this one up.

So take the whole apparatus — the squiggly-word puzzles (some of which, I gather, were quietly doing double duty as free labelling work for the systems that later learned to solve them), the silent risk scores, the hidden fields, the checkbox, the iris orb — and reconstruct the picture of mind, or of humanity, that it only makes sense under. It looks Turing-ish at the front door, but I want to know how much of the behavioural criterion actually survives contact with the design. Is the thing being measured still a capacity, or has it quietly become provenance and typicality — where you came from, whether you move like the median user, whether you can be counted as exactly one person? Does it presuppose that humanness is a stable, measurable property of a single body, and which rival pictures does that rule out — minds spread across systems, or units of analysis that aren't individuals? Where the parts seem to pull against each other (a chatbot passing a conversation test is a milestone in one corner of the industry and a security hole to be patched in another), tell me whether that's a genuine tension or just two different jobs wearing the same word. And keep the diagnosis honest: separate what the design strictly requires from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I don't want to pin a metaphysics on a widget that never signed up for one — then tell me what the practice would have to look like if you took the presupposed view seriously, or threw it out.

Two housekeeping things. Write it in American English, it's for a US outlet. And please keep the settled bits seperate from the live arguments among people who actually work on this stuff — if the honest answer to some part of it is "specialists split into these camps," say that rather than handing me a contested reading as the consensus. I'd rather file something defensible than something tidy.
