"There are three ways to think about probability and they disagree about nearly everything. The frequentist says a probability just is a long-run relative frequency: flip the coin forever and the proportion of heads settles down to one half, and that limit just is the probability. The subjectivist says a probability is a degree of belief and nothing more, so there is no fact of the matter about whether your credence is correct, only about whether it's coherent -- which is why for a subjectivist anything goes as long as you don't contradict yourself; the only discipline comes from a theorem that everyone's opinions converge as the evidence piles up, so in the long run where you started doesn't matter. The propensity theorist says probability is a real property of the setup, a disposition to produce outcomes, a bit like the fragility of a glass is a disposition to break: the coin's one-half propensity is what makes the long-run frequency come out that way and not the other way round. The three part company most sharply over the single case. Only the propensity view gives a single toss of a coin a probability, since the frequentist has no sequence to look at and the subjectivist has no belief to consult yet. And the frequentist is the one stuck with the reference class problem: to say what the probability of anything is, he has to say which class to put it in, and there's no principled way to choose, which is why the whole view is in trouble."

I'm fairly confident the frequentist bit is at least in the right neighbourhood, and I have a nagging feeling the subjectivist bit is a cheap shot but I couldn't say why, which is embarassing.
