For about a year now I've been running what my team calls a decomposition process. When we face a decision too large to argue about directly — whether to enter a market, whether to kill a product line — we break it into a handful of sub-questions, hand each sub-question to a different person, and tell those people not to think about the larger decision, only about the piece in front of them. The instruction is the point of the exercise: if the person answering whether demand is large enough starts reasoning about market entry as a whole, their answer is contaminated by their prior about what we should do. So we keep them in the dark, collect clean local answers, and reassemble.

The pattern I keep hitting is this. In January we sliced the market question one way — is the market big enough, can we build the thing, would we make money on it — and every sub-answer came back defensible, and the assembly said go. In June a different pair of us sliced what was recognizably the same question another way — does it fit our strategy, what else could we do with the same engineers, how would it look to our existing customers — and every sub-answer was again defensible, and the assembly said no. Nobody was sloppy in either round. Walking the two slicings sub-question by sub-question, you cannot locate the error, because there isn't one: the sub-questions are different questions, and each was answered well. A friend had the same thing happen with a personal decision — she cut it into money, colleagues, and city, her partner cut it into growth, risk, and family, and the two assemblies pointed opposite ways while every component judgment was one either of them would endorse. I've also noticed that when someone answers the big question directly, without slicing, their answer tends to line up with whichever slicing they would have chosen, which makes me suspect the slicing is quietly doing the work even though nothing in the process ever treats the slicing as part of the answer.

Is there a name for this? The nearest things I can think of don't fit. The fallacy of composition is about inferring a property of a whole from a property of its parts; here the parts are fine and each assembly is fine on its own terms, and the trouble is that there are two of them. The heap paradox is about a long sequence of individually negligible steps; here there are only a few steps and none is negligible. The discursive dilemma is about aggregating consistent individual judgments into an inconsistent group judgment; here there is no aggregation over people who were all asked the same question — there are different questions, answered by different people, or by the same person at different times. I have also seen this arrangement described, in discussions of AI systems that answer hard questions by recursive decomposition, as a virtue rather than a defect, on the grounds that an answerer who cannot see the whole cannot bias the whole; what I want to know is whether the vice I'm describing has been named, and if it hasn't, what it should be called. What I need from a name is a carving, not a label: exactly what the phenomenon is (that the top-level answer is a function of an unrecorded choice, or that each sub-answer silently imports assumptions about how it connects upward, or something else), how it differs from the nearest named phenomena, including the ones I've listed, whether hiding the top-level question is essential to it or merely one way of producing it, and where the boundary lies — what kind of decomposition is stable, so that re-slicing the same question returns the same answer.
