I chair the selection panel for an annual prize given by a mid-sized arts foundation: one award, one category (prose fiction), seven panelists, a single two-day meeting, and a public citation of no more than 150 words. About a third of the panel in any given year are distinguished practitioners rather than critics, with strong and idiosyncratic tastes; the rest are critics, editors, or booksellers. The board wants a defensible process on the record, and the shortlisted publishers are not shy about challenging it.

I have read Hume's essay on the standard of taste, and I think it is the most useful thing anyone has written about what a committee like mine is actually doing. But it stops precisely where I need it to start: it tells me that the verdict of suitably qualified judges is the standard, sketches the qualifications those judges need, and then leaves me to run the meeting. I want you to turn it into a procedure I can actually run. I don't want a reading list or a summary of the essay; I want an operational document.

It has to do the following work. First, a rule for composing the panel and for weighting its members, given that the board also insists the panel be representative of the foundation's public, which pulls against the idea of the rare qualified judge. Second, a calibration step before any candidate work is discussed — something that exposes each panelist's scale by having them rank a fixed set of benchmark works, so that disagreement about the shortlist isn't confused with disagreement about the scale. Third, an order of operations for the two days: what happens before scoring, what is discussed before scores are visible, what after. Fourth, a way of telling a genuine perception from a mere preference when a panelist dissents — I want something like an audit in which the dissenter must name the specific feature of the work responsible for their judgment, and the panel must then establish whether that feature is really there, while leaving its weight open. Fifth, the aggregation rule, plus an override rule: state when a single panelist's dissent should defeat a majority, and when a majority should defeat a dissenter whose perception has been confirmed. Sixth, a step for works that are not wholly the candidate's own — translations above all, since we regularly shortlist translated novels and none of us reads the source language, but also collaborations and posthumous completions. Seventh, an explicit statement of when the procedure should return no verdict and hand the decision back to me as a matter of institutional judgment rather than taste.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work at each step. Case one: a novel three panelists rank first and two rank last, the two calling it sentimental; both dissenters, independently, name the same specific structural feature — a late resolution that settles by coincidence a moral problem the book spent four hundred pages setting up — which the other five concede is really there. Case two: a widely praised translated novel, where the translator is herself a celebrated writer and the panel cannot assess the prose because none of them reads the original. Case three: a formally difficult book four panelists find unreadable and one defends on the ground that it will be recognized as important in twenty years. At least one of your verdicts should be one I will not like; say so rather than softening it. Tell me also which of the three the procedure cannot decide, and why.

Finally, the honest limits. If Hume's account cannot be turned into a procedure of this kind — if everything below the level of "recruit good judges and let them argue" is decoration — say that instead of dressing it up. If any step depends on a reading of Hume that is contested among people who know the text better than I do, name the step and say what the disagreement is; I would rather see where I am standing on a contestable interpretation than have it buried. And if the procedure will misfire systematically in a case I haven't thought of, name the case.

Format the answer as follows. Open with one paragraph stating what the procedure tries to preserve from Hume and what it deliberately drops. Then give the procedure itself as a table with exactly these columns: Step number | The question to ask | What counts as a pass | What to do on a fail | Judgment call? (yes/no). Then the three worked cases as a table with these columns: Case | Verdict the procedure returns | Deciding step | Rests on a judgment call? | Confidence (low/medium/high) | Would I accept it? After the tables, give a single JSON object with these fields: "procedure_name" (string), "n_steps" (integer), "hard_fail_steps" (array of step numbers), "judgment_call_steps" (array of step numbers), "cases" (array of objects, each with "case_id", "verdict", "deciding_step", "rests_on_judgment_call" as a boolean, and "confidence"), "known_limits" (array of strings), and "contested_hume_reading" (string, or null if there is none).
