I have no background in philosophy. I came across the puzzle about the word grue on my own — I believe it is Goodman's — and it has been sitting with me for weeks. As I understand it, the word is defined so that it applies to a thing if the thing is green and we look at it before some fixed future date, and to a thing that is blue if we look at it only after that date. Every emerald anyone has ever looked at, being green, is also grue, so the two words have picked out exactly the same things so far; and yet "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" strike me as entirely different claims, and I cannot find the difference in anything I have actually observed. When I try to say why one is sane and the other absurd, everything I come up with turns on habit, or on the fact that one of the words is a word we already had, and that seems to me too small a thing for so large a difference to rest on.

What I want is a poem that carries this, with the form doing the work rather than the poem explaining the argument. Specifically:

A pantoum: sixteen lines in four quatrains, in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and the poem ends by returning to the line it began with. I want that form for a reason — in it the past is literally carried forward into the future, and the same words have to do a different job when they come back.

Let the subject be something ordinary and countable: a bird at a feeder, a kettle, the ice on a pond, someone who has never yet let the speaker down. Anything but emeralds — the emerald is the textbook's example, and I want this to land on a reader who has never heard of the puzzle.

The poem must contain a word of its own invention, defined within the poem itself (a clumsy line or two of definition is fine; I would rather it be clear than smooth), whose application to everything the speaker has actually witnessed is indistinguishable from the ordinary word's, and which parts company with it only about what comes after a marked moment — a date, an anniversary, a from now on. The poem needs that marker in it somewhere.

The line the poem returns to at the end must be the line that forks. Read one way it licenses one prediction about what happens next; read the other way, the opposite prediction; and the poem must not tell me which reading to take. Nothing in the wording should change between the two readings — only which word I hear as doing the work.

No jargon: not induction, not hypothesis, not evidence, not projectible, not entrenchment, and no explaining the puzzle inside the poem. The reader should finish it without knowing that it is a puzzle, and should feel the vertigo anyway.

The symmetry is the part I keep returning to: had the language started with the strange word, the ordinary word would be the strange one. If the poem can leave the reader holding that, without ever saying it, I would be glad.

After the poem, add a note of no more than three or four sentences telling me which line does which work and exactly where the two readings divide, so that I can check I have not misunderstood the puzzle and that the poem has not blurred it.

I am not asking the poem to settle whether our expectations are reasonable. That is the part I cannot settle myself, and I would rather it stopped where I stop.
