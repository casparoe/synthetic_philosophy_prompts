I teach a seminar on social choice and mechanism design for roughly twenty participants — upper-level undergraduates with a few graduate students — in a single ninety-minute session, and I want to run a governance simulation that makes them feel both the appeal and the fragility of settling policy by prediction market rather than by vote: the arrangement, often called futarchy, in which a community votes on the ends it wants and then lets conditional markets settle the factual question of which means will achieve them. The activity should make the group perform the values step and the beliefs step as two separate acts before anyone names the distinction, so that the separation registers as something they did rather than something they were told.

Staging. Invent a small municipality facing one decision, and hand the group a slate of three candidate objectives to choose among — say, cutting serious road injuries, cutting average commute time, or cutting particulate emissions. A majority vote fixes which objective counts, and the losers must then use it. Do not reveal that this is the values half of the design. Then present two mutually exclusive proposals, described in enough mechanical detail that they plausibly bear on whichever objective was chosen.

Materials. A visible price board or order book for two conditional assets; a ledger of play money; a one-page payout table; role cards; and a stack of private information cards, some carrying genuine signal about how each proposal would move the objective and some deliberately uninformative. No real money.

The market. Each participant gets an endowment. The two assets are A and B: a share in A pays a fixed sum if proposal A is adopted and the objective then improves past a stated threshold, and a share in B pays the same on the parallel condition for B. Participants trade freely for twenty minutes. At the close, the market's implied conditional values decide which proposal is adopted — the higher-priced asset's proposal wins. Build in three features deliberately, and do not explain them in advance. First, the payout is conditional on adoption, so each price encodes both the chance the proposal is adopted and the chance it works, and the group will have to work out how to divide one out of the other. Second, because adoption is itself read off the prices, the market is self-referential: a bet that A works also shifts the probability that A is adopted. Third, brief one participant privately as a large holder whose own values favor A, and give that person enough capital to move the price if they choose. Midway through trading, slip one late information card into play and see whether the prices absorb it.

Debrief. Map the phases onto the idea. The objective vote is the values half; the market is the beliefs half, and the point of the split is that people who disagree about ends need not disagree about means. Walk through the normalization problem — how to extract a belief about efficacy from a price that also prices adoption — and then the circularity, which is the part most treatments skate over: when the market is the decision rule, there is no clean fact of the matter for it to track, only a fixed point. Take up the whale. Ask whether the attempted manipulation was self-defeating: pushing A's price up to secure adoption also means buying an asset whose payoff, if A is bad, will not materialize, so the manipulation costs money — unless the manipulator values the policy intrinsically more than the loss, in which case it is not self-defeating at all. Close on the two load-bearing assumptions: that the market is liquid and manipulation-resistant, and that the chosen objective cannot be gamed by whoever is measured against it.

What will go wrong. Expect a large fraction to misread the conditional payout as paying out merely on adoption; pre-print a payout table and work one example aloud. Expect thin trading, with everyone holding and prices frozen; seed an opening quote or designate a market maker. Expect the whale to be either too timid to matter or so obvious that the group stops trading honestly; brief the whale to act on their card's instructions and no more. Expect the objective vote to consume the time you budgeted for the market.

What it cannot reproduce. The market has twenty traders, so it cannot exhibit the informational efficiency on which the whole proposal rests; it may well exhibit the opposite, and you must say so rather than let the group conclude that markets fail. The metric's later measurement is stipulated by you rather than discovered, so the truth the market is supposed to track is handed to it, which begs part of the question. And the activity cannot touch the political-economy problem of who sets the objective and the threshold in the first place — you will have to supply that.
