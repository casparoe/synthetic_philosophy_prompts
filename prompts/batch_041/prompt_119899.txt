I'm setting the final for a grad seminar in formal epistemology. The back third of the course was probability kinematics — updating on uncertain evidence, the rigidity condition, the diachronic Dutch book defence of Jeffrey's rule — and I want a single long essay question on it, worth 20 marks, that they get an hour for. I don't want "state Jeffrey's rule and discuss the rigidity assumption"; I want them to have to actually run the rule and then work out what they've learned from running it. So here's the case I've built. Four states, e1 through e4, prior probabilities .1, .2, .3, .4. Two binary partitions: E = {e1, e2} versus not-E, and F = {e1, e3} versus not-F. The student's evidence arrives as two seperate Jeffrey-style shifts: the first moves P(E) to .5 while holding fixed the conditional probabilities of the four states within E and within not-E; the second moves P(F) to .6, again holding the within-partition conditionals fixed. I've allready checked that if you apply these in one order and then the other you get two different final distributions. I want the question to make them do both orders, notice the divergence, and then figure out what it does and doesn't show. My own reading is that the interesting bit isn't just "the rule isn't commutative" — it's that each ordering satisfies the constraint it applied last and lets the earlier one drift, so the pair of evidential inputs doesn't determine an output at all until you say which came first. There's also a student in my office hours who's convinced this proves the rule is straight-up incoherent, and I want the question to force a verdict on that.

So: please write the exam question itself, broken into parts with marks attached that add up to 20, phrased the way it'd appear on the paper, then a model answer of around 800-900 words, then a marking scheme. In the model answer I want the two final distributions computed explicitly for both orders, to the third decimal, and a flag if my claim that they differ turns out to be wrong. I want the marking scheme to name the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific moves that cap someone at a B — in particular I'd like the A standard to require something about what a rule that treats the two uncertain observations as simultaneous constraints would have to look like, and whether that rule is forced on us, and the B standard to include the answer that just says "Jeffrey's rule is order-dependent, therefore defective" and stops there. The last part should reward anyone who notices that the two orders don't even agree on which of the two original constraints the final distribution satisfies.
