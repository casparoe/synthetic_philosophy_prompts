I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am at work on a piece about the rhetoric of contagion in the debate over online falsehood. The frame I keep reaching for, and the one my sources keep handing back to me, is that a viral falsehood is a virus: a piece of content that commandeers attention, replicates through shares, mutates as it travels, and against which a population can be inoculated. On that picture, platform policy becomes public health — quarantine the superspreading post, inoculate the audience before it is exposed, contact-trace the shares — and the vocabulary does a great deal of the persuading. A historian of science I trust told me the analogy is doing the arguing rather than supporting it. Before I file, I want an audit rather than either a defence or a dismissal.

First, the correspondence, made explicit: what plays the role of the pathogen, the host, the vector, the receptor, the immune response, the incubation period, the reproduction number. Then the places the mapping fails, sorted by whether the failure matters for the policy conclusion I want to draw or is merely picturesque. Several strike me as possibly load-bearing, and I would like your judgement on each: that the host of a virus is passive, whereas the sharer of a claim is an agent with reasons; that a virus harms the host it commandeers, whereas a falsehood usually harms third parties; that a virus has a genome and a capsid, so that copying and mutation are well defined, whereas an idea has no crisp boundary and its every retelling is an interpretation; that transmission and virulence trade off in pathogens, and it is unclear what the corresponding trade-off would be for a claim; and that exposure to a pathogen tends to produce immunity, whereas exposure to a claim tends to produce familiarity, which is closer to the opposite.

I would also like the two source models my sources keep sliding between held apart: the Darwinian meme, a replicator passed on with variation, and the virus, a pathogen that commandeers a host. Tell me which one is carrying each step of the argument, and whether the slide between them is doing work that neither could do alone.

Finally, which features of the source domain get imported without argument — that the item is harmful by nature, that it has interests of its own, that a population's exposure is a matter for authorities rather than for speakers — and does the public-health conclusion follow from the mapping, or was it smuggled in with it? If the virus frame cannot bear the weight, say what would carry it better: a different biological model, such as an invasive species or an environmental toxin; a market or ecological model; or an account that drops the pathogen frame altogether. Write for a general reader, in plain prose, and assume I know the standard debate over memetics well enough that you need not rehearse it.
