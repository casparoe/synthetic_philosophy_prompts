I am a novelist, and my new book concerns a fictional state whose sentencing commission adopts an actuarial instrument that scores each defendant from 0 to 1 for the probability of re-arrest within three years. I also teach a graduate seminar on quantification and judgment, and I am writing the final examination now. I want one essay question, ninety minutes, twenty marks, that my students cannot answer by reciting the impossibility result they will have met in the readings.

The scenario is the one in the novel. The commission's enabling statute binds the instrument to three requirements at once: the score must be calibrated within each of the two population groups the state's data distinguish, so that a score of 0.3 means a 30 per cent chance of re-arrest in either group; the average score of those who do not re-arrest must be the same in both groups; and the average score of those who do re-arrest must be the same in both groups. In the state's data the three-year re-arrest rate is 40 per cent in Group A and 20 per cent in Group B. The statute defines the outcome as re-arrest, because re-offence cannot be observed.

Please give me the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth twenty marks in total, each with a one-line note on the error that typically costs a candidate that point. The question should oblige the students to establish for themselves, from the three requirements and the two rates, that the requirements cannot all hold together, and to identify any limiting case in which they could; it should then ask which requirement they would surrender and on what grounds, and finally what becomes of the whole argument if the measured outcome is only a proxy for what the commission actually cares about. The students can do algebra and have read the standard material; they may not use notes, and the question should require no citations.
