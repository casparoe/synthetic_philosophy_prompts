I have been following the long-running argument over whether violence has declined across human history, and I keep coming away with the feeling that the two sides are not really arguing with each other. On one side, people point to homicide rates, battlefield deaths, and estimates of prehistoric lethal conflict, adjust for population, and conclude that the world has gotten dramatically less violent. On the other side, critics say the numbers are unreliable before the modern period, that "violence" gets narrowed to direct physical killing while famine, structural deprivation, and slow institutional harms get quietly excluded, and that a per-capita measure can fall while the absolute number of victims rises. I have read a fair amount of this back and forth and neither side ever seems to move. What I want is not a survey and not a bibliography -- I do not need a reading list or citations, and you should not guess at sources. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, grounded in the strongest version of each side's position rather than a straw man. Is it a hidden empirical question that better data would settle, a disagreement about what counts as evidence at all, a verbal dispute over how the word "violence" is being used, two groups answering different questions under one name, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? I have a working hypothesis that it is mostly two different questions wearing the same label, but I would rather be told I am wrong than have that confirmed. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction, a better dataset, a formal result, a change of question -- or, if nothing would, say so and explain why. Flag anything in how I have framed the disagreement that is itself confused. Please end with a single-sentence statement of your bottom line.
