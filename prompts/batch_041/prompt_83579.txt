I have been sitting in on a joint seminar run by the philosophy department and the behavioural economics group on weakness of will, and after a term I am struck by how little the two halves of the room have to say to one another. The philosophers take up the old puzzle: how it is possible for someone to judge, all things considered, that she should get up and run at six, and then at six not get up — whether that is a failure of will, of attention, or of self-control, whether it shows the judgment was never genuinely all-things-considered, or whether the old denial that anyone knowingly acts against what they judge best might be right after all. The economists point to the familiar laboratory pattern — subjects preferring a larger reward later over a smaller reward sooner when both are distant, and reversing that preference as the sooner option draws near — and describe it as present bias, a discount function steeper over short horizons than long ones. Nobody in the room disputes the behaviour; the philosophers treat it as a puzzle about agency, the economists treat it as a settled feature of preference. Each week the discussion ends in mutual incomprehension, with one side convinced the other is trading in unfalsifiable mentalistic talk and the other convinced it is being offered a redescription of the phenomenon in place of an explanation. A colleague in the economics group tells me akrasia is simply hyperbolic discounting; a friend who works on action theory says that is a category mistake.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that most of the apparent conflict is an artefact of two different theoretical posits: the economist's preference, individuated by choice, and the philosopher's judgment, a normative attitude that claims authority over action. If that is so, the same choice data will be compatible with both readings, and neither side is asserting anything the other could refute. But I suspect a genuine residual remains, and I want you to locate it precisely. One candidate is whether the agent who chooses at eight in the evening and the agent who fails at six in the morning are one agent whose valuations have changed or two agents whose interests diverge — a real metaphysical and normative question, not a verbal one. Another is whether the time-inconsistent pattern is a failure of practical rationality at all, or merely a different but defensible structure of concern for one's own future.

Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says and the concrete cases each takes as data, and be honest about which parts of the dispute are merely terminological. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. In particular: is the residual disagreement empirical, and if so, could any experiment or measure distinguish the reading on which the agent's valuation changed from the reading on which her valuation held and her execution failed — or do the two readings predict identical choice data, so that the question can only be settled by deciding what we are entitled to posit? If you conclude that part of the debate cannot be moved by evidence and should not be expected to converge, say so and explain why. I would rather have a precise account of the structure of the disagreement than a survey of the positions, and I do not need a reading list.
