I have been defending a heterodox position in the mental causation debate for some years, and I now want a candid audit of how much it can actually bear. The position is this. The exclusion argument, as it is ordinarily run, is a red herring, because it assumes that a mental property and its physical realizer are rivals competing for one and the same causal role. They are not rivals. A property is causally efficacious precisely when it is interventionally autonomous: when there exists at least one possible intervention that varies that property while holding its physical realizer fixed, and that variation makes a difference to some downstream effect. Where the mental passes that test, it is a cause in the fullest sense; where it fails the test, it is not a cause at all. The completeness of physics, on this account, is beside the point — nothing about the causal closure of the physical world bears on whether a higher-level property earns its causal keep.

Please build the strongest case against this, playing it straight rather than charitably. I want the objections ranked, from those that would be fatal to those that are merely awkward, with an explanation of the ranking rather than a mere list. I also want you to mark, for each, whether it is something I ought to concede as a defender of the view or something only a critic would press — I am not interested in a catalogue of complaints that presuppose an account of causation I have already rejected.

In working through this, address at least the following, though do not treat the list as exhaustive. First, whether the autonomy criterion is self-undermining: if mental properties strongly supervene on physical ones, no intervention could in principle vary the mental while holding its realizer fixed, in which case the criterion would not secure mental causation but abolish it. Second, whether the criterion over-generates — whether it would confer causal standing on gerrymandered, disjunctive, or merely conventional properties, and thereby reinstate the sprawling overdetermination problem the view was meant to dissolve. Third, whether tying efficacy to possible interventions makes causation a feature of our representational practices rather than of the world, and if so whether that consequence is a reductio or a tolerable deflationism. Fourth, whether the view buys token causation at the price of the type-level causal claims that psychology and psychiatry actually require.

Finally, tell me whether the insistence that closure is beside the point insulates the position from any conceivable evidence, and if it does, whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a metaphysical thesis. For my own part, I would be most moved by an actual, non-thought-experimental case in which a mental property and its physical realizer diverge in their difference-making profiles; if you think no such case can arise, say so and explain why.
