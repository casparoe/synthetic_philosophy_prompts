ok so i'm a third-year phil phd and my decision theory reading group wants everyone to circulate a one-page "here's where i actually stand" doc, and i want a consistency pass on mine before i send it round. it's a rough list, no particular order:

(1) expectationalism, full stop — the rational choice is the one with the highest expected value, and i dont think risk aversion is ever rationally *required*. when someone says they're risk averse about money, that's just concave utility doing its work, not some extra rational primitive sitting alongside expected value.

(2) no ceiling on welfare. there's no best possible life and no best possible outcome; utility is unbounded above.

(3) totalism plus longtermism. what matters is total welfare, and the expected value of the far future swamps everything near-term, so most of our resources should go into shaping the long run.

(4) but i do NOT accept Pascal's mugging. handing over my wallet because some guy claims he'll torture me unless i pay, on a probability i basically invented, is not something i'll do, and i think any theory that tells me to pay is broken rather than me being irrational. i'm not a fanatic and i dont think fanaticism is a real cost of the view.

(5) i also think every well-posed decision problem has a determinate rational answer — some price that's the right price, even if it's infinite or a limit. "it depends on your utility function" is a non-answer, and "there's no fact of the matter" is worse.

(6) and yet i'm pretty risk averse about my own money: i buy insurance, and i wont take a 50/50 on my rent money even when it's +EV in dollars.

can you tell me which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and rank the real ones by how bad they are? for each genuine one, what's the cheapest thing that has to give, and what do i lose by giving it? and if i only get to change one of these, which single change buys me the most consistency — and what does it quietly cost me elsewhere? be specific; i dont want "these are all in tension to varying degrees."
