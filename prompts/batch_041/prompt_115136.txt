For years the veil-of-ignorance argument for utilitarianism struck me as close to decisive, and I was relieved to learn that it is not merely a hand-wave — there is a theorem underneath it. But when I finally wrote the theorem out as an explicit chain of premises, it stopped looking like a proof of utilitarianism and started looking like a proof of too much, and I cannot tell whether my reconstruction is faulty or whether the theorem genuinely overreaches.

The setup. Two people, Ann and Bob, each with a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function over risky prospects, unique up to positive affine transformation and interpersonally comparable. A social prospect is a lottery over outcomes, where an outcome fixes each person's utility level.

The premises.

(1) Welfarism: the social ranking of outcomes is a function of the individuals' utility levels and of nothing else.

(2) Social expected utility: the social preference relation over social prospects is complete, transitive, continuous, and satisfies the independence (sure-thing) axiom — that is, society ranks lotteries the way a rational expected-utility maximizer does.

(3) Weak Pareto: if every individual weakly prefers prospect A to prospect B, and at least one strictly prefers it, then society strictly prefers A to B.

(4) Impartiality: the social ranking of prospects coincides with the ranking an individual would endorse if he did not know which member of society he would turn out to be and treated every position as equally likely.

The consequence. (1)–(3) already force the social ranking of prospects to be a weighted sum of individual utilities — what I take to be Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Adding (4) pins the weights to be equal, so the social ranking is fixed by the unweighted total of individual utilities and by nothing else.

The case that bothers me.

(5) Ann and Bob have equal claims on a single indivisible good — one course of a treatment that will fully restore whoever receives it. Prospect A gives it to Ann for certain. Prospect B awards it by a fair coin.

(6) On the premises, A and B carry the same total utility and the same impartial expected utility, so society must be indifferent between them; anyone who insists that the fair lottery is better than the certain award to Ann is making a mistake about value.

(7) But B is better than A. When two people have equal claims and only one can be helped, the lottery respects the claims and the certain award does not. Preferring B is not a confusion; it is the correct verdict.

(8) So the premises contain an error.

I would give up almost anything before (3): the thought that a change which is worse for nobody and better for somebody is worse overall seems to me the last thing that should go, and I am inclined to say the same about (1). If I had to bet, I would blame (4) — the veil-of-ignorance machinery has always felt like sleight of hand — but I cannot see how to keep (2) and still reach the verdict in (7), and I am not confident I am protecting the right premises.

So: which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? If it is (2), tell me what a social preference relation looks like once it is no longer an expected-utility relation: can it still rank policies, does it stay transitive, and what becomes of the ordinary practice of evaluating social prospects by expected welfare? If it is (4), say what the impartiality requirement is actually doing, what I lose by dropping it, and what verdict it leaves me with in the case above. If I am wrong to protect (3) or (1), tell me what I have been paying to keep them. And if the honest answer is that (7) itself is the mistake, say so and explain why the intuition is so seductive. In every case I want to know why the offending step looked plausible in the first place, because that is the part I keep failing to reconstruct on my own.
