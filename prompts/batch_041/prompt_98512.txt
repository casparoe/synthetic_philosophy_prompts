For the past three years I have sat as a lay member on the ethics panel of a small British fertility charity that advises couples who are considering surrogacy or egg donation, and next week we must decide how far we can properly help a couple who have come to us about an arrangement abroad. Our panel has drifted into a fairly settled pattern, though I have never been able to say what principle, if any, it expresses. We agreed to help a couple who used a surrogate here in Britain, where she was reimbursed her expenses and nothing more; we refused to help a couple who wished to engage a commercial surrogate in a state where that is lawful, because the woman concerned was in genuine financial difficulty and had been offered a large fee; we agreed to help a couple obtain eggs from an unpaid donor through a regulated clinic in Spain; and we refused to assist with an American egg-donation package that was advertised to students and offered a considerable sum to young women who appeared to need the money. Looking back, I think the same two considerations were doing the work in every case — whether money changed hands beyond the woman's costs, and how vulnerable the woman providing the eggs or the womb appeared to be — but I cannot tell which of these our decisions actually track, or whether they are really two considerations or only one wearing two hats.

The new case is this. A woman in a state where commercial surrogacy is lawful has offered, cheerfully and entirely on her own initiative, to carry a child for the couple for a substantial fee, which she says she wants in order to fund a business of her own; she is articulate, comfortably off, fully informed, has her own lawyer, and the arrangement is lawful and enforceable where she lives. What should we decide if we mean to stay consistent with our own past decisions? Which of our earlier cases is the closest precedent, and on what feature does the resemblance really turn? If our record genuinely underdetermines the answer, I would rather see the competing principles set out — what each of them makes of money changing hands, of vulnerability, and of the difference between the two — together with what each predicts about this woman and what further case would force us to choose between them, than be told simply that commercial surrogacy is, or is not, exploitation.
