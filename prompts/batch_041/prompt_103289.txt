I'm building a unit on the shape of history and this one keeps me up. Take the Soviet Union, the whole arc, 1917 to the 1991 dissolution. I want you to read that collapse through Ibn Khaldun's notion of asabiyyah -- roughly, the group solidarity a founding generation forges in hardship, which the second generation inherits by imitation and the third loses to luxury and faction, at which point the dynasty is ripe to be replaced. His story is that a dynasty runs about three generations. The thing I actually care about is whether the Soviet case confirms a cyclical reading like that or wrecks it. 74 years is eerily close to his generational unit, and you can tell a smooth story -- a Bolshevik cadre with the real thing, then a war generation, then a Brezhnev-era gerontocracy that kept all the ritual and none of the solidarity -- but I think thats too easy, and that solidarity is doing unfalsifiable work. So do the mapping honestly: what plays the role of asabiyyah here, what would count as third-generation rot as opposed to ordinary institutional decay, and what would have falsified the cycle. Then set it against the linear reading, and note the irony, because the USSR officially held one: the Marxist-Leninist ladder from feudalism up through socialism to communism, with the Soviet state as its leading edge. There's also the liberal linear version where 1991 is a terminus rather than a turn of the wheel. So which shape of history actually earns this case? And the part I keep going back and forth on -- does the fact that the official linear theory couldn't predict its own subject's end tell against linear views in general, or is it just that that particular linear theory was bad?
