I've ended up pretty convinced by Pascal's take on justice and force: justice without power is helpless, power without justice is tyranny, and since we can't make what's just strong, we settle for making what's strong just — which is why established custom ends up doing the work of justice, and why appeals to a pure rational justice strike me as either naive or a cover for the next power. What would count against this? I want the strongest defeaters sorted by how decisive they actually are, not just the standard objections: historical cases where a widely shared sense of justice constrained power without any force behind it, arguments that I'm confusing justice with legitimacy, the worry that the view just collapses into might-makes-right with extra steps, and whether Pascal's three-orders picture (charity as a seperate order, not a political program) insulates the whole thing from falsification. What would Pascal himself have to concede counts against him, as opposed to what critics would throw at him? What does the view predict that a natural-law account or a plain positivist one doesn't? And be honest with me: does this position risk anything at all, or has it been qualified into something no evidence could touch?
