Some intensive care and neurorehabilitation units, together with the research protocols attached to them, have begun to route decisions about unresponsive patients through a single number. A magnetic pulse is delivered to the scalp, the resulting electrical response is compressed into one value meant to capture how complex, widespread and long-lasting the response is rather than how large, and that value is compared against a threshold calibrated on healthy waking subjects, subjects under general anaesthesia, and subjects in dreamless sleep. A patient who shows nothing at the bedside — eyes open but not tracking, no response to command — may nonetheless score above the threshold, and when that happens the clinical team is expected to treat the patient as possibly conscious: to hunt for communication through other channels, to manage pain on the assumption that it may be felt, and to weigh the possibility of awareness when the family and the team discuss withdrawing life-sustaining treatment. The same number is used in the anaesthesia literature to track how far a patient has descended toward unconsciousness, and its developers describe it as a practical handle on the kind of integrated information that integrated information theory identifies with experience. Meanwhile the diagnosis that governs most legal and administrative decisions remains the bedside behavioural examination; the number is an adjunct, and its authority runs in one direction only. A high score can promote a patient into the category of the possibly aware, but a low score does not demote a patient who turns his eyes on command. The theory that inspires the measure, as I understand it, also contains a postulate to the effect that only one causal structure at one grain of description is maximally integrated, and that this structure, and nothing that overlaps it, is the thing that is conscious — which suggests to me that the conscious subject need not coincide with the patient, or with the whole brain.

I would like to know what this practice presupposes. Does using the index the way these units use it require holding that it measures consciousness itself rather than merely correlating with it, and can the practice be defended on the weaker correlational reading, or does the weaker reading quietly change what the clinicians are entitled to do with the number? Does the practice presuppose that consciousness comes in degrees, and if so, how does it square a graded quantity with the all-or-nothing moral and legal line that gets drawn at the threshold — is the threshold being treated as a discovery about consciousness or as a decision that the measure cannot justify? Whose consciousness is being measured: does the practice assume that the subject is the patient, the person, the organism, and does that assumption survive the theory's own exclusion postulate? Which accounts of consciousness are ruled out by the practice rather than merely inconvenienced by it — for instance, any view on which consciousness requires reportability or a higher-order representation — and which accounts fit it equally well, so that the practice underdetermines the theory? Finally, suppose we replaced the operative presupposition with one of its rivals: with the view that the index tracks a mere correlate and that nothing follows about experience, or with the view that a system is either conscious or not and the number is a proxy for something else entirely. What concretely would have to change in the wards — in the pain management, in the family conversations, in the treatment-withdrawal meetings, in the asymmetry between promotion and demotion? I am not after a reading list; I want the implicit theory reconstructed, with the parts the practice genuinely needs separated from the parts it merely sits comfortably with.
