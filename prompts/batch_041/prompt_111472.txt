The question (20 marks)

A research group has built a source — call it the Cardew source — which on each activation yields one of two outcomes, A or B. Two features of the source matter for this question. First, activations are serially dependent: the chance of an A on an activation is lower when the previous activation yielded an A than when it yielded a B. Second, the group has built one further unit, the one-off, which has never been activated and has since been dismantled; they nevertheless want to state a probability for the outcome that the one-off would have produced on a single activation, and they want that statement to be about that unit on that occasion rather than about a class of units or occasions.

Consider how each of the following would handle the case: a frequentist in the tradition of von Mises, a propensity theorist in the tradition of Popper, and a subjectivist in the tradition of Ramsey and de Finetti. Address in turn (i) the calibration data accumulated from the many activations, (ii) a prediction about the next activation, and (iii) the probability for the one-off. Then say which of the three interpretations handles the case best, and defend your choice.

The student's answer

The three interpretations are best introduced as rival answers to the question of what probability statements are about, rather than as rival calculi.

For the frequentist, probability is a property of a collective: a sequence of outcomes whose relative frequencies tend to a limit, and whose limit is undisturbed by any rule that selects a subsequence on the basis of the preceding outcomes. The calibration data are not evidence about something behind the sequence; the sequence is the object of study, and a probability statement records the limit of the relative frequency within it. Applied to the Cardew source, the frequentist's task is to satisfy herself that the record is a collective, and since a limit plainly exists in a record of that size, she can read the probability of A off the record. The serial dependence bears on the choice of reference class — the view's characteristic difficulty — rather than on the existence of the limit, so it is a complication rather than an obstacle.

For the propensity theorist, probability is a disposition of the generating conditions rather than a property of a sequence: the arrangement of the apparatus is disposed to yield A rather than B with a certain strength, and the sequence is a downstream consequence of that disposition. The familiar complaint is that such dispositions are unobservable and that the view therefore buys no explanatory power with its metaphysics. Popper's own version of the view, however, was still a view about repeatable experimental arrangements rather than about individual trials: the propensity attaches to the arrangement, so strictly speaking no single activation has a probability of its own. The Cardew source makes this vivid, since the relevant generating conditions differ from activation to activation, and since the one-off was never activated at all, so there is no propensity for the view to report.

For the subjectivist, probability is degree of belief, constrained only by coherence — by the requirement that one's degrees of belief be immune to a Dutch book. The calibration data matter only through their effect on credence: the subjectivist begins with a prior over the source's behaviour, conditionalizes on the record, and bets at her posterior. The one-off is no embarrassment, since a degree of belief is defined for any proposition she can entertain. The question is what her posterior converges on, and here de Finetti's representation theorem does the work. An exchangeable sequence is one whose outcomes are independent and identically distributed; the theorem shows that a subjectivist who treats the record as exchangeable will have her predictive probabilities converge on the sequence's limiting frequency, so that in the long run subjectivism and frequentism deliver the same verdicts about the data while differing about their source. The serial dependence of the Cardew source does not obstruct this, because exchangeability is a property of the agent's attitudes rather than of the world, and an agent can hold exchangeable credences about a process that is not itself exchangeable.

The verdict is therefore reasonably clear. The frequentist has to treat the record as the object of study and can say nothing about the one-off. The propensity view, at least as Popper framed it, is in the same position. Only the subjectivist can assign a probability to the one-off activation, and can accommodate the serial dependence without gerrymandering the reference class. Since the case turns on exactly the features that divide the three views, subjectivism is the interpretation that handles it best, and it is the one I would adopt.
