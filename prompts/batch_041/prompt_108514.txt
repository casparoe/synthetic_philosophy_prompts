Not a historian, just someone who reads too much about famines and has gotten into arguments about them. I keep circling the same three cases -- Bengal during the war, Ireland in the 1840s, China after the Great Leap Forward -- and I keep half-seeing a shape in them that I dont have a word for. Wondering if there's a name, or if I'm just pattern-matching.

Heres the shape. In all three, whatever food there was didn't end up where the starving were: it went to the cities, to the army, to the export trade, to whoever could pay, or it stayed unharvested because the people who would have brought it in were dead or gone. The people who died were mostly the ones who grew the food, not the ones who could buy it. And as far as I can tell the authorities didn't respond by moving food to the hungry, they responded by keeping the system going -- same levies, same priorities, same rules -- and it looks to me like these things ease when the politics change rather than when the food shows up, though I might be wrong about that. In Ireland it's the blight, in Bengal it's the war, in China it's weather and bad planning, and all of that is true as far as it goes, but "the crop failed" doesn't cover the part where the grain goes past the people it was grown for.

Is there a name for that part? I dont mean a polite way of saying "bad government". I mean the specific situation where a shortage turns into mass death because the rules for claiming food stop working for a whole class of people at once, and the people who could change those rules dont. And I'd want to know where the edges are: is it the same thing in all three cases or three different things wearing the same word? Where's the line between letting it happen and doing it? Does it still count when the shortage was real and not just a matter of who could pay?

If it has no name, coin one, and tell me what it is and what it isn't. (Not after a reading list -- I'm after the idea.)
