For the past six years I have been the philosopher attached to the clinical ethics consultation service at our academic medical center: I carry the on-call pager one week in four, I am usually expected to have a note in the chart within twenty-four hours, and that note is countersigned by the attending physician and the committee chair, which means I have to be able to defend every move in it to people who will read it as something closer to a legal document than to a seminar paper. A large share of the consults that reach me turn on moral testimony in the strict sense. Someone — a spouse, an adult child, a nurse on the unit, a senior colleague, occasionally the patient's own advance directive — asserts a moral claim, and the question I actually have to answer is not what I think is right but whether I am entitled to let that claim do the work. I would like a written procedure I can run on a consult, grounded in what the philosophical literature on moral testimony and moral deference has actually established and candid about what it has not. What I need is an ordered set of questions that tells me, first, whether what I am being offered is testimony about a moral matter, advice I may weigh, or a preference I must honor for reasons that have nothing to do with its truth, and how to tell when one and the same sentence is functioning as two of these at once; second, what would have to be true of the speaker for deference to be warranted at all, and whether the standards are the same as they would be for deference about a straightforwardly factual matter; third, what deference would oblige me to do in the note and in the conversation with the family, given that I may end up certified as having acted rightly on grounds I cannot myself give; and fourth, where the procedure stops and hands the matter to the committee, because the question is evaluative rather than factual and no amount of my expertise settles it. Two things must survive the simplification: the point that one can be justified in acting on another's moral say-so without thereby understanding why the action is right, and the point that this gap is precisely what makes deference uncomfortable and what leads some philosophers to think it is unavailable in morals even where it is unproblematic elsewhere. Do not smooth that difficulty away; build the procedure around it.

Then run the procedure on three consults, narrating the questions in order and saying what it directs me to do. In the first, the family of a patient with a catastrophic brain injury insists that everything be done, on the ground that a life in this condition is still a life owed absolute protection, and the attending wants me to talk them out of it. In the second, a respected senior intensivist tells me that in her judgment continuing is wrong and that she will not do it, and I notice that I am inclined to agree with her and to write my note as though the matter had been settled by her authority. In the third, a nurse says she cannot in conscience take part in a planned withdrawal, and I find, to my annoyance, that the procedure treats her claim as testimony I am not entitled to overrule, which will please neither the attending nor me. Close with a short section on where the procedure will misfire: cases in which the speaker's moral claim is entangled with a factual claim I can check; cases in which the speaker's standing to be heard rests on relationship rather than reliability, so that deference and respect for autonomy come apart; and cases in which I will be tempted to launder my own moral judgment into a procedural verdict. Assume the reader has the vocabulary but has never had to write the note.
