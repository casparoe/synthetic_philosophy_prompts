In Norway, the national sports confederation's rules for children's sport forbid national championships, official ranking and published result lists for the youngest age groups, require that prizes not differentiate between participants, and enjoin clubs to offer variety rather than early specialisation. Competition itself is not banned: children may compete, may keep score in the game, and may be told who won on the day. What is blocked is the public certification of a hierarchy. The same confederation is also the country's Olympic committee; the same clubs run talent pathways; and the same public celebrates its Winter Games medallists with a fervour that would look excessive in most countries. Meanwhile the wider culture is routinely described, by its own members, through Jantelagen — the suspicion of anyone who claims to be special or better than the rest.

I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory here, not a summary of Scandinavian social democracy. Specifically:

What does the children's-sport rule actually presuppose? Candidates worth distinguishing include: a claim about child welfare (early ranking causes harm); a claim about child development (broad sampling produces better long-run athletes than early selection); a claim about the meaning of childhood (it is not a qualifying round for adulthood); a claim about desert (children cannot be ranked because the traits ranked are unchosen and unstable); a claim about the conferral of status as such (excellence is fine, but public hierarchy among children is a category error); and a Jantelagen-shaped levelling claim (no one should be held above anyone else at any age). Reconstruct the strongest version, then say which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits.

Second, does the practice hang together internally? It permits scorekeeping but forbids publication; it permits a winner but forbids unequal prizes; it forbids national championships but tolerates regional and club-level competition. Is that a stable line, or does it presuppose something arbitrary — for instance that a child can be told she lost but not that she is twelfth in the country? If there is a principled boundary, state it; if the boundary is merely administrative convenience dressed as principle, say so.

Third, is the elite and celebratory side of the same system inconsistent with the children's rule, or does it presuppose a phase theory in which the protected childhood is instrumentally justified by the excellence it later enables? If so, the rule is not anti-elitist at all but a selection strategy, and the Jantelagen reading is a misdiagnosis. Consider whether the two readings make different predictions about, say, which sports adopt the rule most strictly, or how the rule bends when a child is genuinely exceptional.

Fourth, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by each of two rivals: a talent-development view that says early ranking and early selection are better for everyone, including the children who do not make it; and a children's-autonomy view that says the child's own desire for a ranking settles the matter. Be concrete about which parts of the current practice each rival would keep and which it would have to discard.

Close with your own verdict on which commitment the practice most plausibly rests on, how confident you are, and what would change your mind: name the consideration or argument that would push you toward a different reading, and say where your reasoning is likeliest to fail — for example if the practice turns out to be underdetermined between several theories, or if the actual rules vary too much between sports for any single reading to hold.

Please write in British English.
