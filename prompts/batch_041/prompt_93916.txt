Two friends of mine, A and B, spent most of a long drive arguing about a thought experiment A had made up, and they have asked me to settle it. I couldn't, so I want a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, not just your own answer to the puzzle.

The setup: you wake in a windowless room with no memory of how you got there. You know for certain that a fair coin was tossed. If it came up heads, exactly one person was created and placed in a room, with exactly your memories, experiences and situation. If it came up tails, exactly one thousand people were created, one to a room, each with exactly your memories, experiences and situation. The rooms are otherwise indistinguishable and you have no other information.

A: your credence that the coin came up heads should be about 1/1001, because a thousand times as many people are having your experience if it came up tails.

B: it should be 1/2. The coin was fair and you were certain to find yourself in a room either way, so nothing you have learned favours either outcome. B adds that A's reasoning is a machine for generating absurdities: it implies that any hypothesis positing more people is thereby more probable, so you could settle questions about the size of the universe from an armchair.

A: that is not an absurdity but the price of treating your own existence as evidence at all. And B's 1/2 depends on treating "you" as attached to a world rather than to one of its occupants — but you were created by the toss, so there is no antecedent you for anything to be attached to; the only thing "I" picks out here is the occupant of some room.

B: then let us be explicit about the random experiment. There is a difference between tossing the coin and then drawing an occupant uniformly from the rooms that result, which gives 1/2, and drawing uniformly among all the occupants who could have existed under either outcome, which gives 1/1001. Nothing about having been created tells you which of these you are the outcome of; A has simply assumed the second. A replies that the first is incoherent, since there is no fact about which occupant is drawn that is independent of which occupant you are.

B then offers a variant: suppose the single room under heads has a red door, and under tails the thousand rooms are all blue except one, which has a red door. B says that on his way of counting, seeing the red door should make heads roughly a thousand times more likely than tails — about 0.999 — whereas on A's principle the red door changes nothing and the answer stays 1/2. B says this shows A's principle cannot even register an observation that plainly bears on which world you are in. A answers that the red door is equally likely to be seen by someone under either hypothesis, exactly one person seeing it either way, so it is genuinely neutral, and that B is counting people where he should be counting experiences with your evidence.

Please rate each of these moves, say what each party is right and wrong about, and say what both are missing. Say which side is closer to right overall, and end by saying which argument or consideration would change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
