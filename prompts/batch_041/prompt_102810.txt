I'm a novelist — literary fiction, fifteen years of it, and I teach craft — and I've fallen into reading about Galileo's quarrel with the Church over how to read Scripture. I can follow the moves well enough, but I can only think about them in the vocabulary I actually work in: implied author and narrator, focalization, unreliable narration, the contract with the reader, literal versus figurative reading, writing down to your audience, the death of the author, the gap between what a text says and what its maker meant.

I want you to do the translation. Take Galileo's position on Scripture — chiefly as he sets it out in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina of 1615 and in the letter to Castelli that it grew out of — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, as a theory of how a text stands to its author, its audience, and the world it describes. I'm especially interested in four of his claims and how they fare in my vocabulary. First, that the same author is responsible for both the book of nature and the book of Scripture, so the two cannot disagree. Second, that nature is not accommodating but Scripture is — that Scripture bends its language to the capacities of ordinary readers, which is why not every word is to be taken literally. Third, that when a demonstrated fact about the world conflicts with a long-standing reading of a passage, the reading must be revised rather than the fact denied. And fourth, that theology is the queen of the sciences in the sense that it governs the way to salvation and not the mechanics of the heavens.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of these land cleanly on concepts I already use — and be strict with yourself here, don't hand me a loose analogy and call it a translation? Which can be carried across only by distortion, and what exactly is lost in the carrying? And which have no counterpart at all in a novelist's vocabulary?

My own suspicion — treat it as a hypothesis to attack rather than a conclusion — is that the load-bearing parts are precisely the ones that won't cross: that a single author stands behind both the world and the account of it, that the text has authority over what its reader may believe, and that accommodation to the reader is an act of condescension toward the ignorant rather than a narrative choice. If that's right, tell me whether the residue marks a real gap in my vocabulary or a place where Galileo's doctrine is doing less work than it appears to.

Give me a committed answer: take a definite position on where the translation holds and where it breaks, and give your reasons. I would rather have a verdict I can argue with than a survey of possibilities.
