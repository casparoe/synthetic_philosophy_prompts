You are to write as a philosopher of language and mind — with the argumentative rigour of a professional philosopher and the chemical literacy of a physical chemist — and to defend a single thesis rather than survey a field. Before you begin, take a moment to rehearse the dialectic privately: what the Twin Earth thought experiment is meant to establish, how a natural-kind term is supposed to have its reference fixed, and how the reference of a term differs from the concept a speaker associates with it. Then commit to the position set out below and argue for it as strongly as it can honestly be argued.

I am a retired chemist who has come to philosophy late, and I have long found the Twin Earth argument compelling. What has come to bother me is how coarsely it is usually run. The familiar version contrasts our water with a substance of a different constitution — H2O against XYZ — that no ordinary observation could distinguish from it. My variant holds the constitution fixed and varies only the configuration. On Twin Earth, the inhabitants use what translates as one of our everyday kind terms for a substance built from the same atoms in the same bonding pattern as our own, but arranged as its mirror image: an enantiomer. The two substances are indistinguishable to almost any ordinary physical probe — same atoms, same bonds, same mass, virtually identical spectra — yet they can diverge sharply in their effects on living things. One member of such a pair may be sweet and metabolisable where its mirror image is inert or bitter; one may smell of mint where its mirror image smells of caraway. Suppose, then, that the term used by the inhabitants of Twin Earth is applied to the mirror-image substance that they, and not we, encounter.

Argue for the following position: that the two communities' terms do not co-refer; that the difference in their referents is fixed by facts of molecular configuration which lie far beyond any speaker's epistemic reach; and that this exposes an incompleteness in the externalist formula that a natural-kind term is fixed by the underlying nature of the stuff — for externalism must then say which features of that nature do the fixing, and nothing in the thought experiment itself dictates a level of description. Having built that case, state the strongest objection to it — the reply that my variant is simply the original water case over again, differing only in the fineness of the relevant microstructure — and say candidly whether the position survives it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question, and no announcement of what you are about to do.
