I am in my final year and currently taking a seminar on Indian epistemology; the last three weeks have been devoted to the Jaina doctrine of anekāntavāda. In class we took the doctrine to be this. Reality is many-sided: every object has indefinitely many aspects, and no assertion about it states the whole of it. The doctrine's logical expression, syādvāda, is a scheme of qualified predication in which the epithet syāt (roughly, in some respect) attaches to each member of a seven-fold series — for a given object and predicate, the predicate holds; it does not hold; it both holds and does not hold; it is inexpressible; and then each of the first three of these is again combined with inexpressibility. Nayavāda adds that a speaker's standpoint is always one standpoint among many.

I have been trying to state, in the strongest form I can manage, the familiar complaint that this doctrine refutes itself. The reconstruction below is the best version I have produced; each step looks to me either faithful to the doctrine or an unobjectionable principle, and I cannot accept the conclusion.

(1) For any object there are indefinitely many respects in which it may be described, and no single description is exhaustive.
(2) For any predicate and object, the seven-fold series licenses the assertion of the predicate, the assertion of its negation, and the assertion of both, each under a qualification.
(3) So for any assertion p one might make about an object, there is a respect in which p holds and a respect in which its negation holds.
(4) An assertion that holds in one respect and fails in another is not true without qualification.
(5) Therefore no assertion is true without qualification.
(6) Anekāntavāda is itself an assertion: it asserts that no assertion is true without qualification.
(7) By (5), anekāntavāda is not true without qualification; there is a respect in which it fails.
(8) Whatever fails in some respect is not the correct account of the matter it concerns.
(9) So anekāntavāda is not the correct account of truth and predication.
(10) If instead we exempt the doctrine and hold that it alone is true without qualification, we abandon (5), which is the doctrine's central claim, and with it the ground for applying the doctrine to anyone else's position.

Tell me which step to reject. I would rather keep (1) and (2) — I find the many-sidedness claim attractive and do not want to surrender it — than accept (9); but I cannot see how to keep them without (3). For whichever step you nominate, explain why it looked acceptable in the first place, exactly what goes wrong with it, and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step can be rejected, compare the costs, say which rejection is cheapest and which the most expensive, and what each one buys. I have heard three ways out in particular and cannot price any of them: reading syāt as an operator that attaches to the assertion rather than as a weakening of it; reading the doctrine as a second-order claim about assertions, which would therefore not be one of the assertions it ranges over; and reading it as a norm of inquiry, or an intellectual virtue, rather than as a theory of truth. For each, say what it would have to give up, and whether what remains is still a substantive doctrine rather than a refusal to assert anything.
