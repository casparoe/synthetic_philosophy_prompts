For a year my reading group has been circling one question — whether a nation can be guilty for wrongs done by earlier generations — and I want a diagnosis of why we never move rather than another round of arguments. The two positions are stable and fully stated. One side holds that guilt is a predicate of individual agents: it requires having done the thing or having been able to refrain, so it cannot be inherited, and what descendants have is at most an obligation to repair, which is something else. The other side holds that nations persist through time and act through institutions, so that when a state apologises for a massacre or a university acknowledges that its endowment was built by slave labour, the 'we' that acknowledges is the same 'we' that profited, and refusing the word guilt is a euphemism. No one disputes the history, no new evidence is in play, and each side knows the other's arguments by heart.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed: this is not one disagreement but two questions wearing one word. The first camp asks about desert and causal contribution; the second about membership and what the present owes. If that is right, say precisely what each side is claiming and whether both claims can be true at once. If it is wrong, say what the real disagreement is — about the metaphysics of collective agency, about whether guilt admits of degrees or can be transferred, about moral luck, or about whether the concept answers to a practice (apology, reparation, commemoration) rather than to a fact. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a case one side must concede, or nothing, because the disagreement bottoms out in intuitions. Use as a test the case of someone descended from both perpetrators and victims of the same wrong, whom each camp claims. And say whether the kinds of guilt Jaspers separates in The Question of German Guilt (1946) clarify this or merely multiply the vocabulary.
