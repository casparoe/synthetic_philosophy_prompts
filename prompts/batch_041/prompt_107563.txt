My team has spent the better part of a month arguing about compliance — whether "following the rules" is the same thing as doing the right thing — so I've been reading about legal theory on my own. An explainer I found online summarizes the Hart–Fuller debate more or less as follows:

"Hart was a legal positivist: he held that law and morality are two entirely separate systems, so a rule counts as law whenever it has been enacted through the correct procedure, however evil its content. Fuller, a natural lawyer, held the opposite view: an immoral rule is not law at all. For Fuller, law has an inner morality — it must be fair, just, and respectful of human dignity if it is to count as law. So the whole debate comes down to whether injustice can strip a statute of legal validity, with Hart saying no and Fuller saying yes. The Nazi grudge-informer cases settled the matter in Fuller's favor, since the postwar courts refused to treat the Nazi statutes as law. The upshot is that legal positivism is a dangerous doctrine, because it tells citizens they must obey any rule that was properly enacted, no matter how monstrous."

I suspect this is too tidy, but I don't know enough to say where it goes wrong. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a legal philosopher would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are actually mistaken — including what a careful defender of Hart would say the summary gets wrong about positivism? And in particular, does the closing "upshot" follow from anything accurate in the rest of the summary, or does it depend on one of the mistakes? For context, I'm inclined to think the positivists are the ones who would tell you to disobey an unjust law, so that last sentence surprised me.
