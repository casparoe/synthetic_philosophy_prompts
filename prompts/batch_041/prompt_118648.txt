I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-odd years in a lab, hung it up a couple years back — and I've been using the free time to teach myself philosophy, mostly decision theory and some of the early modern stuff, which is a combination that probably says more about me than I'd like. Anyway. I've been chewing on Newcomb's problem (you know, the two boxes and the predictor who's a very good guesser) and also on Pascal's wager, and it struck me they're basically cousins: in both, you make your choice, but the thing that fixes your payoff seems to have already "known" what you were going to choose. So I cooked up a version I haven't seen anywhere and I'd like your honest read on what Pascal would've made of it. Here's the setup: a transparent box holding something decent and finite — call it a comfortable pension — and an opaque box. The opaque box does or doesn't contain the Prize, and the Prize is of literally unbounded value, eternal bliss or a utility no one can put a number on. A predictor thats right the vast majority of the time has already decided whether to put the Prize in: Prize if and only if it predicted you'd take the opaque box alone, nothing if it predicted you'd grab both. The pension is yours either way.

So — one box or both? And my real question is what Pascal would have done with this. He's one of the people who pretty much invented this kind of probability calculation in the first place, and he's also the guy who came up with the most famous infinite-payoff argument there is, so he seems like the natural person to ask, except he died about three hundred years before anyone posed the puzzle, so all I can get is your best guess. Two things I keep circling. First, I gather he was a Jansenist and that predestination loomed large in that whole world — does that kind of theology push him toward treating the predictor's prior choice as already fixed, and so toward taking just the one box, or does it cut the other way? Second, what happens to his wager-style reasoning if there are several predictors offering several different boxes, the way there are supposed to be several rival gods — does that wreck the argument, and would he have noticed it did?

Please be careful to seperate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested — I don't want some confident verdict on a question philosophers are still fighting over served up as though it were the consensus, and if the whole thing really just turns on which reading of Pascal you take, say that straight out. Casual is fine, I'm not writing a paper here.
