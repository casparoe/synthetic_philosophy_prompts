I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science, and I'm drafting a short response paper on Milton Friedman's essay on the methodology of positive economics, the one where he argues that the realism of a theory's assumptions doesn't matter and that a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions alone. Before I commit to the argument, I'd like a careful reader to tell me where it goes wrong, where it's merely restating something familiar, and where it could be pushed further.

My thesis is that the so-called F-twist is true for one class of assumptions and false for another, and that both its defenders and its critics keep running the two classes together. Take the ideal gas law, which assumes that molecules have no volume and exert no forces on one another. The assumption is false, but it is what I call a recoverable assumption: we can dial the falsehood back a little at a time — give the molecules a small finite volume, add weak attractive forces — and the predictions degrade gracefully and converge on the more realistic model. The false assumption is a limit we could in principle approach, so treating it as a harmless convenience seems right. Now take the assumption, pervasive in economics, that agents maximize a stable utility function. I claim this is a different animal, which I call an irrecoverable assumption: there is no continuous dial we can turn to make the agent a little less of a maximizer while preserving the structure of the model, and the more behaviorally realistic models don't converge on the maximizing agent as a limiting case — they replace it with something else. So the F-twist's claim that only predictive accuracy matters looks defensible where the assumption is recoverable, since the assumption is then just a way station on the road to a more realistic model, but it looks suspect where the assumption is irrecoverable, because there the assumption is doing explanatory work that no amount of predictive success can license. I also want to say that this distinction explains why the physics examples always seem to settle the argument when in fact they don't: they are all recoverable cases.

I'd like comments on three things in particular. First, is my reading of Friedman fair, or does he already have the resources to absorb the distinction — does he anywhere separate assumptions that are approximations from assumptions that are placeholders, or does he treat them all alike? Second, is recoverable versus irrecoverable a genuine distinction, or just the old approximation-versus-idealization contrast dressed up in new clothes; and if it's the latter, does anything of my argument survive? Third, please try to break it with a counterexample: I suspect there are cases that are recoverable in my sense but where the F-twist still seems wrong, and irrecoverable cases where it still seems right, and I would much rather hear about them now than in a referee report. I'm working on this on a train with no internet access, so please make your case in your own words and test the thesis with examples and arguments you can lay out from scratch, rather than pointing me toward anything I'd have to go look up.
