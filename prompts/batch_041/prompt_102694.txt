For much of my working life I was a systematist, distinguishing species and subspecies, and since retiring I have been trying to learn philosophy properly. I am now preparing a teaching instrument for a small interdisciplinary seminar—type designers, linguists, and philosophers—on a question to which I keep returning: what makes a mark an instance of the letter a? I would like a dichotomous key, in the spirit of a field guide to a difficult genus, that sorts the positions one might hold rather than summarizing what celebrated theorists have said. Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or a survey of the literature; the instrument should stand on its own.

Treat the key as a decision tree. Each branch node should be a concrete case or a yes-or-no question about a case, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which philosophical position it leads to. Do not begin with a question such as 'Are you an essentialist about letters?' or any equivalent. The cases should include at least the following: the single-storey and double-storey forms of a; a child's reversed or malformed a; a typeface in which a is barely distinguishable from o except for a small tail; a logo or mark that is read as a only because of its context; a historical script in which the ancestor of a looks nothing like the modern letter; an invented alphabet containing a glyph that looks exactly like a but functions as a different letter; a machine-generated form that no human has ever seen but that satisfies all the structural features of a; and a highly stylized font that preserves a's identity while varying almost every measurable feature. Add any further cases you consider necessary to separate the positions.

Use the analogy of biological taxonomy as an organizing device. I want the leaves to include positions such as typological essentialism (a has a necessary and sufficient form), phenetic similarity (a is a cluster of resembling marks), historical or cladistic descent (a is whatever descends from earlier a-forms in a lineage), population thinking (a is a statistical norm with no single essence), functionalism (a is whatever plays the role of a in a reading system), conventionalism or nominalism (a is fixed by communal convention or naming), eliminativism (there is no fact of the matter, only useful labels), and any dual-aspect or hybrid view you think is actually occupied. Name the positions descriptively; you may mention proper names only if they are unavoidable, but do not make the key depend on them. The questions should also probe whether style is part of a letter's identity or merely a veneer upon it, and whether recognition by a reader is constitutive of the letter or merely evidence for it.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Where the tree produces a combination of answers that no one seems to hold, say so and diagnose whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, flag them rather than pretending the key distinguishes them. Keep the whole instrument to no more than about twelve branching questions and eight to ten leaves. The point is to sort by what someone says about the cases, not by which label they prefer.
