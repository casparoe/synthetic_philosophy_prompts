So, reading group emergency. Five of us have been going through the Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments — the Lucas/Penrose line that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind can't be a formal system, or can't be a Turing machine — and after three sessions we've hard-split into two camps that have stopped listening to each other. Camp G says the theorems do refute mechanism, or at least refute the thesis that our arithmetical beliefs are the output of a sound, effectively enumerable theory. Camp M says the arguement is broken somewhere — a scope mistake or a self-trust mistake — and that where it's valid it only refutes a strawman nobody holds. I don't want to referee another round of shouting; I want the document an adversarial collaboration would have produced. So: Section 1, numbered claims both sides can sign. That should include the maths they agree on (first and second incompleteness; that the set of true arithmetical sentences isn't recursively enumerable; and the Löb-type point that a consistent theory meeting the usual provability conditions can't prove the reflection schema — if it proves A, then A — for arbitrary A, since taking A to be the absurdity would give it its own consistency and contradict the second theorem). Then what both agree the theorems don't show by themselves — e.g. that no purely mathematical result refutes the mind-is-a-machine claim in the implementation sense, since a physical machine can implement an unsound or even inconsistent theory and still be a machine; the live target has to be the narrower claim that the mind's arithmetical output is exactly the theorems of a sound r.e. theory. And then the shared skeleton of the disputed argument, stated so neither side has to concede anything in order to sign it. My attempt: let B be the idealised mathematician's set of arithmetical beliefs; suppose (a) B is sound, everything in it is true, and (b) for every sound r.e. theory T contained in B, B also contains the Gödel sentence of T; then B isn't recursively enumerable. Both camps can sign that conditional — the fight is over whether (a) and (b) are premises the anti-mechanist is entitled to, and if not, which one fails.

Section 2, a disagreements table. I want the cruxes, each with both sides' formulations in wording its own side would accept, and for each crux a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. My guesses, use better ones if you find them: (i) self-trust — can the idealised mathematician be non-circularly entitled to the soundness of her own arithmetical beliefs? Note consistency isn't enough here, since a consistent theory can prove false Σ1 sentences, so she needs soundness, or at least Σ1-soundness; the mechanist will definately say Gödel 2 and Löb block any such entitlement from the inside, and that getting it from the outside just means she's climbed to a stronger system. (ii) idealisation — the argument runs on a reasoner with unlimited time, no memory limits, and closure under consequence; does idealising that far hand the mechanist the game, since no actual human is like that, or is it the right idealisation of competence rather than performance? (iii) what mechanism is even supposed to mean — implementation-level computationalism versus the belief-set claim, and whether anyone ever meant the belief-set claim. (iv) the ladder — she sees the Gödel sentence of PA by reasoning in something like ZFC, then can't certify ZFC's soundness without climbing again: is the moral that no fixed system captures us, or that reflection is exactly the kind of thing you can build a machine to do, one rung at a time, with every rung still being some system?
