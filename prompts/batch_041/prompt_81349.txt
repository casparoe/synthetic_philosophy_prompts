I've been arguing with two friends, on and off for months, about what an emotion actually is — whether it is a judgment, a perception of one's own body, a feeling, or something else again — and we have reached the point where only cases will settle anything, so I've been writing down my own verdicts on them and trying to see what, if anything, they add up to. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature: no names to look up, no reading list, no citations, since I'm working from memory and can't check anything. I want the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures the verdicts below; for each clause of it, the case that forces the clause; what it says about cases I haven't put to myself; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot keep. If one of my verdicts is simply a muddle, say so and say which.

The cases. (1) A colleague who is a nervous flyer: during turbulence her heart pounds, her palms dampen, she grips the armrest — and yet she insists, apparently sincerely, that she knows the aircraft is safe and does not expect to die. I count her as frightened. So fear cannot be the belief that one is in danger, and cannot require it. (2) Suppose a man whose body has stopped sending him any of the usual signals: after an illness he no longer feels his heart pound or his stomach lurch, and reports nothing at all from inside his body. He can still tell you, calmly and correctly, that a situation is dangerous; he avoids it and behaves cautiously. But he says he feels nothing — no fear, no dread. I do not count him as afraid. (3) A woman with a bad case of nerves before a talk is given a drug that blocks the bodily signs of anxiety; her heart stays slow, her hands steady. She says she is still frightened, and still feels frightened. I count her as afraid. (4) On a rollercoaster I get what seems to be exactly the state of case 1 — heart pounding, palms damp, stomach dropping — and I love it; I feel no threat whatever, and I am not afraid, even though the flyer and I appear to be feeling the same thing in our bodies. (5) I once realised I was angry only when I caught myself with a clenched jaw, speaking sarcastically. I am sure the anger was already going on before I noticed it, and that noticing it is not what brought it into being; I would say it had been there, unnoticed, for some minutes. (6) My friend's dog moped for weeks after the other dog in the house died — off its food, whining, indifferent to walks. She says the dog is grieving, and I am inclined to agree, though I doubt the dog has any belief about death or any concept of it.

Tentatively I think the truth is something like this: an emotion is a felt evaluation, a feeling that presents its object as mattering in some particular way — as threatening, insulting, delightful — and it is that presentation, not the sensation, that distinguishes fear from exhilaration. On such a view the feeling is necessary (hence case 2); it need not be bodily or have a bodily source (hence case 3); the same bodily sensation can be two different emotions or none at all (hence case 4); the feeling can be present without being noticed (hence case 5); and the evaluation need not be a judgment or belief (hence cases 1 and 6). Please test this rather than restate it. Two things trouble me in particular. First, cases 2 and 5 look as though they cannot both be right: if unfelt anger was already anger, why is unfelt fear not fear, and if no feeling means no emotion, was the anger really there before I noticed it? Second, I want to know whether my formulation collapses into the banal claim that an emotion is a judgment with a feeling attached, and if so what work the feeling is doing in cases 1 and 6. Then give me your principle's verdicts on three cases I have deliberately left out: a man who is paralysed and expressionless, whose body gives away nothing at all, but who tells me steadily that he is terrified; a person who begins to feel afraid only after a doctor remarks on how frightened their face looks; and a creature with no concepts whatever — a fish, say — that flinches from a shock and then avoids the place it happened. Finally, tell me where the principle runs out — the point at which two people could accept every clause of it and still disagree about a case — and whether, to save all six verdicts, I would have to trade a simple principle for an elaborate one, or give up a verdict instead.
