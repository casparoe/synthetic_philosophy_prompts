I'm in a seminar that's supposed to be about historiography but keeps turning into IR theory, and we've spent two weeks on the whole lessons-of-history problem — Munich, Vietnam, and now the so-called Thucydides trap, the idea that when a rising power threatens to displace a ruling power you get a war, named after Thucydides as if he'd come up with it. A friend in the group, who's read a lot of realist stuff, says the trap is just Thucydides' own thesis in modern dress: if you buy it, the US and China are Sparta and Athens, and the Melian dialogue — the part where the Athenians tell the Melians that questions of right only arise between equals, and that the strong take what they can while the weak put up with it — is basically a forecast of what a great power does to a small one when it gets the chance. I pushed back that this is people borrowing a dead man's name for a machine he never built, but I cant tell if I'm being fair. What confuses me is that he does seem to say the war was inevitable, that the truest cause, the one least talked about, was the growth of Athenian power and the fear it put into Sparta; and he does say he wrote the book to be useful to people in the future, on the assumption that human nature is a constant so the same kinds of things will keep happening, which my friend says settles it. But then the book itself is hundreds of pages of particular speeches, bad decisions, plague, luck, and the Athenians eventually wrecking themselves in Sicily, and there's an early scene where the Athenians explain that they took and kept their empire out of fear, honour and their own advantage, which is not what a law of history sounds like. So I suspect the inevitability line and the useful-for-the-future line are doing different work, but I can't say how, and I'm probably mangling the whole thing.
