I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group this term, and instead of letting everyone guess at my view from the comments I've been making, I've been asked to circulate it in writing so people can attack the actual thing. Here is what I hold. (1) Physicalism: a complete physical description of the world, together with whatever functional and organizational facts supervene on it, fixes every fact there is, including facts about what experience is like. (2) Physicalism is an epistemic thesis as much as a metaphysical one: if it's true, then in principle someone who knew all the physical facts and reasoned perfectly could work out every phenomenal fact from them; a permanent, principled gap between the physical story and the experiential story would count as evidence that physicalism is false, not as evidence that the gap is merely verbal or terminological. (3) The knowledge argument gets its premise right: Mary, who has been confined to a black-and-white room her whole life and knows everything there is to know about colour vision, learns something when she first sees a ripe tomato, and the intuition that she does is not an illusion to be explained away. (4) What she gains, though, is not knowledge of a new fact but a new grip on facts she already had — a phenomenal concept, or maybe a bit of know-how, that no amount of third-personal information could have handed her in advance. (5) Zombies — creatures physically and functionally identical to me, with nothing going on inside — are conceivable in the relevant sense; when I try to imagine one I don't run into a contradiction. (6) Conceivability is a fallible but genuine guide to possibility, so I treat the conceivability of zombies as real evidence that zombies are possible, and I don't think I'm entitled to wave that away just because it's inconvenient for the rest of my views. (7) There is nothing it is like to be a thermostat or a language model's tokenizer, there is something it is like to be a bat, and I don't think there's a sharp line between those cases — just a gradient of functional complexity. (8) The hard problem of consciousness is a real problem about the world, not merely a fact about the limits of our concepts.

I have a nagging feeling that at least one of these is doing work it can't cash, but I can't see where the fault line is, and I'd rather find out now than in front of the group. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the remaining package is stable once it's made. Lead with that single revision in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my list back to me.
