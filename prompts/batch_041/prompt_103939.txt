I run a small reading group on free will and moral responsibility, and for our final session — the one on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against the possibility of true moral responsibility — I don't want to close with a summary. I want to close with a poem the group can sit with, and I've become convinced that the pantoum is the right form for this argument, so I'd like you to write one and, in effect, test my conviction. A pantoum is built from quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza should fold the poem's opening lines back in so that the whole thing returns to where it began. The Basic Argument, as I hold it in mind, goes roughly like this: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are; but you cannot be responsible for the way you are, because that would require having made yourself the way you are, which pushes you either into an infinite regress of earlier selves, each of which would in turn have to be responsible for the next, or into the impossible demand that you be causa sui — the cause of yourself. What I find hardest to shake is Strawson's insistence that none of this turns on determinism: an uncaused or random element in your formation is no more yours than a caused one, so indeterminism buys you nothing. He also draws a line between the ultimate responsibility he thinks is impossible and the ordinary social and deliberative responsibility he is content to keep, and I'd like the poem to respect that line rather than flatten it into a general fatalism about blame.

I want the form to do the argument's work rather than merely illustrate it. The interlocking repetition should be the chain of selves handing responsibility back to earlier selves, and the return to the opening line at the end should be the moment the argument lands exactly where it started, with no exit. So choose the two lines that get passed from stanza to stanza with real care: they should carry the argumentative weight, such that a reader who knows Strawson can tell which recurring line is the premise and which is the move, and the closing stanza's meeting of them should state the conclusion without preaching it. Aim for four or five quatrains. It should be accurate enough that Strawson himself wouldn't wince, and legible and genuinely good as a poem for someone who has never heard of him.

After the poem, give me a brief note, and please put it in a structured form so I can paste it into our notes. Return a JSON object with exactly these fields: hinge_lines, an array of the two recurring lines in the order they first appear; argument_map, an object mapping each stanza number to the step of the Basic Argument it tracks; liberties_taken, a list of the places where the poem simplifies, sharpens, or departs from the argument as stated above; contested_reading, the one reading of the argument the poem most depends on and what the poem would have to look like under the main rival reading; and change_my_mind, a short statement of which consideration — about the argument, or about whether the pantoum really fits it — would lead you to conclude that the poem misfires. In that last field I'd rather you be genuinely uncertain and tell me where your reasoning is weakest than defend the piece.
