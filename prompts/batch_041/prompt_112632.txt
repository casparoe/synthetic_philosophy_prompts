I've been going round in circles with my flatmate about how much we actually owe strangers, and before I tie myself in knots I want to work out what I really think. So here are my gut verdicts on a pile of rescue cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle — or small set of principles — they add up to, which verdicts I'd have to give up to get a tidy one, and what it says about a couple of cases I've not listed.

The ones I'm sure about. I'm walking past a shallow pond and a small child is drowning, I'm the only one about, wading in ruins my suit: I have to wade in, no question. The same child is drowning in a pond three miles off, and getting there in time means definately missing my sister's wedding: I still have to go. I get a letter saying thirty quid will save a particular named child I'll never meet from a disease that would otherwise certainly kill them: I have to send it. Thirty quid buys that same named child only a five per cent chance of pulling through: I think I still have to send it, though it feels thinner. Thirty quid is my last thirty quid and sending it means skipping dinner: still have to send it, I think.

The ones where it flips. Thirty quid goes to a programme that will on average save one life somewhere among ten thousand children but saves no one in particular: I dont think I'm obliged to send it — and that bothers me, because it's the same expected life either way. The thirty quid is the money I'd earmarked for my own daughter's swimming lessons: I think I may spend it on her instead. I'd have to give thirty quid every single day for the rest of my life, one child after another: I don't think I'm obliged to, even though every individual gift is exactly the sort I said I had to make. And if the only way to save the pond child is to use the last dose of an antidote that another child elsewhere will then die without, I don't think I have to save the first child that way.

The pair I can't get to sit still is the letter case (must send) and the ten-thousand-children case (needn't send), which look like the same number of expected lives. And there's a case I keep chewing on: I manage a small fund for my employer, and I could quietly divert thirty quid of it to save one of these distant children — but the money isn't mine to give, and I think I mustn't, even though the cost to me is nil and the child is every bit as real as the one in the pond. What principle, if any, gets the verdicts I'm sure about, forces me to drop at least one of the ones that flip, and tells me what to do with the fund case and with one more I've not worked out — where I can save the distant named child only by breaking a promise I made to my dying mother, and keeping that promise will cost the child their life?
