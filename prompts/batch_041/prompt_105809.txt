My cousin's a public defender in a country that went full perdurantist about eighty years ago — no time machines, nothing sci-fi, it's just that their supreme court eventually accepted the four-dimensional picture of persons wholesale and the whole legal system got rebuilt around it. In practice it means that when somebody commits a crime, the part of them that did it is a temporal part parked back in 2019, and the man sitting in the dock is a different temporal part, the 2024 one, and on the official view those two aren't the same person at all — they're just two parts of one longer object, the way your hand and your foot are two parts of you. So sentencing hearings turn into these strange arguments about whether you can punish one part of a worm for what a different part of the worm did.

I want you to write a short story set in that country. Pick whichever seat you like — the defendent, the defender, the judge, the victim's mother — but the metaphysics has to be doing the real work, not just decorating a courtroom drama. Two things I want on the page, and I want both of them argued well:

One, an endurantist. There are still alot of them in this country, they think the entire legal regime is a category error, and one of them should be arguing that she is literally one and the same person at every moment of her life — that's what makes her the one who did it, and also what makes it possible for her to be forgiven and to forgive herself. Don't make that side the dumb side.

Two, the Parfit-ish move, that maybe desert isn't about identity at all but about how psychologically connected you are to the person who acted, so someone who's changed enough deserves less. I want someone to make that argument seriously and someone else to take it apart, and I don't want the story to settle which of them wins.

No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write a story for someone to actually sit down and read.
