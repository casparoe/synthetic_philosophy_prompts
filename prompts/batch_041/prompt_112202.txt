A mate and I keep going round in circles on this and I want an essay-length tiebreaker. We both agree archives aren't neutral — who ends up with a paper trail is decided by power, not by who actually mattered. Where we split is what to do about the gaps. She reckons the silences in a source are themselves evidence, so a historian who just shrugs at a missing voice is quietly letting the powerful write the record. I reckon a silence is a silence — you might be able to infer why the record-keepers stayed quiet, but you can't infer anything about the people who were kept out of it, and pretending otherwise is ventriloquism with footnotes. So write me an essay on whether an archival silence can count as evidence the way a dog not barking can, or whether that's a category mistake. Somewhere in there I'd like you to get at the difference between a silence with a traceable cause — something was recorded and then destroyed, or never recorded because someone made a call not to — and one that's just the ordinary limit of any record, and say whether the two really behave differently when it comes to inference. Reach a verdict, don't leave it hanging on "it depends". British English please.
