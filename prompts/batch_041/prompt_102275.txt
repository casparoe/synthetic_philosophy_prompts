I cover politics and ideas, and I've been sitting in on a series of oral-history interviews with a retired foreign minister — call him M — who's been going back over the hard calls of his career. His scorecard so far: the drone strike that killed a wanted commander along with his family, to stop an attack that was already being planned for that same week, was the right call. Same for lying to the public about a bank's solvency to head off a run that would have wiped out pensions within days. But lying about an affair to save his party's majority? Wrong. Having a foreign leader killed just to install a friendlier government, no imminent threat? Wrong. Cooking up evidence of weapons to justify a war he wanted anyway? Wrong. And bribing a militia leader, knowing full well he'd go on killing, to keep his forces from overrunning the capital? He says right.

The next thing the interviewer puts to him is a case he never actually faced: intelligence that a hostile state is building a bioweapon that won't be ready for maybe seven to ten years but could then kill millions, and the only way to stop it now is a covert operation that will certainly kill a few innocent scientists and set the program back. Do you think M will call that one right or wrong? I care less about his answer than about the rule he's actually running. He seems consistent with at least two: (a) dirty acts are only ever OK to head off an imminent, in-your-face catastrophe, and (b) they're OK whenever the expected good clearly outweighs the wrong, however far off the threat is. Both fit everything above, and they part ways on the bioweapon case. So: predict his verdict, tell me which rule he's really on and why, and name the one further case that would actually settle it between them.

Take the role of a philosopher who works on just war theory and political ethics, and think this through carefully — start by spelling out the candidate rules and the other rules that fit his verdicts just as well before you commit to one.
