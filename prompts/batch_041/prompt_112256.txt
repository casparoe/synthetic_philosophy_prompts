My partner is fourteen weeks pregnant with monozygotic twins, and a question I last thought about in an undergraduate metaphysics course has become the thing I lie awake over. I hold the view — call it origin essentialism — that a human being's origin is essential to it: that there is a particular sperm cell and a particular egg cell from which you came, and no possible world in which you come from any others. A friend in my cohort, who is far better at modal logic than I am, has produced an argument that he says refutes the view outright, and I have not been able to locate the bad step. Each premise strikes me as either plainly true or true by the meanings of the terms, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept. I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. Here is the argument as he set it out:

(1) Every human being originates from a particular sperm cell and a particular egg cell, and it is essential to that human being that it originates from those very cells: there is no possible world in which it originates from any other sperm or egg.
(2) A human being originates from a zygote, and a zygote originates from the fusion of a single sperm and a single egg.
(3) Monozygotic twins originate from a single zygote, and therefore from a single sperm and a single egg.
(4) So each twin has exactly the same origin as the other: the same zygote, the same sperm, the same egg.
(5) Consider a world W that is like the actual world in everything bearing on the conception of these twins — the same sperm fuses with the same egg, the same zygote comes into being — except that the zygote never divides, so that a single embryo develops from it and one adult human being eventually exists.
(6) Since the origin of each twin is that zygote, and origin is essential, both twins exist in W.
(7) But only one human being exists in W, and two people cannot be one person.
(8) Therefore the twins are one and the same person, which is absurd.
(9) Therefore origin essentialism is false.

He regards this as decisive and has been circulating it. I would rather keep (1) and the denial of (9) than give up anything else on the list, so if the price of keeping (1) is a repair to (2) or (4) or to how finely an origin must be specified, I want to know exactly what that repair commits me to and what it costs me elsewhere — in particular, whether I can still say, as I want to, that if a different sperm had won the race then you would never have existed. I am not asking for reading recommendations; I want a diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked innocent, and what each of the available exits from the argument leaves me holding.
