I'm running a 90-minute session for about 18 undergrads (mostly philosophy, a few law students) and I want them to feel the self-amendment paradox in their hands rather than hear me explain it. Here's my starting setup, change whatever needs changing: they play a deliberately dull little game -- roll, move, collect points -- with six rules, and two of the rules are about rules. Rule 5: any rule, including this one, can be changed by a two-thirds vote. Rule 6: Rule 5 can only be changed by a unanimous vote. I'll tell them the winner is whoever has the most points at the end, which is a lie I'm telling so they'll want to rewrite the rules. Peter Suber built a whole game on this in the 80s (Nomic -- "Nomic is a game in which changing the rules is a move"), and his version handles the same problem by splitting rules into mutable and immutable ones that have to be "transmuted" before they can be touched, so borrow whatever of that is useful.

Design the activity: the rules and materials, how I seed the first couple of rounds so the group actually starts amending things, what I do at the moment someone tries to repeal Rule 6 or amend Rule 5 out from under it, and the debrief. What I want them to hit is that nothing in the rulebook settles the clash -- both rules are equally authoritative and there's no referee -- so they have to supply a tie-break from outside the book, and then notice that whatever they supply (a priority rule, "the earlier rule wins", "whoever wrote it decides") is either not in the book at all or is in the book and therefore amendable, which just moves the regress up a level. They should also feel that the entrenchment in Rule 6 was only ever as strong as their willingness to treat it as binding.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect at least one player to try to win by declaring the game broken, and I expect the group to shrug and say obviously the majority wins, killing the trap) and how to handle it. And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce: real constitutions have this same structure and look unproblematic because a court sits outside the rulebook and settles it, so say what I have to supply in the debrief. One more thing to fold in: some people fix games like this by writing in a rule that hands victory to the first player who can prove the rules have stopped working -- have the debrief take up whether thats a solution or just a way of changing the subject.
