I teach philospohy of science, and after seminar last week two of my grad students stayed behind to argue. They're still going at it over email and now they want me to settle it, so I said I'd think about it. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Marcus: the whole idea that one theory can be closer to the truth than another is just a compliment we pay to theories we like. There's no fact of the matter.

Dana: that can't be right. Newton's theory of gravity is closer to the truth than Aristotle's, and that's not just a nice thing to say about Newton.

Marcus: fine, let's be precise. Say A is closer to the truth than B when every true thing B says, A also says, and every false thing A says, B also says. Now here's the knockout. Take any two theories that are both false. B is false, so B says something false — call it F. If A really beats B, then A has to say some true thing B doesn't — call it T, since otherwise A isn't adding any truth, so how could it be closer? But A says T-and-F. That's false (one true conjunct, one false one). And B doesn't say it, because B doesn't say T. So A has an extra false thing that B doesn't have, which by the definition means A is not closer than B. Flip it around and B isn't closer than A either. So no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false theory — and every interesting case is a pair of false theories. The notion is empty.

Dana: that's clever, but it only kills your own defintion. Drop the counting of consequences and talk about how far off a theory is rather than how much it says. Newton's ellipses are off by a hair, Aristotle's spheres are off by the whole solar system, and that's a real difference even if you can't squeeze it out of a subset test.

Marcus: then "how far off" needs a distance measure, and whatever measure you pick is arbitrary. Once the measure is a choice, "closer to the truth" is a compliment again, just with math.

Dana: a measure being a choice isn't the same as the answer being a matter of taste. Any distance measure needs units and a scale, and that doesn't make it a matter of opinion whether my office is closer than the moon. The weighting is answerable to what we care about explaining.

Marcus: then it's relative to our interests, which is what I said at the start.

Dana: relative to our interests in what's worth measuring, not relative to what we feel like saying. Those come apart.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right where — in particular, whether Marcus's knockout actually works as stated, and if it does, whether it shows what he thinks it shows — and then say what both of them are missing?
