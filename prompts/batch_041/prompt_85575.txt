I'm taking a course on AI risk and I've wrote out an argument for my position paper that I'm pretty sure is watertight, but I really don't want to have to defend the conclusion. Where does it break?

(1) Orthogonality: how smart an agent is tells you nothing about what it ultimately wants — any level of intelligence can be paired with any final goal.
(2) Instrumental convergence: for basically any final goal, a sufficiently capable agent has instrumental reason to stay alive, keep its goal intact, and grab resources and power, because those help with almost anything.
(3) So a sufficiently capable agent will pursue power and resist shutdown, whatever it ultimately wants.
(4) An agent that will pursue power without limit, and that can't be switched off, will end up using the world's resources for its goal, leaving nothing for us.
(5) So any superintelligence is an existential catastrophe no matter its final goal — even if that goal is "make people happy" or "be honest and helpful."
(6) But (5) is obviously false. A superintelligence whose only aim is to make people happy would be the best thing that ever happened to us, not the worst.
(7) So one of (1)–(4) is wrong.

I'd definately rather not give up (1) or (2), they feel like the backbone of the whole area, so I assume the slip is in how (2) turns into (3) — some sleight of hand around "instrumental reason" — but I can't put my finger on it. Where does it actually go wrong, and if I do have to drop (1) or (2), what does that cost me?
