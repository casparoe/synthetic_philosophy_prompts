Last spring I attended a two-day workshop on the Anthropocene — mostly Earth-system scientists, with a handful of historians of science — and on the first evening two participants got into an argument that I have not been able to settle since. I am reporting it as a bystander; I did not take a side then and am not asking you to take mine now.

The first, a paleolimnologist who works on lake sediments, argued as follows. (1) The Anthropocene is a fact before it is a label: the Earth system really did move into a state dominated by a single species, and the shift is legible in sediments as a plutonium spike from atmospheric nuclear tests, which is why the Anthropocene Working Group proposed Crawford Lake, in Ontario, as the reference site for a mid-twentieth-century boundary. (2) That proposal was voted down in 2024 by the stratigraphic bodies that would have had to ratify it. She called the rejection a failure of nerve — an unwillingness to accept how recent and how thin the record is — and said the question of who decides is a distraction, because an institution can decline to register a fact but cannot unmake one.

The second, a historian of science, said this gets the order backwards. (3) Every boundary in the geological time scale is a convention ratified by a committee, including the ones we treat as bedrock; the base of the Holocene is a point in a Greenland ice core that was chosen because it is useful to mark, not because nature left a dotted line there. On that view the vote was not cowardice but the ordinary exercise of the only authority that ever makes a boundary real. (4) He added that the Working Group's own choice was made partly for the sake of readers a million years from now — the marker was selected because it will still be legible in rock — which shows the criteria are purpose-relative rather than discovered. So, he said, the question of when the Anthropocene started has no answer until you say what you want the word to do, and different purposes will legitimately yield 1610, or 1780, or 1950.

(5) She answered that purpose-relative does not mean arbitrary: some proposed boundaries are simply wrong, and we can say so, which we could not do if the criteria were merely chosen. She then proposed a criterion of her own — the line should be drawn where the human signal becomes not merely detectable but irreversible. (6) He objected that irreversibility is a fact about the future, unknowable to anyone drawing the line, so it cannot serve as a criterion for drawing it. (7) He then offered what he called a two-step version: keep 1610, the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that followed the collapse of American populations after European contact, as the onset of a long human signature, and treat 1950 as a second, sharper threshold — two boundaries for two different questions instead of one contested date. (8) She said that is only a failure to choose, relabelled.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Assess each of the eight moves separately — which are sound, which are weak, and why — rather than concluding that both parties have a point. Then say what each of them is missing, and, if you can, which of the two is closer to right about the central question of whether the start of the Anthropocene is discovered or decided.
