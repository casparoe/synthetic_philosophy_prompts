I teach a graduate seminar on collective harm and individual responsibility, and the final is a 90-minute in-class essay. The seminar has been organized around the inefficacy objection — roughly, the claim that no single person's emissions, beef purchase, or vote can be shown to make a difference to the outcome, so any account of wrongdoing that turns on making a difference cannot reach it — and I have read enough finals by now to know that students can produce the expected-value reply and the participation-or-complicity reply in their sleep. For this year's final I want one essay question that cannot be passed by reciting either, because it forces students to work at a level of resolution those replies glide over. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 900 words, and a marking scheme.

To keep the question answerable without presupposing reading the students may not have done, build it on material the prompt itself supplies. First, a three-way taxonomy of inefficacy that I use in lecture: production-inefficacy, where your token act is not among the causes that produce the outcome at all; difference-inefficacy, where the outcome is the same whether or not you act, so there is no but-for dependence, even though your act may be one of the causes, with overdetermination as the paradigm; and responsive-inefficacy, where nothing anyone else does would have been different had you acted differently — no imitation, no tipping, no coordination response. Second, a scenario I have used all term: a valley of 900 households whose reservoir will fail, cutting off the town's water for a season and falling hardest on households with infants and the elderly, unless at least 400 households cut consumption by a fixed amount over the dry months; the critical number is exact but nobody knows it, because headwater rainfall drifts it between 380 and 420, and no household can learn where it landed until the season is over, so no household can observe any effect of its own cutback, cannot tell whether it was pivotal, and cannot even assign a determinate probability to being pivotal. Third, one causal test I hand them on a single page: the necessary-element-of-a-sufficient-set test, on which an act counts as a cause of an outcome if it is a necessary element of a set of actual conditions sufficient for that outcome, so that acts failing the but-for test can still be causes.

The question should require four things. Students must use the taxonomy to say exactly which forms of inefficacy obtain in the scenario and which apparent inefficacy is merely epistemic — the difference between not knowing whether you are pivotal and not being pivotal has to be marked explicitly. They must apply the necessary-element-of-a-sufficient-set test to the watering household and report what it does and does not deliver, including whether the test treats the cutbacks and the waterings alike, and what follows for the moral judgment if it does not. They must say what a theory of individual obligation would have to look like if all three forms of inefficacy hold at once, defending one such theory against one named rival rather than listing the options on the shelf. And they must say whether their verdict transfers to voting and to meat consumption, identifying at least one disanalogy in each case. For the marking scheme, give five must-hit points with the marks each is worth out of 30, a one-line note on the error candidates typically make at each point, and a note on which of the five separates the A answers. The model answer should be candid about its own weak link: close it with a paragraph naming the step most likely to be wrong, and saying what argument or consideration would lead me to grade the opposing answer as the better one.
