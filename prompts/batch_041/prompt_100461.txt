I have been reading around Tolstoy's views on art as background for a novel in progress — it concerns a painter who turns against his own work, and I wanted to understand the older tradition of artists who repudiate art — and a friend who teaches comparative literature, knowing I was pressed for time, wrote out for me the central paragraph of the introduction to an old critical edition and told me it was the clearest short account of the theory he had come across. I have copied it below. I have learned not to take such paragraphs on trust, and since the manuscript is at the stage where a wrong idea about what art is for will do the most damage, I would rather have the thing audited now than discover the problem in the third draft.

'Art, on Tolstoy's account, is the deliberate transmission of feeling from one person to another, and what separates it from mere craft or amusement is that the feeling actually arrives — he called this infection. From this he drew a moral conclusion: since art works by making people share a feeling, and the best feelings are those that unite us in Christian brotherhood, the measure of a work is the moral lesson it carries and the number of people it can carry it to. This is why he judged art by the religious consciousness of his own age, and why his theory is often charged with making artistic value relative to a time and a place, and why he could dismiss so much of the received canon — with Beethoven and Shakespeare counted among the most revered of them, and most of his own novels as well — as not really art at all, or else as counterfeit: art manufactured to titillate and distract rather than to infect. Technique, on his view, counted for nothing; a peasant song that makes you weep is worth more than a symphony that leaves you cold.'

What I want is an audit rather than an exposition. Work through the paragraph a claim at a time and tell me, for each, what a sympathetic and well-informed reader of Tolstoy would accept as accurate, what they would mark as a simplification that has gone a step too far, and what is a genuine misrepresentation of the position — distinguishing throughout between what Tolstoy actually held and what is merely a common criticism of him. Then tell me whether the paragraph's overall verdict, that the theory is a moralism which consigns most of the canon to the ash heap, survives the corrections, and if it does not, what verdict the corrected account would support. I should declare my sympathies so that you can check them: I suspect the moral-purpose line is the real heart of the matter and the rest is decoration, and I would rather be disabused of that now than after the third draft. Please lead with your bottom line in a single sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
