So my friend Rosa is a hardcore expected-value person — she'll tell you the only question about any choice is what it does to the expected value, and she's read enough of the thought experiments about the guy who offers you a trillion blissful lives if you just hand over your wallet to be smug about it. But I've been keeping score of what she actually does, and I can't tell if she has a real rule or is just winging it. (The tickets below are hypothetical — we argue about them for fun, which is why the numbers are silly.)

The record:

- A dollar for a 1-in-a-billion shot at $10^12: she buys without blinking, says its free money in expectation.
- A dollar for a 1-in-10^30 shot at $10^40: she passes. Her words were that one in ten to the thirtieth isn't a probability, it's a way of saying never.
- A dollar for a 1-in-a-billion shot at $10^60: passes again, but for the opposite kind of reason. 10^60 dollars isn't better than 10^12 dollars, she says, it's just a longer number — there's nothing you can do with the one that you can't do with the other, so the extra zeros are decoration.
- She's not timid about stakes either: last spring she put $5,000 of her savings on a 1-in-3 shot at $20,000 and didn't lose a minutes sleep over it.

Now our friend Devi, who runs a particle detector at the lab, is offering her a bet: a dollar for a 1-in-10^13 chance at $10^14, with the randomness coming off the detector so the odds are exact. What does Rosa do? I want the prediction, but I'm more interested in the rule. Is she running a cutoff on how small a probability she'll take seriously, or a ceiling on how much a payoff can be worth to her, or something else? Does the record even tell those apart? Which one do you think is actually doing the work, and what's one more ticket I could offer her that would make it obvious which it is? And if the same bet came from a stranger on the street who said he could do it with a magic word instead of from a machine at the lab — does your prediction change, and should it?
