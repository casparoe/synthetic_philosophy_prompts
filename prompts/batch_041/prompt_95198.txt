You are a professional epistemologist with specialist knowledge of the analysis of knowledge, of externalist theories in the Nozickian tracking tradition, and of the norms governing assertion and practical reasoning. I work in the same area, and I want an audit of my own position for internal coherence before I commit it to print. Take the background for granted: do not explain the tracking theory to me, and do not survey the field. Before writing the audit, do a short brainstorming pass in your own words, setting out what each commitment below does and does not license; then write the audit itself.

Here is where I have ended up.

(1) Knowledge is true belief that tracks: if the proposition were false, I would not believe it (sensitivity), and if it were true, I would believe it (adherence), with both counterfactuals evaluated relative to the method I actually used in forming the belief. The view is thoroughly externalist: a knower need not be able to cite reasons, and need not be able to rule out alternatives to what she knows.

(2) Knowledge is not closed under known entailment. I can know p, know that p entails q, and fail to know q. I regard this as a consequence of (1), not a price paid for it.

(3) Knowledge is the norm of assertion and of practical reasoning: you may flatly assert p, and take p as a premise in deliberation, only if you know p. Asserting or relying on what you merely believe truly is criticizable, even when it works out.

(4) I know I have hands. I know that if I have hands, I am not a brain in a vat. I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, and I do not think I need to be able to answer the skeptic in order to know the first two things.

(5) Deduction is epistemically free. Competent reasoning from premises I already know adds no new epistemic risk, so a conclusion I have validly deduced from things I know is something I may assert and act on.

(6) I know a great deal on testimony: my own name, because my parents told me; when the train leaves, because I read the timetable; that the kettle will boil.

(7) I do not count the following as knowledge. A mathematician who believes, on nothing but a numerological hunch about the shape of the sentence, that there are infinitely many primes — who cannot follow a proof of it and could not tell a true arithmetical statement from a false one of similar shape — does not know that there are infinitely many primes. (The statement is a theorem, and so, on the usual view, a necessary truth.)

I have a nagging sense that something in this set does not fit together, but I cannot tell whether it is one commitment or a cluster, and I have no idea which one I would surrender.

Now the audit. Which of these commitments pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest repair in each case? Specifically: (i) state the genuine tensions, ordered by severity, giving the argument that generates each one step by step rather than announcing a verdict; (ii) identify the conflicts that dissolve on inspection and explain why they dissolve; (iii) for each genuine tension, give the cheapest way to restore consistency, say which numbered commitment has to move and what is lost by moving it; and (iv) close by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and what it costs. Do not treat every pair as equally bad, and do not hedge — where two commitments cannot both be true, say so plainly. Argue it out; I want no references and no reading list.
