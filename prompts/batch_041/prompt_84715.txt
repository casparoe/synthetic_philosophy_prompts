I am a journalist who covers decision theory and the governance of catastrophe-risk philanthropy, and for the past year I have served as the outside lay member of the risk committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds biosecurity and AI-safety work. The committee meets Thursday, and I owe it a written opinion. Please draft that document for me.

The committee has two items, which the chair insists are really one item. The first is a message the foundation received last month. It came from an anonymous sender who is, by all external assessments, extremely good at modeling the behavior of institutions like ours — our grantmaking principles are published, our board minutes are public, and our counterpart in another country has announced a policy of never paying. The message states that it was sent to us if and only if exactly one of two things holds: a cache of dangerous material at one of our field sites has gone missing and will be released, or we transfer $400,000 to a designated account. Internal estimates put the harm from a release at roughly $40 million. The sender has sent nothing further and has set no deadline, which several trustees read as a sign that the message is a cheap imitation rather than a real threat.

The second item is a standing arrangement a partner organization has offered us. A random event determines whether we are asked to contribute $400,000 into a shared pool; in the branch where we are not asked, we receive $4 million if and only if the partner's predictor judged that we would have contributed had we been asked. The partner is honest and its predictor is accurate about nine times in ten. Signing would commit us in advance, and signing is itself observable.

The chair keeps saying this is a question about whether a foundation can be "updateless," and the committee is split: some members argue that paying in the first case is indistinguishable from capitulating to ordinary extortion and would mark us as a target, while others argue that a funder which refuses to consider the branch in which its own policy was tested is simply leaving money and lives on the table. I need a document I can circulate and defend. Recommend a concrete course of action on each item, with a time frame; state the general policy the foundation should adopt for future messages of this shape, with criteria staff can actually apply; and give an honest account of the strongest objection to your recommendation, including the objection that a board with public minutes may be unable to commit to anything at all, and the objection that spending present funds for the benefit of a branch that did not occur is a breach of fiduciary duty rather than a subtle form of rationality.
