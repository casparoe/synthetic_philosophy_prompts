I am not a philosopher by training — I read on my own and argue about it with friends — and I have been trying to work out a consistent position on Bernard Williams's complaint about impartial morality, the objection that it makes the agent have one thought too many. Before I commit any of it to paper, I would like you to act as a careful philosopher and audit the set of commitments below for consistency. Please begin by restating each commitment in your own words so I can see that you have grasped the stakes, then map which pairs genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and which single commitment, if abandoned, would relieve the most pressure — and tell me what I would be giving up in abandoning it. I would rather you discriminate than hedge: if two of these can be held together with care, say so and say why, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally bad.

Here is where I have landed. (a) Morality is impartial or agent-neutral: from the moral point of view every person counts equally, and whether an act is right depends on how it affects everyone, not on whose act it is. (b) Consequentialism should be understood as a criterion of rightness rather than a decision procedure — a theory can be self-effacing, so what makes an act right need not be what the agent is thinking about when she acts; she can simply act out of love or loyalty, and the theory can bless it afterward. (c) Williams is right that a morality which demands the sacrifice of an agent's deepest projects whenever the general welfare calls for it alienates her from her own life and leaves her with nothing to be loyal to. (d) In the case of the man who saves his wife rather than a stranger, I think his reason for saving her is the relationship itself, and that this is not merely a permissible bit of psychology but the right description of what he is doing and why; the impartial thought, if it enters at all, distorts the act rather than merely cluttering it. (e) Moral requirements are overriding: nothing I care about can make it permissible for me to do what morality forbids, and the moral point of view is entitled to sit in judgment on any project, however central. (f) Blame, guilt, and the reactive attitudes track the reasons an agent had or acted on; a theory that says an act is right while conceding that the agent need not have been able to see it as right, and would have been criticizable for thinking about it, seems to be describing something other than the morality I actually hold people to.

I suspect I am trying to keep both Williams and the impartialist, and I would like to know precisely where that fails. Please be concrete about which of (a) through (f) I would have to drop or weaken, and whether there is a way of reading them — for instance, of separating the question of what makes an act right from the question of what reasons the agent has — that makes the set coherent without my having to give up the thought that morality is impartial and overriding.
