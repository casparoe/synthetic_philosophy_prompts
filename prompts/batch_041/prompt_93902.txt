I am a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I have been stuck for about a month on something a friend in my democratic theory seminar put on the board. We had been reading around the boundary problem — the thought that a democracy cannot settle who its own members are without already presupposing a demos, so that the people who author the rules are never the people the rules say are the authors — and he claimed the puzzle dissolves into something considerably worse. His argument, transcribed as he wrote it:

(1) A decision is legitimate for a person only if she is among those who author it; anything else is being governed by others rather than ruling herself. (2) A person has a claim to a share in authoring a decision whenever that decision affects her interests. (3) Any decision affects the interests of anyone whose life it alters, however indirectly — through prices, through borders, through the climate, through the precedent it sets for later decisions that will reach her. He calls this the seepage principle and insists it is not a slippery slope but a truism about causation: there is no principled threshold of affectedness, only a difference of degree, and any nonzero degree triggers the claim. (4) Therefore every person affected by a decision, however remotely, has a claim to a share in authoring it. (5) So for any decision of any body — a national election, a city council's zoning vote, my department's choice of a search committee — an indefinitely large set of people, most of whom the body has never heard of, have a claim to a share in it. (6) A claim that no institutional arrangement could in principle satisfy is not a claim anyone can be required to honor, since ought implies can. (7) No arrangement could give everyone affected a share in every decision. (8) Therefore the claims generated at (5) cannot be honored, and by (1) no political decision is legitimate.

He takes this as a proof, not a reductio: he says the argument is valid, that (1) and (3) are the two premises he would defend to the death, and that the upshot is simply that legitimacy is a fiction we should stop invoking and that the demos can never be right about itself. I cannot accept (8). Our seminar voted last week on which of us presents first, and it would be absurd to say that vote exercised illegitimate authority over a farmer in another hemisphere; the same goes for the everyday business of any legislature that at least tries to answer to the people it governs. When I pressed him, he said the only ways out are to deny that self-rule is what legitimacy is, or to draw a line inside the causal stream between being affected and having a claim to authorship — and that any such line is arbitrary, so the argument stands. I have noticed that being affected comes in degrees while enfranchisement is all or nothing, and that a person can belong to many overlapping demoi at once, but he says both observations just restate the seepage principle rather than answer it, and I cannot tell whether he is right.

What I want from you is a diagnosis. Tell me which step or premise you reject, then explain why it looked so plausible in the first place and what rejecting it actually costs — not just in this argument, but for the rest of what I would have to give up. I would rather surrender (3) than either (1) or (2), because the thought that being affected matters seems to me the moral core of the whole idea and I am not ready to say that legitimacy has nothing to do with self-rule; but I want to know the bill before I sign. If the argument instead has a hidden flaw — an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a step that is valid only under a reading the earlier premises do not support — name it precisely and say what the argument would have to look like without it. And if it is valid and the real question is which plausible premise is least costly to abandon, tell me which one the main positions on the boundary problem would sacrifice and what each of them loses by doing so. Finally, I would like to know whether there is any version of the affectedness idea that does not seep — some principled way to distinguish the people who merely feel the ripples of a decision from the people who have a standing claim to share in making it — or whether every such distinction is finally arbitrary and my friend has simply been honest about where the reasoning leads.
