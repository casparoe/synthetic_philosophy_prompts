Ok here's something thats bugged me for a while. In most places voting is treated as a right -- you can't be barred from it for being poor, or the wrong religion, or a woman -- and rights are things you're allowed to exercise or not, on your own time. But a handful of countries fine you if you don't show up, and talk about it like jury duty or taxes. Same ballot, opposite framing. Then the edges of the franchise seem to run on yet a third logic: some places let 16 year olds vote in at least some elections, some let long-term resident non-citizens vote locally but not nationally, and nobody lets actual children vote at all below some line. Nobody's confused about any of this, apparently.

So what does the actual practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the franchise for me: is a vote basically a right, a duty, a little bit of power, or just a piece of a fair procedure? What picture of political equality does compulsory voting need -- equal say, or equal turnout? -- and does that clash with the rights framing, or can you have both? For the age line and the citizenship line, is the hidden test competence, membership/stake, or being subject to the law? Because it looks like different groups get different tests, and I want to know if that's a contradiction or just tiered application of one idea.

Then tell me which commitments the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits comfortably, and where it's underdetermined so several theories would license the same rules. Finally: if we replaced it with a plain affected-interests view -- anyone whose life is shaped by the decision gets a vote -- what concretely would have to change, and what would that break?
