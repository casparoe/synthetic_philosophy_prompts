I'm teaching the pain week of our philosophy of mind seminar next month -- twelve grad students, most of whom have never read a word of the clinical literature and one of whom is a former physio who will fact-check me -- and I'm rebuilding the handout from scratch. Last year I gave them a list of -isms (hedonic primitivism, desire-based accounts, evaluativism, imperativism, the two-component view) and they dutifully memorized the names and then couldn't tell me what any of them actually predicted about a real patient, which is the only thing I care about. So this year I want a decision tree / taxonomy that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like. The question I want it to sort answers to is: in virtue of what is an episode of pain bad for the creature having it, and what does the asymbolic patient show about that badness? You know the phenomenon I mean -- a lesion somewhere in the insula/parietal region, or a cingulotomy, or a decent dose of morphine, and the person can still detect the pinprick, still say roughly where it is, still rate it as intense, but doesn't withdraw, will sometimes chat and smile while you're doing it, and reports that the sensation just doesn't bother them; they'll say they have pain but they aren't suffering. I want the tree to be usable with the reverse case too (the patient who keeps the horribleness but loses the where and the how-much), with congenital insensitivity to pain, with hypnotic analgesia, and with the masochist and the religious flagellant, since those last two are the ones my students always raise and I want a branch that can handle them. Please skip the reading list and the citations entirely -- I know the literature, and anything you half-remember or invent will just annoy me; if you want to name a position, name the position (imperativism, evaluativism, etc.) and not a person or a paper.

Concretely: start by brainstorming the logical space out loud before you attach any names to anything -- I'd rather see the questions first and the positions second. Every internal node should be a question a student could answer about a case without already knowing where it leads, so no node may be "do you think pain's badness is intrinsic?"; ask about the asymbolic patient instead, or about whether the hurt could in principle come apart from the sensation. Each leaf gets (a) the position's name, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, (b) one sentence on what it must say about the asymbolic patient specifically, and (c) one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want at least these as leaves: a view on which the hurt is simply constitutive of pain, so the asymbolic patient isn't really in pain at all; a two-component view, sensation plus seperate hedonic tone; a desire- or aversion-based account of the badness; an evaluativist representationalist account; an imperativist account; and a deflationary view on which the whole dispute is verbal, or is about how we use the word "pain". If two of those leaves come out indistinguishable given the cases available, flag them as a pair the tree can't seperate rather than fudging it -- I'd much rather have a note than a fake distinction. Same for branches nobody occupies: say whether the combination is empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has bothered.

Keep the whole thing to a page and a half or so, and write it for someone answering from the armchair -- no lesion diagrams, no statistics, no "studies show". Think it through step by step before you commit, and if you find the tree collapsing into a single axis (badness inside the sensation vs. badness outside it) then tell me so, because that's roughly what happened last year and I'd like to know whether it's inevitable. And at the end, say which leaf you think is hardest to hold onto once you've got the asymbolic patient properly in view, and why -- that's the part of the handout I actually want them arguing about.
