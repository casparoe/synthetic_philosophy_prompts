I'm trying to get my head around Newton's Scholium in the Principia — the one at the start where he lays out the definitions of time, space, place, and motion, and then gives the bucket experiment. I don't have the book with me and I'm working from my own paraphrase, so tell me if I've mangled anything, but here's the gist as I understand it:

Newton starts by distinguishing absolute, true, and mathematical time from relative, apparent, and common time; then absolute space, which in its own nature and without relation to anything external remains always similar and immovable, from relative space, which is a movable measure of it; then absolute and relative place and motion. He says the common people conceive motion only in a relative way, and that it's possible for nothing to be truly at rest that is commonly taken to be so — the parts of a moving ship are moving, even though they seem at rest relative to the ship. Then he gives the bucket. A vessel hanging by a long cord is twisted up and then released. First the vessel turns while the water stays flat; then gradually the water takes on the motion of the vessel, and as it comes to share the rotation, its surface becomes concave, rising at the edges. Finally, when the water and vessel move together, the water is at rest relative to the vessel, yet its surface is still concave; and when the vessel slows down, the water keeps spinning and the concavity persists. Newton's point seems to be that the concavity — which he reads as the water's endeavor to recede from the axis — tracks neither the water's motion relative to the vessel nor the vessel's motion relative to the water. He also has a second case, two globes joined by a cord and rotating about their common center, where the tension in the cord reveals an endeavor to recede from the axis even in a void where there's nothing else to compare them to.

Here's what I actually need help with. I can state Newton's conclusion, but I can't get clear on what exactly the argument is supposed to establish, and I suspect I'm conflating several things:

First, is the bucket supposed to show that absolute motion is real, or only that rotation is not analyzable as rotation relative to any of the nearby bodies? Those seem like different claims and I can't tell which one Newton needs.

Second, the two-globes case confuses me more than it's supposed to. If there are only two globes and they're tied together and spinning around each other, then their rotation relative to each other is perfectly well-defined — a relationist has no problem there. So what does the void add? Is the argument only supposed to bite when there's a single body rotating relative to nothing at all? And if so, what would a relationist say about a bucket that is the only thing in the universe — no distant stars, no other matter — where the water still climbs the sides?

Third, I want to poke at a variant and I don't know if it's a fair test or just a confusion. Suppose you freeze the water solid while it's spinning and concave, then stop the bucket and take it away, leaving a spinning concave disk of ice alone in space. Does the ice still count as rotating? It has no relation to anything and no vessel to be relative to, but it still has whatever internal state produced the bulge. And a related case: suppose the bulge is produced, then the ice is brought to a stop, but a flywheel inside it keeps spinning. Does the surface state or the internal motion do the evidential work? I keep feeling that Newton is pointing at something real and that the relationist has to either accept irreducible inertial structure or find a way to make the bucket's history and surroundings do the work, but I can't tell whether I'm making a genuine distinction or just dressing up the same question in different costumes.

Can you walk me through what the argument is actually claiming, where the standard relationist replies land, and whether the variant cases I invented are doing any work or are just restatements? I'd also like to know if there's a reading where the whole dispute is verbal — two ways of describing one set of facts rather than two rival pictures of the world. If that's a serious option, say so and tell me what it costs. I'm not looking for a reading list, I just want to understand the shape of the thing.
