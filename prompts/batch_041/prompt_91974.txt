I am a retired geophysicist who has spent the last several years reading my way into philosophy, and I have lately been trying to get clear on Popper's critique of historicism. In a reading group I attend, a fellow member—an economic historian with a broadly positivist outlook—gave the following summary, and I would like a careful audit of it before I decide how much to accept. His account ran roughly as follows. Popper's central claim is that there can be no laws of history, because human beings possess free will and their choices are therefore unpredictable; in addition, the course of history depends on the growth of knowledge, and since we cannot predict what we shall know in the future, we cannot predict history either. Historicism, on this reading, is the belief that history follows inexorable laws or rhythms that social scientists can discover and use to forecast the future; Popper's argument is that this belief is not only false but dangerous, since it underwrites utopian social engineering and the suppression of criticism. The upshot, my colleague concluded, is that social science should abandon the search for general laws altogether and confine itself to describing particular events and trends; any attempt to find regularities in social life is a residue of historicism. He added that Popper's position is essentially a version of indeterminism, and that it has been vindicated by the failure of Marxist predictions.

Something about this leaves me uneasy. I spent a career with laws of nature, and the idea that history has laws does seem dubious to me; yet I suspect my colleague may have made Popper's position cruder than it is. Could you go through his account claim by claim and separate what a philosopher of science would accept as accurate, what is a common simplification or conflation, and what is actually wrong? I am particularly interested in whether Popper denied that there can be any laws in the social sciences, how his distinction between laws and trends bears on the claim, whether his critique rests on free will or indeterminism, and whether the conclusion that social science should abandon general laws follows from his arguments. If the summary misstates what historicism was for Popper, please say so, and explain what his alternative ideas—piecemeal social engineering, situational logic, the unity of method, the Oedipus effect—are meant to accomplish. Finally, I would like to know which parts a defender of Popper would object to most strongly, and whether any of my colleague's critical conclusions survive once the misstatements are corrected. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want the conceptual audit.
