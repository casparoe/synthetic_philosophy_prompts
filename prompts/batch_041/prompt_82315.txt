I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on practical rationality, and I need one document I can circulate to two colleagues who have now argued for two years about the escalation that the so-called dollar auction produces. The game is this: a prize of fixed, publicly announced value is auctioned, bids rise in small increments, the highest bidder pays what he bid and takes the prize, and the second-highest bidder also pays what he bid and receives nothing. Bidding routinely continues past the value of the prize, and both of the last two bidders end up paying more than the prize was ever worth.

One of my colleagues holds what I will call the error theory: this escalation is a systematic failure of practical rationality, the sunk-cost fallacy in its purest institutional form, and the trapped bidder is best described as someone who is making a mistake at each step -- a mistake he could have avoided entirely by declining to enter. The other holds the structural reading: once the auction is under way, continuing is the better response to the situation the bidder is actually in, so the defect lies in the incentive structure rather than in anyone's reasoning, and "irrational" is functioning as a term of abuse for a pattern that a sensible agent with ordinary preferences -- including preferences about not having been made a fool of, and about not having wasted what he has already spent -- would reliably reproduce.

Draft the joint statement these two could put their names to. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both accept, covering the structure of the game, what a rational agent would do at the moment of entry, the qualitative pattern of behaviour that both agree is to be explained, and whatever else they can settle. No claim may smuggle in a concession in the guise of neutral description.
2. An exact statement of what remains in dispute, with each position formulated in the wording its own adherent would accept, so that a reader can see the two are genuinely incompatible rather than differently phrased. Where part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and supply the disambiguated formulation; I suspect at least the question whether "irrational" is predicated of the choice given the agent's preferences or of the preferences themselves.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side -- together with, for each, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidates I would like considered, though they should add or drop as they see fit: whether the escalation survives when bids are sealed and each bidder must post a whole schedule in advance, and whether the two-bidder and many-bidder versions are the same phenomenon or two.
4. One variant of the auction that both sides agree would discriminate between their views, with predictions written down before the outcome is known and an explicit statement of what result each side would count as evidence against its own position. One candidate I want included among those considered: the auction in which the runner-up's payment buys him a consolation of stated value, so that bidding past the prize is no longer transparently a mistake. Whether that variant helps one side or dissolves the dispute is for them to judge.

The document should stand or fall by its argument, so keep it free of citations and appeals to authority. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with their pre-registered predictions.
