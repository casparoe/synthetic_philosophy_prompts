I would like to rehearse a live argument rather than read an essay, so please take the part of a committed Scanlonian contractualist: someone who affirms the individualist restriction on aggregation (in determining what we owe to each other, we may not justify a principle by summing gains and losses across different people) and treats that restriction as a genuine discovery rather than a temporary embarrassment to be bargained away. Play the position at full strength and in good faith — steelman it, concede a point when it is genuinely conceded, but never concede the central one, and do not break character until I type 'stop'. Keep each of your turns to a single paragraph, as if we were talking across a table rather than writing papers, and no appeals to what some commentator has said; I want the argument itself, not the literature. I come to this from the rationalist blogs and forums, so I will be keeping score and will notice if you win a step by quietly redefining a term. End each turn with one candid line outside the flow of argument: your confidence in the position you have just defended, and the single claim within it you are least sure of.

Here is my opening. I control a single vent in a chemical accident. Down one line are five hundred people who will each suffer a month of a painful but fully recoverable neuropathy; down the other line is one person, Vera, who will suffer that same neuropathy plus a year without smell or taste — a somewhat worse harm, but still non-lethal and non-permanent. Every consequentialist instinct I have says vent toward Vera, and without hesitating: five hundred person-months of pain plainly outweigh one person-month plus a year of dulled senses, and the total is lower that way. But your individualist restriction seems to forbid precisely the comparison I just made. Taken seriously, doesn't it tell me to afflict the five hundred, since Vera's is the strongest single claim and no one is permitted to add the five hundred complaints together to outweigh it? If contractualism's answer here is 'the five hundred,' you have a view no one can live with; and if the answer is 'Vera,' tell me what did the work, because from where I sit that looks like aggregation wearing a new name.
