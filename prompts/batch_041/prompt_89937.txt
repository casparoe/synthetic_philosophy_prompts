I'm a novelist and I'm supposed to be good with other people's arguments, but my two best freinds got into one at my dinner table last night and I genuinely can't tell who was being unreasonable. Here's the setup: our mutual friend Jo is trying to decide whether to have a kid. Jo has a genetic condition that runs in the family, and the upshot is that a kid of Jo's has roughly a one-in-five chance of a life that's genuinely hard — a lot of pain and medical stuff — and about a four-in-five chance of an ordinary good life like anyone else's. Jo asked the table what we thought and it went like this.

(1) Dev said Jo shouldn't do it. One in five is a huge number when the downside isn't yours to carry. If you roll the dice and the kid draws the bad life, you're the one who put them in it, and "I couldn't be sure" isn't much of a defense when you knew the odds perfectly well.

(2) Maya said that's backwards. Four in five is also a huge number. If Jo doesn't have the kid, a person who would have been glad to be alive never gets to exist at all, and that's a real loss, given up over a risk. She said refusing is like never getting on a plane because planes sometimes crash — you're letting a small chance of disaster outweigh a big chance of something good.

(3) Dev said the plane thing is exactly the wrong comparison, because you can't weigh "a good life" against "no life" the way you weigh flying against not flying. If Jo doesn't have the kid, there's nobody sitting around deprived of that good life. But if Jo does have the kid and the kid suffers, there's a real person who really suffers. So the harm is a harm to someone, while the good is a good to no one in particular. You can wrong a person by bringing them into a bad life; you can't wrong a person by declining to bring them into a good one, because there's no person there to wrong. That's why it isn't a symmetric gamble.

(4) Maya said that can't be right, because Dev just said the suffering kid is wronged by being brought into existence — which treats existing as something that can be bad for you. But if existing can be bad for you, it can be good for you, and if it can be good for you then not existing can be bad for you, and now the two sides are symmetric again and the asymmetry Dev wanted is gone. Dev said no, it's about what we owe, not about keeping score — we owe it to actual people not to saddle them with terrible suffering, and we don't owe existence to anyone, because there's nobody there to owe it to. Maya said "terrible" is doing all the work in that sentence, and Dev can't say where ordinary hardship ends and terrible begins.

I refereed by pouring more wine. Can you go through it move by move, (1) through (4) — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a line of justification — and say who's right about what, and where each of them is missing something? I don't need a single winner, but if you had to say who's more right overall, who is it, and why?
