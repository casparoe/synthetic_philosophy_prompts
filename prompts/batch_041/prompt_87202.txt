I'm in a graduate epistemology seminar that has spent several weeks on luminosity and the KK principle, and a recurring move in discussion is to say, with an air of finality, "if you know, you know that you know." It came up again over a case we constructed. A doctor, R., has what would ordinarily be conclusive evidence for a diagnosis—a positive result on a test with a very low false-positive rate, plus compatible symptoms—but she has just encountered a credible report that the test performs much worse in patients with a particular comorbidity that R.'s patient may have. R. has not yet determined whether the report applies. One classmate says: if R. really knows the diagnosis, then she knows that she knows it; since she plainly does not know that she knows it—she is unsure whether her evidence is undermined—she does not know the diagnosis after all. Another classmate adds that this is simply what people mean by saying that knowledge is luminous. I suspect the slogan is conflating several different claims, but I cannot say exactly which.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the slogan might be making, from the nearly trivial to the very strong, and for each say what it would require in R.'s case, whether it is plausible on its own, and whether it would actually license my classmate's conclusion? I am particularly interested in where the iterated-knowledge schema Kp → KKp comes apart from a transparency or luminosity thesis about being in a position to know, and where both come apart from a requirement that one be able to rule out defeaters. Please organize the readings by what they assume about the relation between first-order knowledge and higher-order knowledge, and include among the candidates the idea that knowledge is luminous, the idea that knowing entails being in a position to know that one knows, and the idea—call it No Higher-Order Surprises—that knowing entails being in a position to know that no undermining evidence is forthcoming. Flag which reading is probably the one people intend when they use the slogan to shut down a higher-order-evidence worry, which readings are so weak they would settle nothing, and which are strong enough to be false. I would like a menu I can bring to seminar, not a single favored interpretation, and I am not asking for a literature review or citations.
