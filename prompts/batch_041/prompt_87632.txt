For the past several weeks a small reading group I sit in on has been circling the topic of actions that are morally admirable without being morally required, and I've come to suspect that my verdicts on cases are more stable than any principle I can articulate, so I'd like you to tell me what, if anything, they add up to. Here are the verdicts. An off-duty lifeguard who sees a child drowning in a public pool and jumps in: I take that to be not merely admirable but required — if he can swim and no one else is there, it would be wrong of him to walk on, and "he was off duty" doesn't excuse him. A soldier who throws himself on a grenade to save his squad: admirable past anything I can imagine doing, but I don't think he was required to do it, and I don't think he'd have been wrong to dive for cover. A healthy adult who donates a kidney to a stranger: likewise admirable but not required. A person who gives away, say, eighty percent of a large salary to effective charities over decades: admirable, and again I can't bring myself to call it required, though I notice I feel worse about someone who gives nothing at all. An office worker who stays three hours late to help a colleague meet a deadline he could meet alone with effort: that's just decent, not in the same category at all, and it wouldn't be wrong to go home. And a surgeon who, alone at the scene of a crash, performs an emergency procedure at real risk to herself: required, in my view, for the same reason as the lifeguard. Now the pair that defeats me: I think the surgeon at the crash is required to act, but I do not think a surgeon is required to volunteer her weekends at a free clinic, even though the expected lives saved are comparable and the risk to her is comparable or less. Both cases seem to have the same benefit, the same cost, and the same competence in play, so I can't see what makes one required and the other optional — and yet I can't shake the verdicts.

I've tried to state a principle myself and it keeps failing, so I'd rather you build the account from the verdicts. My first attempt was in terms of what I've been calling a demandability ceiling: an act is required when the benefit to others is large enough relative to the cost to the agent, and merely admirable when it exceeds that threshold. That seems to get the grenade, the kidney, and the late-night colleague right, but it either makes the surgeon's volunteering required (which I resist) or the crash procedure optional (which I resist), so it collapses on exactly the pair that puzzles me. My second attempt added that the act is required only when the agent is uniquely positioned — the only one who can do it, or one of very few — which saves the crash case and the lifeguard, but then it seems to make the stranger-kidney case required too, since the recipient dies without a donor and donors are scarce, and I don't believe that. A friend suggested adding that the agent must not have to undergo bodily invasion or surrender a life project, which would save the kidney and the volunteering, but it threatens the grenade case, where I want to say the soldier was permitted to live.

So: is there a coherent principle, or a small set of them, that delivers my verdicts, including the pair I can't reconcile? If some principle saves all of them but needs three or four independent clauses, say so, and tell me which clauses earn their keep and which are ad hoc. If a simpler principle would force me to give up a verdict, tell me which one you'd sacrifice and why. I'd also like the account to pronounce on two matters I haven't settled. First, whether a supererogatory act, once done, generates any kind of entitlement or credit that makes later omissions less criticizable — if I give the kidney, am I thereafter more excused for giving nothing to charity, or is that thought a confusion? Second, whether the very existence of a category of the admirable-but-optional is compatible with holding that one of the available acts is morally better than the other; for if the grenade-diver's act is better than diving for cover, and diving for cover is nonetheless permissible, I can't quite see what "better" is doing there, and I want to know whether I must give up the word, give up the permissibility of diving for cover, or whether there's a way to keep both. Please state the principle precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
