I sit on the board of a small mutual aid fund: roughly forty households in a valley where individual health coverage is expensive and unreliable. Households pay in monthly on a sliding scale tied to income, and when a member is hit with a medical bill, we vote on how much of it to cover. We have never written down a policy. What we have instead is a sentence we repeat at every meeting — we cover what lands on you out of the blue, and we cover less, sometimes nothing, when you went looking for it — and no one has ever been willing to say what looking for it means. We now have a claim that will force us to.

Dana, forty-seven, has been a member for eight years. She has a progressive lung disease that will require something like $180,000 of treatment over the next two years. Her doctors are unusually clear about the mechanism: she carries a gene variant that by itself almost never causes the disease, and for fifteen years, beginning at nineteen, she worked in a small finishing shop where she breathed solvent fumes all day, which by itself almost never causes it either. Together, the doctors say, the two make the disease close to inevitable.

That is the easy half of the case. The hard half is everything around it. Dana took the shop job at nineteen because it paid better than anything else in town, because her father had worked there, and, as she puts it, because it never occurred to her that there was a decision to make. She left at thirty-four, when the shop closed. She joined our fund at thirty-nine, long after the exposure had ended, and because our dues scale with income she has paid in about $9,000 in total.

Two other members bear on this. Marcus has the same diagnosis and the same gene variant; he learned about the risk at twenty-five and left the same trade, taking a large pay cut and moving towns to do it. He has never filed a claim. And Ruth, who has paid in for twenty-two years and never asked us for anything, receives a monthly payment toward an ongoing treatment that comes out of our reserve; paying Dana in full would suspend Ruth's payments for about six months.

The board meets Thursday. What should we do with Dana's claim? What sentence should we write down in place of the one we have been repeating — one that tells us what to do about Dana, about Marcus if he ever claims, and about members like Ruth? Does it matter that Dana's exposure ended years before she joined us, or that she has paid in far less than she now needs? And where does the rule you propose go wrong: what further case would expose its worst implication?
