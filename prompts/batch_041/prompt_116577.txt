You are the advice columnist for the in-house magazine of a society that has already adopted the repugnant conclusion as public policy — a world of very many people whose lives are, on balance, barely worth living. Write one installment of that column: roughly 600 words, and under no circumstances more than 650.

Format it as an agony-aunt page: three letters from readers, each followed by your reply. The venue's masthead motto should appear first and should state the column's governing conclusion flatly and without irony; the final line should be deadpan and should not wink. Give the magazine a plausible in-universe name and the columnist a pen name.

Each letter must press a different genuine objection to the arithmetic that put these readers where they are:

1. One reader is puzzled that adding people whose lives are worth living, however barely, could make the world worse, since no one is harmed by the addition.
2. One reader traces a chain of worlds, each intuitively no worse than the last, that ends somewhere he cannot accept, and wants to know which step to deny.
3. One reader argues that since the people in the crowded world are different people from those in the sparse one, nobody has been made worse off, so his unease must be a confusion.

The humor has to come from the content, not from winking: a population ethicist should find the column funny because it gets the axiology right. So the replies must be internally consistent with totalism throughout. Do not let the columnist quietly retreat into average welfare, a neutral level, or a person-affecting restriction she has not argued for; if she does concede anything, it must be a visible, deliberate concession and the piece must treat it as one. Along the way the column should convey, without ever naming or stating it, the uncomfortable result that no plausible population axiology honors all of our considered judgments at once, and it should leave a reader feeling both the pull of the conclusion and the pull against it.

Include at least one concrete detail of daily life or policy in this world that follows from taking the total seriously — an incentive, a form, a ceremony, a piece of jargon — and let it do some of the satirical work.

Do not quote or reproduce any published text; the letters, replies, masthead, and policy details are yours to invent.
