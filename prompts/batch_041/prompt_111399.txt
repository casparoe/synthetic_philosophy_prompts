Im writing the take-home for my third-year ethics seminar and I need one essay question on the aggregation problem — the one where you pick between one person being tortured for fifty years and an absurd number of people each getting a dust speck in the eye. My trouble is that every version of this I've seen can be answered by a student who has just memorised the two positions: the aggregator does the arithmetic, the anti-aggregator says no quantity of small harms can ever add up to a big one, and then they both stop. I want a question that breaks that script.

So heres the twist I want you to build it around. Keep the shape of the choice, change what the tiny harms are. Instead of a speck in the eye, each of the many people loses one nanosecond of life. Nobody feels anything, no instrument could pick it up, nothing about any of their days is any different. The only true thing you can say is that each of them dies a nanosecond earlier than they otherwise would have, and is therefore short one nanosecond of whatever goods their life would otherwise have contained. The other side is unchanged: one person, fifty years of torture. So the choice is now between one enormous harm and a number of harms so small that you have to say what you think a harm even is before you can start counting.

What I want back:

1) The exam question itself, in three parts, roughly like this — (a) 8 marks: what has to be true of something for it to count as a harm at all, and does a nanosecond of missing life qualify; (b) 10 marks: given your answer to (a), what should you do, and what general principle about adding up small harms does that commit you to, showing it is not just one of the two stock positions with new paint; (c) 7 marks: the strongest objection to your own answer in (b) and what you would have to give up to meet it. Resequence or sharpen the parts if you can make the crux sharper, but keep 25 marks total.

2) A model answer of about 700 words, written the way a strong student would write it, not as a survey of what other people have said.

3) A marking scheme: the specific moves a top-band answer has to make, the specific mistakes that should cost marks, and one line per point on how students usually get it wrong. I also want the scheme to carry one consistency test I can run on any script: if a script says the nanoseconds don't count because nobody can feel them, it has to say the same about a tiny benefit — a nanosecond of extra life handed to a huge number of people. If it treats a nanosecond of gain as worth something and a nanosecond of loss as worth nothing, that asymmetry should cost it marks, and I want the scheme to say so outright. If you reckon that test is wrong-headed, say why in a line and give me a better one instead.

Two constraints. No names anywhere in the question itself — no philosophers, no paper titles, no labels ending in -ism (the model answer can name things if it helps me grade). And the question has to carry the case itself, so a student who did none of the reading can still sit down and answer it. Don't ask them to cite anything either.
