I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend about expected-value fanaticism — I take the view that a rational agent maximizes expected value however the numbers happen to fall out, while she holds that any decision driven by a vanishingly small probability of an astronomically large payoff has already gone wrong somewhere — and we argued to the point where each of us could state the other's position accurately and still neither of us would move an inch. Before we have that argument a second time, I want us to have the document we should have written together. You are a philosopher working on decision theory, population ethics, and the foundations of axiology. Think carefully before you begin, and start by brainstorming, in a few lines, the claims that are genuinely common ground between the two camps and the places where the two of us may simply be talking past each other; then set that brainstorming aside and write the finished text.

Produce the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between the two camps could actually put its name to. It should contain, in order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims, each one short enough that both signatories could defend it in isolation. These should cover: what expected-value maximization is as a formal rule and what it does not by itself settle; the structure of the familiar mugging-style cases and which features of them are contested versus uncontested; the distinction between what a decision rule requires and what an agent may reasonably be blamed or praised for doing; and whatever can be agreed about how credences over tiny probabilities and over enormous or unbounded outcomes should be formed, elicited, and compared.

2. A disagreements section in which each residual difference is stated twice — once in wording the fanatic would endorse, once in wording the anti-fanatic would endorse — such that neither wording is a strawman of the other. Where a difference is at bottom verbal, say so explicitly and show the translation between the two vocabularies rather than listing it as a substantive disagreement.

3. A crux section: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side. I expect at least these to be in play, but use your own judgment: whether the value of outcomes is bounded above or scale-free; whether "more expected value" is even a well-defined comparison when the relevant distributions have unbounded or non-integrable tails; and whether a rule's being formally impeccable is sufficient reason to follow it when it yields counterintuitive verdicts.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance in the same neutral format. As one of these, use the following constructed case, which my friend and I kept circling: a ladder of gambles in which the payoff doubles each rung while the probability halves, so that every rung has exactly the same expected value, from a rung that is nearly a sure thing up to a rung whose payoff is astronomically large and whose probability is correspondingly minute. Both sides should state, before any verdict, whether they predict that an idealized agent is indifferent across the rungs, and if not, exactly where and why the ladder breaks.

5. A short closing section, signed by both, stating what each side would count as grounds for abandoning its own position and whether there is anything the two of them agree would actually move the debate.

Begin the response with the document itself — no preface, no commentary on this request, no restatement of it — and let the first numbered claim be the single proposition the two signatories regard as the most important thing they now jointly affirm. Use a numbered list for the agreed claims and a table for the disagreements, with one column for the crux, one for each side's own wording, and one for each side's prediction.
