I'm a philosophy PhD student and I've somehow ended up in an anthropics reading group this term, and I keep bouncing off the self-sampling vs. self-indication distinction. My advisor says the only way it really clicks is to build a case by hand and watch the two assumptions peel apart, instead of reading yet another recap of the presumptuous philosopher argument — you know the one, the coin flip, the two civilizations, the punchline that you should figure you're in the absurdly bigger one. So that's what I want to do, except I don't want to just re-run the standard case with the numbers filed off. When I say the self-sampling assumption I mean the rule that you should reason as if you were a random one of the observers who actually exist; when I say the self-indication assumption I mean the rule that the bare fact of your existing is itself evidence for hypotheses that imply more observers.

Here's the ask: build me a thought experiment that satisfies a fairly tight set of constraints, then convince me it actually meets them. Constraints: (1) everything finite and small — a handful of people at most, no infinities, no merely-possible observers, no exotic physics or branching worlds, nothing that needs a supercomputer just to state; (2) the 'you' in the story exists exactly once in each branch, so the usual more-copies-of-me-means-more-likely lever is switched off; (3) despite that, SSA and SIA have to disagree about which branch you're in, and I'd like the disagreement to hinge on something other than raw headcount — your position, a serial number, an ordering, an index, something like that. If you think (2) and (3) can't be jointly satisfied, then show me why and give me the nearest thing that does; I'd rather learn where the wall is than get a fudge. (4) The verdicts have to fall out of the assumptions rather than be written into the setup — if the case only works because you stipulated the answer, it doesn't count. Seed, if it helps: numbered sleep-pods, a cloning queue, an incubator with rooms, whatever you like, but keep it small.

Once it's built, walk me through the likelihoods the way each assumption computes them, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work and what happens when I delete it, and say how a defender of whichever assumption comes out looking worse would try to defuse the example. One thing I care about more than the case itself: be explicit about what's actually settled here versus genuinely contested among people who work on anthropics — whether the two assumptions are even well-defined in small finite setups, whether the split I'm seeing is a live disagreement or an artifact of how I set the toy case up, all of it. Don't hand me a consensus where there isn't one; I'd rather see the fault lines. And please don't send me to the literature, no reading lists — I definately can't get to the library this week.
