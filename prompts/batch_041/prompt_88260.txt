I've got a smallish pot of money to give away — enough to keep about six researchers going for three years — and it's earmarked for work on how tight or loose a society should be: how much rule-following it's reasonable to demand of people, and in which parts of life. The awkward bit is that the pot sits between two crowds who don't talk to each other much. On one side there are the people who measure how tight or loose cultures actually are — surveys, cross-country comparisons, what happens to a place after a shock or a threat. On the other side there are the political philosophers who argue about toleration, paternalism and the limits of state coercion. I want the money to go wherever it genuinely moves things along, so what I'm after is a prioritised agenda: which open questions in this area matter most, and why.

Three things I'd like you to hold onto. First, I don't believe tightness is one thing. There's how much variation a society tolerates, how harshly it punishes the variation it won't tolerate, and how far people have internalised the norms so that no punishment is needed at all. My hunch is that those come apart, and that the interesting questions are about which one we mean when we say a society demands too much or too little. Second, I'm suspicious of lumping together tightness that people opt into — a monastery, an army, a strict religious community — with tightness the state imposes on people who'd rather be somewhere looser. If that distinction earns its keep, say so; if it doesn't, tell me why not. Third, I'd rather fund questions on the seam between the descriptive and the normative, the ones where a finding about how tightness actually works would definately change what we think a society is entitled to demand, or where a normative argument tells the empiricists what to go and measure. A lot of the existing work strikes me as one side or the other refusing to meet in the middle, and I dont want to fund more of that.

So give me a ranked list — five or six items — with your reasoning. For each one: why it's upstream of other things rather than downstream of them, whether it's the sort of question a philosopher can make real progress on in three years without a lab, and what evidence over the next couple of years would push it up or down my list. Then tell me what to cut, including anything that looks important but is really a waste of a career, or that's just an empirical question wearing philosophical clothes. No reading list, please — I'm after the reasoning, not the references, and there's no use pointing me at papers I can't get hold of. And don't hedge everything into mush: where two items are close, just tell me which one wins.
