I am drafting a short essay in the philosophy of self-amending rules and would like detailed comments on it. The essay makes one structural claim and does not depend on exegesis of the game it starts from, so please do not spend space explaining what Nomic is; my intended reader knows, and so do I.

Draft: Where the Paradox Goes

Peter Suber's Nomic, introduced in 1982 and brought to a wide audience the same year in Douglas Hofstadter's Scientific American column, was meant to show that a system of rules can authorize changes to itself, including changes to the rule requiring obedience to the rules and to the victory condition, which Suber made deliberately dull on the assumption that players would amend it. The standard moral drawn is that self-amendment is not paradoxical at all but a matter of engineering: keep the entrenchment levels straight, supply a procedure for demoting entrenched rules, and the system runs indefinitely.

I want to argue that this moral is too quick, and that the paradox of self-amendment is structural rather than local. Most treatments locate the trouble in some particular bad rule — a rule that repeals itself, a rule that entrenches itself, a rule whose own amendment procedure cannot be satisfied — and then show that better drafting avoids the pathology. My claim is that it cannot be localized. Here is the case that makes me think so.

Take a system with entrenchment levels 0, 1, and 2: level 0 rules are amended by simple majority, level 1 by two-thirds, level 2 only unanimously. It contains (A) a Demotion rule at level 1, under which any rule of level 1 or 2 may be reclassified downward by a two-thirds vote; (B) a Procedure rule at level 2, stating that the amendment procedure is as specified in A and B and that B is amendable only unanimously; and (C) a Core rule at level 2, forbidding any downward reclassification that would make B amendable by less than unanimity.

Case one: Demotion's scope excludes B. Then B cannot be amended by any procedure the system contains. The system has an unamendable core, and the claim that everything in it can be changed is false.

Case two: Demotion's scope includes B. Then two-thirds of the players can demote B to level 0, after which a simple majority can rewrite the amendment procedure itself, and the rewritten procedure need not preserve any of the constraints under which it was enacted. Iterating, the system admits a finite sequence of legal changes ending in a rule set that owes nothing to the one we began with. Call that a legal black hole: a state reached entirely by legal moves, from which no rule of the original system has any authority over what counts as a legal move next.

Generalizing: for any self-amending system with a rule governing amendment, either that rule's scope excludes something, which is an unamendable core, or it includes everything, in which case there is a legal path out of the system. One might try to escape by allowing infinitely many entrenchment levels, so that every rule can be demoted and there is no bottom; I have not been able to make this work, but I raise it only in passing. The remaining escape is to say that what licenses the final transition is not a rule of the system but the players' acceptance of the outcome — a practice, not a rule. That strikes me as both correct and deflationary, since it is the same move that stops the regress of legal validity in Kelsen's basic norm and in Hart's rule of recognition. If it is right, then the interesting question about self-amendment is not whether it is possible, but where a given system has hidden the outside that makes it possible.

Comments I would like, organized under these headings:

1. Exhaustiveness. Is the dilemma exhaustive? I want the strongest third case, if there is one, and not a merely verbal one. In particular, please assess the infinitely-many-levels escape on its merits: does it genuinely give a system in which every rule is amendable and no core is needed, or does it relocate the core into the rule that assigns levels?

2. The black hole horn. Is the black hole a real consequence of the setup, or an artifact of reading legality as temporally indexed — a change being legal if the rules in force when it was made permitted it? On a cumulative reading, a change is legal only if validated by every rule set along the path, which would make the escape sequence illegal at the last step but also make ordinary amendment much harder than we take it to be. Which reading is correct for self-amending systems, and can that question be settled from inside the system rather than by stipulation?

3. Triviality. My generalization may be true by construction: if the unamendable core is simply whatever the system cannot change, then of course every system has one, and the argument says nothing. If so, tell me how to state a non-trivial version, or why there is none. If it is not trivial, tell me what the substantive content is and what would count as a counterexample.

4. Rule versus practice. Does the practice resolution actually dissolve the dilemma, or does it merely rename the first horn, with the players' acceptance playing the role of the unamendable core? And if the resolution is sound, does my conclusion collapse into a restatement of Hart and Kelsen rather than a contribution to them? If the comparison is doing no work, say so and tell me whether to cut it.

5. The example. Does the Cascade setup do independent work, or is it a rule conflict of the ordinary kind, resolvable by a priority rule, in which case I should be told what a genuinely unresolvable variant would look like?

6. Presentation. What should be cut, what is asserted without argument, and what is the strongest objection I have not considered? Where I am wrong, say so plainly rather than softening it.
