"Suppose Mary has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room and has learned every physical fact there is to know about color vision — the cone types, the visual pathways, the whole story. Then one day she walks outside and sees a ripe tomato.

(a) Set out the knowledge argument and state precisely what conclusion it is supposed to establish.

(b) Explain the ability hypothesis reply and the phenomenal-concept reply, and say clearly how the two differ.

(c) A student in the class says: 'if Mary walks out, looks at the tomato, and says "huh, pretty much what I expected," then the knowledge argument is dead.' Assess that claim."

And here's the student's answer:

"The knowledge argument is Jackson's argument that physicalism is false. Mary knows every physical fact about color vision, but when she leaves the room she learns something new — she learns what it's like to see red. So there is a fact about color experience that she didn't know before. But she already knew all the physical facts. So this new fact can't be a physical fact. Therefore physicalism is false: there are non-physical facts, namely facts about what experiences are like.

A physicalist can reply that Mary doesn't learn any new fact at all; she just picks up a new ability, the way you pick up the ability to ride a bike. This is the ability hypothesis. Knowing what it's like to see red is know-how, not knowledge-that, so the premise that Mary learns a new fact is false. The phenomenal-concept reply makes basically the same move: it says Mary gains a new concept of something she already knew, so the fact she 'discovers' was always a physical fact. So both replies agree that Mary doesn't acquire any new propositional knowledge — they just disagree about whether to call what she gains an ability or a concept.

On (c), the classmate is right. If Mary could predict exactly what the experience would be like before ever having it, then she already knew what it's like, and the feeling that she learns something new is just an illusion. The knowledge argument rests entirely on that feeling, so it collapses. I think this is roughly Dennett's point about Mary."
