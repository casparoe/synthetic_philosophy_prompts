Hey — I host a tiny interview podcast (like 200 listeners, mostly people from my local rationalist meetup) and next month's guest is a philosopher I've wanted on for ages. She's spent her career defending deontological constraints — the "you may not kill the one to save the five" kind — and she thinks the so-called paradox of deontology is an artifact of bad framing. I've read enough of this stuff to know the paradox: if constraints are supposed to protect people from being violated, then a constraint that forbids you from doing the thing that results in fewer people being violated looks self-undermining — five die so your hands stay clean, which is a weird thing for a morality of respect for persons to be for. Her comeback, as far as I can tell from her papers and a couple of talks online, is that this only looks like a paradox if you assume a moral theory is a device for producing the best outcome, and that once you drop that assumption, the five-vs-one comparison just isn't the kind of thing a constraint answers to. She also thinks constraints are agent-relative all the way down, and that what other people do with the people you didn't save never enters into what you may do.

I don't want to ask her to restate that, and I don't want the canned stuff she's answered a hundred times. Write me 8–10 questions, in the order I should ask them, so each one sets up the next.
