For about two years now I have been caught up in an argument I cannot get to the bottom of, in a reading group and in my own work, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than one more round of arguments. The argument is about what makes pain bad for the one who has it.

One camp holds that an episode of pain is bad for you just by being an episode of pain. Its badness is not conferred on it by anything else. You do not have to dislike it, or be averse to it, or want it gone, for it to count against you; the dislike, when it comes, is a response to a badness that was already there. On this view pain is the standard example of something bad in itself, and any account of well-being that cannot say why pain is bad without appealing to your desires or preferences has a problem at its foundation.

The other camp holds that what makes pain bad is a conative or affective state: that you mind it, that you are averse to it, that you want it to stop. The sensory character of the pain, its intensity, its quality, where it is located, does not by itself make anything bad for anyone. On this view the badness of pain is not different in kind from the badness of any other frustration of a person's concerns, and the intuition that it is different is an illusion produced by the fact that we almost always do mind our pains.

The reason we cannot get past this is a set of clinical cases that both sides treat as decisive. Patients who have suffered damage to the insula and its connections, or who have had surgery severing the cingulate, sometimes report that a pinprick or a burn hurts. They can say where it is, whether it is sharp or hot, roughly how intense it is. What they do not do is behave like people in pain. They do not pull the limb away, they do not guard it, they do not wince, they do not ask for anything for the pain, and their thresholds for detecting the stimulus are not grossly abnormal. Asked about it, they say the pain does not bother them. This is not stoic endurance of a pain one hates; on their reports there is no hatred of it to endure. Call this the dissociation case.

Each side reads the case through its own theory, and this is where I think the trouble may lie. The intrinsic-badness side says: the patient is in pain, and pain is bad for the one who has it, so the patient's experience is bad for them; what the case shows is only that the outward signs of suffering can be severed from the feeling itself. The desire side says: the patient is not suffering, is not distressed, is not moved to escape, reports being untroubled; what could it mean to say the experience is bad for them when nothing about their condition is worse? Neither reading seems to be an inference from the case so much as an application of the theory to it.

My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the two sides are answering two different questions under a single heading. One is a question about the nature of a mental kind: what has to be true of an experience for it to count as pain at all, and whether the aversive aspect belongs to the essence of pain or is merely its usual companion. The other is a question about welfare: what makes an experience bad for the subject who has it. The dissociation case looks decisive only because it is read as answering both at once, and if the questions come apart then the impasse may be an artefact of their being run together. I can see at least three rival diagnoses and I want them weighed against mine. First, that the disagreement is largely verbal, a dispute about what the word pain is to be used for: the whole aversive episode, or the sensory-discriminative state that remains when the aversion is stripped away. Second, that it is a disguised empirical disagreement, and that what really separates the two sides is a disagreement about what the cases show, for instance whether these patients have a degraded but present affective response that their behaviour does not reveal. Third, that it is a disagreement about evidence, about whether a damaged nervous system can reveal the components of the intact case or is simply a different phenomenon, and about how much weight a handful of thin clinical reports can bear.

What I want from you is this. Give me a diagnosis, argued from what each side actually says and from the structure of the case rather than from a survey of positions. Say whether the disagreement is verbal, substantive, or a mixture, and if a mixture, exactly where the line runs between the part that could be dissolved by a stipulation and the part that would survive it. Say whether the dissociation case can bear the weight both sides place on it, and if not, what would have to be true of it for it to be decisive. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a better-designed study, a change of question, a demonstration that one side's premise is incoherent, or an argument that nothing should be expected to move it and why. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations, and I do not want the verdict that it is complicated and that both sides have a point; I want to know what kind of disagreement this is.
