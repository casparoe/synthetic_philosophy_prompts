So I'm in this reading group that's been meeting about a year on consciousness, and we've ended up with two regulars who cannot stop going at each other. One is a dyed-in-the-wool illusionist: thinks the notion of phenomenal consciousness as something over and above the functional/representational/access story is a kind of introspective illusion, and that once you've explained why we're so convinced we have it, there's nothing left over to explain. The other is a phenomenal realist in the old-fashioned sense: she thinks there really are phenomenal properties, that our introspective reports are at least roughly tracking something real, and that the illusionist is explaining the explanandum away rather than explaining it. Every session ends the same way — she says he's changed the subject, he says she's positing something no evidence could touch, and we all go home. Last week somebody suggested we try the adversarial collaboration thing instead of another shouting match, and I got volunteered to write it up.

What I need from you is the document they could both actually put their names to. So: a numbered section of agreed claims — what they both accept about the science and what it does and doesn't establish, about what introspective reports are and what they're evidence for, about what any theory of consciousness owes you, and about which moves are off the table for both sides (my hunch is they can both sign off on rejecting "it's just obvious" and also rejecting "no third-person data could ever bear on this," but let them work that out rather than taking my word for it). Then a table of the genuine disagreements, each stated in wording the person who holds it would accept as fair rather than as a strawman of their own view. For each one, name the crux: the sub-question such that if it got settled, one of them would have to move. And for each crux, give a case or argument that both of them agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two things I want you to build in, because we've noticed this is where the conversation actually gets somewhere. First, make them separate two things they keep collapsing: the content of an introspective judgment, and the causal process that produces it. I want them to say explicitly whether one of these exhausts the other, or whether the gap between them just is the disputed territory — and if it's the disputed territory, say so as precisely as you can manage. Second, use this as one of your crux tests: imagine a manipulation — training, suggestion, a drug, pick one and describe it concretely — after which subjects start reporting that their experience has a structural feature they previously denied it had (say, a determinate phenomenal fringe at the edges of the visual field), while every non-introspective measure we have — discrimination, priming, report-independent physiology, whatever — stays put. Is that the phenomenology changing or the introspective model changing? Write down what the illusionist predicts and what the realist predicts, and be honest about whether any outcome of that experiment would actually embarrass either of them. And if part of what they've been fighting about for a year turns out to be a verbal dispute about the word "phenomenal," say which part and why — but don't pad it if the answer is none of it.
