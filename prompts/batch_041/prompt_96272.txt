I am a novelist finishing a book whose two central characters are philosophers who have spent a decade talking past each other. The first holds that moral uncertainty is continuous with ordinary uncertainty: that you should maximize expected choiceworthiness, and that this requires comparing the value of outcomes across theories. The second holds that intertheoretic comparisons of value are not well-defined, so that "maximizing expected choiceworthiness" names no determinate procedure at all, and would replace it with something else — a bargaining or parliamentary procedure, or treating each theory's requirements as side-constraints rather than as values to be traded off. I want the novel to end with the document they could both actually sign. Write it.

Give me a numbered list of claims both would accept, including about the formal structure of the problem and about which cases are uncontroversial; then their remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept, in a table; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each crux the case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Say which parts of the dispute you take to be verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Build in two things, because they are what my characters cannot get past. First: if expected choiceworthiness is computed over a partition of theories, then how the theories are individuated — one theory split into two near-duplicates, or two merged into one — changes what the procedure prescribes, and neither of them has said whether that is a reductio or a bookkeeping problem. Second: each of them is uncertain whether intertheoretic comparison is even possible, so each must say what their view recommends under uncertainty about its own central presupposition.

Finally, tell me where the drafting is most likely to have failed: which of the agreed claims are probably vacuous or slanted, and what argument or case would lead you to a different verdict on which of the disagreements are genuine.
