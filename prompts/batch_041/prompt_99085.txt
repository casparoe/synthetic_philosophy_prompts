You are a philosopher of practical reason with a taste for deadpan satire. Compose a parody of a software terms-of-service agreement — numbered clauses, roughly 700 words — for a fictional firm, Defer, Inc., whose flagship product is Idealized Deliberation as a Service: customers upload their preferences, and the service returns the verdicts of their own idealized selves on the decisions that trouble them. The comedy must come from the substance of indirect normativity rather than from jargon, so that a reader who has never encountered the literature still finds it funny and a specialist recognizes that you understand the real fault lines. Before drafting, brainstorm the several incompatible ways one might cash out "what you would want if you were better at wanting," then let the clauses quietly expose at least two of them instead of settling on one.

The fine print should take positions, without ever announcing that it is doing so, on at least the following: (i) which idealization the service runs by default, and what happens when two defensible idealizations return divergent verdicts; (ii) whether the verdict of an idealized self binds the actual self, and whether it can be waived or appealed; (iii) the refund policy for the case in which the idealized self's verdict is one the customer could never bring themselves to endorse; (iv) whether the idealized self inherits the customer's debts, promises, and obligations or counts as a fresh agent; and (v) the regress clause — who, if anyone, idealizes the idealizer, and what deference amounts to when the deferring party is itself idealized. Include a short in-universe footnote from the legal department conceding that the firm cannot establish whether the idealized self is the same person as the customer, and treating this as a matter for the arbitration clause. Keep the tone uniformly deadpan and legible as satire without a wink; the reader should be able to tell what is being mocked while never being told.
