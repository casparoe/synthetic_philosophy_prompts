I am a novelist at work on a book about a small transaction that quietly ruins a family, and I need a case built to order rather than borrowed, because the examples everyone reaches for first — kidneys, blood plasma, generator prices after a hurricane, a vote — have been worked over so thoroughly that a reader recognizes the machinery before the scene has begun. I want a scenario whose moral structure is load-bearing, so that when a reader or a critic leans on it, the thing holds. A case that merely announces its own conclusion, that declares the price degrading rather than showing why, is of no use to me; the verdicts have to fall out of the views, not out of the telling.

What I need is a single case in which the objection from corruption — the claim that putting a price on this particular good degrades or destroys what the good is, quite apart from any injury to the seller or to bystanders — has real force, while the objection from coercion or exploitation is at its weakest, and while an ordinary welfare analysis says the trade is a clear improvement for both parties. The direction I find hard is the one in which corruption bites even though nobody is pressured, deceived, or left worse off; most people dismiss that direction as taste or paternalism, and I want to see whether a case can be built in which it cannot be dismissed that way.

Constraints: realistic and contemporary, the sort of thing that could happen in a town I could drive to; the parties should have names, a trade, and a livelihood; the transaction must be voluntary in the strongest sense the law and ordinary morality recognize, free of fraud, and free of any obvious third-party injury; the good must not be a body part, a vote, or a commodity whose price spikes in an emergency; and the scenario itself should run no longer than a page. Give me the scene, not a précis of it.

Then do the accounting, in this order. First, state the case as a proposition — who sells what to whom, for how much, and what each party believes they are doing — in terms neutral enough that the corruption objection is not smuggled into the description. Second, walk through three verdicts on it: the welfare-and-voluntariness verdict, the coercion-or-exploitation verdict, and the corruption verdict, saying for each what the view is committed to and why it lands where it does. Third, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — which one change would flip the verdicts — and what happens if that feature is removed: whether the case collapses back into the familiar kidney-sale shape or becomes something else entirely. Fourth, give me the strongest attack a defender of the market would mount against the case, and say honestly whether the attack succeeds.

One instruction on the analysis itself. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this. Several of the claims you will want to make are matters on which the literature is divided — whether the corruption objection is ultimately a harm-based objection in disguise, whether consent given against a background of unjust inequality can count as full voluntariness, whether efficiency considerations can ever settle a question about the meaning of a good — and I do not want a contested reading presented as though it were the consensus. Where you are uncertain, or where the people who know the topic are, say so plainly.
