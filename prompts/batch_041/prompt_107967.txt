I'm making a handout for a seminar on pain asymbolia -- the syndrome where patients, usually after certain brain lesions, insist that yes, they feel pain, it's definitely pain, but it doesn't bother them at all; some will smile and leave their hand in the ice water. I want the handout to be a decision tree that takes a student from "I accept that these patients feel pain but aren't bothered by it" to a named position on what makes pain bad in the first place.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: first the questions, then the positions that answer them. Branch nodes should be things like: does the asymbolic patient count as being in pain at all, or only as having some non-painful sensory state? Is the badness of pain located in the sensation itself, in a seperate affective/hedonic component, in the desire that it stop, or in a higher-order attitude toward it -- minding it, suffering from it? If the affect is separable, is what's left still pain, or just a "pain signal"? Do the patient's own reports settle anything, or can someone in that state be wrong about whether it hurts? And does it matter for badness whether the state moves you to act, or whether you'd pay to be rid of it?

Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody seems to hold it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment -- e.g. what the view has to say about the patient who reports "it hurts, it just doesn't bother me." I want at least one leaf for the view that asymbolia shows pain's badness is entirely in the affective component, one for the view that these patients aren't really in pain, one for the view that pain is bad only because we're averse to it, and one for the view that nothing is bad about pain in itself, only about suffering. Flag any leaves the tree can't separate, and note which combinations of answers go unoccupied and why -- incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Since it's a handout, use a few concrete cases as the branch questions so students can answer without knowing the labels: the asymbolic patient, someone on a high dose of morphine who says it still hurts but they don't care, the athlete who keeps competing on a torn ligament, and a hypothetical creature with pain receptors whose states never motivate it to do anything. Don't lean on citations or reading lists -- I want the tree itself, and keep it to something a student can work through in ten minutes.
