I'm in a small reading group on the philosophy of biology, and there's one member, Cass, who has a real gift for handing down verdicts on cases in a way that's almost impossible to predict from the last one. She's writing about reasons without reasoners — she calls them free-floating rationales — and over the last few months she's ruled as follows. Honeybee waggle dance: yes, the colony genuinely has a reason to fly north-east, even though no bee is thinking anything, and she's happy to say the reason can be wrong, since a forager that gets knocked off course will come home and dance a vector that sends the others to the wrong field. The pheromone trail that gets an ant colony to converge on the shorter of two routes out to a feeder: yes, a reason, even though nothing corresponding to it sits in any ant's head — it's smeared across the ground in the pheromone itself. A toad snapping at a small dark moving speck: yes, that's a reason, namely that it's prey, and she isn't remotely bothered that the toad can't tell a beetle from a pellet and will happily snap at a bit of lead shot dragged past on a string. The river that reaches the sea by the steepest descent: no, its just following the slope. The thermostat: no. The hurricane that drifts over the warmest water: no. And when I pushed her on the mercury column of a thermometer she said no as well — it carries information about the temperature, a meteorologist can read it off, but the column isn't about anything, it just is the temperature, and nothing in the instrument is doing anything with it. I've got alot of time for this but I honestly can't see the pattern.

Next week she has to rule on bacterial chemotaxis: a bacterium swimming up a glucose gradient by run-and-tumble, with the receptor at the cell surface effectively wired straight through to the flagellar motor. She hasn't said anything about it yet and I want to place a bet before the meeting. So give me your best guess at her verdict, and — more useful to me — the two or three different rules that fit everything she's said so far but come apart on the bacterium. For each, spell out what it predicts about the bacterium and why; say which rule you'd bet she's actually running, and what in her past verdicts supports that over the others; and name the single further case that would best tell the surviving rules apart. If you think her record genuinely underdetermines the bacterium, say that instead, and tell me what she'd be committing herself to either way. Write it as plain continuous prose please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just the way you'd explain it to me down the phone.
