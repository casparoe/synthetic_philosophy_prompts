I'm in a small reading group on creativity — mostly psychologists, plus one composer — and two of us have been at each other's throats for about a year now. One of them is a Hofstadter person: for her the crux of creativity is variation on a theme — twiddling the knobs of a conceptual space, sliding concepts along their dimensions, nudging a parameter here and relaxing a constraint there — and she reckons that when you actually inspect so-called revolutionary breakthroughs they turn out to be variations at a higher level. Knobs all the way up. The other one thinks that's a conjuring trick: for him the real thing is transforming the space itself rather than exploring it, and if your theory can redescribe every frame-break as a meta-level knob-twiddle then it's stopped saying anything — it's unfalsifiable, and it also can't explain why some variations are dead ends while others open up whole new territories. They've now had the same arguement four times, and I've been deputised to produce the document they should have written instead of shouting past each other.

So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two. I want, first, the claims they can both sign — about what has to be true of something for it to count as creative at all, about what a "conceptual space" is and who gets to fix its dimensions, about which of their stock examples they actually agree on, and about what any account of creativity owes us. Second, the precise points where they still differ, each one worded so that the side holding it would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own view rather than a caricature. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them one way or the other would actually move at least one of them, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — use cases a general reader would recognise (the drift of a musical genre, a novelty in a chess opening, the introduction of a new notation or tuning system, that sort of thing) rather than anything needing specialist knowledge, and skip the citations, we've all read the literature. If part of their fight is really about words, say which part and why; and if one of them is quietly leaning on a level-relative notion of "the space" that makes the whole dispute collapse into itself, say that plainly. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes. And I'd much rather you committed than played it even-handed: take a definite view on where the real crux lies and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick.
