In a graduate seminar I'm sitting in on — the subject is the so-called WEIRD problem in moral psychology, the suspicion that what gets published as "moral cognition" is really the cognition of a narrow, Western, educated, industrialized slice of humanity — one of the other students keeps reaching for an analogy that I suspect is doing more argumentative work than anyone has noticed. Her claim is that moral cognition is like language. Yes, the ethnographic record is a riot of surface variation: cultures disagree about which killings are permitted, which virtues are admired, how blame is distributed among accomplices, whether a bad intention spoils an otherwise good outcome. But, she says, that variation no more threatens a shared moral core than the mutual unintelligibility of Spanish, Mandarin, and Navajo threatens the existence of universal grammar. Underneath, we all run the same moral machinery; what differ are the settings, not the machine. And she draws a further conclusion from this: that the cross-cultural data leave moral objectivity entirely untouched, since a common moral grammar is precisely what you would expect to find if there were moral truths for us to track. I want you to audit the analogy rather than simply endorse or dismiss it. Start by making the correspondence explicit — a table is fine — saying what in the moral case is supposed to play the role of the universal grammar, of the parameters or dialects that generate the surface differences, of competence as against performance, of the child's acquisition device, and of the native speaker's judgments of grammaticality; then tell me where the mapping breaks, and whether the analogy predicts anything we can check (would it predict moral pidgins or creoles, and is there anything in the record that looks like one?).

I am especially interested in the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion she draws, as opposed to the ones that are merely cute. Language, after all, is not obviously truth-apt — there is no fact of the matter about whether "dog" means dog — whereas moral claims present themselves as answerable to how things are; and while a community cannot be mistaken about its own grammar, it seems it can be mistaken about its own morality. Which of these asymmetries are genuine, which are artifacts of how I have framed them, and which cut against her argument? I would also like to know what the analogy quietly imports from the source side that the target side may lack — whether it smuggles in the contested empirical thesis that language really does rest on a universal grammar, or a picture of moral norms as conventional in the way linguistic norms arguably are, or an assumption that a shared core would have to be truth-tracking rather than a shared projection or adaptation. And most important: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion — that cultural variation is no threat to moral objectivity — actually survive, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? If it cannot bear the weight, tell me what a better analogy would be, and exactly what it would and would not be able to support.
