I'm a fourth-year PhD student in history of science (the "science and empire" track, don't ask) and I've been volun-told to run a seminar session on counterfactuals in history next month. I want to do it through The Man in the High Castle instead of the usual "what if the Confederacy won" fare, because the standard debate bores me: both camps, the "counterfactuals are cheap parlor games" people and the "counterfactuals are just causal inference with a different name" people, seem to assume the counterfactual has to be a single localized tweak — swap one battle, one bullet, one election, hold everything else fixed. Dick doesn't do that. He gives you a world where the Axis won, and then inside that world a banned novel in which the Allies won, and that second one isn't our world either (I genuinely don't remember how it diverges, and I don't care — it's the structure I want, not the exact departures). So you're reading a counterfactual of a counterfactual, and you close the book holding a text that's neither history nor our history but a third thing.

What I'd like from you is a reading of the novel as philosophy of history about inevitability, not just about "what if." Two threads I want taken seriously. First, the I Ching. It isn't local color: characters consult it and it steers what they do, and the novel itself is famously supposed to have been written under its influence, so the text is in some sense produced by a procedure that claims to read the pattern of change in the world rather than to represent an alternative to it. Does that make the novel's counterfactual something other than a possible world in the usual sense — less a branch off the actual timeline than a reading of the actual one? Second, the nesting. If a world can contain a persuasive account of its own negation, what happens to the thought that history has a direction or a necessity? Does the novel dramatize inevitability as a feature of how a history gets told rather than of how it happened, or does it quietly reaffirm a necessity at the level of the whole — that some world had to be the real one, and the people inside just can't tell which?

And push back on the reading where it deserves it. Don't let this collapse into a cute "everything is a text" move. The book is full of people making hard consequential choices under a regime, and Abendsen's retreat behind his fortifications is itself a verdict of sorts. I want a reading that keeps the ethics and the politics in view and then says what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the analytic literature on counterfactuals can't — the thing fiction can do that a possible-worlds semantics can't.

If a scene I gesture at turns out not to be in the book the way I remember it, flag that instead of inventing detail; I'll take an honest reading over a smooth one. No reading lists, no citations — I have plenty and what I need is the argument.

Finish by telling me how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the one point you're least sure about and why.
