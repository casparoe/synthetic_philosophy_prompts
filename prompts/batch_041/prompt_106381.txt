I'm a retired organic chemist, 38 years at the bench, and since I hung it up I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group mostly to keep the brain from rusting. Last week two of the regulars got into it in the parking lot afterwards and I stood there like an idiot because I couldn't referee either of them. So I'm handing it to you.

One of them — call her Dana, she's the philosophy-of-science type — and the other, Marcus, who actually did vision research before he switched over. Went like this, as close as I can reproduce it:

(1) Dana: The Müller-Lyer lines settle the whole thing. You can know, really know, that the two lines are the same length, and they still look unequal. So belief can't reach down into perception. Perception is impenetrable, done.

(2) Marcus: That's one little family of cases. Look at perceptual learning — birders, radiologists. After enough years the warbler just *looks* like a warbler to them, it's not that they infer it in a flash, it's there in the experience. And you can get the same kind of thing to happen fast in the lab, where just giving someone a category to look for changes what they report seeing almost immediately. What you know is shaping what you see.

(3) Dana: Two problems with that. First, the word 'report' is doing a lot of work — the second you have to ask them what they saw, you've already left perception and you're measuring something downstream, judgment or memory or whatever. Second, learning is slow and it rewires the machinery over years; but 'penetration' is supposed to be online, happening right now. So the birding case isn't penetration at all, it's a different machine than it used to be. And those fast lab effects are probably just attention or a bias in deciding, not the percept itself.

(4) Marcus: The slow/fast thing is arbitrary, though. In both cases what you know determines what you see — that's the phenomenon. The only difference is how long it took to get there, which is a fact about the history of the machine, not about what's going on in it now. And if the fast experiments do close the timing gap, then by your own rule you have to call it penetration.

(5) Dana: It's not arbitrary, because it's about explanation, not timing. If the knowledge has been compiled into the visual hardware, then the thing doing the work isn't a belief anymore, it's part of the transducer — and penetration is supposed to be a top-down causal push from a cognitive state onto the perceptual process. Different architectures, different predictions you can test. And a percept is supposed to be something that's just *there* for you, not something you're merely inclined to say. If the only thing that changed is what they're disposed to report, nothing perceptual changed.

They each thought they'd won. Can you go through it move by move — tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall — and then say what both of them are missing? The thing I most want to know is whether Dana's slow/fast line is a real principled distinction or whether Marcus is right that it's just a label for how long something took, and whether 'penetration' is even a single well-defined thing they're arguing about or whether they're just fighting over a word.
