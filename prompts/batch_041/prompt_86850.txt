Alright, I need a case built to order. Context: I'm writing a section arguing that the emotion-as-perception view has one advantage the emotion-as-evaluative-judgement view can't match, namely that it can give emotions an evaluative content that isn't propositional — and I keep getting the same three illustrations from everyone (spider on the windowsill, exam nerves, grief for a dead grandmother), so I want something fresh.

What I need: one episode, one ordinary adult, in which

(a) the feeling view — emotions just are the felt bodily goings-on — has to count the episode as an emotion;
(b) the perception view — emotions are non-inferential, not-up-to-you appearances of value, same family as perceptual experience — has to count it as an emotion;
(c) the judgement view (Solomon's line: an emotion is an evaluative judgement, not a feeling) is forced either to deny that it's an emotion or to stretch 'judgement' until it stops meaning anything.

The crux is (c), and here's the shape I'm after: the person has an emotion directed at a particular thing, and there is genuinely no property she takes that thing to have — nothing she'd assent to, nothing she'd infer from, nothing she'd defend. The perception view can eat that; it can represent value without concepts. The judgement view can only get it back by positing a judgement that plays none of the roles judgements play. Don't just stipulate that, build it so it shows.

Constraints, because otherwise this gets fudged:

- one paragraph, kitchen/bus/office realism. No lesions, no drugs, no hypnosis, no blindsight, no zombies, no fictional objects.
- not a recalcitrance case with the serial numbers filed off ('she knows the spider's harmless but...'). No self-deception, no repression.
- I need to rule out 'she does judge it, she just won't say so' on grounds other than her own introspective testimony, so make the missing judgement conspicuous from the outside — in what she infers, what she'll say when she thinks no ones listening, what she'd concede under pressure.
- she's not an unusually opaque person; the point is that a judgement there would have been visible, not that she's a superb introspector.
- the state has to be object-directed, so nobody gets to say 'that's a mood'. Its aboutness should be evident in attention, memory, action tendencies.
- and it has to read as a full-blooded emotion — anyone watching would say she's in the grip of one. That's the bit people cheat on: don't tack on 'and it is an emotion' as a stipulation, make it come across.

Then walk me through it: the verdict each view gives and why, which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I take that feature away (does it collapse into a standard recalcitrance case?), and the strongest way a defender of the judgement view would attack the case. Two of my grad students tried this and both ended up stipulating the verdicts, which is exactly what I don't want — if it can't be built, tell me where it breaks.
