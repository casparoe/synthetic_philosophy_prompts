ok so for my philosophy class we have to do this "what would they think now" thing where you pick a dead thinker and something that only happened after they died, and argue about what they'd say. I picked John Stuart Mill and British India because it's a weird case: he worked at the East India Company for like 35 years, in the office that handled the letters going back and forth to India, and he got the top job there right before Parliament took India away from the Company and handed it to the Crown, which ended his career. So the guy's day job was administrating an empire while his famous book was about freedom.

The line I keep coming back to, from On Liberty, is: "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified by actually effecting that end." Right before it he's talking about societies in their 'nonage', and he says the liberty principle has no application to people who can't be improved by free discussion yet.

What bugs me is that the second half of that sentence is a condition. It's only ok if it actually works. So please actually commit to positions here, don't give me "he'd have mixed feelings":

1) Say Mill gets dropped into now and finds out how it ended. India got independence in 1947. And now people argue about what British rule actually did: that India was one of the richest parts of the world before and got deindustrialized and drained, that the famines killed millions and were at least partly policy, that the railways and the legal and school systems were built to move troops and cotton and clerks rather than to develop anything. Does he say "that's not what I was defending" or does that wreck the argument? And related: is the nonage thing an exception carved into his liberty principle, or does it mean the principle just never applied to most of the world to begin with? Those are different, so tell me which one he's actually running.

2) Here's the thing that bugs me most. He says despotism is fine if it's aimed at improvement, but he never says who decides when the improvement is finished, and the person doing the deciding is the same one who benefits from it never being finished. Nobody ever flunks the exam. If the tutelage ran roughly 90 years past his death and then ended with a partition that killed an enormous number of people, would he say "great, they graduated" or "they went too early"? And can he even tell those apart without begging the question? Be harsh about it, I want to know if his position is unfalsifiable.

3) Also: modern arguments that Britain owes India reparations, or that looted stuff in the museums and the diamond in the crown should be handed back. Not the generic "he'd be for justice" answer. Does his own framework actually give an answer about who owes what to whom, or does the "we were doing it for their own good" framing mean he'd figure the books are already balanced?

Please don't tell me to go read something or give me page numbers, I don't have library access this week and I don't need a reading list, I need the argument itself. And if part of his view comes out ugly by modern standards, just say so, don't smooth it over.
