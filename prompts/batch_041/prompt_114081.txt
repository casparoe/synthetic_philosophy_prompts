I'm taking a Nietzsche course this term and the final paper is allowed to be on a film, so I'm doing Groundhog Day and the eternal recurrence. Everyone in my seminar calls it the eternal recurrence movie and I think that's way too quick, so I want a reading thats actually careful in both directions.

Here's the tension I want to build the paper around. People split on how to read the doctrine in The Gay Science — either as a cosmological claim that time really does run in a circle, or as a thought experiment, the demon who comes to you in your loneliest loneliness and tells you you'll have to live this exact life again and again, nothing new in it, every pain and every joy, and asks whether you'd bless him or curse him. My problem is that the demon's question only has teeth if there's no way out, and the whole film is a way out. Phil doesn't affirm the day, he solves it — he gets good enough at being a person and the loop lets go of him. So is the movie a dramatization of the doctrine, or a fantasy of escaping it, the wish that affirming your life pays off? I'd like a reading willing to say the film might fail the doctrine, and also willing to say where the film knows something the doctrine doesn't.

Two things I want in there. First, the small-man problem. Zarathustra's nausea isn't really about repetition as such, it's about the eternal return of the small man, the petty and mediocre recurring forever, and Phil starts out with exactly that contempt (Ned Ryerson, the insurance guy who keeps recognizing him from high school, is the purest specimen). His redemption is learning to love the town. So is that amor fati or just becoming a nicer guy? And does the film notice that the doctrine asks you to affirm the small men too, forever, and not just tolerate them for a day? Second, the homeless old man Phil keeps trying and failing to keep alive no matter what he does. That's the one thing the loop won't let him fix, and I think it's the closest the film gets to what the demon is actually asking about.

I also keep thinking the unit is wrong — he repeats a day, not a life, and a day has no shape, so maybe the film dodges the hardest part. And the thing that finally releases him is a relationship, which is a very un-Zarathustra ending.

So: which scenes should the paper be built around (I'm already eyeing the montage where he tries to end himself, the phase where he uses the loop to take whatever he wants, and the mastery stuff — the piano, the ice sculpture), what does the film show that the argument alone can't, and where does it resist the reading? Is the ending purgatorial, or karmic, or just a jerk-learns-empathy arc? If the honest answer is that the film isn't Nietzschean at all, say so and show me why rather than forcing it. No reading list needed, I've got the syllabus.
