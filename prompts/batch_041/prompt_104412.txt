I'm a high school student working on an independent-study unit in epistemology, and I've written a short essay that I'd like detailed comments on rather than a grade. Here is the essay.

**Can You Know That You Know? A Shrinking-Margin Argument**

The KK principle says that if you know something, then you know that you know it. The luminosity thesis says something broader: if you are in a given mental state, you are always in a position to know that you are in it. For a long time I treated these as two names for the same idea. I now think they can be pulled apart, and that the separation shows what is really doing the work in the standard objection to both.

The standard objection is the margin-of-error argument. Take a lamp on a dimmer. At full brightness you know the lamp is on. As the dimmer is turned down, there is a range of settings where the lamp is still on and you still know it is on. The argument says that this range cannot itself be known to you: if the setting were a little lower, the lamp would be off and you, being in a relevantly similar state, would still believe it was on, so you would not know it. So even though you know the lamp is on, you cannot know that you know it, and the KK principle fails.

I want to push this one step further. Notice that the argument gives first-order knowledge a margin: a buffer of settings on either side of the boundary within which you can still know. My claim is that the margin for "I know that I know" is wider than the margin for "I know." The reason is that the second-order belief has to be safe not only against the lamp's being off but also against my first-order belief's being unsafe. So the settings at which I can know that I know form a strictly smaller range than the settings at which I can simply know. Iterating, each added "I know that" shrinks the range again. I call this the shrinking-margin thesis: the reason KK fails is not that self-knowledge is impossible, but that each level of self-knowledge is less robust than the one below it, so the levels eventually run out.

I think this explains something the usual statement of the argument leaves open. If KK failed for a flat reason, we would expect either all levels to fail together or none to. Shrinking margins instead predict a gradual loss: there should be settings where I know that I know that the lamp is on, but not that I know that I know that I know it. It also changes what luminosity should mean. On my reading, luminosity is not the claim that I can always know my states; it is the claim that the margin does not shrink as we iterate, which is a stronger and more interesting claim, and one I suspect is false.

I'm not sure the argument is right. I'd like to know where it breaks, and whether the shrinking margin is a real phenomenon or just the ordinary margin argument repeated.
