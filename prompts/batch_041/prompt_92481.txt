For a graduate seminar on the hard problem of consciousness and its so-called meta-problem, I need a one-page diagnostic decision tree that lets a student locate their own view on the causal and epistemic standing of phenomenal consciousness — the cluster of commitments that makes the paradox of phenomenal judgment bite.

Assume the student already accepts two things: that we make judgments and reports about our experiences (we say that something looks red to us, that a pain is throbbing), and that those judgments are not merely judgments about our behavior or our dispositions to behave. The tree should sort the student by what they take to ground and explain those judgments.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: work out first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Every internal node must be a question with two or three answers, phrased so that the student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. The nodes should turn on the load-bearing distinctions: whether the facts about phenomenal character are entailed by the physical and functional facts; whether the physical domain is causally closed; whether phenomenal states are themselves among the causes of the judgments we make about them; whether our introspective judgments are reliable, and if so in virtue of what; and whether phenomenal states are being affirmed to exist at all.

The leaves must include at least the following positions, each named and given one sentence on its most awkward commitment: epiphenomenalism; interactionist dualism; illusionism; Russellian monism or panpsychism; the phenomenal-concept (type-B) physicalist strategy; and a reductive physicalist view on which phenomenal properties simply are certain functional or representational properties. Add any further leaves the logical space requires, and keep the whole instrument to roughly eight to twelve leaves.

In building it:

- Anchor the nodes in concrete cases, and use at least the zombie conceivability case, Mary's room, and blindsight; you may add others, such as a creature that has experiences but no capacity to form judgments about them.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.
- Note any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

No citations or reading lists; the instrument should stand on its own.
