I'm writing the final for my upper-level ethics course and I want the abortion question to actually hurt. Every year I get the same two essays: the violinist shows that nobody has to be a life-support machine for another person, so abortion is fine; or the fetus has a future like ours, so abortion is wrong. Both get a B+ because at least the labels are right. What I want is a question that puts those two arguments side by side and makes the student notice that they aren't even answering the same question — one is about what a right to life actually gets you when someone else's body is doing the sustaining, the other is about why killing is wrong at all — and then has to say where, if anywhere, the two actually collide rather than just passing each other.

I want to force that with two cases the students won't have seen in the readings, so they can't lean on a seminar answer. First: an embryo created by IVF, frozen, never implanted, eventually discarded. Nobody's body is being used as life support there, so the violinist has nothing to say about it; but the deprivation argument seems to have plenty to say. Second: a woman with an IUD, or on a drug, that works by preventing implantation — is she doing the thing the deprivation account condemns, and if not, what in the account explains why not, given that the same future would have been sitting there if the egg had stuck? I'd also like the question to make them say something about whether the deprivation account makes an early abortion nearly as bad as a late one, since the future being taken is roughly the same either way, and whether thats a feature or a bug. And I want the best answers to notice that granting the fetus a right to life doesn't by itself settle the bodily-use question, and that the deprivation account doesn't by itself settle it either — so the real disagreement is over weight, not over whether the fetus counts.

Please write me: (1) the essay question itself, phrased so it can't be answered by reciting the two positions; (2) a model answer of about 650 words, the kind of thing a strong student could actually produce in 90 minutes; (3) a marking scheme out of 25, listing the five or six moves a top answer has to make, how many marks each is worth, and a one-line note on the specific wrong turn that usually costs people the marks on that move. The model answer should take a stand rather than fence-sit, but the marking scheme should give full credit to other defensible stands as long as they do the work. No citations needed anywhere — they're writing in an exam hall, I don't want a bibliography recited at me, I want them thinking about the arguements.
