I have recently moved to a city where the everyday conventions around tipping, queueing, and jaywalking differ sharply from the ones I grew up with, and the advice I keep receiving is that when in Rome one should do as the Romans do. It has been used on me to settle an argument about whether I am morally required to tip in a place where tipping is not customary, whether cutting a queue is ever permissible, and whether jaywalking is a matter of mere prudence or of right and wrong. What precise claims could that piece of advice actually be making? Lay them out as a menu, from the trivial to the radical: prudential advice, etiquette, a claim about local law, a claim that local custom creates moral obligations, a claim about respect for hosts, a claim about cultural relativism, and any others you think are distinct. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and say whether it would actually support the conclusion that a visitor is morally required to follow the local convention even when it conflicts with a convention from home. I am especially interested in the difference between a convention's being backed by a local moral norm and a visitor's having an all-things-considered duty to comply with it. Finally, say how confident you are in your verdicts and identify the single point you are least sure of. Structure the response as follows: The proverb and its work (2-3 sentences); The readings (the bulk of the answer, each with a verdict); What would actually support the conclusion (one short paragraph); Confidence and least sure point (short).
