Me and a friend from my international law seminar have been going back and forth for weeks about genocide, and I've ended up defending a position I'm not sure I actually believe, so I want you to try to knock it down properly. The position is this: the 1948 definition is basically right as it stands, and all the agitation to widen it — cultural genocide, political and social groups, economic destruction, starvation, whatever the newest one is — is a mistake, and not because those things don't matter. It's because the only thing a distinct crime of genocide can do that crimes against humanity can't is mark one very specific wrong: the intent to destroy a people as such. Once you let the term sprawl it just becomes a synonym for really bad mass violence, which we already have a phrase for. The other half of my view is that the problem was never the definition anyway, it's the will to use it. States refuse to say the word for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the definition is met, and no redrafting fixes that. My friend says this is just me laundering the status quo into a principle, and honestly I can see why. The case that bothers me most is the one where a state never kills anybody but makes a minority's language, religion and family formation impossible within two generations — my view says that's not genocide, and I think I'm ok with that, but I want to be told if I shouldn't be.

What I want from you is the strongest map of what would actually count against me, ranked by how decisive it is rather than how rhetorically satisfying. I already suspect the drafting history is a problem for me: I'm fairly sure Lemkin himself was thinking much more broadly than the final text — I think the destruction of a culture was central to his original idea — and that what got cut out got cut out by a vote, for political reasons rather than conceptual ones. Confirm or correct that, and tell me how much damage it does, because if the narrow definition is just a political compromise frozen in amber, then "it's the right line" gets alot harder to say. I also want to know whether a distinction I keep leaning on actually holds up, between what the label does for institutions (trials, intervention, legal obligations) and what it does for victims (recognition, being counted) — because I have a nagging feeling my whole case rests on the first and the strongest objections all come from the second, and that a view which is right about triggers but wrong about recognition might just be wrong.

Then give me the concessions: what would someone who holds my line already admit counts against it? The parts I'd personally find most persuasive are (a) an argument that the narrow definition does real harm the broad one wouldn't, i.e. some concrete class of victims who lose something by being told they were "only" a crime against humanity, (b) any evidence that the label has actual downstream consequences rather than just being catharsis, and (c) the point that the protected groups in the convention are all the ones you're born into while political groups are the ones you choose — because if that line is doing substantive moral work while pretending to be neutral conceptual analysis, I think I'm in trouble and I want to know it. And please be straight with me at the end about whether my view has quietly made itself unfalsifiable, since every time someone hands me a case where the definition fails a victim I say "that's a political failure, not a conceptual one." Is that a real cost, or just the ordinary shape of a legal concept that has to draw a line somewhere? I'd rather hear that my position is well insulated and that this is fine, or that it's well insulated and that this is exactly the problem.
