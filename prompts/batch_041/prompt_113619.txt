I cover science and ideas, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about credence and chance by going case by case. My gut verdicts: a coin I know to be fair, about to be tossed — 0.5. A coin I know is weighted to come up heads nine times in ten — 0.9, and my hunch that it will land tails this time counts for nothing. A coin I know is either double-headed or double-tailed, but not which — 0.5, even though the real chance is 0 or 1, so I seem to be deferring to my best estimate of the chance rather than to the chance itself. A fair coin tossed last night, outcome hidden under a cup — 0.5; the same coin once I lift the cup — 1 or 0, though the chance was 0.5 all along. A radioactive atom with a known half-life — I take the chance itself as my credence; whether a long-lost manuscript sits in a particular archive — no chance there at all, just an unknown fact, and I still have a credence. And a trustworthy oracle who has been right 999 times out of 1000 tells me that a coin whose chance of heads I know to be 0.9 will come up tails: I feel pulled below 0.9, toward the oracle, and I cannot tell whether that pull is a mistake.

A colleague says the principle behind all this is what philosophers call the Principal Principle — in its simplest form, that credence should match the known chance. My verdicts will not sit still under it.
