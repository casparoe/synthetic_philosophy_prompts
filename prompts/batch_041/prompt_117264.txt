i'm on the scarce treatments panel at our health authority — we decide who gets the handful of very expensive one-off therapies each year, and we set the rules for that. two things go to a vote at next thursday's meeting and i have to bring the written opinion, since i'm the one who asked for the papers.

first, the "equal chance" rule. right now when two patients are clinically tied on expected benefit we break the tie by longest wait. a patient group has written in saying that's arbitrary and that when the patients are genuinely tied we should toss a coin, because that treats them as equals. second, "severity weighting": a proposal to multiply the expected benefit for patients in our worst-off band by 1.5, so that a given gain counts for more if you're starting from further behind.

our staff economist has circulated a note saying both are irrational. his argument is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if the panel's rankings are to be consistent with the usual expected-utility axioms, and if a change that's good for someone and bad for nobody is an improvement, then the panel's ranking of options just has to be a weighted sum of patients' expected utilities. he then says Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance argument fixes the weights as equal, so severity weighting is out. on the coin flip he says the theorem shows we're indifferent between giving the last dose to A, giving it to B, or flipping, so the flip buys nothing except the illusion of fairness. one of our lay members said the opposite: that the flip is obviously better than either deterministic rule, and if the theorem says otherwise then the theorem is the problem.

i'm inclined to take the coin flip and drop the severity weights, but i don't want to do it on vibes, and i don't want to be the one who mangles the theorem in front of the economist. could you write the memo i'll put in front of the panel: a clear recommendation on each of the two proposals, plus what we should actually do about the two tied patients waiting on the last dose right now (we have to settle there case at the same meeting), the reasoning behind it, and an honest statement of the best case for the side i'm leaning against. and please tell me plainly whether the economist has the theorem right — if he's overstated it i need to know exactly where, and if the lay member's complaint has a name in this literature i'd like to know what the reply to it is. no citations needed, just make it something a non-specialist board can vote on.
