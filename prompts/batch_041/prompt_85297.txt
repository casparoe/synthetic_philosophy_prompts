Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, but I spend most of my time in a philosophy of mind reading group, and I have this "state your position" exercise coming up for my committee and I've realised I dont actually know whether what I think hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes. "Belief" and "desire" are posits of folk psychology, folk psychology is a theory, and the theory is going to get replaced by computational neuroscience rather than reduced to it. So strictly speaking there are no beliefs and desires, the way there's no caloric.

(2) Scientific realism. Mature sciences get things approximately right, and the central posits of a successful theory refer.

(3) Predictive processing is not a useful fiction or a mere "level of description" — I think the brain literally runs a generative model and does approximate Bayesian inference. I'm especially attached to the social version of this: the story where we predict each other by running a generative model of the other agent's beliefs and desires. I think that's what mindreading actually is, not a handy gloss on it.

(4) Folk psychology is a genuinely good predictive instrument. I predict my flatmate all day by attributing beliefs and desires to him, and it works constantly, way better than youd expect if the theory were junk.

(5) There's no fact of the matter about what a system "really" believes. Belief attribution is interest-relative and radically underdetermined — no unique correct ascription. I take this as another point in favour of (1).

(6) I read a couple of anthropology papers about societies where people apparently dont attribute beliefs at all: minds are treated as basically opaque, behaviour gets explained by what someone said or by what they want, and there's no belief-ish concept in the language. I found this weirdly vindicating for (1) — the belief concept starts to look like a local cultural gadget rather than a universal bit of human psychology.

(7) None of this stops me saying "she believed the meeting was at noon" to explain why my labmate turned up at noon, and none of it stops me blaming people when they do rotten things. That's just a useful idiom, like "the sun rose."

My supervisor looked at this and said "you can't have all of those," then refused to say which ones, which was maddening. So I want an actual audit rather than a lecture: which of these are really in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? Please rank them by severity instead of listing everything as equally bad — I want to know which pair I have to fix first, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere. And if some of these pull on each other in a chain rather than pairwise, say so.
