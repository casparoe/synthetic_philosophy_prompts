I am teaching the absent-minded driver problem in a decision theory seminar and want to use Christopher Nolan's Memento as the centerpiece, so I need a reading I can trust. Leonard Shelby is an agent who cannot locate himself in his own investigation — at the first motel or the second, before or after a killing he has already committed — and who therefore governs his future conduct through tattoos and Polaroids that function as a standing policy he cannot renegotiate in the moment. Read the film against the problem: does it dramatize the gap between the ex ante optimal policy and the ex post best action at a decision node, and does Leonard's tattooing amount to a commitment device of the kind the problem demands, or a parody of one? Take the technical core seriously — the standard resolution requires the agent to randomize at the intersection, and Leonard's medium can encode only a fixed rule, so ask whether the film shows that the problem's solution is unavailable to an agent built the way he is. I would also like you to press the disanalogy honestly: the driver's affliction is an inability to tell which node he occupies, whereas Leonard's is an inability to form new memories at all, so mark where the analogy holds and where it trades on forgetting in general. Finally, the film narrates backwards, so the viewer grasps each scene's significance only after its consequences; does that seat the audience in the driver's position, and does the film thereby show something the formalism leaves out? Be faithful in both directions — to the film's actual plot and to the decision-theoretic problem — and say plainly where the reading distorts either.
