A friend of mine has been pressing an argument I find hard to answer. On his view, a legal system is at bottom a protection racket operated by whichever gang has managed to monopolize violence: what the state calls taxation is extortion, what it calls punishment is reprisal, and the only thing separating a government from a mafia is that the government won. He claims Pascal is on his side, invoking the thought that justice without force is impotent and force without justice is tyrannical, and drawing the conclusion that justice is merely the name the victors give to their force — so there is no such thing as legitimate political authority, only successful coercion dressed up in ceremony. I suspect he is reading Pascal far too quickly, but I cannot put my finger on where the analogy actually fails. Could you audit it for me? Lay out what corresponds to what between the racket and the state; mark the points at which the correspondence breaks down; sort the disanalogies that genuinely bear on his conclusion from the ones that are idle; and then say whether Pascal's own position supports the conclusion my friend extracts from him or undercuts it. In working that last part out, I would like you to weigh in particular what Pascal says about custom, about imagination, about the ceremonial trappings that surround magistrates and kings, and about the distinct orders of the body, the mind, and the will — with tyranny understood as the attempt to be honored on the terms of one order for something that belongs to another. If a better analogy is available for what Pascal actually holds, I would like to see it.
