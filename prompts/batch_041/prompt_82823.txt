I cover philosophy and the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on a piece about the revival of utilitarian thinking among economists and the effective-altruism movement. My peg is a new book by a respected popularizer, and one chapter turns on John Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Since I intend to report on the argument rather than merely summarize it, I want to be certain I understand where the book is faithful to the result and where it is selling me something. Here, as fairly as I can reconstruct it, is the book's treatment.

Harsanyi, the author writes, asked what it takes to get from individual preferences to a defensible social preference and found the answer nearly forced. Assume that each person's preferences can be represented by a utility number, that society's preferences obey the same axioms of rational choice that individuals' do, and that we accept the modest requirement that when everyone prefers one option to another, so does society; from these, Harsanyi showed, the social ranking must simply be the sum of individual utilities. The author calls the assumptions innocuous and the result the closest thing moral philosophy has to a proof. He then says it vindicates the utilitarian tradition against Rawls: someone choosing behind a veil of ignorance, unsure which position she will occupy, will by Harsanyi's reasoning pick the policy that maximizes average utility, so the difference principle — the insistence on maximizing the lot of the worst-off — is not a rival conception of justice but a mistake about decision-making under uncertainty. Finally he carries the result into population ethics, arguing that because Harsanyi has shown welfare must be added up, we are committed to what is known as the repugnant conclusion — that a very large population of lives barely worth living can be better than a smaller flourishing one — and that anyone who recoils is refusing the arithmetic. For what it is worth, my instinct is that the author is broadly right and that his critics are defending a lost cause, but I have been burned by that instinct before.

What I need from you is an accuracy audit, claim by claim, not a reading list or a set of citations. Tell me which parts a philosopher working on social choice would accept as stated, which are popular simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which are simply wrong. I am especially interested in three joints: which of the author's "innocuous" assumptions are quietly doing the heavy lifting; whether the step from the theorem to the verdict on Rawls is licensed by the result itself or smuggled in from a separate argument; and whether the leap from a result about a fixed set of people to a conclusion about how many people there should be is one the theorem can bear. And tell me, at the end, which of the author's conclusions would survive once the summary is corrected and which would not, since that is what my readers will actually be weighing.
