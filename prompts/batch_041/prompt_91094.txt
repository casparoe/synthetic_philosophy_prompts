I'm taking a seminar on scientific realism and theory change this term, and I'm trying to write a short position paper on what, if anything, we learn about the world when one theory replaces another. Rather than picking a label and defending it, I've been working backwards from my own verdicts on the standard cases, and they have stopped fitting together.

My verdicts are these. Phlogiston: a dead end, and I don't think anything true about combustion was learned from it, even though the theory could be arranged to organise many of the same phenomena that oxygen theory organises. Caloric: abandoned, but Carnot's reasoning about heat engines carried over into thermodynamics intact, so I think something real about heat was learned. Fresnel's ether: the ether is gone and I'm glad it is, yet his equations for reflection and refraction are still in use, so again I think something real about light was learned. Newton's absolute space and absolute time: gone, and I don't believe anything was learned about space or time from them, even though his laws of motion survived. The crystalline spheres of the older astronomers: gone, nothing learned.

The trouble is that the ether and absolute space were both abandoned media, and in both cases the mathematics survived in some form; I nonetheless want to say that one taught us something about the world and the other taught us nothing. A friend in the seminar proposed that the rule should be simply: keep the mathematics, stay agnostic about whatever it was attached to. That strikes me as renaming the problem rather than solving it, since it would license the same verdict about Fresnel and about absolute space. She added, more technically, that any purely structural claim can be satisfied by any collection of the right size, so that the idea we learn only structure is either empty or false. I did not follow that argument and would like it spelled out, since it seems to bear on everything above.

What I'd like from you: the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case; what it implies about cases I haven't put to myself, in particular whether a mathematical survivor that outlives its interpretation counts as knowledge, what to say about a posited entity that is later confirmed, and what to say about a case like dark matter, where we may keep the equations without ever getting the entity; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them I should give up.
