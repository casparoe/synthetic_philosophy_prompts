I am a novelist. For fifteen years I have been writing people who make judgments about beauty — a restorer who can tell a cleaned canvas from a forgery by the way the varnish sits, a conductor who insists the orchestra has the passage wrong, a character who weeps at a building and cannot say why — and I have now been asked to turn the preoccupation into a lecture, to a residency of writers and critics, on whether beauty is objective. I am not going to do Hume exegesis. I mean to state my own position, and before I stand up and state it I want to be sure the position is answerable to everything it ought to be answerable to. So write me the desiderata: not a defense of any view, but the specification that any adequate theory of aesthetic taste would have to meet.

Take the target to be this. Such a theory must make sense of the fact that we treat some verdicts about beauty as better than others — that a judgment can be criticized, corrected, learned, that some people simply have better taste — while also honoring the fact that beauty is not a property like mass or charge, discovered by instruments and indifferent to whether anyone is moved. What I want from you is the list of what a theory has to get right in order to hold both of those at once.

State each requirement, and behind it the case or consideration that forces it. I want the theory to have to answer for the following in particular, and I want to know what each one demands of it:

- Two experienced viewers, neither confused and neither lying, each insisting the other has got it wrong, with no factual dispute between them to appeal to. What must the theory say is going on here, and what is it forbidden to say?
- What I have seen called the acquaintance principle: that one cannot, it seems, genuinely judge the beauty of a thing one has never encountered, that second-hand verdicts, however well supported, ring hollow. What does this require of the theory?
- The perfect forgery: a copy indistinguishable in every perceptual respect from the original it copies. Same beauty, or not? And notice what the answer commits you to about whether beauty lives in the surface or in the history.
- The reliable fact that repeated encounters tend to increase liking. If taste is tracking something real, why should familiarity move the verdict? Put this to the theory as a constraint it cannot simply wave away.
- The trained eye, ear, and palate: the expert discriminates structure in a work that the novice cannot perceive at all. Is this the theory's best evidence for a standard, or its worst embarrassment — does expertise track a property, or ingrain a convention?
- The gap between liking and judging beautiful: the guilty pleasure one would not defend, the acknowledged masterpiece one does not enjoy.

Then lay it out in exactly these sections and order:

1. "The Target" (roughly 150 words): what a theory of taste is a theory of, stated precisely enough that it is clear what would count as failing at it.
2. "The Requirements" (the bulk of the answer, roughly 900 words): the requirements, numbered, each with the case or consideration behind it and a note on why it is a requirement rather than a preference of some camp.
3. "Non-negotiable and Negotiable" (roughly 200 words): which of the requirements you would defend as constitutive of the problem, and which are merely the commitments of one tradition or another.
4. "The Tension Report" (roughly 300 words): any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for the incompatibility. This is the section I most want; if the list is jointly unsatisfiable, then the list itself is the result.
5. "Contenders Against the List" (roughly 250 words): briefly, how Hume's ideal critic, a Kantian universalism, a relativist or expressivist account, and a perceptual-expertise realism each fare against the requirements — a sentence or two apiece, no more.

Do not give me a bibliography or cite sources; I have no library at hand and will not be checking any of it. Argue from the cases themselves.
