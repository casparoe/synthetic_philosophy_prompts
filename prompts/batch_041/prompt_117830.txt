My officemates Maya and Dev have been going at each other for about a week over whether our lab should decide things by majority vote of the whole group instead of just deferring to whoever's the relevant expert. It started because we had to pick between two rival explanations for a wierd result, our PI went with the senior student's call, and it turned out to be wrong. Maya's case is that this is exactly what the Condorcet jury theorem is for: if every member is more likely than not to get the call right, a majority is more likely to be right than any single one of them, and the more people the better, so with twelve of us at, say, 70% each the majority should be right about 99% of the time and we'd be idiots not to vote. Dev says that only holds if everyone's errors are independent, and ours obviously aren't — we read the same papers, share the same advisor, and last term we all got fooled by the same artifact. Their errors come in bunches, he says, and bunched errors don't average out, so the 99% is a fantasy and adding more people to a group that's already wrong together just makes the wrong answer sound more authoritative. Maya came back that correlation isn't all-or-nothing — unless the errors are perfectly correlated, more voters still help, just less, and the theorem is an idealization like a frictionless plane; you don't throw out the baseline because reality has friction. Dev said in this case the friction is the whole phenomenon, and anyway there's a nastier problem: the theorem only tells you the majority converges on the truth if each member is better than a coin flip, but being better than a coin flip is precisely what a group with a shared blind spot cannot know about itself. He added that the theorem assumes there's a fact of the matter and only two options on the table, and our last call was a judgment call among four projects, so it doesn't even get off the ground. Maya said plenty of our decisions do have a right answer, and she'd rather have a procedure with a real accuracy edge than one that hinges on whether the senior student happens to be the one who's actually right. Neither will budge, and honestly I've stopped following who's winning. I have a feeling the 99% number is off but I can't do the binomial in my head.

Then tell me what both of them are missing, and finish with a verdict on which of them is more right overall.
