I'm writing a book in which a character has had the epilepsy surgery that cuts the commisurotomy -- and I've been reading up on the old split-brain work, Sperry and Gazzaniga and the rest, and the thing that hooked me is the left hemisphere acting like a press secretary: it gets asked why the hand just picked something up, and it improvises a reason, in good faith, for a decision it had nothing to do with. The patient isn't lying or even guessing. And the whole time this is offered as a live case in the debate over epiphenomenalism -- the view that experience itself doesn't cause anything -- and specifically over what I've seen called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences don't do causal work, how do we come to make judgments about them at all, and doesn't the epiphenomenalist's own reasoning get undercut by the same move?

So: an analysis that runs in both directions, and please keep two claims apart for me, because I suspect the case looks totally different depending on which is on the table. (a) experiences have no causal effects at all, including on whatever generates our reports about experience; (b) experiences aren't needed to explain those reports, whether or not they have some effects. Tell me which of the two the split-brain evidence actually bears on, and how.

And what becomes of the self-undercutting objection once the faculty we'd use to argue about consciousness is itself a confabulator?
