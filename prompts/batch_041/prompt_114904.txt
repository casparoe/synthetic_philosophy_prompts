I'm the technical half of a two-person startup building a coding assistant, and my cofounder and I keep going in circles on something I suspect is a philosophy question in disguise. The pipeline: user asks for something hard, our big expensive model breaks it into little subproblems, writes an answer for each one, and one of our three contractors (decent coders, not experts) looks at each subproblem's answer on its own and marks it ok or not. Whatever passes goes out the door. Then — this is the part I can't get past — we fine-tune the cheap model on those final answers and ship the cheap one, because nobody will pay big-model prices at scale. The cheap model never learns to break anything down; it just sees question-answer pairs. My cofounder's line is: every step a user sees was looked at by a human, the cheap model is just a compressed copy of the same pipeline, and if it's a bit dumber that's the cost of doing business. To me "every step got checked" and "the thing we ship got checked" feel like two different sentences, and the contractors never see the whole task, so they can't check the thing that actually matters — they can only say whether a snippet looks plausible. Also somethings off about the idea that you can squash a process into a model and keep whatever was good about it. But I can't articulate why he's wrong, or whether he is. Is there a real distinction here, and if so, what would it actually commit us to doing differently?
