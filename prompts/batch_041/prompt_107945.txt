Me and two colleagues are drafting internal norms for a ~30 person AI safety org, mostly around people going off and doing stuff on their own — publishing, emailing a lab, spinning up a project — and we keep running into the unilateralist's curse. Rough version: if any one of us can do X on our own, and each of us privately thinks X is probably fine but isn't sure, the chance that someone does X ends up way higher than any of us would be happy with. Great, but when we tried to turn it into a rule we realized we don't understand it well enough to know which parts even apply to us. So: of the open questions about the curse, which actually matter most for writing norms for a group like ours — small, mutually trusting, able to talk to each other cheaply — and which are red herrings? I want an ordering, not a survey: what's upstream of what, what's settleable with a bit of modeling vs. what's basically a values call, what's tractable for us in a semester, and what would actually change the norm we end up writing. Things I keep flip-flopping on (don't feel bound by these): whether the whole thing secretly assumes consequentialism; whether our real problem is the curse or its mirror image — the action that should have happened because any one of us could have done it, and nobody did; how much it's about independent vs. correlated errors, given we're all reading the same stuff; and whether the story even survives when the "anyone who could act" set is the whole field rather than a fixed group. We also floated our own half-baked refinement — a norm where you only act unilaterally if you'd be fine with everyone in the group acting that way — and I want to know if that's just restating the problem or actually buys us something. Where am I conflating two different problems? And please flag what's settled vs. what's genuinely contested among people who've thought hard about this — I don't want a contested view sold to me as the consensus.
