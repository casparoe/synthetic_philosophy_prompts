I taught physics for thirty-odd years and retired, and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group — historians and lit people mostly, sharp but no maths beyond what they've mercifully forgotten. Next month it's my turn to lead, and I want to do the result that says the expected value of information is never negative: you can always just ignore what you learned and do what you'd have done anyway, so in expectation, free information can't hurt you. I think it's lovely, and it apparently trips people up in interesting ways.

Don't explain the theorem to me, I get it. Plan how I should teach it to this crowd in about forty minutes. What has to be in place before the result even looks like it might be false — I assume decision problems and expected utility come first, but tell me the order and why it's that order and not some other. Give me the one example that should carry the whole thing, and it has to be a case where knowing more feels like it could hurt, otherwise the theorem just looks trivial, yet where the nonnegativity still falls out clean. And give me the misconceptions to expect. My guesses: they'll conflate the ex-ante nonnegativity with "information is always good", they'll insist you need some commitment device to "ignore" the info (I don't think you do — the action set doesn't change, so just pick the old act), they'll drag in regret and the ostrich effect, and someone will ask what happens when the information costs money. Tell me where in the sequence to head each one off, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and one closing question that would tell me whether it landed.

Keep the whole thing to one page, and give me the misconception part as a short table — misconception, and the point in the sequence where I kill it.
