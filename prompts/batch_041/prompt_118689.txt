I have just come out the other side of a long argument with a friend about moral trade, and I want a written answer that takes sides rather than adjudicating politely between us. The idea we were fighting over is this: two people who disagree about values can both come out ahead by trading. You care intensely about one thing and barely at all about another; I have the mirror-image priorities; so I do the cheap-for-me thing that matters to you, you do the cheap-for-you thing that matters to me, and each of us is better off by his own lights even though we never come to agree about what matters. My friend thinks the whole idea is a shell game, and I could not state the case against him cleanly enough to refute it to my own satisfaction. So I would like you to state it for him, at full strength, and then tell me whether it survives.

His objections, as fairly as I can put them. First, the currency objection: if any ordinary form of moral realism is true, then at most one of us has his values roughly right, so the surplus from a trade is measured in a currency that is counterfeit for at least one party. Being handed what you want is not the same as being made better off, if what you want is not in fact worth having. On this view, "both parties gain" is either false or merely indexical, and indexical gains do not add up to any improvement in the world. Second, the baseline objection: every surplus is computed against what each party would otherwise have done, and that baseline is often ongoing wrongdoing. My roommate case is the one that stuck with me — the neighbor who plays music at two in the morning agrees to stop in exchange for the other one doing his dishes for a month. The person willing to do more wrong has more to sell, so willingness to behave badly becomes a bargaining asset, and the trade pays a wrongdoer for restraining harm he had no right to inflict in the first place. Third, the integrity objection: some things are not mine to trade at all — my vote, my testimony, a duty I owe to someone who is not at the table — and even where I am free to trade, keeping my end of the bargain can require me to act against my own moral judgment, which is the very thing I take myself to be forbidden to do. His example there was a trade in which I agree to drop my objection to a development I believe is wrong, in exchange for his giving money to a cause I believe is urgent; he says that is not a trade but a purchase, and that I have sold something that was not for sale.

Write me an essay that reaches a verdict. Is moral trade a legitimate way to make the world better, or is my friend right that it launders wrongdoing and dresses up blackmail as mutual benefit? Say which of his three objections actually lands, which one I can throw back at him, and under what conditions — if any — a trade is permissible; if you think none is, say so flatly. Do not lean on names, citations, or reading recommendations; I want the argument, not the literature. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of what I have asked. Write in American English.
