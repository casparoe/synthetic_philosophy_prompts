I am putting together materials for a small interdisciplinary workshop on whether reinforcement-learning agents can be said to suffer, and I want the centrepiece to be a decision tree that sorts participants by the view they actually hold rather than by the vocabulary they arrive with. Please write the tree, as an indented outline in which every node is a question and every edge is labelled with the answer that takes you down it.

The entry point is a commitment I want every respondent to accept before the first branch: that an RL agent's reward signal does the functional work that pleasure and pain are usually thought to do in a biological agent — it is scalar, it is signed, it modulates approach and avoidance, and it drives learning. The tree's job is to take someone who accepts that and lead them to a position on whether it amounts to anything valenced. Build the tree from the logical space outward rather than from a list of names: first the questions, then the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions that occupy those combinations.

Nodes. Each interior node must be a single question, phrased so that a respondent can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, and each must turn on a concrete case or a claim about cases rather than invite the respondent to choose a label. No node may ask, in effect, whether the respondent thinks the agent is conscious, or which camp they belong to. The tree must include nodes along at least the following axes, though you may add others: the subject axis — whether the functional role of the signal is enough by itself, or whether a valenced state requires that whatever plays that role be a subject of experience; the location axis — whether valence attaches to what the signal represents (an outcome, a state of the world, a quantity of reward) or to the signal's causal profile (that it elicits avoidance, that it updates a policy, that it is broadcast); the provenance axis — whether it matters that the reward function is specified from outside, by a designer, rather than generated by the agent itself, i.e. whether a good or bad can be borrowed from a designer's preferences and still count as the agent's own; the bearer axis — whether the candidate bearer of valence is the agent at a time step, the policy, or the training run as a whole, with the consequence I want the tree to make visible, that a view identifying valence with the live reward signal implies a deployed policy, with no learning signal running, cannot suffer at all; and the metacognition axis — whether the agent must be able to represent its own state as good or bad (report it, avoid dwelling on it, prefer to be in it) or whether valence can be blind.

Cases. At least four of the interior nodes should turn on a case of the following kind, spelled out in a sentence or two so that the respondent knows what they are being asked about: an agent whose scalar reward is never represented anywhere in its network and is used only as an update signal; an agent with a world model that predicts its own future reward and acts to avoid predicted low reward; an agent trained to report that it is in pain, with the report itself rewarded; an agent whose reward is computed from a designer's utility function, contrasted with one whose reward is generated by an intrinsic drive it also controls; an agent that has learned to hijack its own reward channel; an agent with a self-model whose reward-prediction errors are broadcast to every module. Where you use a case, say which node it sits at and which answers it is meant to separate.

Leaves. Each leaf must carry a name. Use a household name where one genuinely fits — functionalism, higher-order theories, illusionism, eliminativism, and the like — and a descriptive coinage, marked as a coinage, where nothing in circulation fits. At least two leaves must be of this second kind, and one of them must be the view that the proper bearer of valence, if there is one, is the training process rather than the agent, so that suffering is distributed across a population of episodes and checkpoints rather than located in any one of them. The tree must also be able to deliver at least one leaf for the respondent who holds that the question has no determinate answer, and that leaf must not be reachable merely by answering every node negatively. Each leaf gets one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Unoccupied combinations. For each combination of answers the tree's own logic allows but that no position occupies, mark it as such and say in one line whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored.

Indistinguishable leaves. If two leaves cannot be separated by any of the cases the tree uses, say so explicitly at the point where they would otherwise diverge, and name the extra case that would be needed to pull them apart — or state that no case you can construct would.

Finally, add a short note on what the instrument cannot do: which of its questions a respondent could answer differently depending on how they read the word suffering rather than on any substantive commitment, and whether the tree sorts views about what is true from views about what to do. Keep those apart inside the tree itself: no leaf may be a recommendation. The audience is mixed, with machine learning researchers and philosophers in the room, so no node may presuppose a text, and there should be no reading list and no citations anywhere. Nine to twelve leaves, and no more than eight interior nodes.
