The episode. In the 1970s Paul Kolers and Michael von Grünau ran a set of apparent-motion demonstrations that have since become a standard exhibit in discussions of the timing of experience. Two dots are flashed in succession at separate locations on a screen — say a blue dot above and a red dot below, a few degrees apart, separated by a short interval (on the order of a tenth of a second). At the right combination of spacing and timing, observers do not report two dots. They report a single dot that travels from the first position to the second, and that changes color abruptly partway along the illusory path. The awkward part is the reported timing of that change: subjects place the color change at a moment when, by the experimenter's clock, the second dot has not yet been presented, so its color is not yet available to be seen. The report thus seems to locate a content that depends on the second stimulus at a time before that stimulus existed. Treat that as the episode to be explained.

The theory. Daniel Dennett, in Consciousness Explained (1991), treats this as a test case for what he calls Cartesian materialism: the picture on which there is a finish line in the brain at which content becomes conscious, so that the order of arrival at that line just is the order of experience. Dennett claims the ordinary ways of accounting for the color phi come in two flavors. An Orwellian account says the experience really did unfold one way and was then rewritten: the subject's memory of the earlier phase is edited after the fact, in light of the later stimulus. A Stalinesque account says the reconciliation happens before the subject is conscious of any of it — the verdict is fixed in advance, and what reaches experience is already the tidied-up version. Dennett's further claim is that the two accounts are empirically indistinguishable, because they agree about the underlying neural processing and disagree only about where to place a line between pre-experiential and post-experiential, a line whose existence he denies.

What I want from you:

1. Map the case onto the pair. State precisely what each account predicts and what each must say about the subject's report that the change occurred mid-path. Be specific about what is being revised, when, and by what mechanism or process.

2. Assess the alleged indistinguishability. Is it a contingent limitation of current methods, or is any conceivable measure — verbal report, reaction time, neural signature, priming, subsequent discrimination — necessarily downstream of the very revision at issue? If the latter, what follows for how the case should be used in argument?

Consider a third, deflationary option that Dennett's framing tends to crowd out: that nothing is revised at all, and that the abrupt mid-path color change is a product of ordinary interactions between the two stimuli, the report simply being a description of a state that was never earlier or later in the relevant sense. Where does this option bite? Does it dissolve the puzzle or merely relocate it?

4. Run the analysis in the other direction. Does the color phi case actually support Dennett's conclusion, or does it support only the weaker claim that we have no evidence for any particular finish line?
