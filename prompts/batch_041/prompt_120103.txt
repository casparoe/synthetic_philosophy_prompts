My friend Annie is retiring in June after nearly forty years teaching history, and I've somehow ended up as the one giving the after-dinner speech at her send-off, which is at a slightly depressing hotel function room with about sixty people in it. The problem is who's in the room. Roughly half are her own generation — they came up on source criticism and old-school archive work, and they get genuinely irritated by any suggestion that the historian "constructs" the past; for them the whole point of the discipline is Ranke's ambition to show things as they actually were, and narrative talk sounds to them like a confession that you're just making it up. The other half are her former doctoral students and the younger faculty, who've read Carr and Hayden White and think the first half are naive about how the historian's questions, and the shape of the story they end up telling, are already there before a single document gets read. Annie has spent her career stuck in the middle of that fight and has been trying to formulate a position she calls granular objectivity, which goes roughly like this: objectivity isn't a property of a story as a whole, it's a gradient that runs with the grain of the claim — the finer the claim, the more the evidence can push back on it, so that "the letter is dated 14 March" or "the garrison had about three thousand men" can be true or false in a fairly robust sense, while any claim about what a whole decade meant is doing work the sources can only constrain, never settle. Her line is that this isn't a fudge or a compromise but the actual structure of historical knowledge, and that the people insisting on global objectivity are making exactly the same mistake as the people saying it's all narrative, just from opposite ends.

I want the speech to be more than a nice tribute — can you write it for me, maybe 800-900 words, as something a person could actually say out loud at a dinner (no bullet points, no "firstly, secondly", warm, but with a real argument running through it)? It needs to give the granular-objectivity idea enough room that the younger half hear their own position in it, while the older half don't feel they're being told the archive doesn't matter, and the best way in is probably one concrete case from her career used as the test — a disputed date, a document someone fudged, a set of accounts that didn't add up — so feel free to invent a plausible one if you don't want to hedge. Two other things. At some point the speech has to let the old guard have their say properly and in their own terms, not as a straw man, because I want the sceptics in the room to feel the speaker has actually heard them; and it should end somewhere that makes the disagreement feel like a reason to keep doing the work rather than a reason the discipline is in trouble. Also keep in mind the audience is half tipsy by the time I stand up, so nothing that needs a diagram. If it helps, her last book was on a provincial tax revolt in the 1840s and she's never once in her carreer said the word "discourse" out loud.
