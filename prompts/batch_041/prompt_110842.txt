I am a novelist, and I am writing about two brothers who inherit a failing family firm and keep it alive years past the point at which any outsider would call another dollar a sane investment; how the book ends depends on which story I am telling, so I need this argument settled rather than surveyed. Write an essay on how escalation of that kind should be explained. One account makes it a failure of the mind: the sunk-cost fallacy, a willingness to let money already gone dictate money still to be spent, and thus something better reasoning would prevent. The other makes it a property of the situation: in the dollar auction, where a dollar is sold to the highest bidder and the runner-up also pays, the trap closes because each next increment is defensible on its own terms, the standing loss being already lost and only the next bid and its odds being at stake, so nobody need be reasoning badly for the bidding to run past sense. Weigh the two against each other, and be careful about where in the sequence you locate the error, since the bid that ruins the bidder is not obviously a mistake at the moment it is made. Then reach a verdict: when we watch someone throw good money after bad, are we watching a mind fail or a structure work as designed? Defend one answer rather than ending on a both-are-true note, and say plainly what follows for the question that concerns me -- whether such a person is a fool, a victim, or something the vocabulary of either story cannot quite name.
