I'm working on an essay about Simone Weil's case against political parties and I want you to tear into my notes before I draft it.

The gist: Weil thinks the party form is poison no matter what the party stands for. A party isn't just a bunch of people who happen to agree; it's an organism with its own survival drive, and the member's thinking gets eaten by it. The member doesn't really hold opinions anymore -- the party holds them and lends them out. So the standard defense of parties, that they aggregate interests and give ordinary people a voice, is answering the wrong question. Aggregation is the disease, not the cure.

The move I want to make, and where I'm least sure of myself: I want to push past Weil and argue she's a threat to democracy as such, not just to party politics. If parties are inherently thought-killing, and you can't run a mass democracy without parties, then either democracy requires the thing that kills thinking or thinking is only possible outside politics. I want to say Weil quietly accepts this, which is why her later writing turns so inward -- the only free space left is the soul alone with truth.

Things I'm unsure about:
- Is this a fair reading or am I making her more anti-democratic than she is? Does she separate the party from other kinds of collective belonging, or does the critique spill over into any group at all? She keeps saying thought has to be impersonal rather than collective -- does that distinction actually save her, or is it just a relabeling?
- The extension I want: parties as an epistemic monopoly. A party doesn't just coordinate action, it coordinates what you're allowed to notice. Legit extension of her point, or am I smuggling in modern media stuff?
- I claim her alternative -- thinking anchored to direct contact with reality, work, attention -- can't scale to a whole society. Right, or is there a version that scales?

Comments please on (1) whether I'm reading Weil right, (2) whether the threat-to-democracy move is genuinely interesting or just a cheap gotcha, and (3) whether the epistemic-monopoly bit is doing any work or just restating her in fancier words. Be blunt -- if the whole thing dissappears into a truism I'd rather find out now.
