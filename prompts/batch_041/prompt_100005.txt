For the past few weeks I've been sitting in on a departmental reading group — mostly philosophers, plus two statisticians from across the road, all of them comfortable with elementary probability and none of whom has ever seen a probability logic — and next month it falls to me to run the session on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. What I want from you is not an explanation of the results but a plan for the explanation. My sense is that the algebra is the easy part and the dependency order is what destroys people: the room cannot be told the conditional-probability thesis, let alone see why anyone would want it, until the difference between a credence and a conditional credence is already firm, and it cannot feel the bite of the proof until it accepts that the thesis is quantified over a whole family of credence functions closed under conditionalization — stated for a single fixed distribution the theorem has no grip whatever, and I expect that to be precisely where the scepticism sets in. Assume a whiteboard, seventy-five minutes, no slides, and a statistician in the front row who will announce by minute twenty that the whole business is a type error.

My instinct for the carrying example is the die: a fair die, the roll is even, the roll is six, so the conditional probability is a third, then condition on fresh information until something visibly gives way — but I have a nagging suspicion that no case confined to a single distribution can display the collapse at all, and that the examples everyone reaches for are traps which make the result look like an artefact of arithmetic rather than a constraint on the thesis. So I want the example picked for what it can and cannot show, and I want to know in advance where each predictable misconception is planted and where in the sequence I defuse it: that Lewis showed conditionals are not propositions, that he showed if is not truth-functional, that the thesis is simply refuted and the suppositional reading dead with it, that the proof is a trick about notation, and whatever the statisticians will manufacture on the spot. I also want the room to leave seeing that the theorem does not kill the thesis outright — that there are several quite distinct ways out — so the sequence has to make each escape route feel like a live option rather than a footnote. Give me exactly five numbered items in the order in which I should build the session: one on the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what; one on the example that carries the whole thing, including why the single-distribution cases fail and what the example must have and lack; one on the misconceptions and the exact point in the sequence at which each is headed off; one on the single question I should ask at the end to tell whether they have understood the result rather than merely followed it; and one on what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so. Each item one or two sentences, with no preamble and no summary.
