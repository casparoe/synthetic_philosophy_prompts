I'm teaching an upper-level seminar this spring that's cross-listed between philosophy and cognitive science — we meet Tuesdays at 4, and the enrollment is about half CS majors and half philosophy majors, which is honestly part of the problem — and the whole back half of the course is Gödel, Escher, Bach plus the little industry of commentary that grew up around it. It's my first time teaching it, and I've been dissapointed by how the students handle Hofstadter. They can all tell you about strange loops and tangled hierarchies, they can explain the Liar and the fixed-point construction, and then the moment you push on whether any of that has anything to do with actual self-awareness, they wave their hands and say the system "refers to itself." I want the final to have one essay question that makes that hand-wave impossible to get away with.

Here's the thing I keep arguing about with a colleague down the hall, and I'd like the exam to force students to go through it rather than around it. Her objection is that Gödel's construction only buys self-reference by way of a coding scheme — some numbering we impose from the outside, with nothing privileged about the one we choose. The sentence that supposedly says of itself that it's unprovable only says that relative to our translation manual; swap the coding and the self-reference evaporates. So on her view the loop is always a loop *for us*, the interpreters, never for the system, and the analogy to a mind that represents itself is doing all its real work inside the head of whoever is telling the story. I half buy it and half don't — I keep thinking that a brain's self-representation is also built out of some contingent encoding, so maybe the objection proves too much and would sink the mind along with the formula. The other thing I want them to trip over is that incompleteness is a limitative result: it tells you what a system can't prove, and yet the GEB story runs it as the engine of something positive, the emergence of a self. That slide from a negative result to a positive claim about minds needs defending, not just narrating.

So: write me the question, a model answer of roughly 700-800 words that a strong student could plausibly produce in 90 minutes, and a marking scheme. Put it in three sections and label them exactly "The question", "Model answer", and "Marking scheme". The question itself should be self-contained enough to hand out as-is, including whatever instructions to students you think it needs — length, time, whether they should be quoting from the book, that sort of thing. For the marking scheme, give me five must-hit points, each with a mark value, totaling 20, and for each one a single line on the way students typically miss it. And please don't let the question be answerable by summarizing Hofstadter — it should turn on whether a student can say what would have to be true for the analogy to be more than a pun, and which of the two objections above survives contact with the details.
