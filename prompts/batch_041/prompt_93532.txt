Background so you see the stakes. I'm a boring old psychological-continuity guy about personal identity. What makes you you across time isn't any particular lump of matter persisting -- it's the right kind of continuity of memory, character, and mental life. On that basis I've been telling the uploading crowd that a *gradual* swap is fine in principle: replace my neurons one at a time with functionally identical chips, preserve all the wiring and dispositions, and I'm still here at the end. I've said this in print. I'm committed to it.

Here's the argument they built.

(1) Personal identity over time is constituted by the right kind of psychological continuity -- overlapping chains of memory, intention, character and the like -- and not by sameness of matter.
(2) Replacing a single neuron in my brain with a silicon unit that preserves every connection and every disposition changes nothing about my memories, character, or mental life at that moment.
(3) So a single such replacement doesn't end me: I survive it.
(4) If I survive one such replacement, I survive the next, since it's exactly the same kind of change; and if I survive that one, I survive the one after; and so on.
(5) Therefore I survive every replacement, and I survive after all of them -- once every neuron is silicon, I'm still here.
(6) Now suppose the surgeons keep each extracted biological neuron alive in a nutrient bath. At the end the bath holds a complete set of my original neurons, and technicians wire them back together into a working brain with the same connectivity they had inside me.
(7) By (1), what makes a later being me is psychological continuity. The reassembled biological brain, when it wakes up, has all my memories and character (it's my original tissue with my original wiring); and the silicon brain also has all my memories and character (that's what (5) told us).
(8) So both of them are me.
(9) But they're two, and they'll diverge -- they'll disagree about what they're doing this afternoon. One of me can't be in two places disagreeing with itself.
(10) So (1) is false.

I would rather die than give up (1).
