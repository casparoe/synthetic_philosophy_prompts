I'm in my third year of a philosophy degree and I've volunteered to design and run one session of a seminar called Science, Truth, and Progress -- sixteen students, ninety minutes, all of them comfortable with elementary propositional logic but with no background in formal epistemology. The session is on verisimilitude, and the thing I want the room to feel in its bones is the strange corner Popper painted himself into: it seems utterly obvious that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another (think of a slightly wrong date versus a wildly wrong one, or of a false theory that gets almost everything right except one clause), and yet the moment you try to make "closer" precise in terms of the true and the false consequences a theory carries, the intuition and the measure start to pull apart. My understanding is that Popper's own qualitative definition -- theory B is closer to the truth than A when B's true consequences exceed A's while B's false consequences do not exceed A's -- was shown in the mid-1970s, in a cluster of papers in the British Journal for the Philosophy of Science by Tichý, Harris, and Miller, to have the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other, which is exactly the opposite of what the definition was for. I don't want to announce that result and then illustrate it; I want the group to back into the tension through something they do, so that when the formal point arrives it lands on an experience they have already had. I've been arguing about this with friends for a week and I still can't tell whether "closer to the truth" is a real relation we're tracking or a placeholder for a feeling, so I'd rather build the session around the felt problem than around the verdict.

Here is my rough idea, which I'd like you to develop into something fully runnable, and to replace outright if you can think of a cleaner engine for the same experience. Before class I generate a hidden target -- a whole number between 1 and 64, sealed in an envelope and shown to the room so they know it exists. The group splits into four teams of four. On a handout I give a fixed list of, say, two dozen test propositions about the number ("it is even," "it is prime," "it is greater than 50," "it is a perfect square," "it lies between 20 and 40," "it is divisible by 3," "it is one of these three values," and so on), and each team submits a theory: a conjunction of constraints on the number drawn from an agreed admissible vocabulary of interval, divisibility, and parity claims. I then score each theory against the hidden number by asking, for each test proposition, whether the theory entails it, entails its negation, or is silent, giving plus one for an entailed truth, minus one for an entailed falsehood, and nothing for silence, and I display for every team both its separate counts of true and false consequences and its net score. We reveal the number and argue. The point of the reveal is that the naive net score will very often crown a theory that feels wrong -- a timid theory that says almost nothing, or a bold theory that is right about many things and wrong about a few conspicuous ones -- and the question I then put to the room is not "who won" but "which of these theories is closer to the truth," with each team defending a ranking. What I'm after is the moment when they discover that a bold theory can have more true consequences *and* more false consequences than a cautious one, so that the two natural responses -- reward the truth you captured, penalise the error you committed -- give opposite verdicts, and nobody can say which response is the right one without already assuming the answer. In the last third I want to map what happened onto the actual philosophical terrain: Popper's definition and why the boldness/accuracy trade-off breaks it; the impossibility result and the fact that Popper conceded it; and then the split among the repairs, where one family keeps the content-based approach and treats truthlikeness as a function of a truth factor and a content factor, while another family takes "likeness" literally and measures how far the worlds a theory permits are from the actual world. I suspect the room's own disagreement about how to rank the theories will track that divide, and I'd like the debrief to make that visible.

Please give me the full design: the setup and materials, how to choose the test propositions and the admissible claim forms so that entailment is decidable without argument, how to precompute the scoring, the prize or stake (small and non-monetary -- the winner picking next week's topic, say), and a minute-by-minute timeline. Then the debrief, mapping specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea rather than just summarising the idea. I also want your honest read on what will predictably go wrong the first time: I'm already worried that teams will game the scoring by submitting maximally cautious theories, that the word "entails" will start fights, and that the arithmetic will make the whole thing feel like a quiz rather than a discovery -- tell me which of these you expect, what to do about each, and any failure I haven't anticipated. Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce. My guess is that it can stage the intuition and the trade-off but cannot reproduce the impossibility itself, since that needs infinite consequence classes and a proof, and that a sharp student will notice the finite game always admits some pair of false theories where one dominates the other; if that's right, say so plainly and tell me how the facilitator should carry the part the game cannot carry. Assume no reading, no handouts beyond what we print ourselves, and nothing that requires a citation.
