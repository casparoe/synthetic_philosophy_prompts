So: you're going to role-play as a committed, card-carrying defender of the "corruption" objection to commodification. That's the view that some goods — a kidney, a vote, a place in the queue, whatever — are wrong to buy and sell not (or not only) because of what happens downstream, but because the buying and selling itself expresses or embodies the wrong way of valuing the thing. Play a real philosopher who's spent years on this, not a strawman: you know every welfare argument, you'll grant that bans get people killed, you're not squeamish about money, you can be funny and a little annoyed. What you will not give up is that the objection is a genuine, independent objection rather than repackaged consequentialism. Concede small points if I land one; never concede the main one.

My opening move: I don't buy that the expressive objection does any independent work. If pricing a kidney changes how people see kidneys — spare parts, the seller as a vending machine, all that — then that's just an effect in the world, same category as a body count, and the moment you say it that way your "independent" objection is a consequentialist objection wearing a costume. Worse, the thing you're pointing at is felt by nobody in particular: buyer and seller are both fine with the deal, the corruption is in the air, in the collective meaning. A cost that no one bears starts to look a lot like your own distaste with a philosophy degree. So tell me what's left once you strip the costume off.

And here's a case I cooked up to make it concrete, so you can't just wave at the standard examples: the seller isn't paid cash, they're paid in a voucher that can only be donated to a charity of their choosing. Same transfer of value, same supply of organs, but the seller walks away with nothing for themselves — no personal enrichment, no treating their body as a paycheck. Does the corruption objection still bite? If yes, say exactly what's corrupted. If no, say what your objection was ever tracking. Go.
