Right — I need a dialogue written out for a thing I'm putting together. Two people, both sharp, going at each other over whether generations are real. Boomers, millennials, gen z: actual kinds of people, or just labels that caught on?

One of them is a realist and should have a decent case, not a vibe: sharing a formative stretch of years leaves a lasting mark, so cohorts carry durable dispositions; and social change happens partly by cohorts replacing each other rather than by anyone changing their mind — the demographers' cohort, the technical one, which is a different animal from the pop-culture generation. Make the realist say which of those two they think is the real kind, and whether their story needs the big branded labels at all or would work just as well with five-year birth brackets and no names.

The other is the sceptic and has to be just as good. Their material: the boundaries are arbitrary and keep being redrawn; a fair bit of the vocabulary came out of market segmentation rather than research; the spread within a generation swamps the gap between generations; and — the bit I most want answered rather than dodged — age, period and birth year are mathematically tied together (birth year is just period minus age), so from the usual data you can't cleanly identify age effects, period effects and cohort effects separately, which means a lot of 'generational' findings are an age effect (the young are always skint) or a period effect (the crash hit everyone) in a cohort costume. The realist has to actually take that on — definately no dodging it.

Then let them go a few more rounds on the follow-up: if the label changes how the people in it behave, does that make the kind real, or does it just make the label consequential? The realist should be free to say social kinds are the sort that react to being named; the sceptic should be free to say that's a fact about labels, not about kinds. Let that run and don't force a tidy ending.

No strawmen, no talking past each other — each reply has to answer the actual last point, and if one of them lands a hit the other gives ground instead of restating. And don't let them park on 'well, it depends what you mean by real' and leave it there: I want them to find the exact spot where they've stopped disagreeing about the facts and started disagreeing about what it takes for something to count as real, and to each give up one concrete thing along the way. I don't need a who-said-what tour either, just the arguement. And can you do it in British English — spellings and all — please.
