A friend who's a philosophy postdoc summarized it for me like this: "The Judy Benjamin problem is the famous counterexample to conditionalization. Judy is dropped into a field split into Red and Blue, each with a headquarters and a second company, and she starts with equal credences in all four. Then she gets reliable information: if she's in Red, she's in Red HQ. Conditionalization can't handle this, because you can't conditionalize on a conditional. If you try to conditionalize on the material conditional, you get the absurd result that Blue Second is impossible and Blue HQ is as likely as Red HQ. Van Fraassen solved the problem by showing that the correct update rule is to minimize relative entropy from your prior, subject to the new conditional constraint. That gives the unique right answer: Red drops to about 0.53, and the probability of Blue stays the same, with only the Red probabilities shifting. The lesson is that conditionalization is not the whole of Bayesian updating, and that conditional information requires a different rule. Some Bayesians have tried to resist this by saying Judy didn't really learn a conditional, but van Fraassen's argument is widely regarded as decisive."
