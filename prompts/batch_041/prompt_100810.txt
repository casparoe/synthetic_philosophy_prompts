For the better part of a year now, a small reading group I sit in on has been returning, meeting after meeting, to a single puzzle and has, so far as I can tell, made no progress on it whatsoever. The puzzle is the one usually introduced with a sentence like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." We long ago agreed on the data: the sentence sounds absurd in the first person and the present tense, sounds perfectly fine once the subject is someone else or the tense is shifted, and can apparently be true, since people are sometimes wrong about their own beliefs. What we cannot agree on is what the absurdity is. One faction holds that the sentence expresses an impossible combination of beliefs and is therefore a kind of contradiction in the logic of belief. A second holds that the two clauses are entirely compatible and that the oddity is purely pragmatic, a matter of what asserting the first clause conventionally conveys and the second clause then cancels. A third holds that the real culprit is the first-person present tense and the peculiar transparency of self-knowledge, so that the trouble is neither a contradiction nor a conversational implicature but a failure of self-knowledge. We can restate each of these positions to the others' satisfaction, and still no one moves an inch. My own suspicion—offered as a suspicion—is that we are not disagreeing about one thing but trading answers to three different questions under a single heading, and that the word "absurd" is quietly doing three different jobs.

I would like you to diagnose this properly rather than adjudicate it. Is the dispute verbal, or does something substantive turn on it? Do the factions differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over starting intuitions that none of them can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question—about how belief relates to avowal, say—doing the work? Are they, as I suspect, answering different questions under one name? And is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? Two recent wrinkles have made things worse and may deserve your attention. Sentences that trade flat-out belief for a degree of confidence—"It's raining, but I'd put it at sixty percent"—strike most of us as markedly less absurd. And cases in which someone sincerely reports a belief they do not in fact hold, through self-deception or through a lesion, strike us as intelligible but strange. Whether those variants cut toward the doxastic reading, the pragmatic reading, or the self-knowledge reading is precisely what we cannot settle. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or a change of question—or why none should be expected.

Please write this as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter. I don't need a survey of the literature or any references; I care about the structure of the disagreement, not its bibliography.
