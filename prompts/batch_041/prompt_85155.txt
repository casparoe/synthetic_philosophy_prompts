I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I run a small quantitative betting model with two friends. Our standing argument is about stake sizing, and it keeps collapsing into people defending labels rather than saying what they would actually do. I would like a decision tree I can hand to the three of us: one that takes someone from what they already believe about a concrete bet to a named position on how to size it, without ever asking them which position they hold.

Start from the one commitment the three of us share: that a positive edge can make a bet worth staking something on. Make every branch node a question we can answer without knowing where it leads, phrased as what we would do in a case rather than as what we would call ourselves. Assume we can follow expected values, variances and logarithms, but do not assume we know the names of any of the positions. The tree has to route at least these cases: (i) a single bet we will only ever get one shot at; (ii) a long run of independent bets with the same edge; (iii) a bet where we cannot tell whether the edge is real or an artifact of overfitting; (iv) a favorable bet that can only be taken for our entire bankroll, with a small chance of total loss and a very large upside; (v) several simultaneous bets on events that are correlated with one another.

The leaves should be the positions people actually take. At a minimum: maximize expected wealth, taking any positive-edge bet and accepting the chance of ruin; maximize expected log wealth, i.e. the growth-optimal or Kelly stake; stake a fixed fraction of the Kelly stake (half-Kelly and its relatives) as a discount for not trusting the model; maximize expected utility under a concave utility function of your own choosing, on the grounds that Kelly is just log utility and nothing privileges log; hold that the average that matters is the one along a single path through time rather than across parallel copies of you, so that growth rate rather than expected wealth is the objective, which is the view I have heard called geometric rationality; and never stake enough that one bad run ends the operation. If you think two of those are one position wearing two hats, or that a leaf is missing, say so and put your version in the tree.

Build the tree out of the logical space first, the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and only then attach names to the leaves. Mark any combination of answers that no position occupies, and say in a clause whether that is because it is incoherent, because nobody would want it, or merely because nobody has explored it. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and name the extra question that would separate them. Keep it to at most five branching questions.

Lead with the tree itself, as an indented list in which each branch node is a question and each leaf is a position plus one line on its most awkward commitment. Put the unoccupied combinations and the inseparable pairs after the tree. The whole reply should be under 900 words.
