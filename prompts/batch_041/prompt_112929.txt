Act as a professional speechwriter who also holds a doctorate in moral philosophy: someone who can handle Kant's ethics at a level that would survive a seminar and still write a toast that lands in a room full of people who have never read a page of philosophy. I need a best man's speech, and I'd like you to think it through carefully before you begin writing.

The groom is one of my oldest friends, a Kant scholar who has spent his career defending the rigorist reading of the murderer at the door — the reading on which you may not lie even to the man who means to kill the person hiding in your house. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for fifteen years, and the stock move in my corner of the internet is to treat that conclusion as a reductio of the whole enterprise and move on. I've watched him take it seriously for a decade, and I've stopped thinking the stock move is free. I'm not asking you to settle whether the rigorist conclusion actually follows from Kant's own principles; the toast has to be built on the version of him who believes it does, though it can be funny about him.

Here is the constraint, and it is the reason I'm asking you instead of writing it myself. He will be at the front table checking every sentence for truth, and I am not going to hand him a counterexample at his own wedding. So nothing in this toast may be a false statement: not the small flatteries, not the embellished anecdote, nothing. The reading I want to work with holds that the duty binds what one asserts, which leaves me silence and redirection — I may leave things out, change the subject, decline a question that wasn't asked of me — but I may not assert anything I think is false, however gently, however much his mother would have enjoyed it. That is the whole difficulty. A wedding speech is a machine for saying pleasant things, and a large share of the pleasant things available about any couple are false.

Here is everything you know about them, and you may not assert anything outside it. He wrote his dissertation on that passage and has defended it in arguments and at one memorably bad dinner party ever since. He met Maya at a conference reception, where she was the only person in the room who asked what he actually worked on and then waited through the entire answer. Two years ago I asked him over lunch whether a book I'd spent four years writing was any good; he took about forty seconds to decide how to say it, and then he told me the truth. He is a miserable person to watch a film with, because he cannot let a bad argument in a plot go by, and he has never once pretended to enjoy something he didn't. Maya is a pediatric nurse; a good half of her family have never met a philosopher. He is bad at small talk and has stopped trying to improve.

I want the philosophy load-bearing rather than ornamental. My angle is that the trait which makes him a difficult dinner guest is the trait that makes his word worth something: a man who grants himself an exception whenever the consequences look bad has appointed himself the judge of his own exceptions, and a judgment made by the party who benefits from it is worth nothing to me. If that thought shows up, it should show up as something the room feels rather than as a thesis I announce. The murderer has to appear, in a single sentence, in a way that the philosophers in the room recognize at once and her mother follows without being lectured; no names of essays, no dates, no interpreters.

One more requirement, and it's the one I care about most. Somewhere in the toast there must be a sentence that costs me something: true, mildly unflattering to him, and said anyway. I want it to end up being the warmest sentence in the speech, and I don't want you to explain why it's warm.

Before the toast, give me one short paragraph of plain prose naming the flattering sentences you were tempted by and threw out, and why each of them was false. Then the toast itself, three to four minutes spoken, in the voice of a friend who loves him and is not intimidated by him. Half the room reads the same blogs I do and will notice if I cheat; his family will forgive a great deal in exchange for a laugh, and the philosophers will forgive nothing sentimental.

Write all of it in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables, and no stage directions, and don't label the parts.
