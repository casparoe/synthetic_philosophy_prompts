I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I want help with a problem that keeps recurring at work: projects, and occasionally whole teams, that continue to absorb money and people because of what has already been spent on them. The cleanest small-scale version I know is the classic dollar auction. A one-dollar prize is auctioned in five-cent increments; the highest bidder pays what they bid and receives the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and receives nothing. I can follow the mechanics well enough to see why the bidding sails past a dollar and does not naturally stop, and I have read enough to know that the standard advice is to disregard costs that cannot be recovered. What I lack is any clear statement of what a theory in this area would have to deliver before I should take it seriously. That is what I am asking you for. I am not after a reading list or citations; I would not follow them up.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of escalation of commitment: a theory of when it is rational to continue an endeavor after resources have been irrecoverably spent on it, and why people continue when it is not. Treat this as a specification. Give me the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, and the results it must be consistent with. For every requirement, state the case or consideration that forces it; a requirement with no case behind it is a preference, and I want to be able to tell the two apart.

I am asking for two things at once, and I would like you to say whether one theory can supply both: an explanation of why the escalation happens even in people who understand the trap perfectly well, and a decision procedure usable by someone who is already inside the auction and has to decide whether to add the next increment.

Here are the cases I care about, in increasing order of how much they strain the obvious answers. (a) The standard two-player auction, no cap on bids, both players rational and each knowing it of the other. (b) The same, except bids are capped at five dollars. (c) The same as (a), except the second-highest bidder is refunded half of their final bid. (d) The same as (a), except the opponent is not a person but a fixed script that always raises by one increment until the cap is reached. (e) A version in which the thing being won is not money but a reputation, worth more the more publicly one is seen to have stuck with the commitment. (f) The real-world analogues I actually care about — a war, a lawsuit, a failing product line — where the prize is not fixed, the loser-pays rule is not literally in force, and more than two parties are involved. Your theory should either render a verdict on each of these or say plainly which structural features it is abstracting away from. I also want it to identify which features of the auction are doing the work: the fixed prize, the loser pays, the absence of a cap, the alternating and public bids, the inability of the players to coordinate.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp. Then flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion about where the conflict will be, but I would rather you locate it yourself than have me plant it; if the list as a whole turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so and say which requirement you would drop, because that is the part I most want to see.

Then score the main candidate accounts against your list, briefly, since I expect the list itself to be where the work is. I have in mind: (i) the plain rule that expenditures that cannot be recovered are irrelevant to a forward-looking decision; (ii) the account on which escalation is an error driven by loss aversion and by reluctance to have wasted one's own earlier decision; (iii) the account on which continuing is rational because quitting damages one's credibility or reputation; (iv) the game-theoretic account on which, once inside the auction, there is no point at which a further bid is worse than stopping.

Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate accounts, and the requirement that is most often assumed rather than argued for.

Number your requirements R1, R2, and so on, and end with a single final line in exactly this form: Verdict: R<n>; conflict R<i>/R<j> — where n is the number of the most load-bearing requirement and the conflict term names the pair you judge jointly unsatisfiable, or reads conflict none if you find no such pair.
