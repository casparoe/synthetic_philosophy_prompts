I run the analysis team at a mid-sized charity — eight people, we turn out cost-effectiveness estimates and forecasts that other people then spend actual money on, so when one of our numbers is off it isn't just embarrassing, it's a few hundred grand pointed at the wrong thing. We have a convention that every estimate goes out with a point figure, a range, and one line from the analyst on how much they trust their own process, and that last bit has turned into a mess. What keeps happening — two or three times a month at this point — is that someone brings me a number and in the same breath tells me they don't trust the process that produced it: they were rushed, they'd half-decided the answer before they looked at the data, someone they rate came out somewhere quite different, or they've just read something about how people like them systematically overrate their own models. They can see the problem perfectly clearly. They just don't know what to do about it and neither do I. One of them now says she thinks the number is right and at the same time thinks she shouldn't believe it, which sounds like a contradiction until you've sat in the meeting; someone told me the word for it is epistemic akrasia and then changed the subject.

I've been reading round higher-order evidence — what you're supposed to do when the evidence about your evidence points somewhere other than the evidence does — and I want it turned into something I can actually run in a twenty-minute conversation with an analyst, not a seminar. So: give me a decision procedure. The questions I ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "keep the number", where it stops with "bin it and start again", and where it hands the decision back to me because the question is what we're willing to risk rather than what the evidence says. There's one distinction I think is the crux and most of what I've read seems to walk straight past it: evidence that the process was unreliable in general versus evidence that it was unreliable in a particular direction — those seem to license quite different things, and I keep seeing them treated as one. Also handle the case where the analyst can name the bias but can't undo it; knowing you're anchoring doesn't un-anchor you, and "just correct for it" is what I keep saying and I don't think it's honest.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your working: (a) an analyst who says her estimate is contaminated because she saw a colleague's figure before she started; (b) an analyst who's confident and has no complaints about his own process, and whose process I happen to know is badly calibrated; (c) an analyst who's the only person in the building who understands the domain, so there's no disagreement to appeal to and the "check with a peer" step has nothing to bite on. I want at least one of those to come out in a way I wont like. Finish with a short section on what the procedure can't settle, where I'm just making a management call dressed up as epistemology, and what to do on the days when my worry about her process is itself the sort of thing I should be suspicious of. Don't give me a reading list, I'll find the literature myself if I want it — I need something I can use on Tuesday, and I'm not a philosopher, just the person who has to sign the thing off.
