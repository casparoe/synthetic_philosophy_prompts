I'm a graduate student putting together a seminar presentation on speech-act theory and statecraft, and I have settled on the Syrian chemical-weapons episode of 2012–13 as my case. The bare facts, which I am stipulating rather than asking you to establish: in August 2012, answering a question at a press conference, the President said that the movement or use of chemical weapons would cross a red line for the United States and would change his calculus; a little over a year later, after the sarin attack on the Damascus suburbs of 21 August 2013, his administration announced that it would seek congressional authorization for strikes, the vote was put off, and the episode ended not with American force but with a Russian-brokered arrangement under which Syria surrendered its declared chemical stockpile; afterwards, the President insisted that the red line had been the world's rather than his own, since the prohibition on chemical weapons is a treaty commitment of nearly every state. I am not interested in re-litigating the intelligence or the morality of intervening, and I don't want a reading list. I want the case analyzed through the theory, and the theory tested against the case.

First, what illocutionary act was that 2012 utterance? Take the candidates seriously — threat, warning, promise, prediction, declaration of policy — and adjudicate them by the standard criteria: illocutionary point, direction of fit between word and world, the sincerity condition, and what the speaker must intend and have the standing to do. Then ask who the hearers were, because the utterance was broadcast: Assad and his commanders, Congress, allies, the American public, the press, and the world at large all had different uptakes, and several of them plainly took it as a threat while the speaker later spoke as though no commitment had been made. I have been calling acts of this shape audience-fixed illocutions, meaning that the force is settled by how the salient audience takes the utterance up rather than by the speaker's intention, which in this case was underdetermined to say the least. Tell me whether that coinage earns its keep, or whether it is Austin's requirement of uptake wearing a new label — and if it is the latter, say what the old label already does that mine does not.

Then the harder question, and the one my seminar is actually about: when the line was crossed and nothing followed, which felicity conditions failed, and how should the failure be classified? Was the original a misfire, so that no act was performed at all, or an abuse — an act genuinely performed but defective in sincerity or follow-through — and does Austin's machinery have room for a case in which an act was performed, was treated by nearly everyone as binding, and was then simply not honoured, with no one able to say precisely when it lapsed? Bring in the perlocutionary side as well: deterrence is a perlocutionary aim and it failed, while the illocutionary act presumably succeeded, so is credibility a felicity condition or purely an audience-side, perlocutionary matter, and does anything in the theory explain why a threat that is not carried out is nevertheless still a threat? Finally, tell me where the theory runs out. Austin and Searle model a single speaker with intentions and a determinate hearer, and a state's utterance fits neither: the speaker is an office-holder whose words commit a collective that did not author them, the hearer is a dispersed public, and the later insistence that the world had drawn the line looks like a re-attribution of authorship, a claim that the President was never the author of the act at all. Say which of these the theory can accommodate, which it cannot, whether some rival account of linguistic commitment handles the case better, and end with the one feature of the episode that speech-act theory, on your view, simply cannot explain.
