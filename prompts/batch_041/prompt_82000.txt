I am the clerk to the seven-member board of a mid-sized credit union: I prepare the voting sheets, put the questions, and draft the minutes that our regulator's examiners read. I do not vote, I am not trained in logic or in social choice, and I have no way to look anything up from the meeting room, so nothing in what follows may depend on a source I would have to consult mid-meeting. The board meets monthly, takes four to twelve motions a meeting, and its decisions bind the institution immediately; we cannot reconvene inside a month, and in practice we never do. The chair signs the minutes, our general counsel reviews them after the fact, and the standing orders can be amended only by a two-thirds vote at a general meeting. Anything you give me must therefore work as internal operating practice; where a step genuinely cannot work without amending the standing orders, say which step and why.

Our standing orders require each motion to be voted on separately, by simple majority of the members present. That works only if the motions are independent, and ours are not. In the last year we twice adopted sets of motions that cannot all be true together, and once adopted a package that, when I counted individual members rather than motions, only two of the seven had endorsed. Nothing in our rules tells me what to do about this, and the failures are invisible in the minutes as I currently write them.

What I need is a decision procedure I can run in the room in about twenty minutes, without amending the standing orders, that produces a defensible outcome and a record that does not misrepresent what happened. It has to cope with the following, all of which are features of our real agenda rather than hypotheticals:

- Our motions are of three kinds: things the board decides (draw down the reserve fund), things the board predicts (the roof work will finish before the rainy season), and things the board evaluates (the quoted price is reasonable). I suspect these should not be aggregated the same way, but I cannot say what the difference amounts to in practice.
- Members disagree about which motions are the premises of a package and which is the conclusion. On the roof package one faction says the board is voting on two spending decisions and a contract; another says the board is voting on one decision and two forecasts. The standing orders are silent.
- Some motions are logically linked: one is the conjunction of two, one is implied by two others, one is equivalent to another and is on the agenda only because a member wanted it recorded separately. I do not want the board to vote twice on the same question, and I also do not want to suppress a member's motion.
- Voting entitlements differ across motions. One member declares a conflict of interest on the branch lease, because a spouse owns the property, and must abstain on that motion while voting on the rest, so one majority is computed over six members and another over seven. The procedure has to say whose votes count for which proposition.
- A member sometimes abstains on a conclusion because he regards the package as incoherent rather than because he is indifferent, and I do not know whether that abstention belongs in the denominator.
- Once members learn which rule I am applying, some of them will vote to control the outcome rather than to state what they believe. I need to know which of your steps this wrecks, and what if anything I can do about it.
- I set the order in which linked motions are put, and the order changes the outcome. The procedure has to fix how the order is set and record it.

Deliver a procedure, not a discussion of one. It should be a sequence I can run in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it stops and hands the matter to the chair or to the board because the question is one of judgment rather than of procedure. Mark every point at which judgment cannot be eliminated, say who exercises it, and say what they must record when they do.

Then run it on these three cases, using the votes exactly as I give them.

Case 1, the roof package. Proposition P, that the board should draw down the reserve fund to pay for the roof; proposition Q, that the work can be completed before the rainy season at the quoted price; proposition R, that the board should approve the roof contract now, which our practice treats as approvable only if both P and Q are approved. All seven members vote on all three. Members 1, 2 and 3 vote yes on P, Q and R; members 4 and 5 vote yes on P, no on Q, no on R; members 6 and 7 vote no on P, yes on Q, no on R.

Case 2, the branch and the tellers. Motion M1, to open a second branch; motion M2, to hire two additional member-services staff. Member 3 abstains on M1 for the declared conflict of interest and votes on M2. On M1, members 1, 2, 4 and 5 vote yes and members 6 and 7 vote no. On M2, members 4, 5, 6 and 7 vote yes and members 1, 2 and 3 vote no.

Case 3, the software package. Proposition U, that the board will adopt the new member-services software; proposition V, that if the board adopts the software it will have to retrain all staff in the first quarter; proposition W, that the board will have to retrain all staff in the first quarter. We vote on U, V and W separately, and every member's three votes are consistent with one another. Members 1, 2 and 3 vote yes on U, yes on V, yes on W; members 4 and 5 vote yes on U, no on V, no on W; members 6 and 7 vote no on U, yes on V, no on W.

For at least one of these the procedure will return an outcome the chair will not like, and in Case 3 it may commit the board to spending that a majority of members voted against. Do not tune the procedure to avoid that. Run it and report what it gives. If the honest answer is that the procedure cannot settle a case and the chair must own the decision, say so, and say what he must record.
