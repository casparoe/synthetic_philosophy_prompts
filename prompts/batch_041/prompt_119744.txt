I've been asked to give the commencement address at the university where I teach: about two thousand graduates, their families in folding chairs on the lawn, a provost who has already suggested twice that I say something about "this remarkable generation," and a student paper that will print the whole thing. I'm a demographer, and I've spent my career on what is admittedly the boring end of this subject — cohort measurement, the fact that when you compare people of different ages at a single moment you cannot separate what the birth year did from what the current year is doing from how old everyone happens to be, and the fact that a cohort label is a line someone drew for some purpose and redraws whenever the purpose changes. So I am in an awkward spot: I'm meant to bless an audience as a generation, and I don't believe the thing I'm being asked to bless exists in the way the request implies. Write me the address.

Some things it has to do. It must not, anywhere, tell the graduates what they are like — no "you are the generation that..." — and it still has to be about them, warmly, in a way that leaves them something. It should give the honest half of the generational idea its due: that what happens to you in your late teens and early twenties can leave a lasting mark, that people born at different times really do inherit different worlds, and that some of what the labels gesture at is real and worth naming. Then it should say, in language the grandparents in the back can follow, why none of that adds up to a kind: the confound, which is that a difference between cohorts measured at one time is three differences wearing one coat — birth year, age, and the year itself — and a test the audience can carry off the lawn with them. Give them the test in three plain questions: does knowing someone's cohort tell me anything about them that their age and today's date do not already tell me; is there a shared cause actually binding the members together, or is the resemblance a coincidence of the calendar; and does the boundary survive being nudged a few years in either direction? Then apply the test out loud, briefly and without cruelty, to the one label I will have to decline to use. Finally, the part I care most about: the label does work on the people it names, so that some of the very difference we later point to as evidence for the label was manufactured by the label itself. Say that plainly, and say what follows from it for someone who is about to be handed a name for themselves whether they asked for it or not.

Keep it to roughly ten minutes spoken, keep the census statistics out of it, and don't scold the audience for using the words — my mother uses them, and she will be in the third row. The ending must not be "go and prove them wrong about your generation"; that would give away the whole game.
