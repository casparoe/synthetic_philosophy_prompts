I need a thought experiment built to order, and I want to be able to check afterwards that it actually does what it's meant to rather than just sounding clever.

Two views. View A: the aesthetic value of an artwork supervenes entirely on the properties of the object itself, i.e. what it looks like and what it's like to experience it. View B: the value depends on the history of production, so how the thing came to exist is part of what makes it the work it is. Give me a case where A says the two objects are equal in value and B says the original is greater.

Constraints. Realistic setting, art world rather than sci-fi, no exotic technology, and nobody is deceived anywhere in the story — nothing is passed off as something it isn't, no fraud, no dupes — so deception can't be what's doing the work. Also stipulate that the two objects are perceptually indistinguishable: no test, no expert, no instrument can tell them apart. And the person who made the copy has to be a serious painter in thier own right who has done something harder than the original artist did — harder technique, harder inference, more years at it — so that B can't be rescued by saying the harder thing is better. Say the copiest never saw the original in the flesh, only photographs and technical records, and still produced something indistinguishable from it.

What it's for: I'm arguing that the value of an original can't be reduced either to its look or to the skill it took, and I want a case that forces B to say exactly which bit of the history is doing the work. So the verdicts have to fall out of the theories themselves, stated in their strongest form — if you have to rig the story so that B obviously wins, the case has failed, and say so.

Format: five numbered points, no introduction and no conclusion, a sentence or two each (point one can run to three or four if the scenario needs the room). Point one, the scenario. Point two, what A says and why. Point three, what B says and why. Point four, which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed. Point five, how someone who thinks B is just art-world sentimentality and snobbery would attack the case.

British English, and no reading lists, citations or further reading — I don't want to have to go and look anything up.
