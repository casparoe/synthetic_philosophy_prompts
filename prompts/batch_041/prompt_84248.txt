I'm teaching the Galileo unit in an upper-level science-and-religion seminar for the first time — sixteen students, mostly juniors and seniors in philosophy and history, one seventy-five-minute session — and I want to build the whole session around an activity rather than a lecture, because my students can already recite the standard line about Galileo and scripture without having any feel for why it convinced anyone or why anyone resisted it. The line runs roughly like this: scripture and nature both come from God and so cannot contradict each other; scripture was written for the salvation of everyone, including the uneducated, and therefore speaks in the language of ordinary experience and accommodates itself to its first audience, the way it attributes hands to God or lists the moon among the great lights of the sky; so when a genuine demonstration about the natural world conflicts with a literal reading, the fault lies in our interpretation rather than in the text or in nature. I find this compelling, but a theologian I know says it is a machine for reinterpreting anything you dislike, and that once you have it you can never lose an argument against a text — you only ever lose the argument about what counts as a demonstration. I want students to run into both halves of that themselves, in the order in which they actually bite, and I want no lecture up front. Please design the activity, with rules, roles, materials, a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophical moves, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. One constraint on the materials: build the game so that nothing in it depends on a verbatim quotation — write passage summaries in your own words and, if you want any real text in the room, say only that I should take it from a checked edition rather than supplying it.

The activity I have in mind, which you should feel free to redesign if you can make the trap tighter, runs in three phases. Phase one, about ten minutes, no framing: each student gets a slip with one sentence about the world written the way an ordinary person would say it — the sun rose at 6:14 this morning, the table is solid, the wind off the water is cold, the sky is blue at noon. In pairs they decide which of the sentences are false. They will mostly say none are; when I press them to apply a strict standard they will say all of them are, more or less, and that gets the accommodation point out of them without my having to state it: ordinary language is not defective physics, it is language pitched to use. Phase two, the bulk of the session, is a staged council. Assign roles: two Advocates, one for each of two petitions; a Guardian of the plain sense, who must argue from the text's availability to people who will never read a commentary; a Printer, who must argue from what a cheap pamphlet will make of our decision twenty years from now; an Assessor, who must certify in a single sentence whether the dossier before the council contains a demonstration or only a probable opinion; a standing Dissenter, whose job is to argue for the reading the council is about to abandon; and the rest as voters. After each petition the Assessor's one sentence goes up on the wall as the criterion the council used, and that sentence binds every later decision — I read it aloud before the third vote, because the third petition is designed to be covered by it. The three petitions each cite a passage that seems to say something about the natural world and ask for a licensed non-literal reading: first, that the moon is not a light of its own but shines by borrowed light, with the demonstration available in the room using a lamp, a ball and a dark corner; second, a claim about the tides, presented as the strongest physical proof that the earth moves, with a dossier that is genuinely impressive — correlations, a mechanism, an explanation of why the effect varies with season and latitude — which the council cannot actually assess and which was in fact wrong, the real cause being the differential pull of moon and sun; third, a crank petition with the same logical shape as the first, submitted by someone who has read the posted criterion and demands to be covered by it, arguing that the moon is a mirror and that by the council's own reasoning every apparent source of light is a reflection of a source further out, so the scriptural language of lights must be read as a figure for reflection all the way down. Before each vote, everyone privately writes on a slip whether they would stake their own name on the dossier's being a demonstration; I collect the slips unread and reveal only the count at the end of the debrief.

The debrief should work by mapping moments rather than by summarizing the history. The slip exercise maps onto accommodation and onto why it is a claim about the purpose of the text rather than a dodge. The posted criterion maps onto the fact that the license to reinterpret is conditional, and that the condition — demonstration, not probability — is doing all the work. The second petition maps onto the objection Bellarmine pressed against Galileo's ally Foscarini, that a reinterpretation is licensed only by a true demonstration and never by a probable opinion, and the wager slips make the cost of that visible: a room that voted to license will typically contain a majority who privately would not stake their name on the dossier, which is exactly the asymmetry Bellarmine was pointing at, since the church pays for a wrong reinterpretation in authority and the philosopher does not. The third petition maps onto the arbitrariness worry: either the council narrows its criterion after the fact, which looks ad hoc, or it accepts the mirror regress, and either way the students discover that a rule generous enough to do the work they wanted in petition one is generous enough to cover the crank. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: my guess is that they will smell the trap in petition one and treat it as a puzzle instead of a case, that the Guardian and the Printer will be played as villains and the Advocates as heroes, flattening a real dispute into a morality play, that someone will know the tides story and spoil petition two unless the dossier is kept anonymous and presented as a period document, that early votes will go unanimous and nobody will want to be the one to reverse, so the Dissenter needs to speak before each vote and I should take a straw poll before discussion rather than after, and that the debrief will overrun, so I should cut discussion of the third petition rather than cut the debrief. Then be candid about the limits. The activity cannot reproduce the actual epistemic position of 1615, because I have calibrated the dossiers and the students know there is an answer; it can only gesture at the institutional stakes of patronage, the Index and the Inquisition; and it cannot show that the plain-sense reading was itself a historically contingent interpretive practice rather than the obvious default, nor that the letter to the Grand Duchess Christina is a piece of advocacy written under pressure rather than a neutral statement of method. I will have to supply the astronomy, the institutional history, and the reminder that the demonstrative status of Copernicanism was genuinely unsettled in the period, and I will have to keep the debrief from collapsing into the comfortable story that the church was simply anti-science — which is the reading this game will most naturally produce in a modern classroom, and therefore the one I most need it to complicate.
