Our town council is considering whether to fund a new playhouse, and one of the councillors has been enlisting Rousseau on the side of the proposal. Her argument is that Rousseau's famous attack on the theatre was really an attack on the sorts of plays staged in his Geneva — frivolous, gallant, morally lax — and that he would have had no quarrel with a house devoted to serious, edifying drama. On her reading, his position amounts to a plea for better content, so a publicly funded theatre that stages morally improving work is squarely in his spirit.

I have been asked to help draft the case, and I want to be sure we are not building it on a misreading. Taking her reconstruction as broadly sound, where would Rousseau have drawn the line? Which sorts of drama would he have approved and which suppressed, and how would he have judged, say, a tragedy built around an unmistakably admirable protagonist? I am also curious what, if anything, he would have put in the theatre's place; if his objection really were to content, one would expect him to have proposed better entertainments rather than none at all. If the question turns out to be misframed, I would far rather discover that now than after the proposal goes to a vote. Please answer in American English.
