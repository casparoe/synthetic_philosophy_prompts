My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about cancel culture, and we ended exactly where we started: he says it cannot be punishment because no state is doing it, and I say that if it imposes hard treatment and communicates condemnation, the absence of a judge is a detail, not a refutation. I want to settle it by working through a real case rather than trading intuitions. Take the 2014 resignation of Brendan Eich from Mozilla, after his donation to California's Proposition 8 became public—not the merits of his donation, but the structure of what was done to him. Analyze that episode through the philosophy of punishment, and in particular through a test I have been using in my own teaching: punishment requires (1) an authoritative rule that has been violated, (2) a determinate sanction imposed for that violation, and (3) a public condemning function, such that the sanction communicates censure rather than merely imposes a cost. Map the episode onto each element: who, if anyone, is the punishing authority; what rule was violated; what exactly is the sanction; what is being communicated, and to whom. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Does proportionality have any grip when the sanction is decentralized and open-ended? Is the resignation better understood as punishment, or as something adjacent—censure, excommunication, outlawry, or a private exercise of market and reputational power that has punishment-like effects? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this case show about the concept of punishment itself? Does it reveal that the concept is too state-centered, or that we need to distinguish punishment from social sanction more sharply than we usually do? Be disciplined about the mapping, and where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a list of sources; I want the case worked through.
