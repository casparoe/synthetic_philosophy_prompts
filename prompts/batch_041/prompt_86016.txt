I am presenting a paper next month to a workshop that is mostly early modern historians and historians of the book, with two historians of technology whose own work is on Cold War weapons procurement, and one economist who studies long-run growth. None of them works on AI; several are openly skeptical of arguments from the lessons of history, and the paper is my attempt to make one anyway.

My thesis has two parts. First, the nuclear-weapons analogy that dominates discussion of transformative AI is mostly decorative: it imports the imagery of the bomb and the state-monopoly assumptions of the nonproliferation regime without earning either. Second, the printing press is the load-bearing analogy, and the reason is not that both technologies spread ideas but that both collapse the cost of producing and distributing persuasive text, so that the binding constraint shifts from the artifact to the capability. I call the recurring failure mode the artifact trap: every sixteenth-century control regime I examine, from licensing to the Index of Prohibited Books to guild privileges, targeted the physical thing — the book, the press, the stock — rather than the dispersed capability that made the thing cheap, and the thing was always the replaceable part. My contemporary punchline is that proposals to govern models, weights, or chips are artifact governance in the same sense, and that the analogy points instead toward governing whatever is scarce and complementary.

I already know where I am exposed. The argument needs the claim that print's effect on belief ran mostly through volume and standardization rather than through any particular content, and I suspect at least two people in that room have counterexamples ready. I also expect the economist to say the comparison has no counterfactual.
