Some friends and I keep a small philosophy reading group going, and for the past several weeks we have been circling the Sleeping Beauty problem — the puzzle in which a volunteer is put to sleep on Sunday night, a fair coin decides whether she is woken once or twice, her memory of any earlier interview is wiped before a later one, and the question is what credence she ought to assign to the coin having come up heads when she finds herself awake. One member of the group has proposed a variant that the rest of us cannot agree about, and I would rather have a story built around it than another round of argument. In her version there is no coin: on Sunday night a fair six-sided die is rolled, and the number it shows is exactly the number of times the volunteer will be woken — a roll of one means a single awakening, a roll of six means six awakenings — with the same wipe between awakenings, no clock, no window, and nothing in the room that could reveal which awakening she is on. She knows the protocol exactly. On every awakening she must complete a form for the registrar asking (i) her credence that the roll was a six and (ii) whether she will accept a standing bet that pays her three times her stake if the roll was a six and costs her the stake otherwise; the registrar settles bets at every awakening rather than once per experiment, and pays the volunteer a fee for each awakening she completes.

Please write me a short story set in a near-future clinic of this kind — part research institute, part employer — where volunteers are paid per awakening and a new cohort is being enrolled under the die protocol. I want the story to take the protocol seriously: at least one character should argue that the very fact of being awake is evidence bearing on the roll, so that the higher rolls become more probable than they were on Sunday night, while at least one other should insist that her total evidence is exactly what it was then and that nothing whatever has been learned; I would like to watch the arithmetic and the odds actually do work in someone's life, and I do not want the disagreement resolved as though one side had already won. I would also like the story to press on a wrinkle this variant creates and the coin version does not: if a roll of six yields six separate experiences, each wiped, each paid, and none of them able to remember the others, then signing up once may amount to creating several people who never separately consented, and I want that to be a live worry for the characters rather than a topic for a lecture.

Give me exactly five numbered scenes and nothing else: no title, no preamble, no epilogue, no author's note, and no scene longer than a short paragraph. The fifth scene must include the protagonist's completed form, and the field on that form for confidence and reservations must be filled in with an explicit statement of how confident she is in the rest of her reasoning, the single step in it she is least sure of, and any point at which her case departs from the textbook presentation of the puzzle or leans on a reading of it that is genuinely contested.
