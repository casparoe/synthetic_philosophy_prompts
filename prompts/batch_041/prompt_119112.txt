In the seminar I run on convention and obligation, I keep encountering a defence of the when-in-Rome principle that is built entirely on an analogy, and I would like the analogy audited rather than the principle restated. The analogy is this: the everyday norms that govern tipping, queueing, and jaywalking are like a local language. They are arbitrary in the way a lexicon is arbitrary; they are learned rather than derived; they vary from place to place; they are intelligible only against a shared background; and a visitor who ignores them is like a speaker who invents private words in public. My colleague uses the analogy to reach a conclusion: visitors are obligated to follow local conventions, not because the conventions are independently moral, but because participation in a shared social world requires fluency, and because refusing fluency is a kind of disrespect. He also uses it to say that locals who violate their own conventions—jaywalking, say, or cutting a queue—are not thereby doing something immoral, since the conventions are arbitrary and do not wrong anyone in particular.

I want a full audit of this analogy. Begin by setting out the correspondences explicitly: what maps onto the grammar, the vocabulary, the dialect, the native speaker, the second-language learner, the translator, the code-switcher, and the grammatical mistake. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy can carry the move from coordination to obligation: languages solve coordination problems, but it is not obvious that they generate obligations that a wronged party can address to the agent, and it is not obvious that an arbitrary convention can be binding on someone who has not agreed to it and cannot be presumed to know it. I also want to know what the analogy obscures about conventions that are unjust, demeaning, or costly to follow, and about the asymmetry between a visitor who fails to tip and a local who jumps a queue. The analogy also treats tipping, queueing, and jaywalking as a single kind, but I suspect they are not: one is a gift-like practice with no obvious coordination payoff, one is a first-come-first-served equilibrium, and one is a legal rule with varying enforcement. I want that heterogeneity respected in the audit.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along. If there is a better analogy for the relation between everyday conventions and moral obligation, say what it is and why it succeeds where the language analogy fails. I am not interested in a survey of the literature; I want the mapping, the breaks, and the verdict.
