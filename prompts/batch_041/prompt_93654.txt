Six weeks ago I took my mother to an emergency department and watched her account of her own pain get quietly rewritten in front of her, by people who were kind and who, as far as I could tell, were not misrepresenting anything they believed. Almost nothing they said contradicted her. That is the part I cannot put down.

I would like you to write a poem about that mechanism. Take the role of a poet with a working knowledge of the epistemology of testimony — someone who knows that the phenomenon in question is not rudeness or disagreement but a credibility deficit that tracks who is speaking, and that it can be administered by a hearer who means well. Before drafting, think through in a few sentences what such a poem would have to get right, then write it.

The constraints matter more than the wording. Make the poem antiphonal: two alternating voices, the patient's and the clinician's, clearly distinguished on the page. The clinician must never contradict her and must never sound cold; each of its lines should be defensible on its own, and it should get through the whole poem by recombining a small stock of phrases rather than inventing anything new. As the poem proceeds, let the patient's lines shorten and hedge, and let her hedges be visibly borrowed from the clinician's earlier lines, while the clinician's stay full and fluent — so that by the end she is qualifying her own testimony and the clinician is quoting her back to herself. No one in the poem should notice this or name it; no villain, no diagnosis, no epiphany. Around thirty-five lines, free verse, and concrete: a real room, a real symptom. It should land for a reader who has never heard the term testimonial injustice, and a philosopher who works on it should find nothing to wince at.
