I work as a software engineer on capacity planning and reliability for a mid-sized backend platform, and two people on my team have been arguing for two years about what a probability is. The first is a long-run frequentist: on his view a probability is a property of a repeatable setup, fixed by the limiting relative frequency of outcomes across a collection of similar trials, and talk of the probability of a one-off event is either shorthand for a class of events or empty. The second is a personalist: on her view a probability is a coherent degree of belief and nothing besides, frequencies count as evidence only by way of assumptions about the sequence — she says the trials have to be exchangeable, which I had to look up — and she denies that there is a further objective quantity our beliefs are supposed to match.

Please write the joint statement these two could sign after a genuine, good-faith collaboration. I am not asking who is right. What I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what probability talk is doing in engineering practice, about what the two of them jointly deny (for instance, whether a single number can be reported as the probability of a one-off event without a stated model), about the reference-class problem, about what calibration does and does not establish, and about the cases where their disagreement makes no practical difference.
2. A table of the disagreements that remain. Each row: the point at issue, then the formulation each side would accept as its own view, in its own voice.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For each, a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about it, written down in advance, including what each would count as evidence against their own position.

To keep it concrete, make them work through three cases: (a) a queue-sizing decision where we say the arrival rate is somewhere between zero and a thousand requests per second and assume it is uniform — ask whether that assumption is a claim about the world, a modeling choice, or a statement of ignorance, and whether anything changes if we describe the same ignorance over mean inter-arrival time instead of rate; (b) the chance that a specific database migration, to be attempted exactly once, succeeds — what number, if any, belongs in the risk register, and under what description it is a probability at all; and (c) a device whose failure rate depends on how long it has already survived, where I want to know whether there is a fact about its current chance of failing that is not a fact about anyone's information.

If any part of their dispute turns out to be about words rather than about the world or about norms, say which part and why; and if the two of them would make identical engineering decisions in all three cases, say so, and say whether the disagreement is doing any work.
