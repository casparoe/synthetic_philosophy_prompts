I teach a graduate seminar in aesthetics and am currently grading the final examination. The examination consisted of a single essay question. I reproduce the question and one student's answer below.

Question:

The literature on aesthetic testimony is often organized around a dispute between pessimists, who hold that one cannot come to know an aesthetic fact — for example, that a work is beautiful — on the strength of another person's testimony, and optimists, who hold that one can. Consider the following principle, which I shall call the Non-Deference Constraint:

(NDC) If a subject S believes that a work W is beautiful solely on the basis of T's testimony that W is beautiful, and S has no first-hand acquaintance with W, then S's belief is not a genuine aesthetic judgment, even if it happens to be true.

Suppose NDC were true. Would it support pessimism about aesthetic testimony? In answering, keep the following three claims apart, and say of each whether NDC entails it:

(a) Testimony cannot justify an aesthetic belief.
(b) Testimony cannot produce a genuine aesthetic judgment.
(c) Testimony cannot transmit aesthetic knowledge.

Then state whether pessimism follows, and why or why not.

Student's answer:

Pessimism and optimism about aesthetic testimony are disagreeing about whether you can come to know that a work is beautiful from what someone else tells you. The pessimist says no: aesthetic knowledge has to be earned through first-hand experience, so a belief formed on testimony alone falls short of it. The optimist says yes: testimony is an ordinary source of knowledge, and there is nothing about aesthetics that specially blocks it.

NDC captures the pessimist's central thought. It says that a belief held on testimony alone, with no acquaintance with the work, is not a genuine aesthetic judgment. That is exactly claim (b), so NDC plainly entails (b).

But NDC also gives us (a). If a belief is not a genuine aesthetic judgment, then it cannot be a justified aesthetic belief, since justification requires that the belief be a judgment of the relevant kind. So NDC entails that testimony cannot justify an aesthetic belief.

And once we have (a), we get (c) for free. Knowledge requires justified true belief, and testimony cannot supply the justification, so it cannot transmit aesthetic knowledge either.

So NDC supports pessimism, and quite decisively: it shows that a belief taken on aesthetic testimony alone is not merely unearned but unjustified, and therefore cannot amount to knowledge. Claims (a), (b), and (c) are really three ways of putting the same point, and NDC underwrites all three of them. The optimist has no room to maneuver here.

Please assign this answer a grade (A through F, with a plus or minus where you think one is warranted), and then give me the specific comments you would write to the student. I want the comments keyed to the question, part by part: for each of (a), (b), and (c), say whether the student's treatment is correct, and assess the validity of the inferences by which the student reaches their conclusions, not merely whether the conclusions sound plausible. Justify the grade through those comments rather than through a general impression, and close with a sentence or two explaining the overall mark.
