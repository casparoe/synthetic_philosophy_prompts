I am drafting a paper with a philosopher of mathematics on what computational complexity does to the epistemology of mathematics, and before we settle on a general thesis I want to check her against her own track record. Call her my co-author. For several months now she has been giving me flat verdicts on whether particular results count as things we can know, where "we" means the mathematical community roughly as it now stands, and "know" carries both factivity and a justification that community could in principle possess. Her verdicts so far:

1. A theorem with a long but entirely human-checkable proof that only a few specialists have actually read end to end: we know it.
2. A theorem whose only accepted proof came from an exhaustive machine search over an astronomical number of configurations, where the search emits a short certificate that a trivial program verifies in a fraction of a second: we know it. She is unmoved by the fact that no human can survey the search itself, because the certificate is checkable.
3. A true sentence of arithmetic for which a proof is known to exist, but whose shortest proof runs to roughly 10^500 symbols, cannot be compressed into any certificate short enough to check, and has no structure a checker could exploit: she says we do not know it, since a proof no one could ever inspect is not knowledge we have.
4. A statement whose truth follows from a short non-constructive existence argument — we can prove the object exists, though the argument cannot exhibit it and gives no way to find it: we know it.
5. A conjecture checked instance by instance for every case anyone could ever compute, with no proof in prospect: she says we do not know it, however overwhelming the evidence.
6. A statement provably independent of our usual axioms: she says we know that it is independent, but not whether it is true.

Now the case I need you to predict. A colleague has proved that a certain true statement has a proof of quite modest length — a few thousand symbols, verifiable in seconds — while also proving that any program that finds a proof must run for at least doubly exponential time in the statement's length, with no foreseeable method doing better. So a short, cheaply checkable certificate demonstrably exists, and nothing in our world could ever locate it.

Predict her verdict. Then do the part I actually care about: state the rule she seems to be running; name at least one other rule that fits every verdict above just as well; say what each predicts about the new case; give your best guess about which rule she is running, with your reasons; and name the further case, ideally one we could actually build, that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or result, if it held, would push you toward the other prediction.
