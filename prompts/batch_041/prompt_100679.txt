Modern cryptography is built on a distinction between two kinds of security. Information-theoretic security is unconditional: no adversary, however computationally powerful, can break the scheme. Computational security is conditional: a scheme counts as secure if every probabilistic polynomial-time adversary succeeds with negligible probability, which means the scheme is breakable in principle by an unbounded search but not by any adversary we expect to build. In practice, banks, messaging services, and military systems treat both kinds as secure, and often prefer the computational kind because it is faster and more flexible; a one-time pad is information-theoretically secure but is rarely used for general communication. The practice therefore treats a scheme that is breakable in principle as secure enough, while treating a scheme that is unbreakable in principle as impractical. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does it require a particular view of possibility—for example, that what is possible for us is not the same as what is metaphysically possible, or that computational infeasibility is a genuine kind of impossibility? Does it presuppose realism about complexity classes, so that the P versus NP question and the existence of one-way functions are objective facts about the world, or can the practice be licensed by a purely instrumentalist or constructivist reading? Does it assume that efficient computation is a natural kind, or only a shifting sociological category? What view of knowledge and evidence is at work in definitions that quantify over all polynomial-time adversaries, including adversaries no one has imagined and machines no one has built? And is the practice internally consistent: it treats information-theoretic security as the gold standard yet does not pay the practical price for it, and it treats computational security as sufficient even though its central assumptions are unproved.

Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of modality, feasibility, and mathematical objectivity would license the same institutional behavior. Finally, suppose we replaced the asymptotic idealization with a concrete-resource view: security would be defined relative to a specified budget of time, memory, and parallelism, and a scheme would be secure only against adversaries within that budget. What would have to change in cryptographic practice, and what philosophical commitments would the replacement force us to give up or acquire?
