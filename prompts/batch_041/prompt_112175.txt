I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small eighteenth-century city that is deciding whether to license a public theater, and for some months now I have been living inside Rousseau's letter against d'Alembert — the long reply to the article on Geneva, in which he argues that a theater would corrupt the city that admitted it and that the actor's profession is degrading in itself. What I keep circling is not the conclusion but the picture that carries it: the actor as a counterfeiter, a man whose entire trained skill consists in passing off a self that is not his own, and who is paid for the passing. This is the old antitheatrical commonplace that Rousseau sharpens rather than invents, and he makes it do an enormous amount of work — it underwrites his claim that the actor is degraded by his own art, that the stage cannot be a school of morals however moral the play (his Alceste, the just man of Molière's Misanthrope, is the standing example of virtue made ridiculous), and that a well-ordered small republic does well to refuse the whole apparatus. I write novels. I spend my days inventing people who never existed and arranging for strangers to grieve over them. If the counterfeit picture holds, I am in the actor's position, minus the stage and minus an audience that knows it is watching a fiction.

What I want from you is an audit of that analogy, not a summary of the letter. Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine if it is the cleanest form — saying what in the picture maps onto the actor, onto the audience, onto the playwright, onto the money, and onto the shared pretense that both parties have entered into. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which features of forgery does the picture quietly import that acting does not possess — a deceived party, an injury, an intent to defraud, the anonymity of the false coin, the fact that a counterfeit becomes worthless the moment it is detected — and which features of acting can find no expression in it at all, such as the body as the medium, the night-after-night repetition, the audience's willing participation in its own deception, or the distinction between playing a role and telling a lie? Then bring the novelist into the frame. If the actor stands condemned by this picture, what does the picture say about me, and does the verdict change when the fiction is consumed privately, in silence, over many hours, by a reader who knows perfectly well what I am doing and has paid for the privilege? I am especially interested in the disanalogy between the two audiences — the theater audience that Rousseau says is moved whether or not it wishes to be moved, and the reader who can shut the book — and in whether that difference does any real moral work or is merely a difference of degree.

Finally, the part I care about most. Rousseau's argument, as I read him, is deliberately local: he is not asking Paris to close its theaters, only asking whether one particular small city, with its particular civic life, should acquire one, and he says as much himself. Tell me what the counterfeit picture still supports once that localism is taken seriously, and whether his conclusion survives the audit or whether the picture was carrying it all along. If the picture is the wrong instrument, tell me what picture would do the work better. And since I am a novelist and not a philosopher, tell me plainly which of my own sympathies the letter is likely to be flattering — because I have come to suspect that I have been enjoying his attack on the stage rather too much for a man whose trade is making things up.
