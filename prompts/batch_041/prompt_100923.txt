I have just come back from a long and rather bruising argument with my friend, a committed causal decision theorist, about a paper I am presenting next month at a small workshop on decision theory and formal epistemology. The paper defends a view I call distributional acausal trade. The core move is to replace the usual picture of acausal trade — two causally isolated agents with fixed utility functions and priors, each trying to predict the other’s decision procedure — with a picture in which the relata are probability distributions over agent-types. Each agent controls, through its choice of policy, the conditional distribution of its own type across worlds in which the other distribution is realized. Trade is then not an exchange of goods between agents but a mutual shift in the measure each distribution assigns to cooperative versus defecting types. I claim this makes acausal trade rational precisely when the agents are logically updateless: their policy is evaluated from a perspective that does not privilege the indexical location of the actual decision. The workshop audience will be mostly causal decision theorists and game theorists, with two people who work on logical induction and logical uncertainty, one philosopher of probability who is deeply suspicious of measures over merely possible agents, and one economist who works on mechanism design and will want to know whether the whole scheme is incentive-compatible. My friend’s parting shot was that the view is either evidential decision theory with extra steps or an unmotivated metaphysical postulate, and I would like to know which of those charges this room is most likely to press and how.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I use a central example: two agents identical up to a random seed, each choosing between a cooperative policy that pays off only if the other distribution assigns enough measure to cooperation and a defecting policy that is dominant under ordinary causal reasoning. I claim the cooperative policy is rationally permissible, perhaps required, under a measure over agent-types; my friend says I am smuggling in a correlation the causal graph does not contain. Give me the eight or so objections I am actually going to get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it — the game theorist’s objection should sound like a game theorist’s, the measure theorist’s like a measure theorist’s — with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility without giving away the thesis, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations or reading lists, please; I know the literature well enough to be dangerous.
