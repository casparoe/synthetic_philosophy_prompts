I promised to bring a handout next week and I want it to be a proper decision tree rather than yet another list of -isms, because I've definately noticed I can recite the labels ('conditional analysis', 'masking', 'fink', 'categoricalism', 'dispositional monism') while having no idea which one I actually believe. So: build me a flowchart that takes someone from their verdicts on a handful of cases to a named position on what the truth of a disposition ascription consists in — whether it's a counterfactual, a categorical basis, a sui generis modal property, a second-order property, or a law of nature.

The cases I want it to run on (add more only if you need them to split something apart): (a) a wire that is live, wired to a device that cuts the current the instant anything touches it, so touching it never shocks you, though the wire is live throughout; (b) a fragile glass packed in foam, so that dropping it doesn't break it; (c) a soluble sugar cube dropped into water that's already saturated with sugar, so it doesn't dissolve; (d) a tough glass with a hidden mechanism that shatters it on impact, so it behaves exactly like fragile glass without being fragile; (e) a brave person who's taken a drug that makes them flee from danger — still brave, just unable to show it. The leaves I need to be reachable: the plain conditional analysis; the version that adds a 'so long as nothing interferes' clause; dispositions as irreducible properties in their own right, tied to their manifestations by something stronger than contingent fact; the view that a disposition just is its causal basis under another description; the view that a disposition is the second-order property of having some property that plays a certain causal role; the view that everything is really categorical and disposition-talk is a useful fiction; and the view that disposition ascriptions are really claims about laws.
