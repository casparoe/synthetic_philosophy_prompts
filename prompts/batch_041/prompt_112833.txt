I am a novelist — a handful of books, no formal training in philosophy — and in three weeks I am speaking to a philosophy of science reading group that invited me because a chapter of my novel-in-progress turns on a scientist reasoning her way to a result she could never have reached at the bench. They have asked me to talk about thought experiments, and I have a thesis I believe but cannot yet defend to their satisfaction.

The thesis: a thought experiment in science yields knowledge neither because it is an argument in disguise nor because it is an experiment genuinely performed on an imagined world, but because it is a piece of fiction, and it works the way fiction works. It stipulates a minimal possible world, hands the reader a situation governed by laws she already commands tacitly, and asks her to run her ordinary competence at navigating counterfactuals. Its warrant is neither deductive nor experimental; it is closer to the warrant we extend to a well-made story when we say it has shown us how something must go. The telling is not packaging for an inference — the imagining is the reasoning.

The audience, as best I can reconstruct it: two or three who hold that a thought experiment is simply an argument with its premises dressed as narrative and that whatever the story adds is rhetoric — they will demand the premises and the inference, and if I cannot produce them they will say I have no epistemology at all. Two who hold that these are real experiments on imagined systems with something like an observable result — they will press me on what it is to run one, to replicate it, to get it wrong. A historian of science who distrusts every unifying account and will offer a case in which the same scenario was read in opposite directions in two eras. And a formal epistemologist who will ask, quite reasonably, what I am supposed to update on and from what prior.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from these people — not the ones I would get from novelists — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, each phrased the way its author would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks, and tell me which single concession I should make early to buy credibility for the rest.

Do not send me a reading list or citations; I will find those myself. And I would rather be told my thesis is confused than be flattered: if the novelist's analogy is doing no real work, if the whole framing is a category mistake, or if what I have asked for cannot be done well, say so plainly rather than assembling a plausible-looking sheet around a bad premise.
