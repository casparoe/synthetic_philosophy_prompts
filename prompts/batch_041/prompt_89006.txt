I spent most of last Saturday evening arguing with an old friend — we sit together on the governing body of a charitable foundation established in the 1760s, which runs a row of almshouses, a chapel and a small museum — and we got nowhere at all. He calls himself a Burkean. I told him he was simply running Chesterton's fence and dressing it up, and he denied it with more heat than the point deserved. I would like an outside view, and I would like it written in British English.

The question before us next month is whether to abolish the annual visitation. The visitation is 250 years old. In its historic form the trustees processed round the almshouses in order, inspected the fabric of each building, heard whatever grievances the residents wished to raise, and read the foundation deed aloud in the chapel. Almost none of that survives as ceremony. The procession was abandoned in the 1980s, the deed is now read by the clerk alone in the vestry, and the residents stopped attending in the 1990s; they bring their complaints to the quarterly forum we set up in 2019, which they prefer. What is left is that once a year the trustees walk round the buildings with the warden and look at the fabric. The governance review we commissioned proposes to abolish the visitation outright — walk included — on the ground that a written report from the warden would convey the same information. The saving is trivial, nothing is disposed of, and a later governing body could reinstate the whole thing by a single vote.

It is worth saying that the walk is not decorative. Two of the last three large repair bills were first noticed on a visitation. The warden's report is written by the person whose job it is to keep the fabric standing.

My friend's record on comparable motions:

2019 — a motion to abolish the office of Bedel, a ceremonial post with a small stipend, duties nobody could describe, and its own endowment, which the motion would have moved into general funds. The review found no measurable output. He spoke against, and the motion failed. His line was that not one person in the room could say what the office was for, and that a review which discovers no output in something it does not understand has told you about the review.

2021 — a motion to scrap the rule requiring two trustees to sign off on any expenditure over £500, introduced only four years earlier after a small fraud. He voted for it. Staff had stopped complying years before and trustees were signing retrospectively; the vote caught the rulebook up with what everyone was already doing.

2022 — a proposal to re-establish the Court of Assistants, abolished in 1974, with new officers and a permanent endowment. He opposed it. His reason was that the presumption of continuity runs to what we have, not to what we have lost, and that the proposers were appealing to antiquity rather than to any present need.

2023 — a motion formally repealing the year's-acquaintance rule, under which new members had to be proposed by two existing members who had known them for a year. The rule was old, but had lapsed everywhere; vetting is now done by the safeguarding checks. He voted for the repeal, though he had voted against a wholesale rewrite of the same rule on first-principles grounds some years earlier.

What I want from you: his likely vote on the visitation, and more importantly the rules he is actually running. I am confident that more than one rule fits all four of those votes, and I want them set out, with what each predicts about the visitation, your best guess as to which he is really running and why, and the further case — something we could put to him — that would best separate the ones that come apart on the visitation. If he is running a rule that none of these captures, say so. Finally, tell me plainly whether I was right that this is Chesterton's fence under another name; if I was not, say what the difference between the two amounts to.
