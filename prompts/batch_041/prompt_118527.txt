I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of physics seminar, and we have just finished the section on Newton's rotating bucket and the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. I can now recite the standard moves on both sides, and that is exactly the problem: the discussion has hardened into a ritual in which the substantivalist points to the concave surface of the water, the relationist answers that the concavity only shows the water to be rotating relative to the fixed stars, and neither position is ever put under real pressure, because the case is always described in the same handful of sentences. What I want from you is a case constructed to order, so that the pressure falls on one specific spot, and so that I can see which side has to pay a price. The purpose of the case is to help me decide whether this dispute is substantive or merely verbal — whether the two parties are disagreeing about the world or only about how to describe it — so a case that simply stipulates a verdict, or that assumes its own conclusion in the setup, is no use to me.

Here is the specification. Build a pair of possible universes, in classical Newtonian mechanics only — no relativity, no quantum theory, no hypothetical new forces, ordinary laboratory materials — that differ in exactly one respect: in universe A the entire material content of the universe, laboratory included, is at rest relative to absolute space, while in universe B that same material content is moving uniformly, at constant velocity, relative to absolute space, with nothing else altered. I want the difference to be a single variable, so hold the laws, the masses, the spatial arrangement, and all internal motions fixed, and vary only the overall motion of the whole collection. State the two universes in no more than a paragraph, and then, without stipulating any verdicts, work out what follows for each of three positions: the Newtonian substantivalist, who takes absolute space to be a real entity; a relationist in Leibniz's style, who holds that only relative motions are real and that there is no fact of the matter about motion relative to space itself; and a Machian relationist, who wants inertia itself to be fixed by the distribution of matter. For each, tell me whether A and B are two distinct possible worlds, one world described twice over, or not even two distinct possibilities, and show that the answer follows from the position rather than being tacked onto it. Then do the check: identify the single feature of my specification that makes the case work, say what happens if I replace the uniform velocity with a uniform rotation of the whole collection (does the case survive, and does the answer change which theorist is embarrassed?), and give the substantivalist's best line of defense — I expect it will turn on empirical indistinguishability not entailing identity, but I want to see it stated as an argument, and then see whether the relationist has a reply to it. Do not give me citations, reading suggestions, or appeals to the literature by name; I am asking you to construct the case and reason it through yourself. If you want to note the analogy to the better-known argument about general relativity, do that only after the Newtonian case is fully built, and explain it in your own words rather than assuming I already know the terminology.
