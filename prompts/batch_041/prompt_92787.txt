You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of killing and saving, on the aggregation of claims, and on the structure of practical reasons. I want your help adjudicating an argument I overheard, but I do not want a summary or a verdict pulled off a shelf. Before judging anything, think the matter through from scratch and map the space of positions a person could take; only then turn to the exchange. Argue on the merits — you do not need to cite literature, and I would rather you not reach for famous labels or paper titles as a substitute for reasoning. Treat the case as new; the particulars matter. I have a view of my own, which I will not tell you and do not want you to guess at.

The setting: a mountain rescue, two ridges, one helicopter with time for a single lift before the weather closes. One stranded hiker on the first ridge, five on the second. Each of the six will certainly die if not lifted. Two friends of mine, Anna and Ben, argued about what the pilot should do. Neither is a philosopher by trade; both are sharp, and neither gave ground. Here is how it went.

1. Anna: "Save the five. Five deaths is worse than one. If you lift the one, four extra people die for nothing."
2. Ben: "Worse for whom? Nobody here loses five lives. Each of the six loses exactly the same thing — the rest of his life — and you have quietly assumed a single scale on which one death is a fifth as bad as five. There is no such scale. If you lift the five, you are telling the one that his life counts for a fifth of theirs."
3. Anna: "I never mentioned a scale. I said that if we lift the five, four more people are alive tomorrow than if we lift the one. That is a fact anyone can check, and it is a reason. And you have the worse problem: you say each life matters equally, so five equal things outweigh one equal thing — unless 'equal' means nothing."
4. Ben: "The worse problem is yours. You want to add lives up, but adding is a move you are entitled to make only when the things added are the sort of thing that can be added, and lives as lived are not. Since we cannot add, we should stop pretending the choice is between six units and one unit, and do the only thing that treats the six as equals: flip a coin. Heads, the lone hiker is lifted; tails, the five are. That gives each of the six the same chance, which is what equal concern amounts to."
5. Anna: "Your coin is worse by your own standard. Before anyone knows which of the six he is, each of the six has a five-in-six chance of surviving under my rule and only a one-in-two chance under yours. You say you take each person equally seriously, and your rule hands each person less of the very thing he cares about, no matter which of the six he turns out to be. So equality cannot be what is driving you."
6. Ben: "An ex ante chance is not what anyone has at stake. When I am the one on the rock, I am not a ticket in a lottery with a five-in-six chance; I am this person, about to die, and you propose to kill me for the sake of a better distribution of chances among people who are not me. And note what I am not doing: I am not writing a rulebook for rescue services. I am saying what this pilot, choosing among these lives, is required to do."
7. Anna: "But a rulebook is exactly what is needed, and it will be applied over and over, to people who do not know in advance whether they will be the one or one of the five. Written from behind that ignorance, the rulebook says: save the greater number. You cannot answer this by saying you were only talking about one case, because this pilot will face it again, and so will her successors."
8. Ben: "Then take your rulebook and be consistent. New case: the lone hiker is thirty, uninjured, with people depending on him; the five are all ninety and trapped in a way that means that even if lifted they will die within the week. Does the rulebook still say five?"
9. Anna: "No — lift the one. But I have not abandoned my principle; I have added a second. How much each person stands to lose matters as well as how many people there are. In your new case the one has far more life left to lose, and that outweighs the headcount."
10. Ben: "Then the headcount was never doing the work. In the first case you reached 'save the five' because of a contingent fact about how much each of the six had left; change that fact and your answer changes. If the number of persons were a reason on its own, what they stand to lose should not have moved you."
11. Anna: "It did not move me on the headcount; it moved me on the total. Two reasons, not one: more people is a reason, and more life at stake is a reason. In the original case they point the same way; in yours they conflict, and one wins. That is not smuggling in a hidden premise; that is having more than one principle."
12. Ben: "Then you owe me an account of what the fifth person's existence adds, once you have already weighed what everyone stands to lose. When the five are ninety, the fifth ninety-year-old was worth nothing to you; when the five are thirty, he tips the balance. So his presence counts only when his life counts. That is the welfare view with extra steps, and the extra steps do no work."
13. Anna: "Here is the case that breaks your coin. One hiker on one ridge, a thousand on the other. You flip a coin between one life and a thousand?"
14. Ben: "If I cannot add lives, then yes — one coin, one chance in two, and I accept that consequence. What I will not accept is your treating 'obviously save the thousand' as a datum. That intuition is precisely what is in dispute, and repeating it more loudly is not an argument."

Please adjudicate. Structure your answer as follows.

(a) Open with a short map of the positions actually in play and say what the disagreement is fundamentally about. In particular, distinguish the several things "the numbers count" can mean — a claim about which action is right; a claim about what reasons exist and of what kind; a claim about headcount versus total welfare at stake; a claim about a one-off verdict versus a rule to be applied repeatedly — and say which of these the two are really disagreeing about at each stage of the exchange.
(b) Then go move by move, 1 through 14. For each: whether it is valid as stated, whether its conclusion is true, and whether it is a good reply to the move immediately before it. Label each move clearly right, defensible, questionable, or mistaken, with a brief justification. Where a move is right but for a reason the speaker did not give, say so; where a move quietly changes the question or the case, say that too.
(c) Then state as precisely as you can what each of them gets right and gets wrong, and what both are missing — including any position neither of them considered that you think is more defensible than either.
(d) Finally, say where you come out on the headcount question yourself, with a degree of confidence, and say which of the two, if either, has the better overall position. Do not end with "both have a point"; if the truth is a mixture, say exactly which parts of each survive.
