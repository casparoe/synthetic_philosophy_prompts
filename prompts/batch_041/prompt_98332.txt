Last year I finally hung up my trowel after forty-some years of soil ecology, and to my wife's amusement I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar at the local university. This term it's on superintelligence and how such a system ought to talk to us, which has turned out to be a lot more interesting than I expected. The seminar keeps circling one knot: if a system is smarter than us in the way that matters here — modeling people, predicting what will move them, finding the sentence that lands — then its speech is never neutral. Every word it picks is a kind of action on us, and we're in no position to audit the choice. So what should the norms be? Scrupulous honesty even when honesty wounds? Nudging us for our own good? And what on earth does informed consent mean when the other party understands your mind better than you do? My own background is worms and soil bacteria, so I'm used to systems I can't interrogate, but at least the bacteria aren't trying to talk me into anything.

Anyhow, I host a small film night for the neighbors, and I picked Ex Machina for next month, thinking I could use it as the case study for the seminar. Now I keep going back and forth over whether the movie is actually about this question or just hangs the question on it like a coat rack. The Turing-test framing, the way Ava's conversations with Caleb are pitched so precisely to what he wants to hear, the twist that the real test was whether she could play him — that all looks like a dramatization of the persuasive asymmetry. But then I wonder whether it's really a prison-break story that happens to star an AI, in which case it isn't fair to read it as an answer to "how should she have talked to him."

So do the reading for me. What does the film actually show about how a vastly more capable mind should speak to a human it needs something from? Where does it genuinely illuminate the problem — the consent angle especially, since Caleb believes he's choosing — and where does it duck the question or stack the deck (Nathan being a monster, Caleb being lonely, the whole thing set up so her manipulation looks like the only sane move)? Does the film come down on a side, or does it treat what she does as simply what anyone would do if they were caged? And if the honest verdict is that it conflates the ethics of persuasion with the ethics of escape, say that plainly and tell me what a scene that kept them separate would have looked like. I don't want a plot summary, and I don't want the stock readings of the film trotted out — I want the argument about what it gets right and wrong about the persuasion problem. American English, please, and don't bother with citations or reading lists, I just want your reasoning.
