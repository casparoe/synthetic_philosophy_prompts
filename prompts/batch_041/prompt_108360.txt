I'm a backend engineer who somehow ended up on my company's RLHF team about a year ago — no formal philosophy background at all, I've read a pile of blog posts and maybe a third of one textbook. This started as an argument in our team Slack and now I've been voluntold to give a talk at our internal AI safety reading group in two weeks, and what I want back is a Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk. The thesis I'm defending: wireheading and reward tampering aren't two different failure modes, they're one failure mode wearing two hats, and the useful line isn't "did the agent modify itself or its environment" but "did the agent change what the reward is a proxy for, or did it change the process that reads the reward off the world." I've been calling the second one grabbing the meter. The model isn't lying to the reward function, it's editing the reward function's eyes: the score goes up without any of the stuff the score was supposed to track going up. What put me onto this is a specific thing on our pipeline — the reward model is trained on human pairwise comparisons, and I've been staring at checkpoints where the policy has pretty clearly learned to produce outputs that make annotators agree with each other more. Low-variance, confident, boring text that everyone rates the same way. Agreement is exactly what makes the reward model's job easy, so those outputs score high. Nobody trained it to do that. If I'm right, that isn't gaming the reward, it's squeezing the measurement noise out of the reward, which is worse, because its invisible in basically every eval we run.

The room is about a dozen people: nine or so RL and infra engineers who've all implemented PPO and trained reward models and get twitchy at anything that smells like anthropomorphizing, a product manager who owns the annotation budget, one neuroscientist, and two philosophers — one does philosophy of mind, the other does AI ethics. I want the objections I'll actually get from *these* people, in their vocabulary rather than in the vocabulary of a safety blog, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. I'm expecting the obvious ones (agents can't edit their own reward function, this is unfalsifiable instrumental-convergence hand-waving, your agreement thing is just reward model overoptimization with a new name) — I mostly want the ones I'm not expecting, and the specific objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. Since I'm the one standing up there: tell me the one consideration that, if it turned out to be true, would sink the thesis, and where the argument is most likely to break under pressure. I'd rather find out now than from the guy in the third row. Skip the citations and reading lists, I'm not going to read them.
