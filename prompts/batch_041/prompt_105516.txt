I have no training in philosophy or economics — I left school at sixteen and have mostly taught myself from whatever I can get out of the public library — but I've been working through a popular book on welfare economics, and one idea in it has been nagging at me for weeks. The idea is Harsanyi's: that if you imagine yourself behind a kind of veil of ignorance, equally likely to turn out to be any one of the people affected by a decision, and you then simply run the ordinary expected-value calculation you'd run for any gamble, the arithmetic resolves into a plain sum of everyone's wellbeing — that is, it turns into utilitarianism. What bothers me is the word "equally." I can't see why, in that imagined lottery, I should be exactly as likely to be the person who gains as the person who loses, and I suspect that if you quietly altered those odds the conclusion would alter with them. My friends say I'm confusing the illustration with the argument, but I can't tell whether that's so, and I'd rather have a case built to test it than a lecture.

So please construct one for me. Keep it to a single paragraph, set somewhere ordinary — a workplace, a village, a hospital rota — with stakes a normal person could actually face, and no exotic machinery; two options only, not more. The case has to be engineered so that: (a) Harsanyi's aggregation, run the way he runs it — behind the veil, each person equally likely to be anyone, plain expected-value reasoning — comes out in favor of one option; (b) a rival standard does the opposite, and for this rival take the maximin or difference-principle idea, that you judge a state of affairs by how it treats the person who ends up worst off; and (c) the clash is not just a matter of taste about the figures — the totals and the spread should be chosen so that neither side is winning merely by having loaded the numbers. Above all I want the equal-chances step to be the pivot: arrange it so that if you hold everything else fixed but make the veil's odds unequal — still a veil, still expected-value reasoning, just not one-person-one-chance — the aggregation flips over to the side maximin was already on, while maximin's verdict does not move. After the case, walk through the verdict each of the two standards gives and the reasoning that produces it, so that I can see the answers were generated by the theories rather than stipulated; then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the real work — the equal-chance assumption, or the expected-value frame, or the fact that one person's wellbeing is being weighed against another's at all — and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Finally, put on the hat of someone who defends Harsanyi and give me the strongest thing they could say to show my little flip is a cheat: for instance, that the equal weights are not a spare assumption one may tinker with but fall out of the axioms themselves, so that a "loaded" veil is simply a different theory wearing his name, and tell me how much of that reply I should accept.

Throughout, please keep separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and in particular don't hand me as plain fact either that Harsanyi's result strictly requires the equal-chance assumption or that maximin is the correct alternative to it, if specialists still argue about those points. I'd much rather have the argument with its seams showing than a tidy answer I later discover was one camp's line.
