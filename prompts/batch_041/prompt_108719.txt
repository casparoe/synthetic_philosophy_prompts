I'm a TA for a second-year grad seminar on formal epistemology — priors, indifference, and all the ways it goes wrong — and I'm writing one long question for the take-home half of the midterm. The instructor is adamant that it not be answerable by writing out "the chord problem shows indifference is inconsistent" and listing the three standard answers, because every year the A students and the C students produce that same paragraph and we can't tell them apart. So the question has to turn on a case they can't have seen, and on what would have to be true for indifference to even get off the ground. Here's the case I improvised in section last week, and it landed well enough that I want to build the question on it: a café serves a drink that's part tea and part milk, and all we know is that the ratio of tea to milk is somewhere between a half and two — i.e. neither ingredient is more than twice the other — and there's no other information. What's the probability that there's more tea than milk? One student said the ratio t/m is in [1/2, 2] and we know nothing else, so by indifference it's uniform on that interval and the answer is 2/3. Another said the information is symmetric, it's the same information about m/t, so indifference applies to m/t on [1/2, 2] and the answer is 1/3. A third said "it's obviously 1/2 by symmetry," which is the answer I actually want them to earn, and I did notice the info really is invariant under swapping the two ingredients, since t/m being in [1/2, 2] is the same condition as m/t being in [1/2, 2], so the problem itself can't tell tea from milk. (I'm fairly sure the third student is right, but I want the exam to force the reasoning out, including whether her argument is just indifference in disguise or something weaker and safer.)

So please write me: the exam question itself, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 with the must-hit moves and the marks each is worth. The question should be doable in 75 minutes with no notes. In the marking scheme I want credit for saying why applying indifference to the ratio is the wrong level to apply it at; for getting the symmetry argument right rather than just asserting it (swapping the labels maps the event to its complement, so a credence function that respects the symmetry is forced to 1/2 — and I want them to see that this needs no uniformity assumption about the ratio at all, and to say whether it's still a form of indifference and why this is the defensible kind); and for the general moral, namely that indifference is legitimate when the problem's information is invariant under a group acting transitively on the space of possibilities and the invariant measure is normalisable, plus why the chord case is unlike my café case precisely because rotating the circle doesn't carry a chord to chords at other distances from the centre, so the symmetry can't reach the thing that's in dispute — and I'd like a bonus line if anyone notices that a symmetry this small pins down the yes/no answer without pinning down the whole distribution.

I also want a sub-part where they have to say what's wrong with each of the two students, and a sub-part where they have to say whether 1/2 is entailed by the stated information or needs some further substantive assumption. And for each must-hit point, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it — the usual suspects are treating 2/3 versus 1/3 as a merely verbal dispute, or claiming the symmetry argument is just indifference in a hat and therefore circular, and I'd like the scheme to say what a student has to say to earn the marks back after either of those moves.
