I teach an upper-level metaphysics of mind seminar and I'm writing the final, and one of my essay questions has to be on the gradual-replacement argument for the possibility of mind uploading — the thought that if you swap out the brain's parts one at a time, each replaced by a functionally equivalent silicon unit, the person survives, whereas destroying the brain and building a duplicate from scratch would not preserve the person. I want a question that can't be answered by announcing a functionalist premise and then declaring victory, so I've built it around a case of my own devising, which I'll call the ratchet. Candidates are to imagine a lab that replaces one cortical unit at a time over a year, with the subject awake throughout and reporting uninterrupted experience, and then to consider three variants: (a) the same total replacement compressed into an hour under general anaesthesia; (b) a version in which the replacement units are only behaviourally equivalent to the originals — matching them input for input and output for output, but not sharing their internal causal profile; and (c) a version in which the lab takes a complete snapshot of the subject at the halfway point and later instantiates it twice. Please write the essay question (answerable in about 45 minutes), then a model answer of roughly 700 words, then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, 20 in total, with a one-line note for each point saying how candidates typically lose it. The model answer shouldn't lean on the literature — no citations; the whole value of the question is that it tests the argument's structure, not the reading. The question should force a candidate to say what the gradualness is actually doing: whether it is an argument for preservation of identity or merely an argument against a certain abruptness-based intuition. It should also make them face the transitivity squeeze, namely that if every individual swap preserves identity then identity is preserved at the end, while if some swap does not, the argument needs a principled place to stop that isn't just the point at which the replacements stop looking gradual — and they should have to say whether the same reasoning would carry identity through a sequence of increasingly unfaithful replacements. I also want them to say how the ratchet case comes out under at least one rival account (animalism, a further-fact or simple view, or four-dimensionalism), and what the defender of the argument must concede about variant (c), where the one-one character of identity means that at most one of the two instantiations can be the original.

In the model answer, please state how confident you are in each substantive claim and single out the one point you are least sure of; I would much rather the scheme flag a contestable claim than dress it up as settled. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single crux the question turns on.
