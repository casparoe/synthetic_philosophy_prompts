My partner and I are to be married in the spring, and a conversation at a family dinner has stayed with me longer than I expected. My brother, who reads a good deal of philosophy and was not, so far as I could tell, arguing in bad faith, put his objection to same-sex marriage as follows. Marriage, he said, is not a private preference or a loose arrangement but a rule-governed practice, and in such a practice the rules are constitutive rather than merely regulative: they do not record what people happen to do, they determine what counts as doing it at all. Chess was his model. The rules of chess are not a summary of how people have happened to move pieces; they are what make a move a move and a win a win, and a player who declares that his king may henceforth move three squares has not reformed chess but abandoned it for another game. And because the rules of a practice fix its point — as the rules of chess fix what counts as winning — the purpose of marriage cannot be reassigned by anyone's preferences, however widely shared. His conclusion: a legislature that redefines marriage to include two people of the same sex has not extended marriage but replaced it with a different practice wearing the same name, and the substitute cannot be defended by appeal to the value of the thing it displaced.

I would like an audit of this argument rather than a verdict on the politics. Set out the correspondence he is relying on — what plays the role of the rulebook, the players, the constitutive rules, the point of the game — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which of the disanalogies matter for the use he is making of the analogy, and which are merely decorative? What does the comparison quietly import about marriage that ought to be established rather than assumed? A colleague of mine offers a cruder version of the same thought — that a statute declaring married men to be bachelors would be nonsense, and that 'marriage' stands to 'bachelor' in this respect — and I should like to know whether the chess version does any work that the cruder one does not, or whether the two amount to the same thing. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if a better analogy for what marriage is and what it is for is available, what it would be.
