Next month I have to defend a chapter of my dissertation in front of our modern-European history seminar, and it is a chapter I would have called undergraduate nonsense when I started graduate school. My claim is that Hitler was a cause, but of a particular kind: that the authoritarian turn of 1930–33 was overdetermined — the Depression, the collapse of parliamentary government, the conservative elites' willingness to use the nationalist right and then be used by it, the party's own losses in the autumn of 1932 — while the racial state and the war of annihilation in the East were not, and that a Papen or Schleicher or military regime would have produced an authoritarianism without that specific content. The whole argument turns on a distinction I had to invent because I could not find it ready-made in anything I had read: between substitution counterfactuals, in which the crisis, the elites, the institutions and the international situation are held fixed and only the man at the top is swapped out, and subtraction counterfactuals, in which he is removed and the system is allowed to run on its own. Almost every structuralist objection I know of refutes a necessity claim of the first kind while leaving the second untouched, and I need to be able to say that in a way a hostile room will actually hear. The chapter exists because I lost an argument at a family dinner — my brother-in-law, a political scientist, told me the question was either trivially true or permanently unanswerable — and I have not been able to leave it alone since.

The room will be our department's seminar, about twenty people: mostly social and economic historians, two of whom work quantitatively on the interwar economy and will want the counterfactual made testable; one who has spent a career on the Weimar welfare state and regards great-man history as a moralizing genre rather than an explanatory one; one who is impatient with the whole inheritance of the Sonderweg controversy; a Weberian intellectual historian who will want to talk about charismatic authority and about how far officials and administrators were already running ahead of any formal order; and a philosopher of social science writing on causal explanation who will ask, quite fairly, what my counterfactuals quantify over and whether a structural cause preempts the individual one. Please write me the question-period prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, in this room's vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do to the chapter, and saying which of them raises it; for each, what I should concede up front, the two-minute reply I should give, and an honest note on where that reply is soft; what I ought to give away before anyone asks for it; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the best answer I have to it anyway. I know the literature well enough — I don't need a reading list, I need the six or eight questions that will actually land.
