I'm a third-year political science student enrolled in a seminar on democratic theory and comparative democratization, and each of us has been asked to design and run one session for the class. We've spent several weeks on the question of what democratic backsliding actually refers to — whether it names a genuine, diagnosable process or whether it functions mainly as a label applied to some governments and withheld from others depending on who is doing the classifying — and what has unsettled me is how quickly my classmates and I reach firm verdicts about a given country while it turns out, once we press one another, that we are quietly using entirely different thresholds and criteria. For my session I want an activity that makes the group feel this rather than hear me lecture about it: the goal is for participants to commit to a verdict about a specific case and only afterward discover that the verdict was driven by a cutoff or a criterion they never stated and never agreed on. The setting is a 90-minute seminar with 16 students; no prior reading should be assumed for the activity itself, though the debrief can draw on the course material.

I'd like you to design the whole thing for me — setup, roles or rules, the materials I need to print or put on the board, a minute-by-minute timeline, and whatever the group actually votes on or decides. I'd prefer it to turn on a fictional case rather than a real country, so that nobody can win simply by reciting facts about Hungary or Turkey from memory. Here is the seed I want to use, which you should flesh out into something with enough texture to argue over: a mid-sized parliamentary democracy, call it Marisia, where a government with a comfortable elected majority has, over roughly a decade, amended the constitution to lengthen the executive's term, restructured the courts by ordinary statute, consolidated friendly ownership of the major broadcasters, and then won two further elections — but where there has been no ballot-stuffing, the opposition still holds some offices and occasionally wins in court, and the government's support is genuinely popular rather than manufactured. I want the activity arranged so that different participants end up applying different implicit standards to this same record — some treating any erosion of the electoral playing field as decisive, others requiring an actual rupture such as annulled elections or open violence — and so that the disagreement surfaces in the decision the group has to make rather than in a discussion afterward. If it helps, you may give participants asymmetric roles or asymmetric briefings about what counts, but the rules should be simple enough to explain in five minutes.

Then give me the debrief: map the specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the conceptual dispute — what the group was really disagreeing about when it disagreed about Marisia, where the threshold was doing the work, and what it means that the label ends up depending on who is authorized to apply it — and tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. Finally, I want a frank note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, since I don't want the exercise to oversell what a 90-minute simulation can establish about a concept this contested.
