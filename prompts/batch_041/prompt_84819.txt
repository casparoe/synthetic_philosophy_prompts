I edit a general-interest science and philosophy magazine and I'm the person who reads the unsolicited essays. Every couple of weeks something comes in where Gödel's incompleteness theorems are doing the heavy lifting — "so no AI can ever really understand maths", "so no theory of consciousness can ever be complete", "so no legal code can close its own gaps", that kind of thing. I did one logic module as an undergrad and retained none of it, I get maybe fifteen minutes per submission, and anything I want to publish has to get signed off by my editor-in-chief, so I need a triage proceedure rather than an explainer. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I can't check them and I don't want them, I need something I can actually run.

So: write me a checklist for judging whether an appeal to Gödel is doing legitimate work. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and I can send a form rejection, and where it stops and I hand the piece to an actual logician because what's left is a question about values, or about what "understanding" means, rather than about the theorems. The hard part is that it has to be runnable by someone who doesn't know the maths. If a step is "check whether the axiom set is recursively enumerable", that step has failed — tell me what to ask the author instead to get at the same thing, and what a bad answer to that question looks like.

I also have a half-formed diagnosis of the pattern and I want you to either fix it or confirm it: (1) a fact about what one particular formal system can prove gets converted into a fact about what can be known, or about what's true; (2) a fact about devices that work by mechanically checking proofs gets converted into a fact about anything you'd want to call a mind; (3) "incomplete" quietly slides into "inconsistent" or "broken", so the conclusion becomes that the system is defective rather than merely limited. If those are the wrong cuts, give me the right ones, and be specific about which of the three is the one actually doing the damage, if any of them is.

Then run the procedure on three submissions I've got sitting here. (a) A physicist arguing that physics can never arrive at a final theory of everything, because any such theory would be a formal system of the relevant kind. (b) A legal theorist arguing that adjudication is always inescapably creative, because legal systems are incomplete in Gödel's sense. (c) A philosopher arguing that no machine can match human mathematical insight, since for any such machine there's a sentence it can't prove and we can see that it's true. I want to know what the procedure does with each, including the one where the answer is going to annoy me — (c) is the one I'd most like to publish.

And be commited. If you think one of my three cuts is bogus, say which one and what replaces it, don't just lay out the options. Where the procedure bottoms out in a judgment call, tell me what you'd actually do rather than saying it's a judgment call, and tell me where the procedure is going to misfire — which submissions it will wrongly wave through and which it will wrongly reject.
