I'm a novelist — third book; the first two were about a lighthouse keeper and a divorce, this one is about a woman who leaves a philosophy PhD to go work at a lab that opens up language models and stares at their insides. So I've been reading alot of the interpretability literature for texture, and I keep hitting the same argument pattern without being able to find a proper name for it, which is annoying, because I want to put it in someones mouth in chapter nine and have them be right about it. Here's the shape of it. Someone trains a little linear probe on the model's internal state at some middle layer and it can tell, with high accuracy, whether the text so far is set in the past or the future, and the write-up says the model represents time. Then someone zeroes out that one direction, reruns everything, and the model's outputs don't budge. And the reply is: well, then it wasn't a representation, that was just information that happened to be decodable. Fine — except the same move runs in reverse. Someone ablates a direction, the behaviour breaks, so the direction gets pronounced the real thing; then someone points out that the model has eighty layers of redundant machinery, and that if you'd knocked out a different direction, or trained a second model on the same data, the work would get done somewhere else, or by a whole cloud of directions none of which individually matter much. So "you can read it off" is true of lots of things the model never uses, and "removing it changes the behaviour" turns out to be a fact about one particular model's plumbing — and nobody seems to hold both tests to the same standard at the same time. Whichever one supports the claim of the day gets to be the criterion.

What I want is for you to pin down the thing itself rather than hand me a word for it. Is there an established name for this gap — or for the slide between the two tests — or is the honest answer that there isn't one and I should coin it? If there is a name, tell me exactly which named thing it is and how it differs from the nearest neighbours I already have in my notes (probing, ablation, superposition, and the general "correlation isn't causation" complaint — is this just that complaint in a lab coat, or is it structurally different? and is the redundant-routes case a second problem stacked on top, or the same one seen from another angle?). Where are the edges — what would have to be true of an intervention for it to actually license the sentence "the model represents X"? I write fiction, so I'm professionally suspicious of sentences that report what something really believes, and I half-remember some old philosophy of mind where a state only counts as representing something if its job in the system is to carry that information, but I don't know if that's a real criterion or just a relabelling of the puzzle — feel free to tell me it's a relabelling. Don't bother with citations, I'm not writing a lit review. And take a definite position: I'd much rather you tell me which camp is wrong and why than give me a balanced tour of the options, and if you think my framing is confused, say where instead of humouring it. Finish with a single last line in exactly this form: \answer{the established name, or your coinage — then a dash and a gloss of at most eight words}.
