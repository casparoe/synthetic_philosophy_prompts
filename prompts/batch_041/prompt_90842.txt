So I'm grading the midterm for my history-of-evolution course and I want a second read on one answer before I lock in a mark. The question was:

"Darwin argues in The Descent of Man that the moral sense is a product of natural selection. Explain how he gets from the social instincts to a genuine moral faculty; say which entity he thinks selection acts on in that process; and say how he takes the moral sense to relate to religious belief. Finally, do you think his account makes morality merely a biological or social convention? Explain."

Here's the answer I'm looking at (student #41):

"Darwin's key move is to treat the moral sense as continuous with the social instincts. Dogs and monkeys already show sympathy and mutual aid, and we inherit those dispositions. What's distinctive about humans is that our intellect is developed enough to remember past conduct and hold it up against present impulses, so when a social instinct has been left unsatisfied we feel a lingering dissatisfaction with ourselves. That retrospective dissatisfaction is conscience -- on Darwin's view it's basically the social instinct reasserting itself after the fact, once memory and reflection are in play.

Since these instincts are products of natural selection, so is the moral sense. Darwin is clear that the relevant unit is the individual: individuals inclined to help others were more likely to receive help in return, so selection favored a disposition toward mutual aid. He deliberately avoids any appeal to the good of the group or the species, because selection can only act on advantages that accrue to individuals.

He also thinks the scope of the moral sense expands over time. Early humans cared only about their own tribe; sympathy gradually widens until it takes in all humans and eventually the animals. And as the scope widens, the standard of right action shifts along with it, which is why he treats our moral standards as relative to whatever community we happen to live in. He also holds that the highest standard is the greatest happiness of the greatest number, which he takes over from Bentham, and that the golden rule is the practical expression of it.

On religion, Darwin doesn't think the moral sense depends on it. Conscience works through the social instincts and through our regard for the opinions of those around us, not through fear of divine punishment, and in his autobiography he describes losing his Christian faith without losing his moral convictions -- though he does note, ruefully, that he lost his old taste for poetry and music along the way, which he took as a kind of deadening."
