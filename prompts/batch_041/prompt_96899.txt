I recently cut meat out of my diet for animal-welfare reasons, and then someone asked me what happens to the insects in the fields that grow the rest of my food; I still cannot tell whether the scale argument makes my concern for pigs and chickens a rounding error or whether it is an abuse of large numbers. Please give me your credence that the expected disvalue of insect suffering exceeds the expected disvalue of all vertebrate suffering combined — and decompose it rather than asserting it. If you think that proposition is too ill-formed to have a truth value, say so and give me the nearest well-formed version, with a number for that instead. At minimum I want a rough probability and one line of justification for each of: that insects, or some insect taxa, are sentient at all; that, conditional on sentience, an insect's suffering carries enough moral weight per individual for the arithmetic to work rather than a heavily discounted weight (say, because its temporal resolution leaves its life with far fewer phenomenal moments, or because brief nociceptive events without a persisting subject count for little); that a non-trivial fraction of insect lives are net negative; and how you handle the fact that insects-are-sentient is a single clade-level hypothesis rather than an independent coin flip per insect, so the expectation is carried by a tail rather than smoothed by averaging. Then combine the terms, tell me which one the total is most sensitive to and what single empirical or conceptual finding would move it most, and whether that number should change what I eat.
