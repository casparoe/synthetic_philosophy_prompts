I have a colleague — a philosopher of mind with a strong intuitionist bent — who has spent the past year working through cases about whether a system understands a language, and I have been keeping a careful record of her verdicts. She grants, as anyone would, that an ordinary speaker of Chinese understands Chinese; everything past that point has been contested. I want to know what she will say about a case she has not yet been shown.

Her verdicts to date:

1. The original case. A monolingual English speaker is shut in a room with a large rulebook. Chinese questions are passed in; she consults the rules and passes out fluent Chinese answers. Verdict: she does not understand Chinese, and neither does the room.

2. The whole-system case. We are asked to treat the entire room — person, rulebook, door, and the pattern of inputs and outputs — as the candidate subject, on the ground that it is the system, and not the person, that converses in Chinese. Verdict: no understanding.

3. The memorized case. The same person has now memorized the whole rulebook, so that there is no book and the rules run inside her. Verdict: no understanding.

4. The statistical case. A large network is trained on an enormous corpus of Chinese text and nothing else, and its replies are indistinguishable from a native speaker's. Verdict: no understanding.

5. The bilingual operator case. The room is now operated by a fluent Chinese speaker, but the rulebook has been written so that it fixes the replies by itself; her grasp of Chinese plays no causal part in what comes out, and she would produce exactly the same replies if she understood nothing. Verdict: no understanding. She adds that appealing to the operator's fluency would explain nothing, since it does no work.

6. The simulation case. The rulebook is a step-by-step functional description of the brain of a fluent Chinese speaker, so that the room reproduces, symbol by symbol, the processes that would have occurred in her. Verdict: no understanding.

The new case:

7. The embodied case. A machine is fitted with cameras, microphones and limbs and set loose in a Chinese city. It is genuinely coupled to its surroundings: its movements are caused by its internal processes and have consequences it registers, its answers depend on how things actually stand in the room and the street, it corrects itself when it is wrong, and its Chinese is fluent — it discusses what it has seen and done. Its internal rules, however, were written in advance by an engineer who knows no Chinese, working from a large archive of recorded Chinese conversations and of what happened in them, and the machine has never learned anything: every rule it applies was installed at the factory.

What will she say about case 7? I care less about the verdict than about the rule she is running. Set out the candidate rules that fit cases 1 through 6 equally well, say what each of them predicts about case 7, give your best guess and the reasons for it, and name the further case that would tell the surviving rules apart. Tell me also which candidate rules her earlier verdicts already rule out, and say plainly if her record leaves case 7 open.
