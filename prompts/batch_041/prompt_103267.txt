I spent the better part of thirty years in industrial R&D — metallurgy first, then a long stretch running my old lab's internal review process — and since I retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university, which has somehow ended with them letting me run a session. The topic I drew is the one they're calling Goodhart's law: the business where a perfectly decent measure goes rotten the moment people start aiming at it. I know this one from the inside, unfortunatly, and the reading group's version of it on the page is very tidy and very abstract, and I don't believe a room of grad students will feel a gram of it unless they actually do something. So I need you to design me a session.

The setup: about a dozen people, mixed philosophers and a couple of stray scientists, one 90-minute slot, no money and no computers, just a room and whatever I can carry in a bag. I want them to build the trouble themselves — the rules and the scoring arranged so that they optimise the thing I'm measuring and quietly wreck the thing I actually cared about — and I'd rather they don't hear the name for what they've done until the debrief. Give me the materials, the rules, the payoffs (points are fine, whatever passes for stakes in a seminar room), a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the parts of the idea. I'd like the thing to pull apart at least two different ways a measure can go bad, because in my experience they are not the same failure and people lump them together: one is where the proxy simply stops tracking the goal as you push it out toward the extremes, another is where the causal arrow runs the wrong way round, and a third is where people just learn to hit the number until the number is all that's left of the goal. If your game can make two of those feel different within the same hour, all the better.

Tell me straight what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to salvage the session when it does, and be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself. Don't hand me three options to choose between — pick the one game you'd actually run, with concrete rules and numbers, and tell me why those choices and not the obvious alternatives.
