I am a teaching assistant in a second-year undergraduate course on the philosophy of physics, and I am marking midterms. The question reproduced below was worth ten of the hundred points on the exam, and students had roughly twenty-five minutes for it. They were told that they would be marked on the argument rather than on the prose, and that no citations or names of secondary literature were expected. What follows is one student's answer, transcribed from the exam booklet.

**Question**

The relationist about space holds that space is nothing over and above the spatial relations among material bodies; the substantivalist holds that space is itself a thing, existing independently of whatever bodies it contains. Newton's bucket is commonly offered as a test case. A bucket of water hangs from a twisted rope. In the first stage the bucket is spinning and the water has not yet begun to spin with it, and the water's surface is flat. In the second stage the water has been dragged around and is spinning with the bucket, and the surface is concave, climbing the walls of the bucket. In the third stage the bucket has been stopped while the water continues to spin, and the surface is still concave.

(a) Explain why the bucket experiment is supposed to be a problem for the relationist.

(b) Suppose we could remove everything else from the universe, leaving only the bucket, the rope, and the water, and suppose the experiment ran through the same stages as before. What should the substantivalist predict about the water's surface in the second stage, and what should the relationist predict? Defend your answers.

(c) Does the bucket experiment settle the dispute between the two views? Answer yes or no, and give your reasons.

**Student's answer**

(a) The bucket is supposed to be a problem for the relationist because the shape of the water's surface does not track the water's motion relative to the bucket. In the first stage the bucket is spinning and the water is not, so the water is moving relative to the bucket, and the surface is flat. In the second stage the water has been dragged around by the bucket and is spinning along with it, so the water is not moving relative to the bucket at all, and now the surface is concave. If the surface were a function of the water's motion relative to the bucket, we would expect the opposite. So the surface must be registering the water's rotation with respect to space itself: in the second stage the water really is rotating, even though the bucket is not moving relative to it, and in the first stage it really is not rotating, even though the bucket is sliding past it. Newton's conclusion is that a body's true motion is its motion with respect to absolute space, and that the shape of the surface is what registers the difference.

(b) In the emptied universe the substantivalist should predict the same concave surface as before. Whether the water is rotating with respect to space does not depend on what else happens to exist; if the water is rotating in the second stage in our universe, then it is rotating in the empty universe as well, and the centrifugal effect on the surface is the same. The relationist should predict a flat surface. If space is nothing over and above the spatial relations among bodies, and the only body in the universe is the water, then there is nothing for the water to be rotating relative to, so there is no rotation for the concavity to consist in. This is the honest consequence of the relational view, and it is why relationists have generally wanted to keep the rest of the matter in the universe in the picture. A relationist might reply that the water is rotating relative to its own parts, but in the second stage the water rotates rigidly, so the distances among its parts never change, and there is no relational change for the rotation to consist in.

(c) I think the bucket does settle the dispute, and in the substantivalist's favour. The relationist's most promising line is that the surface registers the water's rotation relative to the matter in the rest of the universe, the fixed stars, rather than relative to space; but that line is unavailable in the emptied universe, where there is no other matter at all, and the relationist must still say that the surface stays flat. So the shape of the surface cannot be registering a relation among bodies, and what it registers must be a feature of space itself; and since that feature would be there whether or not any bodies were around, space must be a thing in its own right. Leibniz's objection to absolute space was that it can never be observed, and that what can never be observed cannot be legitimately asserted to exist; but the bucket shows that we do observe something, the shape of the surface, that only absolute space can explain. Modern physics does not rescue the relationist either. General relativity replaces absolute space with a dynamical metric field, but a field that assigns a determinate value to every point of the manifold is absolute space under another name.
