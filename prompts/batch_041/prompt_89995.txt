You're going to write a dialogue for me. First, put yourself in the head of someone who has spent an unreasonable amount of time in climate-policy seminars and also reads philosophy for fun — you're equally at home with elasticities and with arguments about whether growth is a good thing. Before you write a single line, brainstorm: sketch the strongest possible version of each character's position, list the places they'd genuinely collide, and note which clashes are empirical and which are normative. Take your time with that part. Then write the scene.

Two people, one room, no moderator. One is a carbon-pricing economist — thinks the whole problem is a missing price on carbon and that everything else is second-order. The other is a degrowth person — thinks pricing is at best a side dish and that the real move is shrinking the throughput of the economy and redistributing work and income. Neither one is stupid, neither one is a villain, and neither one is secretly the author's mouthpiece. Both are competent and both should be able to land real hits.

They're fighting about a specific proposal a friend of mine sketched, which I'll lay out here so nobody has to look anything up (you can't anyway). Call it the Carbon Escalator. A carbon price starts at $40 a tonne and rises by $8 a year automatically as long as national emissions are falling by less than 5% a year — so the escalator speeds up when progress stalls. Revenue gets split three ways: half back to every citizen as an equal per-capita dividend, a quarter into a fund that pays for a move to a 32-hour week at the same pay plus retraining, and a quarter into transit and building retrofits. If the price ever crosses $200 a tonne, mandatory rationing kicks in on top. There's also a border adjustment so imports from places without a price get charged at the border.

The economist thinks this is just a carbon tax wearing a costume, that the dividend is the only part worth keeping, and that the shorter-week stuff is either irrelevant or actively counterproductive. The degrowth person thinks the pricing half is the weak half, that the dividend and working-time parts are where the actual politics and the actual emissions live, and that the escalator is a fantasy because growth will simply outrun it. Let them go at each other.

Some things I want them to actually get into: whether the escalator can outpace growth, the rebound effect and whether it eats the dividend, whether rich countries have genuinely decoupled growth from emissions or just outsourced the dirty parts, whether leakage through trade makes the border adjustment a fix or a trade war, whether you need growth to fund the transition or whether the transition is what forces you off growth, who actually bears the cost and whether the dividend is enough to make it fair, and the plain political-economy fact that carbon taxes keep losing elections while the degrowth people keep losing votes. Let them respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past each other — if one makes a move, the other should engage with that move specifically, not with a stock speech.

Two more things. Have them find at least one point of genuine agreement and at least one moment where they realize their disagreement isn't empirical at all but about what the economy is for. And don't let them melt into a mushy middle — end with each of them naming the single piece of evidence that would actually change their mind, and make those two pieces different from each other.
