I'm a novelist, and the narrator of my current book is a psychologist in the 1920s who trusts his own introspection rather more than he should. To get his idiom and his assumptions right, I've been reading William James's *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), and one stretch of the preface has me stuck:

> I have kept close to the point of view of natural science throughout the book. Every natural science assumes certain data uncritically, and declines to challenge the elements between which its own 'laws' obtain, and from which its own deductions are carried on. Psychology, the science of finite individual minds, assumes as its data (1) thoughts and feelings, and (2) a physical world in time and space with which they coexist and which (3) they know. Of course these data themselves are discussable; but the discussion of them (as of other elements) is called metaphysics and falls outside the province of this book.

I can't tell what this commits him to. Does he mean that introspection is reliable enough that psychology may take its deliverances as given, the way physics takes bodies in motion as given? Or is he being deflationary in a subtler way — treating thoughts and feelings as data only by declining to ask whether they are trustworthy, and handing that question to metaphysics so the science can get on with its work? And if it's the second, is that a principled division of labor or a postponement of the very question that would make introspection a scientific method at all? I need to know what the passage licenses, what it merely concedes, and where a reader like me — eager to make my narrator overconfident — would be overreading it.

Please give me:

1. **The passage in plain terms** (~150 words): a restatement of the claim in ordinary language, including what work the words "assumed" and "uncritically" are doing.
2. **What this commits him to** (~250 words): what follows about the evidential status of introspection, and what does not follow.
3. **Where I would be overreading** (~200 words): the tempting inference for someone writing a character who leans hard on introspection, why the passage doesn't support it, and what it actually leaves open.
