I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of religion, and I sit as one of the independent lay members of the content standards panel of a public service broadcaster. We adjudicate complaints against our editorial code, which requires due impartiality on 'contested claims of fact' and expressly exempts 'the expression of personal belief or opinion'. On Thursday the panel hears a complaint the code was plainly not drafted with in mind, and I have to circulate our written decision beforehand.

The programme was a weekly religious affairs slot. It carried a twelve-minute interview with a woman who described being given a terminal diagnosis, being prayed over at a healing service, and later being told that her tumour had gone. The presenter's only follow-up was to ask how the experience had changed her faith. A complainant, a retired oncologist, argues that the programme broadcast a medically contested causal claim — that the prayer was responsible for the remission — with no opposing view, and that this is precisely what an impartiality rule exists to prevent. The broadcaster answers that the item was personal testimony about a religious life, not an assertion about what caused a medical outcome, and that requiring a sceptical counterweight to someone's account of her own experience would be both absurd and cruel.

What has split the panel is a disagreement about what the woman was actually doing when she said that God had healed her. I think the line that runs from Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief is doing genuine work here, whether or not my colleagues want to hear it. On that view her words are not a hypothesis competing with the oncologist's, so 'balance' is the wrong remedy; they express something more like a picture that governs how she lives, and the distance between her and the complainant is not the distance between two rival predictions — not a disagreement that further evidence could settle either way. But I am uneasy about where that leaves us. It slides towards the thought that religious utterances are not really claims at all, which is not how most believers understand themselves, and it would be an odd basis on which a regulator should protect religious expression. It also seems to license broadcasters to air any religious assertion unchallenged, provided they redescribe it as testimony.

Please draft the decision memo I will put in front of the panel. It should contain a clear recommendation on this complaint — uphold, reject, or uphold in part — with reasons my colleagues can actually engage with, and a separate recommendation on whether we should propose an amendment to the code, since we will keep meeting cases of this shape. The memo should say what the woman's utterance ought to be treated as, and defend that classification rather than assume it. It should give the strongest version of the case I am inclined to reject: that treating religious assertions as non-factual is itself a contested and arguably patronising move, that the panel's business is protecting audiences from being misled rather than adjudicating the grammar of religious language, and that a regulator which starts distinguishing real claims from mere pictures will end up deciding which believers are sincere and which are merely expressing themselves. Say plainly what we lose if that objection is right. If you think a third code category is needed — something like first-person religious testimony, sitting between a contested claim of fact and a personal opinion — draft it and flag the difficulties of wording it.

No citations or reading list are needed; I want the reasoning, not a survey of the literature. Address it to the panel, keep it to about 1,200 words, and write in British English.
