I'm teaching the Schematism chapter in a grad seminar on transcendental arguments next week and I've gotten myself stuck on a passage. It's from Meiklejohn's 1855 translation, so forgive the antique diction:

"In all subsumptions of an object under a conception, the representation of the object must be homogeneous with the conception; in other words, the conception must contain that which is represented in the object to be subsumed under it. ... But pure conceptions of the understanding, when compared with empirical intuitions, or even with sensuous intuitions in general, are quite heterogeneous, and never can be discovered in any intuition. ... Now it is quite clear, that there must be some third thing, which on the one side is homogeneous with the category, and with the phænomenon on the other, and so makes the application of the former to the latter possible. This mediating representation must be pure (without any empirical content), and yet must on the one side be intellectual, on the other sensuous. Such a representation is the transcendental schema."

Three things I can't get a clean grip on. (1) That first sentence — is it a conceptual point about what subsumption *is*, or a psychological claim about how we represent? Couldn't you just have a brute rule pairing them with no shared middle term, so that the demand for a "third thing" is smuggled in rather than earned? (3) Is what he's doing here a transcendental argument in the same sense as the Deduction, or does it just presuppose the Deduction and describe a mechanism?
