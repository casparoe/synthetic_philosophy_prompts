I am designing a case for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and its purpose is to isolate exactly which hypothesis of Aumann's agreement theorem is doing the work. (The theorem: two agents with a common prior who have common knowledge of their posterior probabilities for an event must assign that event the same posterior.) Construct a scenario in which two agents share a prior and each updates by Bayes' rule on private evidence, in which their posteriors for some proposition genuinely differ, and in which it is common knowledge between them that they disagree — each knows the other's credence differs from their own, knows that the other knows this, and so on — so that the case appears to violate the theorem. Constraints: the setting must be finite and mundane (urns, cards, a shared sheet of readings; no infinite partitions, no measure-zero events), the agents must be stipulated to be perfectly rational Bayesians with the same prior, and the divergence must be genuine rather than a rounding artifact. Then: (a) give the state space, the two information partitions, the shared prior, and the proposition, and compute each posterior at the actual state; (b) show, level by level, that the disagreement is common knowledge, and explain why this does not contradict the theorem by naming precisely the hypothesis that fails; (c) identify the one feature of the case that does the work and say what happens to the disagreement once the missing hypothesis is imposed; (d) state the strongest objection a defender of the theorem could raise against your case and reply to it. End with a final line beginning "Load-bearing assumption:" that names the single hypothesis which must fail for the case to exist.
