I write long features about science and ideas for a general readership, and right now I'm deep in one on the repugnant conclusion — the thing where you're supposed to end up agreeing that a vast world of lives that are only just worth living beats a small world of very good ones. My problem is the explainer bit. Every time I try to lay the options out for readers I collapse into the same total-versus-average framing, which is not how anyone actually thinks, and which flattens alot of distinctions that turn out to matter. So I want a decision tree instead: a thing a reader can walk down and land on the view they actually hold, whether or not they've ever heard a name for it.

Build it from the reader's verdicts on cases, not from the labels. I don't want the first question to be "are you a totalist or an averagist" — I want it to be something like, is a world with a million people whose lives are barely worth living better than a world with ten thousand people living well? But don't just take my cut. Work out what the real branch questions are — whether the number of lives has value in its own right, whether there's a threshold below which adding a life isn't a plus at all, whether the view only counts things that are good or bad for some particular person, whether a tiny loss to the worst off can be outweighed by a huge gain in headcount, whether comparing two populations with entirely different members is even a meaningful operation — and then hang the actual positions on the leaves where they land.

Leaves I need to be able to reach: plain total utilitarianism (the bullet-biting one), average utilitarianism, a floor or threshold view where a life only counts as a plus if it's above some level, the family that says how good it is to add someone depends on how well off the existing people already are, a view where no number of mediocre lives can outweigh one excellent one, and the line that says adding people is nobody's business — you can't wrong a person by not creating them — so the only real constraints are on how you treat the people who exist. If some combination of answers doesn't correspond to anything anyone defends, mark it "unnamed" and tell me why its empty: is it incoherent, just too repulsive to hold, or has nobody bothered? I'd genuinely like to know where the empty spots are. And if two positions come out identical on your tree, say so instead of papering over it.

A few things I keep running into that the tree has to handle. One: there's a result I know about roughly, and I don't need the citation, that you can't have everything you'd want a population axiology to have at once — some set of attractive properties has to give. So I want to see, branch by branch, what each position is giving up. Two: I want the case where you replace a small happy population with a much bigger, mildly happy one and nobody in the first group is worse off — alot of my readers will say that's fine, and I want to see where that answer puts them. Three: there are people who think the whole exercise is a category mistake, that only individual people can be better or worse off and a "population" isn't the kind of thing that can be better or worse at all. That has to be somewhere — a leaf, or the thing that falls off the tree entirely, you tell me which and why.

Keep it to a page or so, no more than seven branch points. And write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullets, no tables, no bold, just paragraphs the way you'd lay it out in an email to me. I know a tree reads better as a diagram, but I want to be able to read this on my phone and paste it straight into my draft.
