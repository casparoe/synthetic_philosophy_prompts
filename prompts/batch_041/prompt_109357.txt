I've been keeping a commonplace book of my own positions on persistence and identity — partly to keep from contradicting myself across a long series, partly because a philosopher friend keeps telling me that novelists hold their metaphysics the way other people hold their politics, i.e. incoherently — and I've reached the point where I want the thing audited before I put any of it in a character's mouth. Here is where I've landed. (1) The ship rebuilt plank by plank while it is at sea is the same ship that left harbour; the ship later reassembled from the discarded original planks is not. What makes something the same thing over time is the continuity of the whole — its history — rather than the material it happens to be made of. (2) What matters in a person's survival is psychological continuity: memory, character, the thread of first-person experience, and the carrying-forward of projects. That continuity can hold to a greater or lesser degree and can in principle branch, and where identity is determinate at all it is not itself what matters. (3) A literary work is individuated by its text. Alter a sentence and you have a different work; alter every sentence and you have a different work, even if the same author carried it over from the same manuscript in the same room. (4) There are no composite objects — no ships, no persons, no novels — only simples arranged in various ways, with our talk of such things being a convenient fiction that earns its keep. (5) These questions have answers. Which of two candidates is the original ship, whether a revised manuscript is the same novel, are matters of fact rather than merely verbal disputes, however hard the facts are to find.

Two things from my working life that I'd like you to keep in view, since they are why I care. I have just brought out a 'definitive edition' of my first novel in which I rewrote nearly every paragraph while holding the plot, the characters and the title fixed; my publisher, my readers and I all treat it as the same novel, and I would defend that as more than sentiment, though I could not immediately tell you on what grounds. And I have carried one character across three books who, by the last page, shares with her first appearance nothing but a name and a scar on the left wrist; I take her to be one person throughout, and I take the question whether she 'really' is one person to be a real question with a real answer.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through (1) through (5) and tell me which of them genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to and why the appearance is misleading, and how severe each real tension is — I would rather have them ranked than listed as so many equally bad problems. Where there is a genuine conflict, give me the cheapest revision that would resolve it and tell me what that revision costs me elsewhere; and if some single change would clear up more than one conflict at once, say which one and what I would be giving up to make it. I am prepared to be told that one of these has to go, but I would like to know precisely which, and I would like the apparent tensions not to be swept into the same pile as the real ones.
