I am a high-school student and the secretary of my school's philosophy club, and I have been asked to prepare the materials for our next session. The session is a ranking exercise, and I would like you to produce the ranking itself, so that we have something concrete to argue with. Please do not give me a general survey of the literature on personal identity. I want the ranking to fall out of the specific cases below, and I have deliberately chosen cases that are not the ones that usually get discussed.

The question we are ranking against is a single one, asked in the plural where necessary: where am I? More exactly, after a procedure has been performed on a person, which entity, at which place, is entitled to the name, the memories, and the legal standing of the original, and how many such entities are there?

The criteria to be ranked are these six. (1) Bodily continuity: you remain the same person as long as it is the same living body. (2) Continuity of the brain, or of whatever part of it is the seat of the mind: you go wherever that organ goes. (3) Psychological continuity: you remain the same person as long as memory, intention, and character are linked by a chain of overlapping states. (4) Narrative continuity: you remain the same person as long as the story you tell about your life can still be told as one story. (5) The pattern view: you are an abstract pattern of organization that could in principle be carried by more than one physical substrate. (6) Animalism: you are simply the living human organism, whatever happens to its mind.

Now the four cases.

Case one, the vat and the far body. A person's brain is removed and kept functioning in a life-support apparatus in a laboratory. The brain is linked by a radio channel to the person's body, which is in another country. The person wakes up seeing through that body's eyes, feeling its limbs, and speaking with its mouth, and also knows, because the experimenters have told them and because they can watch the equipment on a monitor, that the brain is in the laboratory. Asked where they are, they hesitate.

Case two, the split and the double transplant. The two hemispheres of one person's brain are separated, and each hemisphere is transplanted into a different donor body. Both bodies wake up. Each of the two reports the same childhood, the same name, and the same unfinished argument with a sibling. Neither can tell which of them, if either, is the original, and they are not in contact.

Case three, the slow substitution. Nothing is cut or moved. Over five years, the neurons of a person's brain are replaced one at a time, in the ordinary course of metabolism, by functionally identical silicon units, so that the person is never unconscious and never notices a seam. At the end, the entire brain is silicon and behaves exactly as before.

Case four, the snapshot. A complete record is made of a person's brain, and two new bodies are grown and given the record. Both wake up reporting the same life. The original is then destroyed; then we run the case again, and this time the original is not destroyed.

Please give me the ranking as follows. First, assign each of the six criteria a tier, from strongest to weakest, using the tiers S, A, B, C, and D, with each tier containing as many or as few criteria as you judge appropriate. Then, for each criterion, give me one or two sentences of justification that refer explicitly to at least one of the four cases by number. Then give me a table scoring each criterion from 0 to 5 on each of the four cases, where 5 means it gives a definite and non-arbitrary answer to the question and 0 means it gives none, together with the total for each criterion. State whether the totals and the tier list agree; if they do not, tell me which of the two I should trust and why.

After the ranking, answer four further questions. Which single case does the most work in producing the order, and why that one? Is there any criterion in the list that cannot be scored against these cases because it is answering a different question, and if so, what question is it answering? If we altered one detail in one case, would the top two swap places; which detail, and which case? And finally, if the club had to commit to one criterion for the purposes of this exercise, which would you put first, and how would you defend it against the case that embarrasses it most?

One constraint on the whole answer: I am not asking which of these views is true, and I do not want the ranking to be a disguised popularity contest among positions you have read about. The order should be one that a reader could reconstruct from the four cases alone.
