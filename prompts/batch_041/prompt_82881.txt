Two colleagues of mine have been at each other for about two years and I want to make them write something together. One is a diehard counterfactualist about causation: he thinks 'A caused B' comes down to B not having happened if A hadn't, with all the usual patching for cases where the effect would have happened anyway, and he thinks the interesting question is what the world has to be like for that counterfactual to hold. The other is an interventionist: a causal claim is a claim about what would happen under a (possibly merely possible) intervention, causation is relative to a chosen set of variables, and the counterfactualist is doing metaphysics on a relation nobody needs, since the real action is in the semantics and pragmatics of causal talk and its use in explanation and policy. They agree on almost every case and still can't agree on anything. Please write the joint statement they could actually both sign.

Ground rules for the document: no tour of the literature, no bibliography, no as-so-and-so-argues moves, no named works or page numbers -- I'll be reading this without the library, and I don't want guesses dressed up as scholarship. Every argument in your own words.

I want, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including claims about particular cases, not just platitudes.
2. The points where they still disagree, each stated in wording that the side it belongs to would accept as a fair rendering of its own position rather than a straw man.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them.
4. Then a disagreements table: for each crux, the case or argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance like a bet.
5. A verdict on how much of this is a verbal dispute -- two different questions wearing the same word -- and what's left once you strip the verbal part out.

I've been trying to cook up cases that don't just recycle the standard preemption and double-prevention examples, and I want you to stress-test them rather than admire them. First: a hospital assigns patients to a drug using a rule that reads off the previous cohort's outcomes, so the assignment variable is itself caused by the outcome it's supposed to be an intervention on. It has the surface marks of an intervention -- set by a mechanism outside the patient, not reading the patient's own downstream state -- but it isn't independent of the outcome variable. Does the interventionist have to deny the causal claim, or just go looking for a different intervention? Does the counterfactualist get to keep it for free? Second: two variable sets over the same system, both defensible, one of which makes X a cause of Y and the other of which doesn't. I suspect the interventionist shrugs and says causation is relative to a variable set while the counterfactualist says one of the sets is just wrong, but I may be setting up a straw man on one or both sides. Third: a world with no possible interveners in it -- no agents, nothing that could intervene, just rocks falling and leaving dents. Does the interventionist still say the impact caused the dent, and if so is that a claim about the world or about our concepts? If any of these three collapses into something already chewed over, say so and say why; don't pretend it's novel to be nice.

Be committed. If you think one side is simply making a mistake -- that the relativity move is a dodge, or that the counterfactualist's metaphysics is idle -- say so and give your reasons. Where you're guessing at what one of them would say, flag it as a guess instead of smoothing it over. I'd rather have a draft I have to argue with than a neutral survey that hands all the choices back to me.
