I'm building a seminar session on collective memory, and I want to run the banana massacre stretch of One Hundred Years of Solitude through the memory-versus-history debate: Halbwachs on collective memory, Pierre Nora on how lived memory gives way to official sites of memory once history takes over, and the later suspicion that a community's memory can be manufactured rather than inherited. My problem is that the novel seems to invert the usual setup. In Macondo, the lived and shared memory is the false one — the town agrees that nothing happened, that the strike was settled and everyone went home — while the record, the courts, and the army all back that up. The one person who remembers it truly, José Arcadio Segundo, who rode the train of corpses out of the station, ends up alone and can't hand it on. So Nora's hunch that lived memory is the warm authentic thing and history the cold reconstruction looks backwards here. On top of that, the novel's own number, three thousand dead, is far larger than anything the historical scholarship supports (the official figure for Ciénaga in 1928 was 47, and estimates run from there up to a couple thousand), which makes the book itself a suspect carrier of memory. I want a reading that says where the novel actually lands on which side should give way when a community's memory and the record conflict, where it resists being turned into a lesson (does it romanticize the solitary witness, or does it show that a memory no one can share is already lost?), and what it contributes to the argument that neither Halbwachs nor Nora quite supplies. No line-quoting please, I know the book.
