I sit on the board of a small neighborhood tool library, and we are stuck on a governance question that has turned out to be less about tools than about what makes a shared expectation binding. For years, when a member returned a borrowed item late, the next person on the waiting list was offered the item first, and the late returner was expected to wait an extra cycle before borrowing again. No one ever voted on this, and it is not in our bylaws. It seems to have begun as an accident during a busy summer, when our volunteer coordinator happened to hand a returned drill to the next person in line rather than back to the member who had returned it; members liked it, began to anticipate it, and now most of them treat it as binding. A newer board member wants to write it into the bylaws, with a formal penalty schedule, because she says an unwritten practice is not really a rule and cannot be enforced fairly. A longtime member objects that writing it down will change its character: people will follow it because it is written, not because they expect one another to follow it, and we will lose the flexibility that lets us make exceptions for someone who was in the hospital or whose project ran long. Last month a member who had been away for a year returned a saw late and, when told he would have to wait, said he had never agreed to any such rule and that the rest of us were simply treating a habit as a law. The board has to decide whether to codify the practice, leave it informal, or abolish it, and I keep feeling that the answer depends on questions we have not asked: what makes a regularity into something binding, whether it can be binding without being written down, and whether the fact that most members now expect it is enough to make it authoritative for someone who never opted in.

I would like advice on how to think through this, not just a recommendation. Suppose we could ask everyone what they expect others to do and what they expect others to expect them to do; would that settle whether the practice is already binding, or would it only show that we have settled on one stable arrangement among several? What role should the history of how the practice began play—does it matter that it started as an accident and became salient, or that a rival tool library across town handles late returns differently, so our practice is only one of several possible arrangements? If we do write it into the bylaws, are we creating a new rule or merely recording an old one, and does the answer change the obligations of members who joined before the writing? I am also interested in the reverse question: if we leave it informal, what would have to be true for it to be fair to sanction the returning member who says he never agreed? Please separate the parts of this that are about coordination from the parts that are about fairness or legitimacy, and tell me what would count as a decisive reason to codify, to leave it alone, or to treat the practice as having already been binding all along.

One more wrinkle: our volunteer coordinator has kept a private notebook of every exception granted over the years, and it turns out that exceptions have gone mostly to members who are friends of the coordinator. Some members now say the practice was never a neutral arrangement at all but a way of distributing favors. Does that change whether the practice could be binding in the first place, or only whether it is just? And if the notebook was never public, could the practice ever have been common knowledge in the way that would make it binding for everyone, including the member who had been away?
