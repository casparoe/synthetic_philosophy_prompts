I'm a third-year PhD student in legal theory and I run a tiny podcast for our department — constitutional theory, philosophy of law, whatever I can talk people into. Next week's guest is someone I've been reading for years: she works on self-amendment, plays a lot of Nomic, and thinks the game is the best working model we have of a legal system that can rewrite itself.

Quick refresher, in case it shapes how you frame things: Nomic is a game where changing the rules is a move. The standard starting ruleset splits the rules into immutable and mutable; immutable rules take precedence over mutable ones, and you can't modify or repeal an immutable rule until you've transmuted it into a mutable one, which is itself a rule-governed move with its own voting threshold. Everything is fair game, including the victory condition and the rule that says players have to obey the rules.

Her thesis, as far as I can reconstruct it: the paradoxes people generate in Nomic — a rule that rewrites the procedure for rewriting rules, a rule that's immune to the very procedure that brought it into force, two rules that each authorise the other's removal — don't show that self-amendment is impossible or that the ruleset is incoherent. They show that no ruleset can absorb the moment where somebody has to decide what the rules now say, and that entrenchment (immutable rules, unanimity clauses, the bits of real constitutions that say they shall never be amended) doesn't make amendment impossible, it just raises the price and the visibility of that moment. So entrenched clauses don't bind us the way we assume; mostly they make the rupture auditable. She's happy to bite that bullet, which is exactly why I'd rather the questions made it harder for her to keep biting it.

Draft me eight or nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on: (a) what the immutable/mutable split is actually doing if she's right, and whether transmuting an immutable rule is a real constraint or just a formality; (b) a concrete ratchet case — the players vote to require unanimity for all future amendments, then want to get back to majority rule and there's no rule-governed path back; (c) the fact that Nomic players can quit while legal subjects mostly can't, and whether her argument trades on cheap exit; (d) whether she's just relabelling the positivist story about what officials accept, in fancier vocabulary; (e) what could count against her thesis, if it's meant as a claim about law rather than a necessary truth about norms; and (f) versions of the game where the rules are written in code and a program enforces them — whether her "someone has to decide" moment survives when the interpreter's own parsing rules sit outside the ruleset.

Nothing generic, please — no "what drew you to Nomic," no "so what is self-amendment really." Press where a hostile commentator would press, and where she has a standard deflection ready, aim the follow-up at the deflection rather then at the original question.
