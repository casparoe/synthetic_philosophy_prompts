Hey — I'm a third-year PhD student (my thesis is on models of arithmetic, but dont hold that against me), and I've been trying to write a short piece for our department's informal philosophy-of-math reading group. It's not a proper paper, more of an essay, and I'd love someone to tear into it before I inflict it on people in person. Heres the gist of what I've drafted:

We all act as if PA (or ZFC, pick your poison) is consistent, and we're taught to treat a consistency proof as the gold standard, with Gödel telling us we can't get one for a system from inside itself. But I think there's a distinction people gloss over. Call it ideal consistency — there is no derivation of a contradiction, full stop — versus practical consistency — there's no derivation short enough that any being will ever actually write it down. Suppose PA is ideally inconsistent but the shortest proof of 0=1 is longer than anything any physical computer could ever produce (there's a busy-beaver-flavored argument that if you could compute certain absurdly large thresholds you could decide this outright, which is basically another way of saying you cant). Then what's the actual harm? A contradiction only hurts you if you stumble into it. If it's buried past the reach of any method we'll ever have, the system is "consistent" in every sense that matters for doing math. So maybe the gold standard should be practical consistency, and maybe that's all we ever needed.

But two things nag me. First, practical consistency isn't a property of the axioms at all — it's a property of axioms plus our finite resources, and those change over time, so a system could drift from practically consistent to practically inconsistent without a single axiom changing, which seems like it shouldn't be how a mathematical property behaves. Second, there's a nasty asymmetry: if the system is ideally consistent we can never verify it (Gödel), and if it's ideally inconsistent we might also never verify it (the proof is too long), so we can end up permanently unable to settle the thing in either direction — not "we don't know yet" but "there is no fact we will ever be in a position to learn." I cant tell if that second point is profound or just a confused restatement of incompleteness.

What I want from you: tell me if the ideal/practical distinction is doing real work or if it's just the old consistency-vs-provability point dressed up, where a logician would first stab at it, and whether the "drift" objection actually breaks the idea or can be patched. Also just tell me if it's boring, I can take it.
