I'm a novelist — third book, slow going — and I've spent the last two weeks at a writing residency in a converted monastery with a climate scientist and an economist, which means dinner has turned into an arguement every night. The fight is about what the economist calls the unilateralist's curse: you've got a bunch of people who could each do some thing on their own, each of them only seeing part of the picture, and so the thing ends up getting done even though if they could all compare notes they'd say don't do it. He thinks that's a reason to treat solo action on big shared risks as basically presumptively wrong — wait for the room, defer to whoever can see the whole board. She thinks that's a recipe for nobody ever doing anything, since the norm of deference is itself something somebody has to unilaterally impose, and she says the economist's own argument proves too much. Then she threw in a distinction that's been rattling around my head for days, and I want you to actually take it seriously rather than gesturing at it: she says the curse only really bites on what she calls single-key actions, where one person turning the key is enough to cause the harm, and it mostly dissolves for multi-key actions, where a thousand people each have to turn their own key before anything happens — and that in the multi-key case the curse flips into something like its opposite, because now the danger is that everyone reasons "my key doesn't matter" and nobody turns it. (She also claims jaywalking is a multi-key action, which I think is a category error, but she's very stubborn about it.) The economist's comeback, for what it's worth, is that the multi-key rescue is a cheat, because in any real multi-key case there's always a marginal person whose key is the one that tips it over, and that person is a unilateralist whether they like it or not.

So here's what I want from you. Write me an essay on this controversy: does the unilateralist's curse actually justify a general presumption against unilateral action on shared risks, or does that presumption collapse under its own weight? Weigh the strongest version of each side — not a summary of the argument, but the version that's hardest to answer, including the obvious move that whoever decides to obey the norm is making a unilateral call about coordination. Then put my friend's single-key/multi-key distinction to work and tell me whether it's doing real philosophical labor or just relabeling cases we already knew were different. And land somewhere: give me a verdict with reasons attached, even if it's conditional — something like "presumption against unilateral action in single-key cases, no presumption in multi-key cases, and here's the test for telling them apart" would be perfectly acceptable, a shrug would not. Don't hedge at the end and don't tell me both sides have a point and leave it there. No citations, no reading list, no "scholars have argued" — I'm not writing a dissertation, I'm trying to figure out what my character does in chapter nine, so just argue it out with me.
