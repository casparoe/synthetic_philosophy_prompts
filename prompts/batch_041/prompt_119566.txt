I retired six years ago, after a career in experimental physics that ended with a decade of arguing about whether our instrument could see what we claimed it was seeing, and I had promised myself that when the laboratory stopped taking my mornings I would finally learn some philosophy. I have kept that promise in a haphazard way — I sit in on whatever the faculty will let me sit in on — and this year I have attached myself to a small reading group on comparative constitutional law, a subject for which I have no training whatever and which has proved far more interesting than I expected. One of the members is a retired professor of constitutional law, a woman of about my age with a very firm hand on the tiller, and she delivers verdicts on historical episodes with a compactness I find admirable and slightly unnerving. Her verdicts, as I have them in my notebook, are these. William IV's dismissal of Lord Melbourne's ministry in 1834, when that ministry still had a majority in the House of Commons: improper. The Danish king's dismissal of the Zahle government at Easter 1920 over the Schleswig question, again with the Folketing behind the government: improper, and in her account the reason the Danish crown has been a purely ceremonial institution ever since. King Baudouin's refusal in 1990 to sign the Belgian law liberalising abortion, together with the one-day device by which the government declared him unable to govern and promulgated the law in his absence: improper — she allows that the device was ingenious but holds that the objection should never have been made. The Japanese emperor's televised message in 2016 indicating that he wished to step down, followed by legislation permitting him to do so: proper, and in her telling exemplary, because he took no power into his own hands. The Spanish king's televised address during the attempted coup in 1981: proper; she calls it the paradigm of a head of state behaving well.

Our next session is on a hypothetical the convenor has circulated, and I have been asked to open the discussion. The setting is a small parliamentary monarchy with a single elected chamber, fixed four-year terms, and a head of state whose formal powers are the familiar ones — to summon, prorogue and dissolve the chamber, to assent to bills, to appoint a prime minister — under a written constitution that says little about the conventions governing their use. The first question is this. A bill establishing a statutory commission empowered to compel serving and former ministers to give evidence under oath is before the chamber; it has the support of the opposition and of enough government backbenchers to pass. The prime minister retains his majority and is under no difficulty about governing; he advises the head of state to prorogue the chamber for eight weeks, which would end the bill with the session. The advice is lawful on its face, and he has let it be known that if it is refused he will not resign but will make the refusal the central issue of the coming election. The second question is set eighteen months later, in the same monarchy. The prime minister has just lost a vote of confidence by four votes; the leader of the largest opposition party has told the head of state that she can assemble a government that would survive a confidence vote; and the prime minister declines to resign, advising instead that the chamber be dissolved and an election called, on the ground that the voters are entitled to decide.

What I want from you is what I would once have demanded of a junior colleague: the rules, the predictions, and an honest statement of what the evidence cannot settle. Please set out the candidate rules that fit her recorded verdicts, flagging any that fits all five and any that fits all but one and saying exactly which verdict defeats it; give the verdict each surviving rule predicts for each of the two new questions; commit yourself to a best guess for each question with a confidence and your reasons, and name the precedent from her list that comes closest; tell me plainly where her record underdetermines the answer and what principle she would be adopting with each available verdict; and describe one further case, not among those above, that would most efficiently separate the rules that survive. I am the only person in that room who is not a lawyer, and I shall be reading from the page on Thursday, so please give it to me as structured data rather than an essay — a single JSON object with these fields: `candidate_rules`, an array in which each entry has `id` (a short label such as R1), `statement` (the rule in one sentence), `fits_all_verdicts` (true or false), `verdict_it_cannot_explain` (the episode, or null), and `notes` (any ambiguity in the rule's scope); `predictions`, an array with one entry per new question, each having `case` (either prorogation or dissolution_after_confidence_vote), `by_rule` (an object mapping each rule id to the verdict it predicts here), `best_guess` (the verdict you would bet on), `confidence` (a number from 0 to 1), `reasoning` (a few sentences), and `closest_analogue` (the precedent that comes nearest, with a clause of explanation); `best_rule`, an object with `id` and `why`, or an explicit statement that the record does not decide between two of them; `underdetermination`, a short paragraph naming precisely what her record leaves open and what she takes on with each verdict; and `discriminating_case`, an object with `description`, `separates` (the rule ids it divides) and `how`. No reading list, please — I have enough unread books.
