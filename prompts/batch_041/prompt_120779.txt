And here is what the student turned in:

Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created all at once, fully grown, suspended in empty air. His sight is blocked or there's simply nothing for him to see, his limbs are held apart so they never touch each other or anything else, and no air moves against him. So he gets no information at all from the senses. Avicenna says that even in that state the man would affirm that he exists. He would not affirm that he has hands, or a heart, or a brain, or any length or breadth; he would know only that he, himself, is. The point of the experiment is that the soul's awareness of itself does not come through the body and does not depend on the body, which shows that the soul is not a body, nor a power of a body, but an immaterial substance. And since the soul can be aware of itself with the body stripped away, Avicenna concludes that the soul is immortal and survives the death of the body — the floating man is his proof of life after death. Compared with Descartes the resemblance is obvious: both begin from a first-person "I am" that cannot be doubted, and both treat the body as something the self can be known apart from. The difference is mostly in what they do with it afterward. Descartes uses doubt as a method, stripping away everything he can possibly doubt until only the thinking thing is left, and Avicenna is doing basically the same thing here, taking the senses away one at a time until what remains is the bare self. Descartes then goes on to prove that God exists and that the external world is real; Avicenna is more interested in the nature of the soul, and he uses the man in the air to show that the soul is a substance in its own right rather than an organ like the heart or the brain. In the scenario the man is essentially a disembodied soul, which is why Avicenna thinks the soul isn't the sort of thing that has to be attached to a body.
