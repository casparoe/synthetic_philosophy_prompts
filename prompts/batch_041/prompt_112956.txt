I have written the following short essay on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert and would like a critical reading of it, with particular attention to whether the text can bear the weight of my central claim. The essay follows.

**The Spectator as Subject: What Rousseau Was Really Objecting To**

The occasion of the Letter is easy to state. In the article on Geneva that he contributed to the Encyclopédie, d'Alembert praised the city's manners, dwelt on the tolerance of its pastors, and regretted that a lingering rigor still kept theatrical performances out of it. Rousseau, a citizen of Geneva, answered at book length. The answer is usually read as moral polemic. The stage, he argues, inflames the passions it claims to purge. Comedy teaches us to love the vices we laugh at, since the surest way to make a vice lovable is to make it amusing. Tragedy spends our pity on the misfortunes of the great, and the pity it spends is a self-regarding thrill that costs the spectator nothing. The institution itself is a luxury: it wants idleness, inequality, and a great city, and it ruins a small republic. Add the figure of the actor, who lives by displaying feelings he does not have, and the indictment looks complete.

My claim is that this moralizing surface is not the argument. The deeper objection is structural, and it is the same objection Rousseau brings against political representation. What the theater does is insert a mediating image between the citizen and the world. It converts a gathering of people who might act together into an audience: a body whose relation to what it sees is spectatorship, and whose relation to one another is mediated by what they are watching. The citizen in the stalls is not doing anything; he is being shown something. And a people that is shown things is, in Rousseau's sense, a people that is represented.

On this reading the actor is not an incidental target but the emblem of the whole. He is a man whose being consists in appearing, whose self is constituted by the gaze of strangers, and who is therefore the perfected specimen of the self-love that lives on the opinion of others: he exists only in that opinion, and he is paid for it. This is why the objection is not, at bottom, an objection to the content of plays. Rousseau can condemn the theater and still have written for it, because what he condemns is the structure of the spectacle and not the morality of any particular script.

The reading also explains what he puts in the theater's place. His alternative is the public festival: an open-air gathering with no stage, no separation between those who perform and those who watch, and no admission. The point of the festival is that in it the community is present to itself instead of being represented to itself.

The difficulty is that the argument, if it is right, proves too much. A novel is also a mediated address, and Rousseau himself went on to publish a hugely successful one. My reply is that the novel is consumed in private and does not constitute a public: it makes readers, not an audience. Rousseau's objection is to the manufacture of a passive collective, not to mediation as such. I am not fully satisfied with this reply.

What I want from you is a critical reading of the argument rather than of the prose. Specifically:

1. **Accuracy.** Have I misattributed any position to Rousseau, or overstated what the Letter actually says? In particular, does the Letter itself contain the anti-representational argument I attribute to it, or am I reading back a doctrine that is stated more fully only in the Social Contract, a work that came later?

2. **The alternative.** Is the festival genuinely an alternative to representation, or is it also a way in which the community is represented to itself? If the latter, my thesis, which turns on the difference between presence and representation, is in trouble, and I would like to be told so plainly.

3. **The novel.** Is my reply about private reading adequate, or does the logic of the Letter condemn the novel as well? If it does, say why, and say whether Rousseau had any resources for resisting the conclusion.

4. **Economy.** Name the single sentence or paragraph that does the least work in the essay, and say what you would put in its place.

5. **The opposing case.** State the strongest objection that a defender of the ordinary moralistic reading would make to my thesis, and say whether it succeeds.

Please close with a verdict on whether the thesis is worth defending or whether the moralistic reading is the better account of the Letter, and identify the one thing I would have to establish, textually or otherwise, for the thesis to hold up.
