I've been in a reading group on conditionals for a term and we're stuck in a way thats getting a bit embarrassing: we can't agree on whether there even *is* a distinction between indicative and counterfactual conditionals, and we keep re-litigating the same examples. Put on the hat of a philosopher of language who knows both the semantics and the pragmatics literature, brainstorm a bit before you commit, and give me a diagnosis rather than a survey of who said what.

Here's where we are. One camp treats them as two different animals: a counterfactual is a claim about how things would go in worlds like ours in the relevant respects, so it's answerable to the facts, while an indicative is a conditional assertion whose acceptability tracks how likely the consequent is given the antecedent, so it's answerable to the speaker's epistemic state. Another camp says there's one 'if' that just combines with a modal (overt or not), and everything that looks like a semantic difference is really a difference in the modal, the tense marking, or what's being taken for granted. A third says the contrast is morphological: the past-tense-looking bit in counterfactuals isnt about time at all, it's marking remoteness from reality, so there's a grammatical distinction with no extra semantics. Same data, no movement in a year.

Two things we can't settle. First, the same sentence flips between the two readings depending on what the speaker thinks is still open — is that ambiguity in the word, context-sensitivity, or just a fact about the speaker's mind? Second, 'if you mow the lawn I'll pay you five dollars' sounds like a biconditional; half of us think a theory of 'if' should predict that, half think it's a conversational effect that's irrelevant to meaning, and then we discover we can't agree on what counts as a fact the semantics has to predict in the first place.

My hypothesis, tell me if it's wrong: the real disagreement isn't about the data but about what a semantic theory is *for* — predicting truth conditions for embedded occurrences vs. predicting when a speaker is licensed to say the thing — and about whether a difference in use, driven by common ground, has to be mirrored in meaning. If that's the diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here — a distinction nobody's drawn, a formal result about whether one 'if' can do both jobs — or is the question under-specified enough that we should just change it? And if my hypothesis is off, what kind of disagreement is it actually?
