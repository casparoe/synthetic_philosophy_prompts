I am a high-school junior, and two afternoons a week I volunteer on the memory-care floor of our local hospital. In three weeks I am giving a twelve-minute talk at the regional student bioethics forum, which the hospital's ethics committee sponsors; the audience is mostly people who work in the building rather than students my age, and the question period runs longer than the talk. My thesis is this: an advance directive has authority over a later, cognitively impaired self only when that later self fails what I call the standing-self test, and where the later self passes the test the directive drops from a binding order to a strong presumption. The test has three conditions, all of which must hold. Comprehension: the later preference is expressed with some working grasp of the alternatives, so that the person can say roughly what she is choosing and what it will mean. Stability: the preference recurs on at least two occasions separated by a day or more and survives a change of questioner and a change of phrasing, because on our floor I have watched residents' answers track whoever is standing at the bedside. Ownership: the person can offer something recognizable as a reason, and the reason is not merely an aversion to being moved or a wish to please the person asking. If the later self fails, the earlier self governs; if the later self passes, the later self governs. The corollary that keeps getting me into arguments is that I think demotion clauses should be void, that is, a directive that says "disregard anything I say once I stop recognizing my children" should not be honored, because it tries to fix the test rather than the treatment and because a person cannot be the judge of whether she is still a judge. This sits badly with my main claim, since the whole point is that the earlier self can bind the later one.

Three cases from my own experience and reading give the argument its shape, and you should treat them as the targets the objections will be aimed at. Ruth is eighty-four, moderately demented, cheerful, devoted to pudding and the sing-along; her directive, written after her husband died, asks for comfort care only, no antibiotics, no transfer. She now has aspiration pneumonia, and when a nurse asks her directly whether she wants to go to the hospital she says yes, then twenty minutes later tells the aide she would rather stay. Marcus wrote his directive at diagnosis and put in it something unusual: that if he ever becomes placid and contented, everything should still be done, because a contented later self is not the self he is trying to protect. He is now exactly that, placid and content, and under my test he fails it, so my rule says treat him aggressively; when I floated this to the nurses on my floor they found it monstrous. And there is a third case I have not resolved: a woman who, already mildly demented, re-ratified and amended her own directive, which raises the question whether an amendment made by a self that may already fail the test can count as the earlier self speaking.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Tell me the objections I will actually get from this room and not the ones I would get from a graduate seminar, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Be precise about who is objecting: the two hospice nurses, who care about the patient in front of them and are suspicious of abstractions; the hospital's clinical ethicist, who will want a decision procedure that works at three in the morning; the geriatric psychiatrist, who I suspect will say my stability condition measures how the question is asked rather than what the person wants; the disability-rights advocate from the community, who will say my test is a quality-of-life judgment wearing a procedural costume; the hospital's lawyer, who will point out that our state statute already gives a bright-line rule and that my test manufactures liability; my philosophy teacher, who is a Kantian and will want to know how one will can have two legislators; and the chaplain, who will object that a cheerful later Ruth may be closer to herself, in the ways that matter, than the woman who wrote the directive. Also tell me what to concede in the first thirty seconds so the room stops bracing, which single concession would cost me the thesis rather than just a clause, the one reply that absolutely has to land for the talk to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep it in plain language that I can say out loud, and do not send me a reading list or citations; I will be talking, not writing a paper.
