After thirty years in earthquake hazard assessment — building probabilistic maps of where the ground will shake over the next fifty years, and never once being able to say when a particular fault will break — I retired and finally did what I had been putting off for decades: I started reading philosophy. I have since been recruited onto the board of trustees of a private foundation, and on Thursday we vote on whether to launch an eight-year, sixty-million-dollar program called Early Warning: teams of modellers building quantitative forecasts of state collapse, mass political violence and democratic breakdown across a named list of twenty countries, with a stated ambition of five-to-ten-year lead times and quarterly dashboards for funders and policy clients. The proposal is well made and flatters my old field — it cites hurricane tracks, seasonal climate outlooks, seismic hazard maps and epidemic models as proof that forecasting in messy, high-dimensional systems has come of age. Two things trouble me. The first is that I have been reading Popper on why there can be no laws of historical development, and I suspect the proposal is a specimen of precisely what he was attacking. The second is more concrete: our charter requires published results, so any forecast anyone acts on is a forecast about a world that has already been altered by its own publication. I am too new to this to trust my own reading, and my program officer has a strong reply ready — that the proposal promises only conditional forecasts and probability estimates, never prophecy, and that the same objection would once have condemned numerical weather prediction. I do not want to be the trustee who kills a serious program because a philosopher with a grudge against Hegel said history has no laws.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, approve with conditions, or decline — with a time frame the board can act on, and give reasons the program officer could actually respond to rather than restating a doctrine. In particular, I would like the critique sorted into the parts that depend on the proposal promising unconditional prophecy and the parts that bite even on its modest probabilistic claims; a judgement on which of the natural-science analogies it leans on are apt and where they break, including where my own hazard-map analogy misleads me (I suspect it is a much weaker model here than the proposal assumes, and I would rather be corrected than flattered); and an honest account of the strongest case for funding, including what is genuinely lost by declining and whether a smaller or differently framed program would survive the objections that sink this one. I want the argument, not the bibliography — no citations or reading list, please.
