A psychology research group has just published a large, four-part study program, and I would like you to predict how Aristotle would react if the results were placed in front of him, with no knowledge of the philosophical debate they have since provoked. The program ran as follows. First, participants' willingness to help a stranger in distress varied by tens of percentage points depending on whether they had just found a coin on the pavement, whether they were running late, and how many other bystanders were present. Second, a longitudinal sample showed that most people's helpfulness across a dozen different situations was almost uncorrelated, but roughly one person in five behaved generously in nearly all of them; that consistent minority, however, lost its consistency as soon as the cost of helping rose sharply, and regained it when reminded of a promise they had earlier articulated in their own words. Third, an intensive character-education curriculum changed behavior in the specific situations it drilled but produced no measurable transfer to novel situations, even ones that seemed morally similar. Fourth, participants' self-descriptions of their own character predicted their conduct only in low-cost situations; in high-cost ones, the best predictor was whichever norm happened to be most salient in the room.

Given these findings, which of Aristotle's doctrines would he take them to confirm, which to leave untouched, and which, if any, to refute? I am especially interested in three questions. Does his conception of virtue as a stable state of character commit him to cross-situational consistency of the sort the second finding reports to be rare, or is his notion of stability thinner than that? Does his insistence that virtue requires practical wisdom, and that the virtuous act is the one done at the right time, toward the right people, and for the right reasons, give him resources to treat the third finding as unsurprising rather than damning? And what would he predict about a fifth study, testing whether people trained to notice a morally relevant feature in one domain would notice it in another? Where the results genuinely threaten his ethics, identify the premise he would have to surrender; where they do not, name the commitment that does the protective work.
