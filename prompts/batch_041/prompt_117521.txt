I have been reading around the split-brain cases, and I keep meeting an analogy that seems to be doing more argumentative work than anyone admits. It runs like this. The two cerebral hemispheres are two computers in a single chassis; the corpus callosum is the bus that lets them exchange data; commissurotomy is therefore like unplugging that bus. Each processor keeps running, keeps its own inputs and outputs, and merely loses the channel to its neighbour. On this picture the operated patient is not one subject of experience but two — one that speaks, one that does not; one that can name what it is shown, one that cannot — and the unity of consciousness in the rest of us is exposed as nothing more than a fat enough cable. A colleague presses the point: if a cut cable yields two streams, then a thin cable already yields two streams, and since nobody can say how much bandwidth a single mind requires, any verdict of oneness looks arbitrary. Unity, on this view, is a matter of degree, and the ordinary case is simply the case where the connection happens to be thick.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a survey of who has said what about it. Specifically:

- Set out the correspondence explicitly, as a table if that helps: what in the split-brain case plays the role of the processor, the bus, the chassis, the power supply, the operating system, and the data being exchanged.
- Identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusions from those that are merely picturesque.
- Say which features of the source picture are being imported into the target without argument — for instance, that the two components are pre-existing and independently competent agents; that the connecting channel is a passive conduit rather than something that shaped what the components are; that minds come in whole numbers; that information exchange is the only thing at stake in being one subject.
- Test the analogy against what it must accommodate: the patient's own singular report and singular sense of agency; the partial character of any such operation, since subcortical routes remain and different cases spared different fibre bundles; the functional asymmetry of the hemispheres, only one of which commands full language; the possibility of cross-cuing through overt behaviour; and the fact that the two halves were co-constructed in development rather than two agents later joined.
- Say whether the analogy, taken at face value, commits us to counting minds wherever coupling is weak — across a telephone line, say, or between a person and a notebook — and whether that is a reductio of the picture or a consequence worth accepting.
- Then tell me whether each of the two conclusions survives: that the patient literally has two streams of experience, and that unity in general is merely a matter of degree. If either survives only in weakened form, state the weakened form precisely. If the analogy cannot carry them, say what would carry them better, and what that replacement would change.
