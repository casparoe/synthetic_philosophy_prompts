I'm preparing the opening session of a graduate reading group on decision theory; we've been working through the Kelly criterion and the adjacent material on geometric rationality, and I want to start with a constructed case rather than another tour of the stock examples. Please build me a thought experiment in which expected-value maximisation and Kelly (log-wealth) maximisation prescribe opposite actions on a single decision, subject to the following specifications. First, the agent and both criteria must agree entirely on the probabilities and the payoffs: the disagreement has to be about what to do, not about what is true. Second, the gamble must have strictly positive expected monetary value, and the expected-value recommendation must be genuinely tempting — the sort of offer a prudent, non-reckless person would have to sit with for a while. Third, Kelly must recommend declining, and not because the agent has simply been handed a concave utility function or a brute aversion to risk: the divergence must fall out of the structure of the opportunity itself. I have in mind something like a minimum ticket — the stake is divisible in principle, but only in units too coarse to reach a safe fraction of wealth, as with whole shares, a minimum deposit, or a buy-in that cannot be part-staked — though an all-or-nothing gamble on a fixed lump would also serve. On either construction, the smallest permissible stake must be large enough that the expected log-growth at that stake is negative while the expected money gain remains positive. Fourth, it must be a one-shot decision rather than a repeated gamble, and it must not be the well-worn example in which wealth is repeatedly multiplied by 1.5 or 0.6; the setting should be mundane and contemporary — a mortgage decision, a stake in a small business, an offer to buy into a partnership.

Then do the work the case is for. Give the numbers, and verify both verdicts arithmetically: the expected value, the expected log-growth at the minimum permissible stake and at the Kelly-optimal stake, and the threshold stake at which log-growth turns negative. State the action each criterion endorses and why. Identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work, and then remove it: allow arbitrarily small stakes, and separately allow the bet to be repeated many times, saying in each case whether the divergence collapses and, if so, into what. Finish by saying how a defender of expected-value maximisation would try to discredit the case — including the charge that a preference for log wealth, rather than anything about the bet, is really doing the work — and how a Kelly defender should reply. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph; the analysis may run to whatever length it needs, though I doubt it needs more than about eight hundred words in total. Please write in British English throughout.
