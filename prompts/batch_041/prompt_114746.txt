For some weeks a friend and I have been arguing about whether markets create value or merely move it around, and he has produced the following argument, which I have been unable to refute. I would like you to tell me which step fails, and what I give up by rejecting it.

(1) A trade occurs only if each side prefers trading to not trading: the buyer prefers the good to the money, the seller prefers the money to the good.
(2) Each side's preference rests on that side's estimate of what the good is worth.
(3) Two people who have the same information and reason correctly will not differ in their estimates of what a given thing is worth; if we know the same facts about the car, we should put the same value on it.
(4) So whenever a trade occurs, the buyer and the seller must be estimating the good's worth differently.
(5) Given (3), a difference of estimate between two rational people can come only from a difference of information — or from different starting assumptions, which are simply information that one of them has and the other lacks.
(6) Hence in every trade, at least one side is working from information the other does not have.
(7) Each side knows (6). So a rational buyer who sees a seller willing to part with the good at the offered price should infer that the seller knows something that makes the good worth less than the price, and should decline; a rational seller who sees a buyer eager at that price should suspect that the buyer knows something, and should hold out.
(8) Whatever the price, then, the other side's willingness to accept it is evidence against it. Rational parties should never trade.
(9) But trade happens constantly and plainly leaves both sides better off.

Something in (1) through (8) must go. I am confident of (9), and I have no wish to abandon (3), which seems to me close to constitutive of rationality. I am fond of (7) as well, since it is the reasoning I would use on a used car, though I will surrender it if I must. What I want is a verdict: which single step do you reject, why did it look so compelling, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, what becomes of the inference in (7) once that step is gone, and what does the repaired argument still show?

Begin with the step you reject and a one-sentence statement of your reason for rejecting it, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my setup.
