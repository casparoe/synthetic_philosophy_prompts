A colleague of mine, whom I will call R., has spent the past term giving verdicts on a series of cases about what a person should believe when someone she takes to be an epistemic peer disagrees with her. "Peer" is meant strictly: the same evidence, comparable competence, and no antecedent reason to think either party is better at the question at hand. Her verdicts, in the order she gave them, are these.

(1) Two forecasters working from the same data disagree about whether it will rain tomorrow. Verdict: each should lower her confidence substantially and move toward the other's estimate.

(2) Two radiologists examining the same scan disagree about whether a shadow is a nodule. Verdict: neither may keep her confidence; each should move toward the middle and treat the matter as unsettled.

(3) Two listeners with the same recording disagree about which of two similar-sounding words was spoken. Verdict: both should suspend judgment.

(4) Two lawyers with the same file disagree about whether a defendant's conduct was negligent. Verdict: each should moderate her confidence.

(5) Two people with the same information disagree about whether a redistributive tax is just. Verdict: each should moderate her confidence.

(6) One of the two has read a page the other has not, and on that basis disagrees. Verdict: the party with the thinner evidence should defer; the bare fact of disagreement asks nothing further of the better-informed party.

(7) One of the two has a long track record of getting this kind of question right and the other does not. Verdict: the one with the inferior record should defer; the other need not revise.

Her stated grounds are unstable. In case 1 she said that a peer's disagreement is by itself a reason to split the difference. In case 2 she said that the point was that the disagreement made it likely that at least one of them had misread something. In case 4 she offered both. She has separately and explicitly rejected steadfastness, the view that disagreement as such requires no doxastic revision.

The new case, which we have put to her: two logicians of identical training, each fully competent, are looking at the same short, completely written-out proof. They agree on every line but one, where they disagree about whether the rule cited licenses the inference. Each has gone through that line twice, each can reconstruct the derivation from memory, and each finds the step compelling — or, in the other's eyes, plainly illicit. Neither can identify a specific mistake in the other's reasoning; each simply cannot see how the step could go the other way. There is no third party, no proof-checker, and no further evidence to be had.

I would like the following.

1. Your prediction of R.'s verdict on the new case.
2. The rule she is actually running, stated as precisely as you can.
3. The other rules that fit her recorded verdicts equally well, what each of them predicts about the new case, and why nothing in her record distinguishes them.
4. Your own best guess at the correct verdict, with your reasons.
5. The single further case that would best separate the rules that fit her record, together with what each of them would say about it.
6. If her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so, and state what principle she would be adopting under each of the live readings.
