I am the senior drafter at a public regulator that issues individual decisions — permits, benefit determinations, enforcement actions — under statutory standards phrased as gradable predicates: significant risk, material change, substantial benefit, reasonably practicable. Our decisions are appealed constantly, and the appellate body quashes them either for being too vague to bind us or for being too arbitrary to be fair. I need a drafting-and-adjudication procedure my team can actually run, first when we must put one of these standards into a rule, and then when we must apply it to a case. My drafters are lawyers, not philosophers; they have a week per rule, not a semester, and the chief executive signs the rule while the appellate body signs off on the case.

Write it as an ordered procedure: the questions to ask, in sequence, from the moment a statute hands us a gradable term to the moment we issue a determination; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the point at which the procedure forces us to fix a bright-line cutoff and defend it as a cutoff rather than a measurement; the point at which it stops and hands the case to a person as a value judgment; and the point at which it requires us to record a decision we cannot justify internally, so that the record remains auditable.

Ground it in what the philosophical literature on vagueness and the sorites actually establishes rather than in folk wisdom about gray areas: the difference between vagueness and ambiguity or mere underspecification; the difference between a borderline case and a case we simply do not yet know how to classify; the possibility of higher-order vagueness, i.e. borderline cases of borderline cases; and the fact that any consistent cutoff licenses a sorites march in which every individual step looks defensible. Give my drafters two tests they can run: one that walks a proposed cutoff along a chain of cases from clearly-in to clearly-out and asks whether the justification survives each step, and one that asks whether the procedure itself has a penumbra. Propose a way of recording penumbral decisions so that later decisions stay consistent with earlier ones without pretending the cutoff was discovered rather than chosen.

Then run the procedure on three cases — one threshold of risk, one threshold of degree or similarity, one threshold of effort — at least one of which returns an outcome I will find unjust and arbitrary. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do, and which questions a court or a legislature, rather than my team, has to settle.
