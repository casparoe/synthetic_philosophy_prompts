For about a year now, a reading group I belong to — a mix of lawyers, a couple of historians, and three or four people who did graduate work in philosophy — has been arguing about referendums, and the argument has stopped moving even though nobody disputes any of the facts. The shape of it is this. When a national referendum returns a narrow majority, one camp holds that the result expresses the will of the people, and that a legislature which then thwarts or dilutes it is usurping a decision that belongs to the people themselves. The other camp holds that there is no such thing as a popular will to be expressed: there are individual preferences, aggregated by whichever rule happened to be in force, and the same electorate with the same preferences would have returned a different result under a different agenda, a different threshold, a different wording of the question, or a different sequencing of votes. On that view, talk of the will of the people is a rhetorical way of claiming a victory that the arithmetic alone does not confer. A third member of the group insists that the first two are answering different questions — one about who ought to have final authority, the other about whether a certain kind of collective entity exists — and that the disagreement only looks intractable because both are filed under a single heading. My own hunch, which I offer as a hypothesis rather than as a premise, is that the impasse is neither a straightforward factual dispute nor a merely verbal one, and that it partly dissolves once we separate three claims that keep getting run together: that a collective will exists, that voting reveals it, and that it ought to govern.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I want it to engage the positions as I have stated them rather than to survey the literature — we have all read the standard material on aggregation and on populism, which is precisely why the argument has stalled. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, and be specific about what each side is committed to: whether the dispute is about the metaphysics of collective agency, about the epistemology of what elections can reveal, about which institution holds final authority, or about standards of legitimacy that each side is applying without acknowledging. Where a party is equivocating, say so and say on what. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result about aggregation, a change of question, or the exposure of an unstated empirical premise on one side — and whether any of it could actually move people who have dug in. If your honest conclusion is that nothing short of a change of subject would end it, say that plainly, and explain why the dispute is worth having anyway.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restating of my question.
