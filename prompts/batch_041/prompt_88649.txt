I run intake and the study registry at a community health center, which in practice means I'm the person who fills in the race/ethnicity field on our funder's intake form for every participant — 15-30 a week, participant sitting in front of me, sometimes impatient, sometimes with a kid on their lap — and I'm also the one who hands the analysts the final dataset, so whatever I type in becomes the truth about these people for the next five years. Our own clinic form was designed with our community advisory board and has write-in lines and boxes the funder's form doesn't have; the funder's form has six boxes, no write-in, and a quarterly reporting deadline. The PI's answer to every mess is "just make it consistent."

I want a procedure I can actually run: a checklist in order, with the words I can say out loud to a participant, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, the point where the answer is "no, you don't record that," and the points where I have to hand it up to the PI or the IRB because it's a values call and not a data call. The same procedure should cover the sex/gender field too, which has its own version of all of this.

Some things I've picked up that I want the procedure to actually use, in my words, and I don't want a lit review or citations — I can't check them anyway, and don't just name-drop the famous people who said this stuff, I want the mechanism:

- a category gets built for one purpose (counting a population for a legal claim, say) and then reused for a completely different one (tracking exposure to discrimination, or adjusting a model), and the original purpose leaves fingerprints in the box
- invented isn't the same as arbitrary. A category can be made up and still track something real and causally heavy, because people get treated differently on the basis of it. So "socially constructed" doesn't mean we can stop counting it, and it doesn't mean it's in the body either
- the counting loops back: offer a box and people start using it about themselves, and then it looks like it was always there. So some of the distribution I record is an artifact of the form, and my data will drift every time the form changes
- once its in the dataset, the number gets read as a property of a person's body, which is a different claim from the one I actually recorded
- a blank isn't a fact about the people, it's a fact about the recording — about what got written down, what got kept, what the fields even allowed me to ask, and what we later decided it meant

Cases I need it to handle, with a worked pass through the procedure for each: (1) a participant who says she's Black, her family's Haitian, and wants "Haitian" written somewhere, which the funder's form can't take; (2) a 74-year-old whose old chart has a race term nobody uses anymore and who describes herself differently; (3) two siblings enrolling together who pick different boxes, while the analyst assumes siblings match; (4) someone who flat out refuses and asks why we need it; (5) the PI asking me to "adjust for race" in a model of a blood-pressure outcome. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like — I'd rather find out now than after the deadline.

Also tell me where the procedure will misfire, what it genuinely can't decide, and what I should write in the data dictionary so the next person knows what the field does and doesn't mean. Keep it usable by someone with no theory training and about three minutes per participant.
