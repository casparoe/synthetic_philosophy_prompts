I'm in a reading group on philosophy of economics and we've been chewing on Friedman's old essay about positive economics — the one where he says the realism of a theory's assumptions doesn't really matter, and that a theory can even be better the more "unrealistic" its assumptions are, as long as it predicts well. (I think it's from 1953 but don't hold me to it.) Most of the group either shrugs and mutters "instrumentalism, whatever" or gets mad and says of course assumptions matter. I want to actually understand the conceptual issue underneath rather than just pick a side, so I'm hoping you can walk me through it slowly.

Here's the thing I keep bumping into. Everyone talks about "unrealistic assumptions" as if that's one single phenomenon, but it seems like there are at least two very different cases. Case one: you assume frictionless planes, or perfectly divisible goods, or a population that's infinitely large, and if you relaxed the assumption a little your predictions would only shift a little — the assumption is a convenience, it's doing bookkeeping, and you could in principle de-idealize it without the main result collapsing. Case two: you assume something false in a way that the result actually depends on the falseness — a model whose punchline is "agents are systematically biased in way X," where the conclusion evaporates the moment you let X go to zero, or a market model whose interesting result only exists because of an assumption you know is wrong (say, that nobody can ever learn anything). Let's call those "harmless fictions" and "load-bearing falsehoods" — I made that up, so if there's already a standard name for the contrast please tell me, but I care more about whether the distinction is real than about the label.

So: can you explain, fairly carefully, whether that distinction holds up? What actually makes an idealization harmless rather than load-bearing — is it about the sensitivity of the results to relaxing the assumption, or about the role the assumption plays in the explanation, or about something else entirely (and are there cases where those criteria come apart)? Then, once the distinction is on the table, what does it do to Friedman's F-twist? I have a vague sense that the F-twist is only even tempting because it slides back and forth between the two cases — that "unrealistic assumptions are fine" is true-ish when they're harmless fictions, and that the scandal is when the same line gets used to license load-bearing falsehoods. Is that the right diagnosis, or am I flattening a more interesting story? And is there a version of Friedman's view that survives once you seperate the cases — some defensible instrumentalism that's explicit about which kind of assumption it's licensing?
