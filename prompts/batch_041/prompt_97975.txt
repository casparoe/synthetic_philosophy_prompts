I have no background in philosophy or law, but I have spent a long time turning over John Stuart Mill's idea that the only reason to interfere with someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others, and I want to use it to organize my thinking about free speech. The difficulty I keep running into is that harm turns out to be several different things wearing a single coat, and the principle seems to give quite different verdicts depending on which one I have in mind. I have come to separate the kinds of damage speech can do into four categories:

1. A direct material setback to an identifiable person — a threat, an incitement that a particular listener acts on, a false accusation that costs someone a job.
2. A cumulative setback to the prospects of a whole class of people, produced by a great many individually minor utterances, none of which could be blamed on its own.
3. Damage to the shared stock of public knowledge — coordinated falsehood that leaves everyone, including those it does not target, less able to act on reliable information.
4. Damage to the speaker's own character or understanding — the thought that some speech degrades the person who utters it, so that they are harmed by their own words.

I would like you to rank these four by how much weight each should carry when deciding whether speech may legitimately be restricted, from the kind of damage that most clearly licenses restriction to the kind that least clearly does. Give a brief justification for the placement of each, and say where you think the line between harm and mere offense falls. I am especially interested in whether the cumulative case (2) and the epistemic case (3) can be accommodated by a principle written for a world of face-to-face speech, or whether they quietly demand a different principle altogether.
