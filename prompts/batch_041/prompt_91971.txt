I need a best man's speech, written so that it survives a room containing exactly one moral philosopher and roughly forty people who are not.

The wedding is in three weeks and the groom is my oldest friend. The awkward fact I want the speech built on is this: I have almost no first-hand moral convictions. Where other people have views they arrived at for themselves, I have his. I stopped eating meat because he did; I broke with my brother for eighteen months because he said I should; when I have needed to know how to treat someone, I have asked him, and then done as he told me. In twenty-five years I cannot point to a single moral position I worked out on my own. I have lived my adult life on another person's moral testimony.

That is what I want said, and it is also what makes it hard to say. Testimony obviously transmits knowledge in most domains: I know that Lima is the capital of Peru, or that the Battle of Hastings was fought in 1066, because someone told me. Whether it can do the same in moral matters is contested. I can be told what to do, and be right, and even act rightly, and still be holding a borrowed belief rather than something I have understood for myself; and there is a further worry that if I do the right thing for his reasons rather than my own, the action is not really mine to be credited with. If that is right, the standard best man's flourish — that he made me the man I am, that everything decent in me is his doing — is a more dangerous thing to say than it sounds. It would mean the decency is his and not mine, and that I have spent my life borrowing rather than knowing.

I cannot be coy about this, because my friend and his fiancée have asked me to be godfather to their daughter, who is eleven months old, and I am expected to accept in the speech. So the question of what testimony can and cannot hand on stops being academic: whatever I end up saying about borrowed moral beliefs, I am about to be installed as a source of them.

Please write the speech — four or five minutes, warm and funny where it can be, and genuinely honest rather than merely self-deprecating. I do not want the philosophy used as a way of ducking the toast's real business, which is to say what I owe him and what I am taking on. Do not name philosophers or cite any literature; the ideas should be voiced plainly, as something I have been chewing over, not as a report on a debate. And it must not feel like a lecture: the forty non-philosophers should hear a man being honest about his own character, and his sister should hear that the distinction was got right.
