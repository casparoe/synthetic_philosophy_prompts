My friends Sam and Priya got into a huge fight at lunch about whether it's pointless for one person to give up beef for climate reasons, and I said I'd get an outside verdict since neither of them would budge and I honestly couldn't referee. They're both pretty sharp, which is the problem. Can you go through the argument move by move -- who's right on each step, whats right and whats wrong in each one, and who's more right overall?

Sam: my burgers are a rounding error. The atmosphere doesn't care whether I switch to chicken, so switching costs me something and changes nothing.

Priya: that only works if harm counts only when you can point at the difference you personally made. Picture a thousand people each turning a dial up one notch on a machine wired to one person; one notch is unnoticeable, but all thousand together are agony. None of them made a difference you could detect, and yet the crowd did something awful, and it's weird to say not one of them did anything wrong.

Sam: in that picture every one of them is plugged into the same machine. My burger is a purchase in a market, and the market sells the same amount of beef whether I'm in it or not -- it just goes to somebody else. My counterfactual footprint is basically zero, so what exactly am I guilty of?

Priya: if everyone at school reasoned like that, nothing changes, and the reason nothing changes is that each person is using everyone else's refusal as their excuse. You're also not just causing harm, you're participating in a practice and kind of endorsing it, like showing up at a rally. I don't have to think my one body in the crowd swung the election to think I shouldn't be in the crowd.

Sam: then say that. Say it's about what kind of person you want to be, and I'll respect it as a personal project. But don't tell me I'm a cause of harm, and don't tell me I'm obligated -- you can't say my act is pointless and also that I have to do it. Also the harm isn't smooth anyway, it's about totals crossing lines. Somewhere there's one burger that tips a total over, and it's silly to say that burger is mine; sure, it could be, but the odds are like one in a million, so the expected harm from my lunch is basically nothing.

Priya: under your line-crossing picture, whoever happens to be last is the real culprit and everyone before them is innocent, which can't be right either. And one in a million of something catastrophic isn't nothing, thats the whole point of expected value.

I couldn't tell who was winning. Please adjudicate, and if there's a distinction both of them are missing that would clear this up, say what it is.
