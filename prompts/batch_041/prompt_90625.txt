I'm presenting next month at a faculty workshop a paper arguing that online deplatforming and so-called cancellation are best theorized not as criminal punishment — which requires a sovereign, a charge, and a sentence — but as a decentralized practice I call reputational outlawry: the withdrawal of the cooperative infrastructure (platforms, invitations, credit, employment, access to audiences) on which a person's public standing depends. On this view the live normative questions are not about due process but about the terms of the cooperative scheme being withdrawn and about the collective-action trap facing bystanders who would prefer not to join in yet bear real costs for abstaining. The audience is roughly fifteen law faculty: mostly criminal law scholars, three or four First Amendment people, a couple who work on platform regulation, and nobody who reads social-media theory. Prepare my question-period prep sheet as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no headers. Order them by how much damage each would do to the paper, and phrase each objection the way this audience would actually put it, not the way a media-studies critic would. Each item must be at most two sentences: state the objection, then give the reply I should have ready, flagging what I should concede and where the reply is soft. Make the fifth item the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
