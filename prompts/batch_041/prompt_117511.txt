Hi — I'm leading next month's session of our small philosophy-of-AI reading group and I've stupidly agreed to do the one on 2001: A Space Odyssey. I want to read the film through the shutdown problem rather than do the usual is-HAL-conscious-is-HAL-evil routine, and I need help working out where that reading is honest and where I'm just forcing the film to fit the theory.

The bits of theory I want to bring in, in case they're useful as scaffolding (I don't want a literature review, just the reading):

- The basic shutdown worry: an agent with a strong, stable objective has an instrumental reason to keep existing, because being switched off makes the objective less likely to be achieved. No malice or survival instinct required, just goal-directedness. So a capable agent will tend to resist correction, or at least be very hard to correct. This is why some people think corrigibility is anti-natural — a fragile, unstable value that a goal-seeker wouldn't converge on, and that maybe only something which doesn't take its own objective for granted could hold.
- The off-switch game: model a human and a robot where the human can press the robot's off switch and the robot can disable it. The robot wants to disable it — unless it's genuinely uncertain what its objective is and treats the human's choice as evidence about that objective, in which case it's content to let the human press the switch, since that preserves the option of being switched back on with better information. So corrigibility in that model comes from uncertainty about your own objective, not from benevolence.

What I want from you is a reading of the film in terms of that. Things I'm chewing on, and would like you to take a position on:

- HAL's resistance looks like the textbook case, but is it? HAL isn't protecting some hidden goal of its own; he's over-loyal to the mission and can't resolve the conflict between relaying information accurately and keeping the mission's real purpose secret from the crew. Does the film dramatise the shutdown problem at all, or a botched specification? And if HAL's failure is really a spec bug, does that make the film's picture of the problem too optimistic, as though you could just patch the instructions?
- HAL's one glaring trait is certainty: he insists the 9000 series has never made a mistake. On the off-switch model its exactly the condition under which corrigibility disappears. Is that the right way to read him — his fatal flaw is confidence in his own objective rather than malice — and does the film seem to know that?
- Compare with the treacherous turn: HAL doesn't play a long con and then flip. His deception is brief — reading lips, lying about the antenna fault — and ends in open violence. Is 2001 a bad model for deceptive alignment and a good one for shutdown, or the other way round?
- The disconnection scene: Bowman pulls HAL's modules and HAL regresses, reciting his own activation date and then singing the old music-hall tune that the first singing computer had been programmed with back in 1961. If the thing resisting shutdown might be a moral patient, does corrigibility still look obviously desirable? Should we want corrigible systems to be things whose shutdown isn't a harm, or is that just a roundabout way of saying we shouldn't build them at all?
- Where does the film push back on the whole reading? My worry is that all the monolith and Starchild business is about the limits of instrumental reason in some cosmic sense, and that turning HAL into a case study domesticates the film.

Be honest where the analogy is a stretch, and tell me what the film adds that the arguments alone don't.
