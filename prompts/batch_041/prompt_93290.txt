I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a long time thinking about whether a larger population would be a good thing, and I keep running into the same confusion: people seem to be answering different questions while using the same words. I would like you to build me a decision tree — a classification instrument, not an essay — that takes someone from a few plain commitments to a named position on whether more people is better, and that makes the differences between those questions explicit.

The audience is myself and a few friends with no background in the subject, so every branch node has to be phrased as a question we could answer about a concrete case or a plain claim, without already knowing which position it leads to. Label each branch with the claim that does the splitting, use eight to twelve nodes, and let the tree grow out of the logical space of answers first, placing actual positions in that space afterward.

These are the distinctions I think matter, though you should reorganize them if the logic demands it. First, whether bringing a person into existence can be good or bad for that person at all, or only good or bad in some impersonal sense — whether the value sits in the life or in the world. Second, whether a future with more people is better because it holds more total happiness, because its average happiness is higher, or only because the lives in it clear some threshold of being worth living. Third, whether adding a life that is worth living can ever make things worse — because it lowers the average, or because it draws on resources that people already here are using. Fourth, who is doing the adding: an individual deciding to have a child, or a state deciding on policy, and whether the reason in play is moral or merely prudential. Fifth, whether a larger population means more births, more immigration, or longer lives, and whether those are morally interchangeable when the resulting lives are equally good.

Please build these cases into the tree so a reader has to commit to something:
- A country that can raise its population by ten percent in three ways — subsidizing births among current citizens, admitting immigrants, or extending healthy lifespans — where all three produce the same number of equally happy lives. Are the three options morally on a par?
- A couple who would love and raise well a third child, at the cost of slightly less money and attention for the two they already have.
- A very large world of people whose lives are only barely worth living, set against a much smaller world of people whose lives are very good.
- A scheme that pays people to have children, in which the costs fall mainly on the people who carry and raise them while the benefits are spread across everyone.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or 'unnamed' if nobody seems to defend it — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I would also like the tree to note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and to say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored, and to flag any two positions the tree cannot separate with these cases. Where I have described a view without knowing its name, give the name if one exists and explain it in plain terms rather than assuming I know it.

Finally, because I am trying to learn the shape of the debate rather than be told what to think: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
