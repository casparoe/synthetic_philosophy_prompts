hey, so I'm a second-year phil student and I've somehow ended up running the tutorial session for my medieval political thought course next week. 16 people, 75 minutes, and most of them have read maybe two pages of Aquinas in their lives. Topic I got assigned: Aquinas on killing tyrants. I don't want to just stand up and explain the two tracks he seems to run at once, I want them to feel the bind in their bodies before anyone names it.

Here's the bind, as I understand it, so you know what I'm aiming at. Aquinas clearly thinks tyranny is unjust because the tyrant rules for his own private good instead of the common good. He also says that disturbing a tyrannical government isn't sedition. But he says sedition is always a mortal sin, because it attacks the unity of the people in the common good, and he warns that rising against a tyrant often leaves you with a worse one. And there's the bit where the remedy depends on how the tyrant got there: if the community originally had the right to set up its ruler it can depose him, if he's under some superior you go to the superior, and if there's no superior you're stuck appealing to God. So the question isn't really may we kill this guy, it's who counts as we, who holds the authority, and what the attempt does to the very common good it's supposed to be serving.

Can you design the session around that? Rough setup I had in mind, change whatever doesn't work. A fictional city-state with a ruler who's genuinely awful. Run it in two rounds with the same tyrant and the same grievances, but different origins: in round one he seized power illegally, in round two he inherited it legally and then turned brutal. See whether the group's willingness to act shifts, and whether they can say why. Give everyone a role with a secret stake: a magistrate whose office comes from the tyrant, a merchant who profits from the regime, a widow, a cleric who thinks the suffering is punishment for the city's sins, the tyrant's nephew, a captain whose soldiers will follow whoever feeds them. Add a mechanic for authority, like tokens representing legal mandate that most people don't have, and something that converts unauthorised action into chaos tokens which later produce a worse ruler. No real money, points or cards are fine. I want them to hit the wall where the obviously justified killing also looks like it hands the city to someone worse, and where nobody can agree who gets to decide what the common good is.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual distinctions: injustice of tyranny vs. sinfulness of sedition, private vs. public authority, the risk of a worse tyrant, and the sincerity problem where everyone thinks they're defending the common good. Tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce, because I know there's a whole theological frame I'm going to have to supply myself. Also, please don't quote Aquinas at me or give me reading lists, I'll have the texts in the room.
