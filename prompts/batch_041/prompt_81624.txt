Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about biological function, and we did not settle it. His line was that ascribing a function to a trait when nothing in its history ever selected for the effect in question is a category mistake, because function talk is historical through and through; my line was that this reduces function talk to genealogy and leaves the working biologist unable to say what a trait does. We kept talking past each other, and I eventually realised that each of us was quietly working with a different account of what a function is. I would like my students to be forced into exactly that collision rather than to recite the accounts as the textbook presents them.

I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology course (final-year undergraduates with a few MA students), and I am writing the final examination now. Please write me a single essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. The exam is closed-book; the question should take a strong student about seventy minutes and should be worth 25 marks. The students have met three accounts: the selected-effects or etiological account in the tradition of Millikan and Neander; the causal-role or systems account associated with Cummins; and the propensity variant, which keeps the etiological form but asks about a trait's current propensity to be selected rather than about its actual history (associated with Bigelow and Pargetter). What I will not accept is an answer assembled from the stock examples, the heart, the swim bladder, the bee dance, since those can be classified by memory alone. So I have invented a case set, and I want the question organised around it.

The case set concerns one hypothetical species, a small ground beetle on an isolated island, and a heritable wing feature I will call the notch:

1. The notch appeared as a byproduct of a change in wing-vein patterning and spread through a founder population by drift. In the population as it now stands, beetles with the notch fly more steadily in crosswinds and survive to reproduce at a higher rate than those without, and heritable variation for the notch is still present, but the episode is recent enough that nobody claims selection has yet had time to act on it.
2. In an ancestral population the notch was selected for the grip it gave on a host beetle that has since gone extinct. The current population lives on a different host; the notch no longer contributes to any capacity one would independently identify, yet it remains at high frequency and is still developmentally integrated into the wing.
3. The notch was selected for its grip, as in case 2, but on the current host's surface it also stiffens the wing in a way that improves escape flights, and this stiffening is now why the notch persists; selection has acted on it in its new role.
4. The notch contributes to flight stability exactly as in case 1, but the population is a single clone: the notch is fixed, no heritable variation remains, and nothing can be selected for anything.
5. The population is kept in a laboratory, where the notch's contribution is to a colour pattern that a breeder is selecting for. Nothing in the field rewards the notch, and beetles removed from the lab lose the pattern within a few generations.

I want the question to do three things. First, it should make students say, item by item, whether the account they favour grants the notch a function and on what grounds, and it should require them to identify at least one item on which two of the three accounts come apart and to explain precisely why. Second, it should force a commitment: the essay half must make them defend one account, or a two-concept view with the division of labour stated exactly, against the item that most embarrasses it, and must make them say what that defence costs them elsewhere. Third, it should contain at least one item on which the accounts agree, so that a student who reflexively hunts for a disagreement gets that item wrong.

For the model answer, take a position. Do not lay the options out and leave the verdict to me. If you think I should have won last night's argument, say so and show where my friend's position breaks; if you think he should have won, say that just as plainly and tell me what I was missing. Aim for roughly 800 words. Then give me a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, summing to 25, with a one-line note for each on the error that typically costs marks. I expect it to punish at least these confusions: reading selected as selected for; reading useful now as having a function; assuming the etiological account requires a designer or a purpose; assuming that every item must generate a disagreement between the accounts; and treating the question of which account is correct as though it were settled by which one is more convenient for field biologists. If one of my five items does not in fact separate the accounts, replace it with one that does and tell me why.
