I am a high school student, and our philosophy club has spent most of this year circling the dogmatism paradox: if someone knows a proposition, then any evidence against it must be misleading, so it seems they are entitled to disregard that evidence without even examining it. We all accept the basic steps, but we divide over whether the conclusion is a reductio of one of the premises, a tolerable consequence of knowledge, or an artifact of how we are using the word evidence. I recently proposed that the real fault line might be between evidence that is misleading and evidence that is epistemically inert, and that the paradox only seems to force us to ignore counterevidence when we conflate the two. Some of us think that distinction is the key; others think it merely renames the problem. Please diagnose why this dispute has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about knowledge and justification, a verbal dispute about evidence or defeaters, or are the two sides answering different questions—one about what we know and the other about what we are permitted to believe or do in the face of counterevidence? If a hidden formal or factual question is doing the work, identify it. Finally, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a counterexample, a revision of the paradox, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
