Last term I sat in on a jurisprudence seminar, and I've been chewing on one exchange ever since. We were discussing the aftermath of a fallen regime — the kind of setting in which a person has informed on a neighbour under a statute that was perfectly valid by the lights of the regime's own rulebook, and the questions afterward are what to do with the informer and what to do with the statute. Someone in the room kept returning to a single line, offered as though it settled everything: an unjust law is no law at all. In one breath it was meant to release the informer's victim from any duty to have complied; in another it was meant to license the new court to refuse to apply the old statute; in a third it was offered as the reason the whole question is confused. I cannot tell whether those are one claim repeated three times or three different claims wearing a single sentence, and I am no longer trying to win the argument — I want to see its joints.

Please lay out the menu for me. What are the distinct, precisely statable claims that the line could be making? I want at least these separated: readings on which no law means the norm fails some condition of legal validity by the system's own criteria of recognition; readings on which it means the norm is law but imposes no moral obligation to obey; readings on which it means the norm is law but a judge is not bound to apply it; readings on which law is a functional or purposive kind of thing that a sufficiently unjust norm cannot be an instance of at all; and any further reading you think is genuinely in play, rhetorical ones included. For each, tell me whether it is a tautology, a substantive but defensible thesis, or simply false, and be explicit about three things: which sense of law is in use, who is supposed to bear the obligation (the subject, the judge, or no one), and whether the claim is descriptive or normative. Where it matters, distinguish a claim about a single norm from a claim about a whole legal order, and distinguish a norm that was never law at any time from one that was law and has become void or merely defective.

Then bring it back to the three jobs the line was doing in that room — excusing the subject, freeing the judge, and dissolving the question — and tell me for each which of the readings actually delivers the conclusion and which leaves the speaker short. End by saying which reading the people who repeat this line usually have in mind, and whether they tend to notice when they have slid from one reading to another mid-argument.
