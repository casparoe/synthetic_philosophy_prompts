I'm in a grad seminar on practical reason and we've spent the last three weeks circling what everyone keeps calling the paradox of deontology, and honestly the whole thing has stalled in a way I find kind of maddening. Here's the shape of it as I understand it. Deontology says there are agent-centred restrictions — you mustn't kill one innocent person even if doing so would stop someone else from killing five. But then the restriction starts to look like it's just a rule for minimizing the number of rights-violations, except with a strange carve-out for whatever you personally would be the one doing. And if that's all it is, it either collapses into a funny kind of consequentialism or it's just irrational. My friend who's a card-carrying consequentialist thinks this is basically checkmate: the deontologist either cares about minimizing violations, in which case the restriction does no work, or she doesn't, in which case she's just fetishizing her own clean hands. My deontologist friend says that's a question-begging way of putting it — the restriction is about what you may *do*, not about which state of affairs you may bring about, and the consequentialist has simply assumed that all reasons have to be agent-neutral. Someone else in the room suggested the real fix is that deontology also includes an agent-centred prerogative, a permission to give your own projects and your own integrity extra weight, and that once you add that in the restriction stops looking arbitrary. But that just seemed to relocate the question for me: fine, I get to weight my own life more, so why don't I also get to kill one to save five? They've been going round like this all term and nobody has moved an inch.

My hypothesis — and I'd like you to actually test it rather than just nod along — is that this isn't a disagreement about first-order morality at all. I suspect the two sides are assessing different objects. The deontologist is answering a question about an act (what may I do, here, with my hands?), and the consequentialist is answering a question about a pattern of outcomes (which distribution of violations is better?), and "the paradox" is an artefact of forcing the first kind of claim to answer a question that was posed in the second kind. If that's roughly right, is the debate then a genuine substantive disagreement about the structure of reasons, a verbal dispute about what words like "reason" or "deontology" are doing, or literally two different questions wearing one name? And if it is substantive, where exactly does it bottom out — in a disagreement about whether agent-relative reasons exist at all, in a disagreement about whether a single act can even be assessed apart from the pattern it's part of, or in something more like a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for?

I want an actual diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what — I've read enough of that. So: tell me what kind of disagreement this is, argue for it by engaging with what each side is actually committed to, and then be honest about what would count as progress. A distinction that would let one side genuinely see the other's point? A case that would force a revision on one side? Or is this one of those disputes where the correct answer is that no argument is going to move anyone, because it bottoms out in what each side is willing to count as a reason in the first place? If it's that last one, just say so and say why, rather than pretending a clever enough distinction will fix it.
