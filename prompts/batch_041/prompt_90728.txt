I keep tripping over the same thing in law and can't get it to sit right. Courts will sometimes let a defendant's culture do real work — mitigation at sentencing, or even reshaping what the act was, so that what looks like an assault reads as discipline, or what looks like a killing reads as something else entirely. But the same courts wont let a man say his culture made it honorable to kill his wife's lover; provocation only helps if the provocation would unhinge a reasonable person, and as I understand it that reasonable person is supposed to be culturally colorless. Meanwhile the state carves out exemptions to animal-cruelty laws for relgious slaughter and lets ceremonial knives into schools, so it obviously isn't saying culture never matters. So which is it — is the law quietly relativist, or does it not need relativism at all? What exactly does this practice presuppose: that wrongness itself is culture-relative, or only that you can't blame someone for what he couldn't see as a reason, or that an action's description is culture-constituted, so that he literally did a different act than the one on the charge sheet? Separate what the practice actually requires from what it merely fits, say where it's underdetermined and several theories would license the same outcomes, then tell me what really changes if we drop the relativist reading for straight moral objectivism — does the cultural evidence disappear, or survive fine under a culpability story? (I take the Montaigne-ish point to be that we call other people's customs barbaric while ours are just ours, not that nothing is wrong anywhere, so I'm not sure he'd even back the relativist version.)
