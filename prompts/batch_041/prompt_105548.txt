I've been going down the commitment-races rabbit hole alot lately, and I built an argument that I'm pretty sure proves too much, but I can't find the exact crack.

Setup: two of us are splitting a surplus of 100, and we both just want more. Each side can either (a) sit and negotiate, or (b) commit — publicly and irrevocably adopt a take-it-or-leave-it demand. If one side commits and the other is still free, the free side's best reply is to take whatever beats its outside option, since the committed side literally can't be bargained with anymore. So going first makes you the Stackelberg leader and lets you grab almost everything.

The chain:
(1) If I commit before you, you best-respond, so I get the leader's payoff.
(2) The leader's payoff is at least as good as what simultaneous negotiation would give me.
(3) Committing early is basically always available to me and costs me nothing.
(4) So I should commit as early as I can, and to the most aggressive demand you'll still accept.
(5) You reason the same way, so we're in a race.
(6) Whoever commits first wins, and the more extreme the demand the more it extracts, so each of us is pushed toward the most extreme demand the other will still accept.
(7) So the split gets decided by who's willing to be the most ruthless, not by who has the better claim or more at stake.
(8) Generalizing: a rational agent in any conflict should precommit, as early as possible, to the most destructive threat it can make credible — "give me everything or I burn it all down" — and that's what rationality recommends.

(8) is obviously insane, and it would also make cooperation impossible to justify, which I don't buy. I'd most like to keep (1), since the whole point of commitment is that it lets you move first.
