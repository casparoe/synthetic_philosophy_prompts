I've been going round in circles with a friend about what makes something alive, and I've realised I can't actually state the rule I've been using — I just have gut verdicts, case by case. A bacterium: alive, obviously. A flu virus: not alive, though it sits closer to the line than I'm comfortable with. A prion: not alive, no hesitation at all. A mule: alive — and so is a sterile worker ant — so being able to reproduce on your own clearly isn't required. A tardigrade in cryptobiosis, freeze-dried for twenty years with no measurable metabolism: still alive, just switched off, so an active metabolism isn't required either. A flame: not alive, even though it eats, grows, spreads and dies. A growing crystal: not alive. A tumour: alive, which I find genuinely disturbing. A HeLa cell line in a flask: alive. A mitochondrion on its own outside the cell: I want to say not alive, it's a part of something bigger, but it was a free-living bacterium once and I can't articulate why the past should matter. A self-replicating RNA molecule someone built in a test tube: I keep flip-flopping; my gut says 'not yet', which worries me precisely because I can't say what's missing. A computer worm crawling across the internet making copies of itself: not alive. And those little blobs made of frog stem cells that scoot about and repair themselves — the xenobots, or whatever they're called — I honestly have no idea, and I suspect that whatever I say next I'll turn out to be inconsistent.

My friend's proposal, which I half like: a thing is alive if and only if it does work to hold itself together in a state that would otherwise fall apart, and can hand that same trick on to a next generation. I don't think that survives all my verdicts, but I can't tell which ones it fails, or whether the fault is the principle's or mine. (For what it's worth I'm in a reading group on philosophy of biology, so I've seen the usual candidates, and none of them definately feels like the one I've actually been running.)
