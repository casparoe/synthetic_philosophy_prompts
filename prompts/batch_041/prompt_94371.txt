I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of economics, and my office-mate is an econ theorist, and we've been re-fighting the same argument about the no-trade results for about three months now without either of us moving an inch. Here's the fight, as charitably as I can put both sides. She thinks the no-trade results are basically a success story for markets: take rational agents who share a prior, make rationality and the information structure common knowledge, and there is literally nothing to trade over at equilibrium prices -- which shows how tightly the informational discipline bites, and lets you read any observed volume as a rough measure of how much private information or genuine disagreement must be out there doing work. I think the results are closer to a reductio of their own assumptions than a vindication of anything: real markets churn enormous volume, most of it close to zero-sum, and the only reason the theorem gets to say "no trade" is that it has baked a common prior into the setup -- drop that and "efficiency" stops being an empirical finding and becomes a modeling choice we made. She says disagreement in priors is just irrationality wearing a nicer coat; I say the common prior is a normalization, not a fact about the world, and nobody has ever told me what observation would count against it.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
