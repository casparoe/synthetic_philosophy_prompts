I belong to a small metaphysics reading group, and we have spent the last several sessions circling the special composition question — the question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing. As you would expect, we ran through the familiar menu: composition always occurs, so that any plurality you like has a sum; it never occurs, so that there are no composite objects and our talk of them is at best a useful fiction; or it occurs only under some restriction, whether the restriction is to living things, to things fastened or stuck together, to things unified by a common function, or to something else. What we kept stumbling over is the shape of the dialectic rather than the individual answers. Any non-trivial restriction seems to face two pressures at once: the arbitrariness worry, that whatever feature is picked out is gerrymandered to fit the cases we already like, and the monstrosity worry, that a criterion generous enough to deliver tables and cats must explain why it does not also deliver the sum of my left shoe and the Moon, or the sum of this paragraph's letters, or the scattered halves of a broken cup. Meanwhile the unrestricted answer and the nihilistic answer each seem to buy consistency at an enormous price.

One member of the group floated a criterion she called causal unity, and I would like you to take it seriously for the length of an essay. Her suggestion: some things compose a whole just in case their behaviour is irreducibly joint — just in case there is a causal process or mechanism whose state cannot be factored into a product of states belonging to those things taken separately. The model she liked came from physics, where certain systems have joint states that are not determined by, and do not decompose into, the states of their parts taken individually, so that the pair or the aggregate functions as a single dynamical unit; she thought that sort of inseparability, rather than spatial contact or biological function, is the mark of the composite. She was attracted to it because it seems non-arbitrary, since it names a real and measurable feature rather than a convenient one; because it promises to sort organisms, atoms and perhaps some artefacts in, and heaps, mere pluralities and arbitrary sums out; and because it makes the composite's status as a unit of causal explanation prior to its status as a thing, which she took to be the right order of explanation. Her proposal is unpublished and, I suspect, undeveloped, so I am not asking you to track down a source; I do not want a bibliography or a reading list.

Write an essay that reaches a verdict on whether this criterion is the best answer to the special composition question, or whether it fails and something else is preferable — a more usual restricted answer, universalism, nihilism, or the view that there is no informative answer at all, either because composition is brute or because the question is somehow defective. Defend your verdict rather than surveying the options. Among the matters I want you to press on: whether irreducibly joint causal behaviour comes in degrees, and if so whether the criterion yields vagueness in composition or can draw a sharp line without gerrymandering; whether what counts as a single causal process, or a single mechanism, depends on a choice of model or level of description, in which case the criterion may make composition relative to us rather than discoverable in the world; whether the criterion gives the right verdicts about the awkward cases, including a pair of mutually orbiting bodies, a pair of coupled pendulums, two particles in an inseparable joint state that are nevertheless far apart and never interact again, a heap of sand, a statue and its clay, and an organism whose metabolism has been suspended; whether an answer that appeals to causal processes is circular, since processes seem to presuppose persisting things whose identity over time is precisely what is in question; and whether the criterion is best read as an answer to the special composition question at all, as opposed to a proposal to replace that question with one about units of causal explanation — if you take the latter line, you owe an account of why the replacement is not a dodge. I care most about the argument and the verdict, so keep the exegesis of other positions brief and spend your words on the objections and your replies to them.
