I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've somehow become the person who picks the films for our monthly movie night, which has slowly turned into a semi-serious discussion group. Last month we watched Inherit the Wind (the 1960 film, from the 1955 play) and I know it's a fictionalized Scopes trial with a lot invented -- the preacher and his daughter, the town being a mob, Brady dying in the courtroom -- and I've read that one of the playwrights said it was never really about science versus religion but about the right to think, as a parable for McCarthyism. Anyway, one of my friends said something like this is the Galileo problem all over again, and I realized I didn't actually know what Galileo thought, so I read a summary of his letters, the one to Castelli and the longer one to the Grand Duchess Christina, and now I'm more confused than before, because Galileo's argument looks nothing like the argument the movie makes and I can't tell if that's the movie being shallow or me misunderstanding Galileo.

Here's my rough version of Galileo, correct me where it's off: God wrote two books, nature and scripture, and they can't contradict each other; scripture is written so uneducated people can follow it, so it talks the way people talk -- it says the sun rises, it gives God hands -- and you're not meant to read all of it literally; so when a conclusion about nature is genuinely demonstrated by sense and reason, the fix is to re-read the scripture, not to ditch the science; and scripture's job was never to teach astronomy in the first place (he borrows a line from some cardinal to the effect that the Holy Spirit's intention is to teach us how to get to heaven, not how the heavens work). But he's also way more conservative than I expected -- he only wants to reinterpret where the proof is actually solid, he's nervous about handing ammunition to unbelievers, he keeps insisting he's a good Catholic, and he's leaning on Augustine the whole time rather than treating science as a rival authority. So he isn't quite a church-and-science-are-separate-spheres guy either, which is how I'd always heard him.

What I want is a reading of Inherit the Wind through Galileo's actual position on scripture: where does the film line up with him and where is it making a different argument wearing his clothes? Concretely -- is Drummond arguing Galileo's case, or a twentieth-century free-speech / science-versus-religion case that just looks similar? Does Brady (or Rachel's father) ever say anything Galileo would have conceded, like the worry about a teacher presenting as settled fact something that isn't demonstrated, or about scandalizing ordinary believers -- I have a feeling Galileo would have had more sympathy for that complaint than the film does. The deck-stacking bugs me too: Brady becomes a buffoon, the town becomes a mob, and I'm told the real Bryan offered to pay Scopes's fine, so is the film cheating, and does it know it's cheating? And the last image, where Drummond ends up with the Bible and the Darwin book together, is that Galileo's picture or a different, later idea about the two not touching? I'd like the reading faithful in both directions -- don't reduce the film to an illustration of Galileo, and don't bend Galileo to fit the film; if the honest answer is that the Galileo lens mostly doesn't fit because the film is really about bullies and conformity, say so and tell me why. Point me at the scenes worth arguing about (the cross-examination over the days of creation and Joshua stopping the sun, Rachel on the stand, Hornbeck being a cynic, the Golden Dancer bit) -- no quotes or reading list needed, we're just going to hash it out over beers.
