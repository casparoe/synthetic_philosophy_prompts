I'm a PhD student in the history of economic thought, and I keep bumping into Berkeley's monetary theory in the margins of stuff I read for my dissertation. The gist, as far as I can tell: wealth really comes from labour and land, money matters only insofar as it stirs up industry and circulates credit, and the true idea of money is basically that of a ticket or counter — he actually asks "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" and somewhere says less money swiftly circulating is equivalent to more money slowly circulating.

What I want is to actually use that as a lens on a real episode: the South Sea Bubble and the crash of 1720. Not the standard financial-history telling, I already know that. I want the mapping. Who are the players in Berkeley's terms, what's actually doing the work at each stage (credit? fashion and appetite? industry? mere opinion?), and what does the bubble look like if you take the ticket/counter view seriously instead of treating the stock as a claim on real assets. Then the part I care about most: where does the lens break? His whole move seems to be that the metallic content of money is nearly irrelevant and that what matters is circulation, but 1720 was pretty clearly a crisis of trust in paper and in the state's backing of it — can his framework even register that as a real problem, or does it have to write the panic off as confusion? And if it can't handle it, does some other framework do better here, or is this a piece Berkeley should have had and just didn't? Argue it out, don't just list parallels.
