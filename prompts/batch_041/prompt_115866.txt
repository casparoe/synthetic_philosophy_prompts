I am a software engineer by background, with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to contribute one question to the final examination of a graduate module on the foundations of probabilistic reasoning, whose students come mostly from statistics, computer science, and economics. I want the question to be on the reference class problem, but I do not want it to be answerable by reciting the familiar observation that any frequency judgment presupposes a choice of class. I want it to force a student to say why the choice cannot be made either by "use the largest class for which we have data" or by "use the most specific class possible," and to say what a genuine resolution would have to accomplish.

Here is the scenario I want the question built on, stated in the terms I would naturally use. A hospital runs a model that, for each incoming patient, reports a number it calls the patient's probability of developing a particular complication within thirty days. For one particular patient the model reports 0.30. Three of my colleagues dispute what that number is a probability of. The first says it is the frequency of the complication among the patients the model was trained on, that being the broadest class for which we have data. The second says the only defensible class is the one matching everything known about this patient, including the variables the model never saw; taken seriously, that class has a single member, so the relevant frequency is either 0 or 1 and we do not know which. The third says the number is well defined only relative to the model's own output: among all the cases on which the model reported 0.30, thirty per cent developed the complication, so the number is a fact about the model rather than about the patient. I want the question to make the student show why each of the three positions is both defensible and unsatisfying, and to decide whether the three are disagreeing about the world or about our evidence.

The question must require the student to do the following.

1. State the general problem precisely enough to make clear why it arises for any probability assigned to a single case, and not only for frequencies.
2. Explain why "use the broadest class for which there is data" and "use the most specific class" are each defective, and say what each of them gets right. I gather from a colleague that the crux is the requirement that a class be homogeneous, in the sense that no further subdivision should change the frequency, and I want the student to work out what happens to that requirement as the class is narrowed toward the single case.
3. Say whether the problem is an artefact of frequentist interpretations or survives when the 0.30 is read as a rational degree of belief, and defend the answer.
4. Distinguish the problem from the base-rate fallacy, and from the separate claim that some events are objectively chancy.
5. Take a stand on what a satisfactory resolution would have to accomplish, and on whether the hospital's 0.30 can be defended at all. As a test of whether the student has actually committed rather than hedged, include a final part: if the hospital adds a variable and the reported number for this patient moves from 0.30 to 0.28, has the probability changed or has the estimate changed? The student must answer rather than sit on the fence.

The question should be answerable in about forty-five minutes. Please produce three things: the examination question itself; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty-five marks that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, with a one-line note for each part on how students typically get it wrong. Please keep the marking scheme about the moves rather than about the literature, since I do not want students rewarded for dropping names that I could not fairly police myself.
