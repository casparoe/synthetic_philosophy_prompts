I run the lessons-learned desk for a state health department's outbreak response unit — I'm the person who writes the after-action report for every significant outbreak or weather emergency we respond to, which works out to five or six a year, each one about a day and a half of writing, and every one of them goes into a public database that feeds into our guidance documents and next year's budget lines. So a causal claim in one of these reports has a way of hardening into policy, and a reporter once read a sentence out of a four-year-old report of mine back to me on the phone, which was not a good day. The trouble is that every report I write comes out as a story: three or four hinge moments, somebody who dropped the ball or somebody who saw it coming, and by the last page the whole thing looks like it was always going to unfold the way it did. A retired historian who sits on our advisory board keeps telling me my reports are hindsight dressed up as analysis and that the same archive could support three different stories depending on where I decide the interesting part starts. I think he's right and I don't know what the alternative is. I understand the two things he's gesturing at well enough — hindsight bias, where knowing how it turned out makes that outcome feel like it was the likely one all along and quietly erases how uncertain everyone actually was in the middle of it, and the narrative fallacy, where we squash a messy sequence into a tidy story with a couple of causes and a moral, and then mistake the story's coherence for evidence about the world. What I don't have is anything to do on Tuesday.

So: can you turn this into a procedure I can actually run on a draft, in order, over the course of an afternoon? I want the sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and says there is no causal finding here rather than a weak one, and the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or of what the department is willing to say in public. It has to survive the specific things that go wrong in my drafts. The timeline: I write it knowing the ending, and the actors didn't. The descriptions: the "first case" is only the first case because of how things turned out, and nobody in the room could have been thinking in those terms on the day. The counterfactuals: I write sentences like "had the exclusion order come on day three instead of day nine, this would have been a twelve-case outbreak," and I have no idea what license I have to write that, or what I'd have to be able to show for it. The cast: whether to name people, and how to keep the report from needing a villain. And the compression: I always end up with one or two causes because that's what makes it readable, when I suspect the honest picture is a net with several paths to the same outcome. Also please tell me which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of method, and what the procedure simply can't do — I'd rather know where it misfires than have it paper over the gap.

Then run it, step by step, on three drafts I'm actually sitting on. (1) A measles outbreak, 43 confirmed cases over eleven weeks, seeded by a seven-year-old returning from travel, in a county where the affected elementary school had about 84% MMR coverage; the county health officer ordered unvaccinated students excluded on day nine, and my draft says the order came six days late and would have capped the outbreak at twelve cases — that twelve is a number the epi lead produced on the back of an envelope in a meeting and I've never been able to reconstruct it. Two families also refused to cooperate with contact tracing, and the draft has quietly made them the hinge of the whole thing. (2) A four-day heat event in a metro area of about 900,000, with six excess deaths identified after the fact, where we pushed roughly 40,000 text alerts to people on a heat-buddy registry; my draft credits the alert system with averting an estimated 25 to 30 deaths, by comparison with a neighboring metro that didn't send alerts but also has different housing stock, much higher air-conditioning penetration, and a hospital labor dispute running that week. The director loves that finding, obviously. (3) A norovirus outbreak at a long-term care facility where, once I strip out the story, I think the only defensible claim is that the place was short-staffed in a way that everyone already knew about, and the director is going to want a lesson out of it anyway. I'd rather you tell me honestly which of these three the procedure returns nothing useful for.
