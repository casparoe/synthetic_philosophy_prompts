For the past several months I have been sitting in on a hospital ethics committee as an observer, part of a fellowship year I took away from my dissertation, and the case that keeps returning is one a colleague put to us almost casually: a couple who have already lost one child to a degenerative condition, deciding whether to have another, knowing there is a substantial chance the second child inherits it. If the child comes, it will have a life worth living, and it will also have a life considerably worse than the one it might have had without the condition — and of course it will be a different child from the one they would have had otherwise, so there is no one to compare. I knew the shape of this from Parfit, and I had taught the standard cases more than once, but I had never sat in a room where the shape mattered to actual people. Which is a long way of saying that I have gone back to Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go*, and I am now fairly convinced it is the most sustained treatment of the nonidentity problem in fiction — which is strange, because the novel is not about reproduction at all.

I am running a session on it for a deliberately mixed group — a few philosophers, a handful of clinicians, and some literature people who will be annoyed if we treat the book as a case study — and I would like a reading of the novel through the nonidentity problem specifically, rather than the general ethics-of-cloning reading that everyone reaches for. I want to know which scenes carry the weight and why: Kathy's flat, retrospective narration; the rumour about deferrals that the students never quite stop believing; the discovery, near the end, that there were never any deferrals and that the art the students made as children was collected to answer a question about whether they had souls; the students' search for their "possibles"; the way Tommy's and Kathy's late, brief happiness is sandwiched between donations. My rough hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the novel's whole machinery of souls, art, and love is a way of refusing the nonidentity problem's central move — it keeps insisting on something about these particular people that survives the observation that they are not worse off than no one at all — and I want to know whether that insistence is a philosophical error, a genuine alternative to the person-affecting framework, or simply a different question being asked.

Two things I would like the reading to take seriously, because I think they are where the interest is. First, the nonidentity problem blocks the complaint that the donors are harmed by having been brought into existence, but it does nothing at all to block the complaint that they are used as mere means, and I want you to say whether the novel knows this or quietly trades on the confusion — whether its moral force comes from the harm it cannot establish or from the rights it can. Second, I would like you to test how much of the novel's force survives a modified case, which you should take as stipulated rather than as something to look up: suppose the donations were entirely painless, suppose the donors lived comfortably to fifty, and suppose they were never deceived about their fate — does the novel's indictment still stand, and if so, on what? Where the book resists the nonidentity reading, please say so plainly rather than forcing it; I would rather have a reading that is honest about the friction than one that is elegant. Close by telling me what the novel supplies that Parfit's cases do not — I suspect it is something about time, and about what it is like to be the person whose existence is the thing under discussion — and give me two or three scenes I can put in front of the clinicians, who will otherwise take the whole thing as an argument about policy. No citations or reading lists, please; I know the literature well enough, and I want your reading of the book, not a survey of who has written about it.
