"Nietzsche's Genealogy is a debunking project in the sense that he wants to show our moral values didn't come from where we assume they came from. Rather than being handed down by God or discovered by reason, they grew out of very human, and often pretty ugly, drives.

In the First Essay he separates master morality from slave morality. The masters, the strong and noble, called their own way of life 'good' and everything weak and common 'bad'. The slaves, who couldn't act out their hostility directly, took an imaginary revenge: they inverted the masters' valuation and called the masters 'evil', and then called themselves 'good' by contrast. This is the slave revolt in morals. It's driven by ressentiment, and it's where the kind of morality we still live with — pity, equality, the worth of the suffering — comes from.

So what gets debunked is slave/Christian morality. Once you see that 'evil' was just a projection of the resentment of people who couldn't get what they wanted, you can't take those values at face value anymore. Nietzsche's point is that they aren't true, they're a symptom.

The Second Essay carries the same move into our inner life. The bad conscience is basically guilt. Once people were locked into societies and couldn't discharge their aggressive instincts outward anymore, the instincts turned inward and they started to feel bad about themselves. Nietzsche thinks this is what made us a sick animal, but also what made us capable of responsibility, of memory, and of making promises — the sovereign individual who can stand surety for himself. So the debunking reading does carry over: the moral feelings we're proudest of are the same aggressive instincts, just turned inward."
