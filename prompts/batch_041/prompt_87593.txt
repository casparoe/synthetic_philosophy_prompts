So I'm a novelist by trade but I teach a small upper-level seminar on the side, and I'm putting together the take-home final for the unit that always goes over well: choice when you've got no reason to prefer either option, the donkey between two identical hay bales. My students can all recite "Buridan's ass" and then mumble something about the will being free to break the tie, or about it starving, and I can't stand it.

I want one essay question that makes them earn the grade. What I want them to see is that the starving-donkey version is a trap — if the agent genuinely can't act, the puzzle isn't about freedom, it's about what it would even mean for two reasons to be exactly equal. So build the question around a case of my devising (invent one, dont reuse the donkey) where the two options match on every first-order consideration but the agent can adopt a tie-breaking procedure — going with whichever option is on the left, flipping a coin, whatever. The question should force them to say whether adopting such a procedure is itself a reason, and whether that dissolves the puzzle or just relocates it. It should be unanswerable by reciting anything about Buridan, and it should seperate the people who've understood what "no reason to prefer" is doing from the people who've memorized the label.

Then give me a model answer of about 500 words, and a marking scheme that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that cost marks — I want at least one deduction for "the will is free so it just picks," and one for treating the tie-break rule as if it were a substantive reason without argument.

Upper-level seminar, take-home, no time limit, 20 marks. And in the model answer have it say outright where it's least sure of itself — which argument or consideration would flip its verdict on whether the tie-break procedure actually resolves the case rather than just moving the bump in the rug.
