I'm a graduate student in philosophy of physics, and I'm writing a short essay for a seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics. The seminar has spent several weeks on the measurement problem, and I've become convinced that the most interesting objection to the Everettian or many-worlds interpretation is not the familiar complaint about empirical adequacy but a prior worry: whether probability claims can mean anything at all if every outcome of a measurement is realized. I've drafted an essay that tries to meet this worry head-on, and I'd like you to comment on it — specifically on whether my reconstruction of the objection is fair, whether the central move I make is sound, and whether the essay is worth developing further. My draft is below.

Most discussions of probability in the Everettian interpretation, as I understand them, present the interpreter with a dilemma. Either the branching structure of the wavefunction is a genuine, observer-independent fact about the world — in which case there is a definite fact about how many successor branches a measurement produces — or it is not, in which case the interpretation has not yet said what a probability claim is about. If the first horn is taken, the Everettian owes an account of why the amplitudes attached to branches should be read as probabilities at all; since every branch is realized, the natural thought that a probability measures the chance that some outcome obtains has no purchase, and simply stipulating that the squared amplitude is the probability looks like an unexplained addition to the theory. If the second horn is taken, the interpreter seems to have abandoned the realist reading that made the interpretation attractive in the first place.

The decision-theoretic response, associated with Deutsch and Wallace, answers the first horn by denying that Everettian probability claims are descriptive. On this view, to say that a measurement outcome has probability p is not to say anything about the world's branching structure; it is to say something about how a rational agent who accepts the theory would bet, and the Born rule is then recovered as a theorem about the preferences of such an agent given certain axioms of practical rationality. I find this the most serious response on offer, but I think it has a cost that its proponents understate. The derivations need a principle to the effect that an agent should be indifferent between branches of equal amplitude, or that branching itself should not be treated as a source of uncertainty; and I cannot see how to motivate such a principle except by appealing to the very probabilistic intuitions the derivation was supposed to ground. The derivation, in other words, seems to take in at the axioms what it pays out at the theorem.

I want to propose a different diagnosis. Consider what I will call the ledger constraint. Suppose an agent knows the pre-measurement state of a system and knows that she is about to be branched into several successors, and let her have credences over which successor she will be. I propose that these credences are rationally permissible only if they satisfy the following condition: the agent's expectation of the value of any bet, computed before the measurement using her credences, must equal the average, over her successors, of the value that each successor would assign to that same bet after the measurement. The constraint is meant to express a mundane thought — that your future selves are you, so your present and future evaluations of a gamble cannot come apart in an unprincipled way. But when I work through it, the constraint forces the agent to assign equal credence to every successor, regardless of amplitudes. That is, the ledger constraint entails branch counting, and branch counting is empirically wrong: it does not reproduce the Born statistics that quantum mechanics has been confirmed to give. So a constraint that looks like a platitude about personal identity and rational consistency conflicts with the interpretation's empirical success. My resolution is to reject the ledger constraint, and to argue that its intuitive appeal rests on a mistake about the kind of thing Everettian credence is. If the constraint is right, credence is a subjective expectation over an ensemble of successors, and the ensemble has no natural weighting; if instead Everettian credence is a decision-theoretic weighting of the kind the Deutsch-Wallace program describes, then the averaging step in the ledger constraint is simply invalid and its branch-counting conclusion never gets off the ground. On this reading the incoherence objection dissolves: it presupposes that probability in the many-worlds must describe a distribution over branches, and once that presupposition is dropped the objection has no target. I concede that this leaves the Born rule's status closer to that of a norm of practical consistency than to that of a prediction, and I am unsure whether that is a virtue or an evasion.

I'd be grateful for comments of the following kinds. First, on accuracy: is my reconstruction of the dilemma fair to the Everettian, or have I set up a position no one actually holds? In particular, is the claim that the decision-theoretic derivations must assume branch indifference at the outset correct, or is that a caricature? Second, on the central argument: does the ledger constraint really entail branch counting, and if so, is rejecting it the right response, or is there a better-motivated version of the constraint that avoids the conclusion? Third, on the overall shape: does my resolution actually engage the incoherence objection, or does it merely relabel the problem by calling the Born rule a norm rather than a probability? Fourth, and more impressionistically, is the essay interesting, or does it re-tread familiar ground in unfamiliar vocabulary? If you think the argument can be salvaged, I'd like concrete suggestions for tightening it; if you think it collapses, I'd like to know precisely which step fails.
