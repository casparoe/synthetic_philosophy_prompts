I chair a regional parole board in a jurisdiction that has just revised its release criteria. The case before us on Thursday concerns a man serving a sentence for a serious assault; on paper his institutional record is the strongest we have seen — no infractions in nine years, every mandated programme completed at the top of his cohort. What troubles us is the independent psychologist's addendum. She does not dispute any of the record; she argues that it shows cue-responsiveness rather than reason-responsiveness. His conduct, she says, tracks the prison's script with great fidelity and collapses whenever that script is perturbed: on two occasions a trivial change in routine produced a disproportionate reaction before he recovered. She borrows a term from the ethologists and calls his behaviour sphexish, after the digger wasp that completes a fixed sequence of movements but cannot cope if the sequence is interrupted. Her conclusion is that he is not reformed but conditioned, and that releasing him would be a category error.

Half the board thinks this is sophistry. The man has done everything asked of him, and the demand that he also be flexible is one we can neither measure nor fairly impose on someone whom nine years of institutional routine have themselves made rigid. The other half treats the report as decisive. Please draft the written opinion I shall circulate before the hearing. It should recommend a concrete course of action — grant, refuse, or defer with specific conditions and a date for review — and give reasons the board can act on. In doing so, confront the hardest version of the objection to the psychologist's view: that any bounded agent is sphexish relative to some perturbation, so that flexibility is a matter of degree and of level rather than a property one either has or lacks. Say plainly which perturbations you think the decision should turn on, and why.
