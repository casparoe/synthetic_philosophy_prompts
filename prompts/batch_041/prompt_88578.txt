I teach a graduate seminar on autonomy, influence, and the ethics of manipulation, and I want to close the unit with a session built around Christopher Nolan's Inception — the 2010 film in which a team enters a sleeping target's dreams in order to plant an idea in his mind rather than to steal one. My students will have read the usual material, which distinguishes persuasion (influence that addresses a person's reasons and leaves them free to assess it) from manipulation (influence that works around those reasons, or subverts them, or hides the influencer's intent). I want the film to stress-test that distinction rather than decorate it. Please act as a philosopher working at the intersection of moral philosophy and film criticism, and give me a reading of the film through the manipulation-versus-persuasion debate. Begin by laying out for yourself the competing criteria that are actually on offer — influence that bypasses or bypasses-by-exploiting rational agency; influence that is covert about the influencer's intention; influence that installs a preference the target would not endorse on reflection; influence that leverages a specific vulnerability such as grief or filial guilt — and then decide, carefully, which of them the film is genuinely working with and which it merely gestures at. The pivot for me is Cobb's stated rule for inception, that the planted idea must be simple and must be experienced by the mark as his own. Press hard on that rule. Is it a criterion of non-manipulation or only of effective manipulation? Robert Fischer's decision to break up his father's company is one he appears to own and would plausibly endorse, yet he is deceived about nearly everything that produced it; the film seems to treat the ownership as what redeems the act and the deception as what damns it, and I am not persuaded those two commitments can be held together.

You will need to choose the scenes that carry the reading and be explicit about why they carry it: the hospital room and the safe in the snow-fortress level, where the forged memory of the father's wish does its work; the earlier confidence trick in which Fischer is persuaded of a false frame about his own situation; and above all the backstory, in which Cobb performed inception on Mal, for her sake as he tells it, with the result that she carried the implanted belief out of the dream and died for it. That last case looks to me like the film's real argument — that a manipulation can be paternalistically motivated, sincerely intended, and still dismantle the person it was meant to save, and that the planted idea outlives any control the planter has over it — but I want you to tell me whether I am over-reading. Where the film allows Ariadne to voice misgivings, take those seriously as the film's internal ethical position rather than as dialogue to be paraphrased. The reading should be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of a taxonomy, and do not bend the taxonomy to flatter the film. Where does the film's contrast between planting an idea and merely telling someone something track a real fault line in the philosophical literature, and where does it rest on a confusion — for instance, does the film ever entertain the possibility of manipulation that operates through the target's reasons, or is its imagination of influence confined to deception and emotional exploitation? End by saying what the film contributes that the arguments do not: my working suspicion is that it dramatizes the persistence and self-concealment of installed beliefs in a way the analytic literature handles too cleanly, but if the film is finally more interested in the metaphysics of reality than in the ethics of influence, say so plainly, and tell me which scenes I should still use and which I should warn my students not to over-read.
