I've spent a long time in the rationalist corner of the internet, which has left me with two habits I now want to apply to a case that has been bothering me: the habit of asking what a claim is actually tracking, and the habit of asking what to do given that I don't know. Assume I have no way to look anything up, so take the facts below as the whole record, and don't bother with a reading list. I want the argument, not the sources.

The case is a real policy decision, and a recent one. In 2021 the UK government published a review it had commissioned, led by Jonathan Birch and a team at the London School of Economics, on the evidence for sentience in two invertebrate groups: cephalopod molluscs (octopus, squid, cuttlefish) and decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, crayfish, prawns). The review did not look for a single marker. It graded candidate animals against a checklist of indicators — nociceptors and a central nervous system that integrates their signals; responses to noxious stimuli that outrun simple reflex; modulation of those responses by analgesics or anaesthetics; motivational trade-offs, in which the animal forgoes something it otherwise wants in order to avoid the noxious stimulus; and flexible, context-sensitive behaviour that is hard to explain without something like pain. It concluded that the evidence for both groups was strong enough to treat them as sentient. In 2022 Parliament passed the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, which covers all vertebrates and, after amendment, also cephalopods and decapods; the upshot is that these invertebrates fall within the remit of a statutory committee on animal sentience, and questions that were previously open — how to kill a crab, whether boiling a lobster is acceptable, what humane transport for a squid would even look like — become matters of policy.

Now the lens, which I want you to refine rather than accept. I hold three computational criteria for moral status, in a crude form:

1. Global workspace. A state matters if it is broadcast within a limited-capacity system that makes it available at once to many downstream processes — report, memory, planning, motor control.

2. Higher-order representation. A state matters if the system represents that state to itself in the right way; first-order processing, however sophisticated, is not sufficient.

3. Integration or recurrence. What matters is a certain causal structure: densely recurrent, mutually constraining loops, or, on the integrated-information line, a quantity measuring how much the system's state as a whole constrains its own past and future.

What I want from you:

First, the mapping. For each criterion, say what it would look for in an octopus and in a crab, and what the evidence above does and does not speak to. Be concrete about the anatomy-to-computation translation: the octopus's nervous system is large and distributed, with a substantial share of its neurons in the arms rather than the central brain, while the crab's is far smaller and more centrally organized around a fused ventral nerve cord. Say what each of those facts is evidence for under each criterion, and where a criterion has nothing to say about them.

Second, the divergence. I expect the three criteria to disagree about at least one of the two groups. Locate the disagreement precisely: is it a disagreement about the animal, or about what the criterion is measuring? If two criteria diverge and no experiment can settle it, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over.

Third, the uncomfortable part. The legislation adopted none of these criteria. It adopted something much closer to a precautionary attribution rule: where the evidence is strong enough, extend protection, and don't wait for a theory of what sentience is. Say what that does to my original question. Is the computational question doing any work in the actual decision, or is it downstream of a decision-theoretic one — what to do under moral uncertainty about a creature you cannot interrogate? If it is downstream, does the computational criterion survive as anything more than a way of organizing verdicts we had already reached?

Fourth, the case testing the theory. Which criterion comes out of this episode looking worse, and why? Steelman the loser rather than convicting it quickly. Consider whether a criterion that implies crabs do not matter but octopuses do is nearly unfalsifiable in practice, given that we cannot measure integration in a crab, cannot rule out a workspace in a crab, and cannot run the decisive experiment on either. If that is the position the criteria are in, does the case support treating moral status as a threshold on a computation at all, or does it support treating the computation talk as rationalization of a stance taken for other reasons?

Fifth, the limits. Say where the framework is silent: what the criteria tell us about a creature that sits in the middle of the scale, whether the question of the boundary is even the right question as against the question of how much weight partial cases get, and what these theories simply do not address. End with one short paragraph on what your answer commits you to about substrate-neutral criteria and artificial systems, since the criteria as stated do not care what the system is made of.

Be disciplined about who the actors were and what they were choosing — the review team, the minister, the animal welfare organizations, the fishing and aquaculture industries — and keep the science and the ethics from sliding into each other. Where a step depends on an empirical claim I have not given you, mark it as an assumption rather than asserting it.
