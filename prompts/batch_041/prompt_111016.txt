Last night a colleague and I argued for three hours about what people call the alignment tax and the competitiveness constraint, and we kept talking past each other. My position was that a tax is only a tax if you are the only one paying it, so the whole picture changes once safety work becomes a public good that everyone can draw on; hers was that any lab that unilaterally slows down simply hands the race to a lab that does not, and that this makes the honest advice to a safety-conscious actor either naive or self-defeating. We never converged, and I suspect we were each missing part of the structure. I would rather have a group feel that structure than hear it summarized, so I want to build a game around it.

The setting is a workshop-style reading group on AI governance that I facilitate: fourteen participants, ninety minutes, no prior technical background assumed, and no lecture at the front. What I want them to come away seeing is that the severity of the competitiveness constraint is not a fixed fact about the world but a function of a few structural features — whether the prize for leading is winner-take-all or shared, whether safety knowledge leaks or is hoarded, whether the bad outcome is correlated across actors (so that one actor's neglect endangers everyone) or idiosyncratic, and whether anyone can cheaply verify a rival's claims about its own safety spending. I want the participants to discover, in the playing, that the individually rational move depends on all of these at once, and that the intuitive verdicts they walk in with — that restraint is obviously right, or obviously suicidal — both dissolve.

Design a runnable game with rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, and a structured debrief. Please make the mechanics carry the ideas rather than the facilitator's narration. The game should be arranged so that the participants run into the problem themselves: for instance, let them spend the first couple of rounds believing that safety is a private cost they can choose to pay, and then let the consequences of one team's choice land on everyone. A few features I would like built in, and I am open to your judgment on how: a private prize for the team that finishes ahead, so that safety spending has an opportunity cost in the race; a bad outcome whose probability depends on the weakest safety level across the teams, so that safety behaves partly like a weakest-link public good; a lagged spillover by which safety techniques discovered by one team later lower every team's cost, so that the tax decays over time and hoarding versus sharing becomes a live strategic choice; and an optional auditor role that can, at some cost, verify after the fact whether a team's safety claims were truthful, so that cheap talk about safety is possible and the verification problem shows up in play. Keep it runnable in ninety minutes with a facilitator who has never run it before.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the ideas: where the opportunity cost of safety was felt, where the weakest-link rule made one team's neglect everyone's problem, where the lagged spillover changed what looked rational, and where the auditor's limits reproduced the difference between a claim being true and a claim being credible. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — including what to do if the random bad outcome fires early and ends the game before the interesting structure has emerged, and what to do if the group treats the game purely as a contest to win and resists the debrief. Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce and what I therefore have to supply myself: the real uncertainty about how fast capability is advancing and whether advancing it is itself the danger; the possibility of a single decisive move rather than a race; the fact that in reality the tax is not a fixed price list but a contested and possibly reducible quantity; and the moral weight of the bad outcome, which a die roll cannot carry. End with two or three discussion questions I can put to the group once the game is over, aimed at the gap between what the game made obvious and what the real situation leaves undecided.
