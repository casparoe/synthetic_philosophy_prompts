Last night I got into a long, increasingly heated argument with a friend who has turned into a gold-standard absolutist: he insists that anything worthy of the name 'money' must be a commodity with intrinsic value, that banknotes and credit are at best IOUs for metal, and that any unbacked currency is a fraud waiting to collapse. Trying to hold my ground, I cited George Berkeley — the idealist philosopher, of all people, who in The Querist, a sequence of pointed questions about Irish poverty and trade first published in 1735, asked whether 'the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter.' My friend shrugged it off, and honestly I couldn't defend the line, because I don't know what it actually commits you to. So I'd like the possibilities spelled out rather than settled in one stroke. What are the distinct, precise claims that 'money is a ticket or counter' could be making, ranging from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical? A few I can imagine: money is a mere conventional symbol; money has no intrinsic value, only exchange value; money is a claim on real goods rather than a good in its own right; any token whatever will do provided people trust and circulate it; 'wealth' properly denotes the produce of land and labour, so the whole mercantilist passion for hoarding bullion rests on a confusion; or, more dramatically, money is a collectively sustained fiction. Lay these out as a menu and tell me, for each, whether it is defensible, what it would take to refute it, and what argumentative work it could actually do.

Two further things. First, The Querist is written entirely in interrogative form, so it's not obvious Berkeley is asserting any of this at all; I'd like you to say whether that hedging matters for how much weight the line can bear. Second — and this is what I really want out of it — tell me which reading my friend and I were each tacitly relying on, and which reading, if any, would actually settle the question of whether gold is the only real money; I suspect the slogan gets invoked to close arguments it doesn't come close to closing. Finally, please be careful to separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the history of monetary thought, and don't hand me a contested reading of Berkeley as though it were the consensus view.
