Hey, I'm putting together a seminar paper on Maimonides on animal sacrifice and I want you to tear into my draft before my advisor does. Here's the gist, more or less in order.

Thesis: in the Guide, the chapter where he gives the reasons for the commandments (III:32), Maimonides treats the whole sacrificial apparatus as a concession. His picture: the Israelites were steeped in the general ancient practice of worshipping through animal sacrifice in temples, with incense and prostration, and rather than command them to give it all up cold turkey — which he says would be like asking people today to worship God only in thought and to give up praying, fasting and petitioning — God let them keep the form and transferred it to Himself. He even compares it to the gradual hardening of the nerves and to weaning an infant on milk, which I think is doing real work: the point is a general principle about human nature not jumping between extremes, not a one-off excuse.

Then I argue three things. (1) The concession isn't ad hoc; it's the same gradualism that runs through his theory of the commandments, where the law aims at improving the body as a means to improving the soul. (2) It's consistent with him being a halakhist who codifies the sacrificial laws in detail, because for him a commandment's reason and its binding force are seperate things — the concession explains why these particular laws were given in this form, not why they obligate. (3) The standard objection, that this makes the Torah hostage to a passing historical circumstance and so contradicts the principle that the Torah is immutable, misses the target, since immutability is about not being added to or abrogated, not about every commandment being intrinsically valuable.

The objection I'm most worried about is Nachmanides, in his commentary on Leviticus. He basically says Maimonides is slapping a bandage on a serious wound: the Torah calls the offerings a fire-offering and a pleasing odor, which sounds like they matter in themselves, and he points out that Noah and Abel brought offerings long before anyone worshipped idols, so the cure-for-idolatry story can't be the reason. He prefers an account where the offerings mirror the thought, speech and action of the worshipper.

What I want from you: tell me where I'm misreading the texts, especially whether III:32 really supports the gradualism reading or whether I'm importing it from the animal-development stuff that preceeds it. Is my reply to the immutability objection an answer or a dodge? Is Nachmanides' Noah and Abel point decisive, or is it a category mistake — maybe Maimonides only needs the concession to explain the specific forms (why these species, this temple) rather than the institution of sacrifice as such? And if the sacrifices are purely a pedagogical concession, how do I handle the fact that he still seems to expect them restored in the messianic era — does that not make a nonsense of calling them dispensable? Don't worry about citations, I've got the texts in front of me, I just want to know where the arguement is soft.
