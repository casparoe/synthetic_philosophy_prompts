My mate did a philsophy of biology module last year and now won't shut up about it. His pitch: take a bee doing its waggle dance. There's a perfectly good reason it dances — it sends the others to the good flowers, which keeps the hive fed — and you can write that reason out as a proper if-then arguement. But nobody has to actually run through it. The reason does its work without being anyone's reason, and he calls these 'free-floating rationales'. Fine so far, I think. Then he goes further: since the rationale doesn't need a mind, he reckons 'wants' and 'believes' are only shorthand for describing behaviour, convenient and nothing more, so there's no real fact of the matter about whether anything believes anything — not us, and definitely not a chatbot, which is the same sort of thing as the bee except we built it. And when I say a reason has to belong to a reasoner, he says I've missed the point, which feels like a bit of a cheat. Can you go through it: which bits would someone who actually works on this accept, which are common oversimplifications, what would a defender of the idea say he's got wrong, and does the conclusion about us and chatbots actually follow from the bee stuff?
