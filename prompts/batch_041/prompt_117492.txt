I am the head teaching assistant for a 300-level course on the foundations of quantum mechanics, cross-listed between the physics and philosophy departments, and I am working my way through the long-answer section of this term's final exam. The question below was the capstone item, worth twenty of the exam's hundred points; students were instructed to answer in at most one page and to be explicit about every assumption they invoked. It reads:

"Bell's theorem is often summarized loosely as showing that quantum mechanics is incompatible with local hidden variables. (a) State carefully the assumptions that go into the derivation of a Bell inequality, distinguishing those that are strictly required from those that are sometimes assumed but can be dropped. (b) What do the experimental violations of Bell inequalities — including the so-called loophole-free experiments of recent years — actually establish about the world? In particular, do those experiments close off every route by which a defender of local causality might still escape the conclusion? (c) A classmate objects that Bohmian mechanics is a counterexample to the theorem, since it is a hidden-variable theory that reproduces the quantum predictions. Evaluate the objection."

The answer I am now holding, transcribed exactly as written, is:

"(a) The derivation requires three assumptions. First, locality: no influence can propagate faster than light. Second, realism: every observable has a definite value before it is measured (these are the hidden variables). Third, determinism: the hidden variables evolve deterministically. From these, one derives an inequality — the CHSH inequality, which generalizes Bell's original 1964 result — that every such theory must satisfy. (b) Quantum mechanics predicts, and experiment confirms, correlations that violate this inequality, first in the Aspect experiments and more recently in the loophole-free tests. These results show that the world is genuinely nonlocal: there are influences between distant events that outrun any light-speed signal. The loophole-free experiments close the detection and locality loopholes, and with them the last escape route available to a defender of local causality, including the free-choice, or superdeterminism, loophole. It is worth adding that these influences cannot be used to send signals faster than light, so special relativity is not threatened. (c) The objection fails. Bohmian mechanics is indeed a hidden-variable theory, but it is explicitly nonlocal — the guiding equation depends instantaneously on the positions of all the particles at once. It therefore does not reproduce the quantum predictions while remaining local; it is exactly the kind of nonlocal theory the theorem leaves standing. So it is not a counterexample to the claim that no local hidden-variable theory reproduces the quantum predictions."
