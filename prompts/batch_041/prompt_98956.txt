I'm teaching a grad seminar this term on the Scottish Enlightenment and its economic and political afterlife, and last week a colleague from the econ department sat in and gave the class an impromptu little summary of Smith on empire that I've been chewing on ever since. He said, roughly: Smith was the great theorist of the free market, and everything he said about colonies is just that doctrine applied. On his view the empire was an economic dead end for Britain -- the whole project was a mercantilist scheme cooked up to enrich a small ring of merchants and planters at the expense of the general public, and Smith's own numbers showed Britain would have been better off simply letting the American colonies go. And since he held that the state has no business meddling in trade, he obviously regarded things like the Navigation Acts as a flat contradiction of his principles. So Smith was basically an anti-imperialist free-trader ahead of his time, the kind of guy who'd have cheered decolonization on principle, and the invisible hand shows that free trade between Britain and America would have enriched everyone without the empire at all. I nodded along in the moment, but I have a nagging feeling several of these claims are doing something a bit to tidy with a thinker who's messier than that. I'm not a Smith specialist and I don't want to mislead the students in either direction.

Could you audit it for me? Go through it clause by clause and tell me what a careful reader of Smith would accept, what's a real oversimplification, and what's just wrong -- and in particular whether that last move, the inference from Smith's economics to the conclusion that he'd have welcomed the colonies going their own way, actually follows from anything he held. The students have read the relevant parts of the Wealth of Nations, so feel free to get into the weeds about what he really argues concerning colonial trade and what he proposes should actually be done about it, and about how central the invisible hand really is to that argument. Also, if part of the muddle is mine rather than my colleague's, say so -- I'd much rather have the corrections than a tidy summary.
