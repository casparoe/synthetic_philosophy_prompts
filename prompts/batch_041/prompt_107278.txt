I have a colleague who works on intentional-action judgments — specifically on the side-effect asymmetry, the tendency to call a foreseen side effect intentional when it is bad but not when it is good — and I have been keeping a record of the verdicts she gives when I put cases to her. Each case describes an agent who, while pursuing some goal, brings about a foreseen side effect; I ask her one question only, namely whether the agent brought that side effect about intentionally. Her record so far is this:

1. A shipping director switches his fleet to a cheaper fuel, knowing that as a side effect it will raise sulfur emissions and worsen the asthma of children in a port town. He says the savings matter more and that he does not care about the asthma. Her verdict: he intentionally worsened the children's asthma.
2. The same director switches to a cheaper fuel, knowing that as a side effect it will cut sulfur emissions and improve those same children's asthma. He says he does not care about the asthma and cares only about the savings. Her verdict: he did not intentionally improve the children's asthma.
3. A landlord, to save money, shuts off the heating for a winter night, knowing that as a side effect it will give a tenant with a lung condition a bad night. He feels terrible about it but proceeds. Her verdict: he intentionally gave the tenant a bad night.
4. A landowner drains a ditch to raise the value of his field, knowing that as a side effect it will probably, though not certainly, shrink a downstream wetland. Her verdict: he intentionally shrank the wetland.
5. A manager ships software that, as a side effect, will slightly change the shade of the icons on users' screens. He does not care about the shade. Her verdict: he did not intentionally change the shade.

I am trying to recover the rule she is running, and I keep landing on two that fit the record equally well. On the harm rule, she ascribes intentionality exactly when the foreseen side effect is a harm or a bad state of affairs, as opposed to a benefit or something neutral. On the blame rule, she ascribes it exactly when the agent is blameworthy for the side effect — that is, when the effect is bad and the agent is not justified or excused. Rules I have already discarded include: she ascribes it whenever the agent is indifferent to the effect; whenever the effect was foreseen with certainty rather than merely as likely; and whenever the effect was foreseen at all.

Here is a case I have not yet put to her. A woman is attacked and uses force to save her life, knowing that as a side effect the force will break her assailant's arm.
