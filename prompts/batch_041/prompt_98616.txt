I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and I keep getting tangled up in the combination problem: I can follow the standard menu of objections, but I can never tell which of them is doing the real work and which are just restatements of one another. So I'd like you to construct a thought experiment to my specifications, and then convince me that it actually meets them. Here is what I need. The scenario should involve a small, fixed set of micro-subjects — three or four named parts, each with a simple, non-overlapping experience such as a patch of colour, a tone, or a pang — rather than a vague appeal to billions of particles. At the macro level, stipulate a single unified subject having one experience. Now the case has to make two features come apart. The first is whether the macro-experience contains any quality not present in any of the micro-experiences. The second is whether the number of subjects is conserved in the move from micro to macro. Engineer the scenario so that one of these comes out one way and the other comes out the other way — I am most interested in a case where the qualities combine without producing anything novel, yet the macro-subject is not recoverable by simply adding the micro-subjects up, but if you think the reverse pairing is the more revealing one, build that instead and tell me why you chose it.

Constraints: no quantum mechanics, no appeal to a sui generis bonding relation that is simply posited to do the work, no science-fictional machinery, and the scenario itself should fit in a single paragraph. Then do the demonstration. Say what constitutive panpsychism implies about the case and what cosmopsychism implies about it, showing exactly where they diverge and which feature of the case is responsible for the divergence. Tell me what happens if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into a standard illustration of the combination problem? Say how a committed constitutive panpsychist would try to defuse the case, whether by redescribing the micro-subjects, denying that the macro-subject must be composed of them, or something else.

And I don't want the verdicts smuggled into the setup: the two theories should disagree because of what they say, not because of how you have described the parts. To close, tell me which of the two features you take to be the genuine locus of the combination problem and which is a red herring, and why.
