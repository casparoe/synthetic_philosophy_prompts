I am a third-year student in a course on truth, relativism, and cross-cultural epistemology, and our unit on the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness has produced an argument that my study partner and I cannot get past. As we have reconstructed it, the doctrine (anekāntavāda) holds that every real thing has indefinitely many aspects, so that any statement about a thing is true only from a particular standpoint (a naya) and never captures it wholly; the associated logic of conditional predication (syādvāda) then says that in some respect a thing is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it both is and is not, and so on through its seven modes, each prefixed by a qualifier restricting it to a respect. The evident purpose of the machinery is polemical: it is meant to show that every rival philosophical view is one-sided — it mistakes a partial standpoint for the whole — while the many-sided view alone escapes the charge. Here is the argument we wrote out. (1) Every assertion is true only relative to a standpoint; taken by itself, no assertion states how things are without qualification. (2) Call an assertion one-sided when it advances a standpoint-relative claim as though it were unqualified. (3) Anekāntavāda asserts, as its central claim, that every assertion is one-sided. (4) Consider (3) itself. If it is asserted without qualification, then by (2) it is one-sided, and by (1) it is true only from a standpoint; but then its negation is also true from some standpoint, so it does not exclude the position that some assertions are true without qualification. (5) If, instead, (3) is advanced only from a standpoint, then it again fails to exclude that position, since it claims for itself only standpoint-relative status. (6) Either way, the doctrine cannot rule out the view that some claims are unqualifiedly true, and so cannot perform the polemical work it exists to perform. (7) Yet the tradition clearly takes itself to refute one-sided views and to be the correct account of truth. (8) Therefore the doctrine is either not correct, or not the thesis stated in (1) through (3). Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If something has to go, I would rather preserve the claim that the doctrine can be asserted as correct than preserve the claim that it silences every rival, but I want to see the bill.

I am also worried that the argument proves too much. The same shape seems to refute any theory that diagnoses an error in its opponents' partiality — a theory of ideology, say, or of motivated reasoning — since the diagnosis would apply to itself; and I find it hard to believe that every such theory is incoherent. If the flaw is in the general form rather than in anything specifically Jain, I would like to know which premise carries the flaw, why that premise looked so plausible, and what the alternatives are: for instance, whether the distinction the tradition draws between a mere standpoint (naya) and comprehensive knowledge (pramāṇa), or the claim that the doctrine is not itself a standpoint, is available to me, and what each of those moves costs. Please do not simply tell me that the doctrine is subtle; I want the diagnosis and the price.
