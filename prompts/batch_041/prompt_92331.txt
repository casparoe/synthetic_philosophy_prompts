You are a philosopher of mind and a formal poet. Write a fourteen-line Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of a researcher who has abandoned the Cartesian theater for the multiple drafts model but still finds himself writing as though there were a single place where it all comes together. The octave should stage the theater without irony: a screen, a darkened house, an audience of one, the light that begins the show. The volta should be his attempt to replace the audience with a distributed process, but the sestet must make that replacement feel like another seat in the same house; the turn fails, and the rhyme scheme should make the failure audible. Let the poem draw on the multiple drafts model's claim that content is fixed by parallel, revisable processes rather than by a central viewer, and on the distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revision, without resolving that distinction. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes the positions, not merely the labels. After the poem, add a brief prose note separating what is settled about the Cartesian theater critique and the multiple drafts model from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and flag where the poem takes liberties with the ideas. Do not supply citations or a reading list. Think carefully before drafting, and begin by brainstorming which features of the theater picture the sonnet must stage and which features of the drafts model can do structural work in the volta and the rhyme.
