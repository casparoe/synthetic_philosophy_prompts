I came to philosophy late, after forty years in a wet lab, so forgive the vocabulary. Please act as a philosopher who works on the ethics of aggregation — someone at home both with the 'separateness of persons' literature and with the clinical triage literature — and think carefully before you write. Start by spelling out in a few lines what the separateness-of-persons objection actually claims; I've never been sure whether it's just an objection to summing welfare across people or a deeper claim that persons are inviolable, and I suspect the two get run together by people who should know better.

Then take a real episode. In 2020 a number of health systems published crisis-standards-of-care protocols for allocating ventilators and ICU beds if demand outstripped supply. Most said, roughly: save the most lives; where that's a tie, save the most life-years. Some docked points for particular comorbidities or for poor predicted survival, and disability advocates objected very loudly indeed. I know the protocols were uneven and I'm not asking you to adjudicate any single document — treat the family of them as the case.

What I want: does the objection actually bite on a protocol like that, given that someone is going to be left to die whoever decides? Is the life-years part the same sin as the lives part, or a worse one — it looks to me like it's where the seperate-existences complaint has real teeth, since it puts a 30-year-old's expected decades and an 80-year-old's expected few onto one scale. Say something about the ex ante / ex post distinction here, because I keep getting tangled in it. And then the part I really care about: what does the episode show about the objection itself — does it survive contact with a forced choice among identified victims, or does it turn out to have been a point about ordinary, non-scarce cases all along? Be honest where the theory is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak.

British English, please. No reading lists or citations — I can't get online, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.
