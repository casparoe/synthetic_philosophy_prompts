Last week I sat in on a two-day workshop on the science of religion — I'm a reporter, I was there for a feature — and at the dinner afterward I ended up wedged between a cognitive anthropologist and a philosopher of religion who went at each other for the better part of an hour. I didn't take sides, partly because I couldn't; they both sounded sharp and both kept landing hits. I wrote the exchange up as best I could and I'd like you to referee it, move by move, because I have to file something and I don't want to pretend I know who won.

Here's the gist. Her opening shot: religion should be studied as a purely natural phenomenon, and once you really do that, the question of whether any of it is *true* sort of quietly falls away — not because the beliefs are false, but because the natural history shows they were never in the truth-tracking business to begin with. Religious ideas are byproducts of ordinary cognition, she said: agency detection, theory of mind, our memory for minimally counterintuitive concepts. The mind coughs up gods the way it coughs up faces in the clouds. A cough isn't a diagnosis, so a god isn't a hypothesis.

(1) He said that's the genetic fallacy on stilts. Where a belief comes from has no bearing on whether it's true. And worse, he said, the same faculties she's dismissing also produce our belief in other minds, in the external world, in numbers — so if "byproduct of ordinary cognition" is a debunker, it debunks everything, including her science.

(2) She said it isn't the mere origin that does the work, it's that the mechanism is *insensitive* to the specific content. Swap the god out, keep the mechanism, and you get the belief anyway. That's what makes it a debunking explanation rather than just a causal one. And she claimed there's an asymmetry: belief in other minds gets corrected by interaction, whereas the supernatural claims don't get tested against anything.

(3) He said the asymmetry is just the old evidentialist point and it begs the question — whether the claims "get tested" depends on whether you think there's anything to test them against. And he said it's empirically false anyway: religious traditions are stuffed with claims that DO get tested in ordinary life — ritual efficacy, providence, answered prayer — so you can't say the mechanism is globally blind.

(4) She came back with what she called the social acid: religious beliefs stick because they're good group badges, which is why the content wanders so wildly from culture to culture. The wild variation is itself evidence that nothing is being tracked.

(5) He said variation doesn't show that — cosmologies varied wildly too and astronomy is still truth-tracking. And "arbitrary content" is just false; there are cross-cultural regularities, moralizing high gods, afterlife beliefs, that look constrained, maybe even tracked. Then his jab: if you can dissolve any belief by finding its social function, the same move dissolves her commitment to naturalism — this very dinner is a costly signaling ritual.

(6) She said the self-defeat charge is cheap; naturalism makes predictions and gets tested and religion doesn't. And the "regularities" are exactly what the byproduct-plus-group-selection story predicts, so they're not independent evidence.

(7) He said fine — but then her theory can't lose. Regularities confirm it, wild variation confirms it, and that's not a virtue.

That's where they ran out of steam. Can you go through it move by move — rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly why — then tell me who's right about what, what the real crux is, what both of them are missing, and who's more right overall? Don't just pick a winner; I need to know where each of them actually stumbled.
