I've been reading about art forgery and my intuitions dont add up. I'd like to know what principle, if anything, they come to, and whether I have to give one of them up. Here they are.

Van Meegeren's Vermeers. I've seen them in reproduction. Competent, dead, I'd never call one a good painting, and the technical brilliance doesnt rescue it. As curiosities they're fascinating — I'd happily spend an hour in a room of them with a wall text about how he aged the paint — but as art, nothing.

A Rembrandt that the experts demote to studio of Rembrandt. Same canvas, same paint, same experience standing in front of it, and I think it is now worth less, and that I'm right to value it less, not just being a snob. The drop is real, not an illusion.

But if the fact never surfaces — a painting everyone takes to be his own hand that was mostly an assistant's, and nobody ever finds out — I don't think it's worth less right now. I can't make myself say a thing is worse when the worse-making fact is inaccessible to everyone. Then the reverse: a real Vermeer that the field has decided is a modern fake. I think it's a great painting today, before the lab report comes back. So in one case my valuation waits for the news and in the other it doesn't. Related: the Getty kouros, the one nobody can decide is archaic Greek or a twentieth-century fake. I notice I'm rooting for it to be real, and that I'd lose all interest if it weren't, even though it's the same stone and the same evidence either way.

A workshop piece: master's hand in the face, assistant did the rest. Worth less than an all-his-own-hand one, sure. But not a fake, no scandal, no feeling of being cheated. The assistant's hand and Van Meegeren's hand are both not Rembrandt's, so I can't see what the difference is, except that one was made inside the master's workshop. That can't be doing all the work, can it?

A Roman marble copy of a lost Greek bronze. I think that can be a great sculpture, full stop, no discount. Same for the copy of a painting that burned: if the original is gone and the old copy is the only thing left showing you what it looked like, the copy goes up in value, not down.

Vintage prints. A photograph printed in 1930 versus a modern print pulled from the same negative by a good lab: the image is the same image, the modern print is often better made and will outlast the old one, and I still want the vintage one and would pay more for it. It's not the picture. The picture is identical.

Heavy restoration. A painting that's 60% later repaint, well done, hung next to a grubby unrestored one. I prefer the grubby one, and I think I'm right to, even though the restored one is more legible and closer to what the painter wanted you to see. The restored one feels like less of a witness. But a witness to what? The grubby one is a witness to four hundred years of dirt as much as to the painter.

The Donation of Constantine. A forgery, known to be a forgery, and I don't feel cheated by it at all. It's one of the most consequential documents in European history because people believed it. Valuable precisely as a successful lie. Meanwhile Van Meegeren's lies, also successful for a while, are worth nothing to me as paintings. One lie makes value and the other destroys it.

Last one. Someone with real skill paints a perfect Vermeer-grade forgery, never offers it for sale, never claims anything about it, hangs it in his kitchen, tells every visitor it's his. Nobody is deceived. I still think it's worth less than the Vermeer, and I can't say why, since there's no deception, no harm, and it looks the same.

The best principle I can come up with on my own is: the value is the experience plus the true story of how the thing came to be. That handles the forgery and the workshop piece, maybe. It dies on the reattribution, because the true story doesn't change on the day of the announcement and my valuation does.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, gets most of this, which of my verdicts has to go, and what does the winner say about two things I haven't settled — an artist who paints a second version of his own famous picture thirty years later, indistinguishable from the first, both now in museums, and a forgery that a museum keeps on display with an honest label next to the real ones?
