Consider how insurance pricing works. A car insurer sets your premium according to the objective frequency of claims among people like you — your age, your driving record, the model and age of your car, the neighborhood where you park it. The insurer is effectively required to let its prices track the real, mind-independent chances of loss. Yet in many jurisdictions the law forbids insurers from pricing by certain equally predictive facts — race, or the results of a genetic test. An actuary who ignores a known risk factor is behaving irresponsibly; an insurer that uses a legally forbidden one is behaving illegally, even though both factors are just as real a feature of the objective chance that a given policyholder will file a claim.

What conception of the relation between a rational agent's credences and objective chance does this practice presuppose? It looks as though the practice commits us to some principle of deference — roughly, that one's subjective probability for an event ought to equal its objective chance — but the prohibition on certain risk factors suggests the principle must be restricted, so that only some objective chances are the ones we are permitted to defer to. Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; identify which rival accounts of chance, credence, and rational action it quietly rules out; and say whether the practice is actually coherent, or whether it is committed to a notion of an "admissible" chance that no theory of chance supplies. If you conclude the practice needs such a notion, say where it might come from. Finish by saying what would have to change in insurance regulation if we gave up the deference principle altogether and let prices track whatever facts were predictive.
