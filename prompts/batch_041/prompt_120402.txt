I am three weeks into a third-year course in the philosophy of science, and the unit on falsificationism and the demarcation problem has just ended. As part of the course, each student has to run one 90-minute session for the rest of the seminar: 14 people, mostly third-years, all of whom have read Popper and one short treatment of the Duhem–Quine problem. I have been assigned the session on the criterion itself, and I want it to be a runnable activity rather than a discussion, because last week the group agreed within ten minutes that the criterion is obviously too crude, and I do not think anyone felt why it is attractive in the first place.

What I want to stage is an editorial board meeting. The participants sit as the board of a general science journal and have to apply a single explicit admission rule to a set of submitted research programmes. I will write the programmes myself, so that nobody can win by knowing the real history and so that the rule is doing all the work. There should be six or seven submissions, one paragraph each, each defensible by a competent scientist, and each sitting awkwardly with the rule in a different way: one that forbids a great deal but that the group will want to reject, one that has so far only accommodated results it did not predict, one whose supporting observations are themselves contested, and one where a cheap extra claim or a blamed auxiliary assumption is enough to satisfy the rule. I want the group to arrive by itself at three discoveries, roughly in this order: first, that any programme can be made to satisfy the rule by conjoining something to it or by blaming an auxiliary, so that the rule alone excludes nothing; second, that the moment they begin refusing the cheap rescues they have introduced a distinction between a modification that adds independent testable content and one that merely saves the phenomenon, and they will have to say what that distinction is; third, that the observation reports they are judging on are not innocent, so that a reasonable member of the board can reject the evidence rather than the hypothesis. The session should end with the board unable to agree, and with no sense that one side has obviously won.

Then a debrief that maps the moments in the activity back onto the criterion, and that is honest about which of the objections they found are real criticisms of it and which are artefacts of my setup. I would like them to leave thinking the criterion is neither obviously right nor obviously wrong, and to have some idea of what it is for.

Please give me the following. The rule itself, in the exact words I would print and hand out: strict and simple, in substance the falsifiability criterion rather than a watered-down testability requirement, and framed as a rule of admission rather than a criterion of truth. I do not need a quotation from Popper or anyone else; I want a sentence I can put at the top of the page. The case sheets, with the awkward feature of each case identified for me but not for the students, and with no real historical episodes and no proper names on the sheets. The board procedure: roles if you think they help, how verdicts are reached, and any constraint or scoring that keeps the group from converging on the verdict that it is all a matter of degree within the first ten minutes. The timeline for the 90 minutes, including how much time to leave for the debrief. The debrief itself, mapped to the moments in the activity. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — which case they will misread, where the deadlock will come from, and what I should do about it in the room. And a frank statement of what this activity cannot reproduce about falsificationism, so that I know what I have to supply myself. Nothing should be lectured at the start, and the words demarcation and falsification should not appear on anything I hand out.
