I'm putting together a panel for a public-history festival — the theme is judging the dead — and the other panellists are a museum curator, a philosopher who works on blame, and, unfortunately, a guy from a reputation-managment firm who keeps saying that his clients were men of their time and that we can't apply our standards to them. I want to open the session with something funny that also lands the arguments properly, and I've had this idea of writing the FAQ page for a fictional consultancy that sells the man-of-his-time defence as a service. Can you write it?

About 700 words, in deadpan corporate register — the kind of copy that says our solutions and stakeholder without any irony. Prospective clients ask the questions: a family foundation sitting on a slaveholding founder, a city council with a statue it can't defend, a university whose donor's name is on a building, a novelist trying to bring an explorer back into fashion. The answers should quietly do the philosophical work, and I care more about that than about the jokes landing one per line: the difference between excusing someone and denying the act was wrong at all; whether the defence is a claim about what morality actually demanded of him or about what he could reasonably have been expected to know, and why the firm can't have it both ways; the awkward fact that there were always people around who did know better, which makes the whole idea of his time sound like a statistical claim about the neighbours rather than a fact about the man; the praise-and-blame asymmetry, since the firm is perfectly happy to market an ancestor who was ahead of his time and I want a reader to notice that this shouldn't be available on the firm's own theory; a moral-luck rider somewhere in the packages, because if the gap between the client and the abolitionist next door is just a matter of where he was born then the firm's premium praise product gets shaky too; and the gap between deciding what goes on a plinth and deciding what someone deserved, which is where the firms whole business model lives.

It has to stay in character the entire way — no winking, no concessions, no slipping into well, of course it was wrong. But it should also be quietly running a list of clients it won't take, and I'd like that list to be the punchline, because the way the line gets drawn tells you the defence was never really about the dead person's epistemic situation. Don't say that anywhere; let the reader arrive at it. No footnotes, no citations, no philosopher name-drops — it's marketing copy.
