I'm putting together a session for my metaontology reading group and I want to use the Pluto business as the case study, mainly because it's the one episode in this area where everyone already knows the facts, so nobody gets to hide behind not being an astronomer.

Here's the case, so we're all working off the same sheet. Clyde Tombaugh finds Pluto in 1930 and it goes straight in as the ninth planet. Decades later it turns out to be tiny — smaller than our Moon — and from the 1990s we start finding Kuiper Belt objects sitting on orbits very like its. Then in 2005 something at least as big turns up further out, Eris, and now you either call all of them planets or you stop calling Pluto one. The IAU sets up a committee; the committee floats three rival criteria: a cultural one (it's a planet if enough people say so), a structural one (it's a planet if it's massive enough to have gone round under its own gravity), and a dynamical one (it's a planet if it dominates its orbital neighbourhood). In Prague in 2006 the general assembly votes for a definition that takes the first two and adds a third clause, that the body has "cleared the neighbourhood" around its orbit, and on that basis Pluto is demoted to dwarf planet alongside Ceres and Eris. A few hundred of the members present actually vote. The public goes bananas, schoolchildren mourn, some symbolic resolutions get passed somewhere. And there's a real rival camp of astronomers who think roundness is the whole story and the dynamical clause is a fudge.

What I want from you is a case analysis running in both directions, using the verbal-dispute machinery. Stated plainly so we're not just trading slogans: the diagnostic is (i) identify the disputed term, (ii) ask whether the parties actually disagree about any non-linguistic fact, and (iii) if they don't, ask whether the residual disagreement about what the term means is itself a substantive disagreement or just more words. If step (iii) comes out substantive, what you've got is a disagreement about which concept to use — a normative question about what our words should be doing, not a factual one about what's out there. Bear in mind the two control cases everyone reaches for: the whale, where ordinary usage said fish and was just wrong, so the correction was a discovery and the dispute was real; and the tomato, where the greengrocer's vegetable and the botanist's fruit coexist peacefully because the two classifications serve different purposes, so the apparent disagreement is verbal.

Now, the questions:

1. Map the parties. Who is actually disagreeing with whom, and about what? Keep separate (a) dynamical camp versus structural camp, (b) astronomers versus the public, (c) the IAU as an institution versus everyone else. For each pairing, say precisely what the disagreement is over: facts, the extension of a word, the purpose of a taxonomy, or who has the standing to decide.

2. Run the diagnostic on it. Does the Pluto case pass? My hunch is step (ii) fails in an interesting way: the camps don't disagree about any of the measurements, but they do disagree about which features are taxonomically relevant, and I genuinely don't know whether "which features matter" is a factual question, a verbal one, or a third thing the framework has no slot for. Tell me which it is and whether the machinery can accommodate it.

3. Try my test and tell me honestly if it's a bad test. Suppose the IAU had coined a fresh word for the dynamical category — helioplanet, say — and left planet entirely to ordinary usage. Would anyone still be disagreeing about anything? If yes, say exactly what survives, because that residue is the non-verbal part. If nothing survives, then either the dispute really was verbal all along or my test is too weak, and I want to know which, and why.

4. Then run it back the other way: what does the episode show about the theory? The deflationary line is that verbal disputes are shallow and worth dissolving, but here everyone had agreed on the facts long before the vote and the fight carried on regardless, and it doesn't feel shallow to the people in it. Is that a counterexample, or is the persistence just the normative question — what should planet be doing for us — reasserting itself in a different register? And which control case does Pluto actually resemble, the whale or the tomato? Pick one and defend it, then say what would have to be true for the other one to be right.

5. And the bit I care most about: the IAU is a body of scientists with no standing whatsoever to legislate English. Does that make what it did conceptual engineering rather than conceptual analysis — a proposal about how we should talk rather than a discovery about what there is — and if so, is the public backlash best read as a disagreement about the concept, or as a complaint about the legitimacy of the engineer? Where the theory is simply silent on this, say so rather than inventing an answer.

Ground rules: argue from the facts above, don't cite papers or give me a reading list (I know the literature and I want the arguement, not the references), flag any fact you need but haven't got instead of guessing at it, and write the whole thing in British English.
