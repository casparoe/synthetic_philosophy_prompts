ok so I'm writing a term paper for a phil of language seminar and my thesis is basically that the intention-and-uptake crowd have got the order of explanation backwards — what makes an utterance a promise (or a consent, or an undertaking, or a waiver, whatever) is the standing practice, not the speaker's inner intentions or the hearer actually taking it up. I need a worked case for the paper, so I want you to build me one to spec. Spec: one scenario, ordinary and modern — an office, a clinic, a landlord, a uni admin thing, a card game, whatever — no sci-fi, no brain vats, no invented legal systems, it has to lean on a real practice everyone already knows. Short, one paragraph. In it there's one utterance, by one fully competent adult who knows exactly what the relevant practice's rules are, addressed to one hearer. The words have to be the very words that would unambiguously count as that act in a normal case — dont get the split by making the words ambiguous, or by having him speak in code or in a language he doesn't really have, thats cheating.

What I need out of the case is a three-way split. On the convention-first account (take constitutive rules seriously and the act is constituted by conforming to the standing practice, so it's done once the rule is followed) the utterance does constitute the act — it really does bind him. On the intention-plus-uptake account (the act requires that he mean to be understood and that the hearer actually hear it and take it up — the reading of Austin on which you haven't warned anybody who never heard you) it does not. And I want a third leg that lands somewhere of its own, not just agreeing with one of the other two: an account on which what fixes the force is neither his intention nor the hearer's uptake, but what a reasonable member of that community would take him to have committed to, given the words and the setting. Now the important bit, the thing that makes it hard: the reason the intention account says no has to be a total absence of any communicative intention plus a total absence of uptake. Not insincerity. I do not want the boring insincere-promiser case (convention says yes, sincerity says no) — I've got a million of those already. So he must not be secretly planning to break his word; he must genuinely not be trying to get anything across to anybody, and the hearer must genuinely never register it. Make me believe the practice is carrying the entire load by itself, with no help from either side.

After the scenario, walk me through the three accounts, what verdict each gives and exactly which feature of the case is doing the work for each one. Then the checks: pull that feature out and tell me whether the case collapses back into the ordinary insincere-promise or failed-uptake story, and what each account says then. And tell me honestly whether the convention-first verdict in my case proves too much: if following the rule is all it takes, does the act come apart from communication altogether, and is there a principled place for the convention theorist to draw the line?
