"Consequentialism holds that an act is right just in case it produces at least as much good as any alternative open to the agent. The demandingness objection is that this standard asks too much of us. Because there is almost always something we could do that would produce slightly more good -- giving more to famine relief, choosing a more useful career, spending our free time volunteering -- the theory implies that we are almost always falling short, and that we should be perpetually sacrificing our own interests. The shallow-pond example makes the point vivid: if you are obliged to ruin your clothes to pull a drowning child out of a pond you happen to pass, then, since distance and nationality make no moral difference, you seem obliged to give away comparable sums to save strangers in other countries.

The distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure matters a great deal here. A theory can specify which acts are right without being something we consciously apply case by case; we can guide ourselves by rough rules of thumb while the theory remains the standard of correctness. Once we read consequentialism as a criterion rather than a decision procedure, the demandingness objection loses most of its force, because the objection really only bites against the picture of an agent who recalculates the consequences of every choice. It therefore does not tell against the criterion itself, and so it is not fatal to the theory.

The other standard reply is that ought implies can. We are plainly able to give away a large share of our income, so consequentialism is not demanding the impossible; on this view the objection simply fails, and the real problem is that our ordinary moral intuitions are too lax rather than that the theory is mistaken.

I conclude that the demandingness objection is an important worry about how morality should guide us, but it does not show that consequentialism is false."
