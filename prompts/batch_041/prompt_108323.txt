Compare Dennett's design stance with his intentional stance, and then compare the two criteria of realism that his account of real patterns appears to offer. On the one hand there is the compression criterion: a pattern counts as real, or at least as a serious candidate for reality, when describing a system in its terms yields a more compact encoding of the data than any rival description and licenses predictions that would otherwise be unavailable. On the other hand there is the success criterion internal to the stances themselves: the design stance is vindicated when treating a system as having a purpose generates reliable predictions, and the intentional stance is vindicated when treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires does the same. The comparison you are asked for runs in both directions, between the two stances and between the two criteria.

Address the following.

First, is the intentional stance a special case of the design stance, as Dennett sometimes suggests — the case in which the designed system's purpose is to represent and act upon its environment — or is it a genuinely distinct explanatory level with commitments of its own? If it is a special case, what work does the notion of belief still do that the notion of design does not already do?

Second, does the compression criterion give the design and intentional stances ontological weight, or does it merely restate their instrumental usefulness in information-theoretic dress? If a pattern is real whenever it compresses, is the physical stance ever the uniquely correct one, or is it merely the stance that compresses best for certain purposes?

Third, consider two asymmetrical cases: a system that admits of a design-stance description but no useful intentional-stance description, such as a thermostat or a heart valve, and a system that admits of a useful intentional-stance description but no tidy design-stance description, such as a market, an ecosystem, or a large institution. What does each case reveal about the supposed nesting of the three stances, and about whether the reality of a pattern can be stance-relative without collapsing into instrumentalism?

Fourth, say what your answers imply for the status of the reasons that can be attributed to a system's design without there being any reasoner who entertains them — the notion Dennett discusses under the heading of free-floating rationales. Are these genuine reasons, genuine patterns, both, or neither, and do your answers to the earlier questions settle the matter?

Please write in British English. Be explicit about where the comparison breaks down rather than forcing the stances into a single hierarchy, and do not treat the three stances as if their ordering were either obvious or costless.
