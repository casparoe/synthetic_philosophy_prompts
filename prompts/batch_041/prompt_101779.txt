Bit of a weird one. I'm a software engineer, no economics background, but I got sucked into the whole "why are some countries rich" rabbit hole. The thing I can't get past is a method that shows up everywhere in it: take a pile of countries, regress income on institutions (property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement) and on geography (latitude, disease, landlocked or not), and whichever one stays significant when both are in the model gets called the cause. Fine, whatever. But the same literature also insists that this kind of evidence is worthless and that you need a natural experiment -- some historical accident that shifted institutions without shifting geography, or the other way round -- before you can say anything causal at all. Are those two things actually inconsistent, or do they just presuppose different views of what a cause is? Can you spell out what the regression practice presupposes about causation and about what it would even mean to know an answer here, which rival views of causation it quietly rules out, and whether the parts of the practice are consistent with each other?

And then the second thing. Both sides seem to treat "institutions" as one lump and "culture" as either a leftover or a nuisance term you control for. Does that presuppose that causes have to be seperable from each other, and what would have to change in the practice if you took culture seriously as a cause in its own right? I genuinely don't know if there's a single view that fits all of it, so tell me where it's underdetermined.
