Someone in my dept has an argument that the principle of indifference isn't just unreliable but flatly inconsistent, and its been bugging me for a week. I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it actually costs.

(1) If your evidence puts A and B on a par, you should be equally confident in each.
(2) A jar holds one ball; all you know is it's red or blue. By (1), P(red) = P(blue) = 1/2.
(3) Same jar, but all you know is it's red, blue, or green. By (1), P(red) = 1/3.
(4) Now group the other two: your evidence doesn't favour red over blue-or-green either, so by (1) P(red) = 1/2. But (3) said 1/3.
(5) This isn't special to jars. Take any outcome E. You can present the space as E versus not-E, and indifference hands you 1/2; or as E plus any number n-1 of further cells your evidence doesn't rank, and indifference hands you 1/n. For a tautology the evidence is identical in every presentation.
(6) So P(E) = 1/n for every n, hence 0 for every E, while the cells of a partition still have to sum to 1.

He says this shows there's no coherent way to fix a prior, so Bayesianism never gets off the ground. I'd rather not hand him that, but I can't locate the bad step, because (3)-(4) is just Bertrand's paradox and I've never seen a fix I believed. So: which premise goes, and at what price? If I keep (1), what's the extra principle that tells me which partitions I'm allowed to feed it -- is there a real story there or is it a promissory note? And if (1) has to go, what's left to do the work of getting a prior assigned at all?
