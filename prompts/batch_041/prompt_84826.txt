I sit on the honorary degrees and commemorations committee of a large public university. Ten or so nominations a year come to us, and an increasing share of them are people whose careers include decisions that killed civilians in large numbers: generals, heads of state, wartime scientists, one police chief, once a former prime minister of our own country. We are not a court and not a truth commission. We cannot compel archives, most of the nominees are dead, families and donors watch the minutes, and our meetings are ninety minutes with an agenda that also has to cover three other items. What we lack is any defensible way to decide, in the room, whether such a record disqualifies a person from being publicly honoured by us.

The precedent the committee keeps circling back to is Elizabeth Anscombe's protest, in 1956, against Oxford's decision to award an honorary degree to Harry Truman after Hiroshima and Nagasaki. As I understand her: the bombings were murder, because the deaths of the innocent were intended as the means to the end sought and not merely accepted alongside it; the good that came of it, or that was hoped from it, is no answer; and the university's award was itself an act of approval rather than a neutral compliment to a man's other achievements. I want that argument turned into something my committee can run.

Here is what I take the procedure to have to encode, in my own words. Correct me at the end if I have her wrong, in a short note; do not silently repair the procedure to fit a better reading, since I need to see what the argument I am actually working from will and will not do. First: what an agent does is fixed by what he intends, and his intention includes what he intends as a means, not only his final goal — so killing the innocent as a means to a good end is murder however good the end. Second: effects that were foreseen but not intended are a different case, though this distinction is routinely abused and does not survive the agent's having needed the deaths to get what he wanted. Third: the agent's own answer to the question why he acted is the primary evidence of what he did, so the committee should hunt for that answer before it starts tallying consequences. Fourth: consequences do not settle the matter — that the bombing shortened the war, or that more would have died otherwise, is not a justification. This is the premise most of my colleagues will reject, and the procedure must make its dependence on that premise visible rather than bury it in a step. Fifth: the honour is itself an act. Awarding a degree to someone whose career includes the deed asserts publicly that the deed does not disqualify him; the committee cannot say that it honours the man and not the deed.

Please produce the following. A numbered procedure, with the questions in the order the committee should ask them, and for each question: what a sufficient answer looks like, what to do when the answer is missing or disputed, and whether this step is one that can stop the process with a refusal. The steps have to be usable by members who have not read Anscombe and will not read her; give me no citations, no reading list, and no quotations, since I have no way to check any of them. Include the specific test questions we should ask about intention, phrased so that they can be put to a documentary record rather than to a living mind, together with the ways each test can be gamed and the follow-up question that catches the gaming. Mark plainly every point at which the procedure stops being mechanical — where the facts are unrecoverable, where the description of the act is genuinely contested, where a member rejects the premise that intention is decisive — and say what the committee should then do: refuse, defer, or take a recorded vote on the premise itself. Then tell me what this procedure cannot do, and where a colleague who rejects Anscombe's premises must go instead, so that we do not launder a value disagreement as a finding of fact.

Apply it to three cases and show your work on each. One: a leader who ordered a city destroyed by incendiary bombing in order to force a surrender. Two: a leader who ordered a raid on a military target, knowing civilians would die, without needing their deaths to obtain what he wanted. Three: a nominee who is a national hero, a personal friend of our largest donor, and whose record contains a decision the procedure rejects — I want the answer I will not like, stated flatly and without hedging. Finish with a short section on the reverse case, where the procedure clears someone whom the committee and I find repugnant, so that we know we are permitted to be unhappy without mistaking our distaste for an argument.
