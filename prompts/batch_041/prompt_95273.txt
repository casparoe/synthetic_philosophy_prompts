I'm in a third-year metaphysics seminar on dispositions and causal powers, and I've been asked to run one 60-minute session for the group of sixteen on why the simple conditional analysis of dispositions fails. I want the failure to come out of the activity rather than out of my mouth: no mini-lecture at the start, and the terms of art should not be used until the debrief.

Here is the mechanic I'd like you to build around — adjust details as you see fit, but keep the structure. Two teams. One secretly builds objects, each with a hidden intervention card; the other must decide, for each object, whether it is genuinely fragile. The detectives may run any physical test they like (drop it, tap it, squeeze it) and may also ask what would happen under a test they have not run, but that is all the evidence they get. The builders' cards come in three kinds, labelled only by their mechanical effect: one alters the object at the moment it is tested, one blocks the manifestation while the object is under test, and one produces the breaking behaviour in an object that is not fragile at all. Detectives bet tokens on their verdicts and builders pay tokens to play cards, so both sides stand to lose something.

Give me the rules and materials, a payoff scheme needing no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the analysis and its counterexamples: the cases where the counterfactual probe said no while the object was fragile, the cases where it said yes while the object was not, and why patching the analysis by adding a clause that excludes interference either makes it trivial or begs the question. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about the one thing the activity cannot reproduce: in the game there is a fact of the matter about whether an object is fragile, fixed by the builder's card, whereas in the real dispute that fact is precisely what is at issue.
