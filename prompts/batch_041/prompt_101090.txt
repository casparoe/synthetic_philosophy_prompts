I've been trying to get clear on something that came up at work and won't leave me alone. I write software, and I keep catching myself and my colleagues talking about our systems as if they had minds — the load balancer "wants" to spread traffic evenly, the classifier "thinks" a photo is a cat, the scheduler "decides" which job to run next. Someone on my team lent me a popular-philosophy book that described three ways you can approach any system: you can describe its physical parts, you can describe what it's for (its design), or you can describe it as if it had beliefs and desires that make its behavior intelligible. That framing seemed obviously right until I tried to apply it consistently, and now I can't tell where the joints actually are. I care less about whether it's "literally" true that anything has a mind than about a prior question: when is a mind-shaped description of a thing a real feature of the world, and when is it just a convenient fiction we're projecting?

Here are the verdicts I actually have, and I want to know what single principle, if any, they add up to. A chess engine that keeps its queen out of danger: I'm fully comfortable saying it "wants" to protect the queen and "thinks" my knight is pinned — that feels like it's tracking something real, not a metaphor. A thermostat that clicks on when the room is cold: I resist saying it "thinks" it's cold; pressed, I'll grant only a very thin, deflated sense of the word. A gene described as "selfish" or as "wanting" to be copied: I'll use the phrase, but I think it's a deliberate fiction — useful for organizing the biology, not a claim about a tiny agent. A slime mold that reproduces the layout of a rail network when it forages: I'm happy to say it "solves" the problem, but not that it "knows" the solution. "The market is nervous about the rate decision," or a colony that "decides" to move: I think these are real patterns, not just talk, even though nobody is nervous and no one holds a meeting. A neural network that outputs "cat" for an image: I think its "belief" that there's a cat is a real pattern, but a purely deflationary one, and I'd flinch at saying it believes the cat is on the mat rather than merely in the image.

The pair I can't reconcile is the chess engine against the thermostat: both are feedback systems implementing a function, and I can't articulate why one earns the mental vocabulary and the other doesn't — my only honest answer is "the chess engine is more complicated," which feels like a cheat. I suspect the missing ingredient is something about compression or prediction — that a pattern is "real" when the mind-shaped description lets you predict and compress the system's behavior better than any description that stays at the physical or design level — but I can't get that to yield my verdicts on the thermostat and the slime mold without also licensing mental talk about the thermostat. Please act as a philosopher of mind working on the realism question, think this through carefully rather than pattern-matching to the usual positions, and brainstorm the candidate principles before committing. Then give me a committed answer: state the principle, or a small ordered set of them, as precisely as you can; tie each clause to the specific verdict above that forces it; and tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed — a large language model that "wants" to be helpful, a hurricane "heading for" a coastline, a corporation that "intends" to enter a new market. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and if it forces me to drop one, say which, and why I should give it up rather than keep the principle complicated enough to keep it.
