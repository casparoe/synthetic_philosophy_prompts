I'm a science-and-ideas writer and I've been circling a piece on weakness of will — the 11pm-you-sets-the-alarm-for-6am thing — and the more I read the more it seems like the philosophers and the behavioral economists are describing two different animals with the same word. So I want to build a proper decision tree that sorts the positions, partly for the piece and partly because my editor wants an interactive quiz to run alongside it. Start it from the most basic fork — is weakness of will even possible, or is the Socratic line right that nobody knowingly does the worse thing — and then split from there. I want one big branch for views that treat it as a single-moment conflict (a judgment about what's best, formed and then somehow not acted on) and another for views that treat it as a problem across time (what I want now versus what I wanted last night), since I'm fairly sure that's where a lot of the confusion lives. On the across-time branch, the first question should be whether the later reversal is a change in what the person actually values or just a change in how heavily she weights the near future — present bias, hyperbolic discounting, all that — and then, if it's the weighting story, whether she knows in advance that she'll flip (so she can tie herself to the mast with commitment devices) or is naive about it. On the single-moment branch I want a node for whether the judgment that lost was ever really all-things-considered, and a node for whether the whole business of a will, or willpower as a limited resource, is doing any explanatory work at all or is just a placeholder. And somewhere I'd like a leaf for the accidie idea — the case where the person can't even bring herself to try, which feels different from trying and failing — and a leaf for the flat deflationary view that says there's no faculty here to be weak, so the whole question is a grammatical illusion.

For the leaves, please land on something like: the Socratean denial; the Davidson-style view that weakness is real and the judgment gets overridden by a non-judgmental motive; accidie; naive present bias; sophisticated present bias with commitment devices; a bargaining/dual-self picture in the picoeconomics vein (Ainslie); and the deflationary no-will option. Phrase every node as a question a reader could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to — no node should be answerable only if you already know the label. Where two of these leaves come out at the same point on the tree, flag it and say what extra distinction would be needed to pull them apart, and where a branch of the tree is logically available but nobody seems to occupy it, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or simply hasn't been explored. Don't hedge on the architecture — make the calls, tell me which leaf you'd actually bet on and why, and if you think two of these positions are really the same position wearing different hats, say so and defend it.
