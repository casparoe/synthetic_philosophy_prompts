I write about the mind for a long-form science and ideas outlet, and I am two thousand words into a piece about the methodological quarrels inside consciousness science — the fight over what a third-person study of experience could even be a study of. At a panel last month a postdoctoral researcher put an argument to me that I have not been able to shake, and which I have since written out in my notebook in the tidier form below. It is aimed at heterophenomenology, the method Daniel Dennett named and defended, in which a subject's sincere reports are gathered and treated as data about what the subject believes and says, while the theorist stays officially neutral on whether the things reported are really there; the resulting inventory of the subject's commitments is what Dennett calls the heterophenomenological world. The argument runs like this. (1) Heterophenomenology takes a subject's sincere reports as data and is officially neutral about whether what the subject reports is actually the case: the reports establish what the subject believes, not what is true. (2) A method that is neutral about whether the subject's reports are true must also be neutral about whether there is anything it is like to be the subject, since a subject who produced all of these reports with nothing it is like to be them would yield exactly the same data. (3) So any finding the method produces is compatible with the subject being a zombie, a being that talks and behaves as we do but has no experience at all. (4) So no finding the method produces could confirm or disconfirm that the subject is conscious; it can deliver a well-organised inventory of what people say and believe, and nothing further. (5) A science of consciousness that cannot, even in principle, tell a conscious subject from an unconscious one is not a science of consciousness but interpretive sociology with better notation. (6) Therefore heterophenomenology should be abandoned, and the neutrality that looked like its chief virtue is precisely what sinks it.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Tell me which step or steps to reject, and why the offending step looked so compelling when it was laid out in front of me; then price the alternatives — if you think the chain is sound and something simply has to be paid for, say which premise you would sacrifice and what that costs. I would rather not surrender (1), since without reports as data the whole enterprise seems to have nothing to work with, and I am uneasy about (6) for a reason I cannot quite articulate: the argument seems to reach its conclusion too easily, and I notice that the same chain would apparently condemn any third-person method whatsoever, including ones that swap reports for brain scans, since those data too are compatible with a zombie. So I suspect I am missing something, but I cannot see where. Take a definite position and defend it; do not lay out the options and leave me to choose. Please write in British English, and please do not give me citations or a reading list — I am after the argument itself, and I have no way of checking references from where I am writing.
