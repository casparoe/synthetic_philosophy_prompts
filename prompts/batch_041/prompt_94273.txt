I do animal sentience work, mostly fish, and next month I've got the after-lunch outsider slot at a regional farmed-fish and shrimp producers' meeting. About 120 people: production managers and fish-health vets off salmon and shrimp farms, a handful of feed-company people, the association's economist, one crustacean biologist who is openly skeptical that crabs feel anything at all, and one animal-welfare scientist who thinks my whole approach is a sellout. 25 minutes from me, then as long as they want for questions, which is definately the real event.

The talk makes three moves. One, probable suffering is a matter of degree, and the operational test I use — I've been calling it the three-signature test — is this: we have good reason to think an animal suffers when it gives up something it otherwise wants in order to avoid a noxious stimulus, when the change outlasts the stimulus by hours or days (guarding, not using a body part, appetite gone), and when an analgesic reverses it. Two, finfish and decapods clear that test. The argument isn't "they're like us" — it's that the fish pallium and the decapod's central nervous system are doing the work the mammalian cortex does in the trade-off and persistence parts. Three, if you grant one and two, the ranking that follows is boring and expensive: rank interventions by suffering avoided per dollar, which puts slaughter method, live transport, crowding, and oxygen/ammonia thresholds miles ahead of enrichment and "happy fish" branding, and puts decapod killing methods at the very top (no more ice slurry, no more slow air exposure) because the volume is enormous even if your credence per animal is low. A few countries have started writing decapods into their welfare law; I think that's the right direction and I want this industry getting ahead of it rather than being dragged.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for the question period. Not a defense of the argument — I know the argument — the questions this room will actually ask.

Give it in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

The hit list — eight or so objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me, each phrased the way the person who'd raise it would actually phrase it (their vocabulary, not translated into philosophy-speak), with the faction it comes from and a line on how likely it is. (~500 words)

What I say back — a reply to each, written as speech I could actually deliver in about two minutes. (~150 words each)

Where the reply is soft — one or two sentences per reply, naming the exact spot a sharp person in the room will push on. Be blunt; I'd rather have a sharp wrong answer I can fix than a survey.

Concede before they take it — what I should grant in the first two minutes so it can't be used against me later. (~150 words)

The reply that has to land — the single answer the whole talk's survival depends on, and how to rehearse it. (~200 words)

The one I'm dreading — the question I'm hoping nobody asks, why it's dangerous, and what I'd say if it comes anyway. (~200 words)

No citations and no reading lists — I'm not putting references on a slide and I won't remember them at the podium. And if two of the objections are the same question wearing different hats, say so instead of padding the list.
