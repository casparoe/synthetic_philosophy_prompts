I write the Ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about the quiet automation of civic deliberation — cities and agencies that have begun to replace contested public consultations with machine-mediated ones. The reporting is done; what I still need is the closing section, which poses a deliberately anachronistic question: what would John Dewey have made of all this? Dewey never saw a chatbot and died decades before anyone thought to simulate a public, but he spent a career insisting that democracy is less a set of procedures than a way of living together, and that inquiry is a pattern running from a troubled situation through hypothesis to a test in experience. I want that framework brought to bear on a concrete development rather than invoked as atmosphere.

Here is the scenario I need him to confront. A mid-size city has stopped holding town halls on a divisive rezoning question. Instead, residents submit their views one at a time to a chatbot, which interviews each of them and draws out their reasons; a model then synthesizes the whole corpus into a single statement of the city's considered judgment, the council votes on that synthesis, and no resident ever encounters another resident's reasoning directly. Elsewhere, researchers forecast public opinion not by asking people but by prompting language models to answer as though they were a representative sample of them, so that the public being consulted is a simulation of a public.

Please write the section as an argued prediction — roughly 700 to 900 words — of what Dewey would say, not a summary of his philosophy. I need four things from you. First, locate the strongest Deweyan objection to the machine-mediated consultation, and make it turn on something specific in his thought rather than on a general preference for face-to-face politics. Second, confront the real tension in his position: Dewey had considerable faith in scientific method and in the disciplined expertise that method requires, so explain whether that faith would incline him to welcome the synthesis as a better instrument of inquiry or to reject it, and say which of his commitments would have to give way for the other to prevail. Third, tell me what he would have made of the simulated public — the respondents who are not respondents — and whether it is a tool for inquiry or a contradiction of it. Fourth, name one point at which I should not pretend to know his view: a question on which his own principles pull in two directions, or on which he might simply have been surprised, and say plainly why certainty there would be a fabrication.

No citations or reading list are needed — I'm after the argument, not the scholarship — and I would rather you flag where you are extrapolating than dress a guess up as exegesis.
