I am putting together the final session of a graduate seminar on confirmation theory, and I want to hand my students a defensible tier list rather than yet another survey. The session is on the problem of old evidence: the apparent fact that evidence a scientist already knows — the precession of Mercury's perihelion, say, which was measured long before general relativity accounted for it — can nonetheless confirm a hypothesis, even though an agent who is already certain of e cannot raise its credence in anything by conditionalising on e. Rather than have you summarise the literature, I want you to evaluate the following strategies. I have stated each in my own words so that we are all working from the same descriptions:

(a) the counterfactual-credence strategy: measure e's confirmation of h by the difference it would have made had the agent not known e, that is, by the posterior the agent would have had on learning e from scratch;
(b) the likelihoodist strategy: locate confirmation in the likelihood ratio P(e|h)/P(e|¬h) rather than in a change of posterior, so that e can confirm h even when the agent is already certain of e;
(c) the bootstrap strategy: permit a hypothesis to be confirmed by data it entails, provided the data are used in the right way, so that the explanation of a known anomaly is itself the confirming instance;
(d) the contrastive strategy: what confirms h is not e on its own but the fact that e is more probable under h than under the live rivals, so that old evidence confirms precisely when it discriminates;
(e) the novelty strategy: evidence confirms only insofar as it was not antecedently known or predicted, and the appearance of confirmation by old evidence is an illusion to be explained away;
(f) the partition strategy: define the agent's credence function over a richer space in which the proposition that the agent has observed e is itself a variable, so that observing e and knowing that e come apart and conditioning on the former can still move credence;
(g) the reliability strategy: treat confirmation as a matter of the dependability of the process that produced e rather than of any arithmetical relation between e and h, so that old evidence confirms exactly when its provenance is trustworthy.

Please sort these into four tiers, from the strategy you would be willing to defend in print down to the one you consider hopeless, giving a sentence or two in justification of each placement. Close with a short remark on which two strategies are hardest to keep apart and what would separate them. Keep the whole reply to no more than 700 words.
