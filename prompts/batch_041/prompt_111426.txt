My old type teacher is retiring after forty years and I have to give the toast at her dinner. The room splits cleanly in two: her former students, who are all type designers and will flinch if I bend a single curve the wrong way, and her family, who love her and think she 'makes fonts.' The one thing she drilled into every first-year was that the whole trade comes down to answering what makes every 'a' an 'a.' Her favourite way to make us squirm was to hold up two 'a's — the Futura kind, basically a circle with a stick on the right, and the Times kind, a bowl with a curved lid and a little hook — and point out that the two share almost no ink, yet nobody hesitates before calling both of them 'a'. So the sameness can't just be a shape; she'd say it's something about how the parts relate, a closed counter and where the stem hangs off it, and that a typeface's style is just the space of ways you can vary all that without the letter falling apart.

Write the toast, five minutes, warm, a bit funny. It has to actually take a stand on what makes an 'a' an 'a' and use that stand to say something true about her forty years of work; it can't just call her letters uniquely hers without earning it, and it definately can't float the letter up into some Platonic heaven she'd have rolled her eyes at. Land it for the designers, and don't lose her family.
