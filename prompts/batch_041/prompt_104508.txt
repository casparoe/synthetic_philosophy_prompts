I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the conceptual history of atrocity categories, and I've hit a puzzle I'd like you to untangle. As I understand it, Lemkin coined "genocide" in his 1944 study of Nazi occupation law as a legal as well as a moral category, and the 1948 Convention more or less codified his concept, albeit in narrowed form. So the label is not merely rhetorical: once an atrocity has been legally recognized as genocide — by the UN, or by the ICJ — the states party to the Convention come under an obligation to intervene to stop it. My puzzle is why the politics of naming has nevertheless been so contested ever since. Is it simply that states resist the label in order to dodge that obligation, or is there something about the category itself that makes it so hard to apply? I'd also like to understand how the gap between Lemkin's original conception and the narrower legal definition bears on all this, since I suspect that gap is doing more work than my framing admits.
