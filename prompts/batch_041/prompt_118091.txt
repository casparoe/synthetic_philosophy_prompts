I need an outside read on an arguement I keep having with a colleague. We both work at a small policy shop — she's the economist, I do the philosophy-ish side — and right now we're trying to value two designs for a bus network. She runs a choice experiment: people pick between hypothetical trips, she fits a model to the choices, out come "utilities" for each attribute (fare, walk time, transfers, crowding), and we rank the designs by how much utility they generate in total, with a dollar figure attached so we can put it next to the construction cost.

I keep objecting that the numbers are artefacts of the questionnaire. Reorder the questions, add a third option nobody would take, describe the same service as "10% of trips are late" instead of "90% on time," and the implied utilities move — sometimes enough to flip the ranking of the designs. That's not a subtle finding, it's in every methods class. So, I say, you're measuring your survey, not what people care about.

She says: fine, that just means preferences are context-dependent. "Preference" in my framework is whatever the choice data reveals; if the answer changes when you ask differently, the preference changed. There's no further fact about what people "really" want for you to be wrong about. And when I bring up people who adapt to a bad situation — someone who stops wanting something because they'll never get it, someone who says the long commute is fine because they can't move — she says that's their preference and we should weight it the same as anyone else's. That's where I really dig in, because i don't think a preference formed by having no options should count the same, and she thinks that's me smuggling a moral judgement into her science.

We've had this four or five times now and it always ends the same way: her saying I want something her model can't represent, me saying her model has already decided the question it claims to be neutral about.

My hypothesis is that this is an empirical fight in disguise — that it's really about whether people's preferences are stable and well-defined enough for choice-based measurement to mean anything, and that once we know that, the conceptual fight dissolves. Is that right? Or is it a verbal fight about what "preference" and "utility" are allowed to mean; or two different questions sharing one word, one about what people will pick and one about what's good for them; or a values fight about what welfare is, which no amount of data will settle? And if the sour-grapes case is doing real work here, tell me which part of it is conceptual and which is moral.

Then tell me what would actually count as progress on this: a distinction, a piece of evidence, a formal result? Or is it the kind of dispute that should just be abandoned?

Be honest about how confident you are in the diagnosis, and flag the point you're least sure about.
