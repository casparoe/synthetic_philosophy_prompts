I'm trying to put together a two-page research proposal for a small fellowship — about six months of part-time work — and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into something with an actual shape. Topic is the "is the brain a computer" question. My current suspicion is that most of that debate is a mess of an equivication, but I don't know yet whether there's a real question hiding inside it.

Here's the thought. "Computer" seems to get used in at least three ways in these arguments. (a) Anything that computes some function — on which reading the brain is trivially a computer and so is a rock under a clever enough redescription, so the thesis is uninteresting. (b) A thing with a particular architecture — stored program, addressable memory, serial instruction execution, a real hardware/software distinction — on which reading the brain pretty obviously isn't one and the thesis is just false. (c) A claim about how you should explain the brain: that the right level of description is the function computed rather than the wetware, and that this level is autonomous from whatever implements it. I suspect (c) is the only one actually up for grabs, and that when people assert the brain is a computer they mostly mean (c) while arguing as though (a) or (b) were at stake.

But (c) has an obvious problem, which is the triviality worry: if any physical system can be described as computing some function, then "explain it as computing F" is always available and never explains anything by itself. So (c) needs a principled restriction on which computational descriptions count as explanatory. My half-baked suggestion is that the restriction has to do with the computation being recoverable from the system's own causal organisation — the parts of the system have to be doing things that map onto the parts of the computation, and the mapping has to survive small perturbations in a way that the trivial redescriptions don't. I don't know whether that's a genuine constraint or just me quietly re-describing the mechanistic alternative. Nor do I know whether it makes computationalism a claim about the world at all, or only a claim about good modelling practice — and whether that distinction matters or is itself a verbal squabble.
