So I've somehow ended up agreeing to give the commencement address at the business school where I did my PhD. It's an eight-minute slot — they run five of them, one from each decade of alumni, and I'm the nineties guy, which apparently means I'm the one who's been around long enough to be honest. The room: about four hundred MBAs, thirty-ish faculty (a couple of whom taught me, including one decision theorist who will find me in the parking lot afterward if I say anything sloppy), and alot of parents and grandparents who have never heard the word "utility" and are mainly there to take pictures of somebody in a gown. The dean's office sent along a suggested theme for all five speakers: "Maximize Your Potential." I want to gently take that apart using satisficing — the Herbert Simon idea, where you don't scan every option for the best one, you take the first one that clears a bar you've set. And the bar isn't fixed, it drifts up after a good outcome and down after a bad one, which is either the deepest part of the theory or an embarrassment depending on who you ask. I was up till one in the morning rereading about it and I still can't decide which.

What I need is a speech where the philosophy is actually load-bearing rather than an anecdote with a concept stapled to the end. So: I want the audience to come away understanding why this isn't just "good enough is fine for people who can't do the math," and I want the faculty to be unable to say I caricatured the other side. In particular there's an objection I know is coming — that satisficing is secretly just optimizing once you count the cost of thinking, i.e., that a firm or a person who stops searching early is really maximizing net of the time it takes to decide. I think that objection is partly right, and I want the speech to concede it and then still make its point, which I think has to do with the regress: if deciding how hard to deliberate is itself a decision you optimize, you never start deliberating, so some threshold has to be taken as given. Build the address around that. Written to be spoken, no bullet points, no "in conclusion," warm enough that the grandparents laugh at least twice, and please don't quote anybody — I'm not confident of the exact wording of anything and I don't want to get it wrong in front of the decision theorist.
