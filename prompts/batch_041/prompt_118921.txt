I am a novelist at work on a book set in Edinburgh in the 1740s, and the whole middle third of it turns on an argument my protagonist has with a young advocate over whether a promise can bind anyone when no one is watching. She is a widow who runs a subscription library and keeps, in a locked drawer, a ledger in which she records every promise ever made to her — the plot is that a promise entered in that ledger is broken after the promiser's death, and she has to decide what, if anything, she still owes in return. Because she has been reading her lodger's copy of David Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature*, and has come to the section titled "Of the obligation of promises," I need to be able to follow the reasoning well enough to write both sides of the scene. Here is the stretch that has defeated us both, copied exactly as it stands in the text I have:

> "THAT the rule of morality, which enjoins the performance of promises, is not natural, will sufficiently appear from these two propositions, which I proceed to prove, viz. that a promise wou'd not be intelligible, before human conventions had establish'd it; and that even if it were intelligible, it wou'd not be attended with any moral obligation.
>
> The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions. […]
>
> All morality depends upon our sentiments; and when any action, or quality of the mind, pleases us after a certain manner, we say it is virtuous; and when the neglect, or non-performance of it, displeases us after a like manner, we say that we lie under an obligation to perform it. A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise. But 'tis certain we can naturally no more change our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens; nor by a single act of our will, that is, by a promise, render any action agreeable or disagreeable, moral or immoral; which, without that act, wou'd have produc'd contrary impressions, or have been endow'd with different qualities. It wou'd be absurd, therefore, to will any new obligation, that is, any new sentiment of pain or pleasure; nor is it possible, that men cou'd naturally fall into so gross an absurdity. […]
>
> Now 'tis evident we have no motive leading us to the performance of promises, distinct from a sense of duty. If we thought, that promises had no moral obligation, we never shou'd feel any inclination to observe them. […] But as there is naturally no inclination to observe promises, distinct from a sense of their obligation; it follows, that fidelity is no natural virtue, and that promises have no force, antecedent to human conventions. […]
>
> If any one dissent from this, he must give a regular proof of these two propositions, viz. that there is a peculiar act of the mind, annext to promises; and that consequent to this act of the mind, there arises an inclination to perform, distinct from a sense of duty. I presume, that it is impossible to prove either of these two points; and therefore I venture to conclude, that promises are human inventions, founded on the necessities and interests of society."

What I need first is simply to understand it: a plain paraphrase, sentence by sentence, and then the argument laid out as numbered premises with the conclusion stated, flagging any premise that is doing work without being said out loud. I am especially unsure about three things and would like each addressed directly. First, how the two halves of the opening claim hang together — the claim that a promise would be unintelligible before conventions, and the separate claim that even if intelligible it would carry no obligation; is the second argument supposed to be independent of the first, and which one is actually carrying the weight? Second, whether the argument against a natural obligation is a fair one or whether it wins by trading on a shift in the meaning of "natural" or of "obligation," and where exactly a defender of natural promissory obligation would plant his foot. Third, what the positive story is: granted that promising is an invention founded on the necessities and interests of society, I want the mechanism by which an artificial practice acquires the sentiment that makes it obligatory, and whether that story makes promise-claims true or false or something else. I do not want a reading list or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the argument in your own words, and if there is a standard objection to this section that I ought to know about, state it plainly and tell me whether it lands.

The reason I need the mechanism rather than the summary is the case I have to write. My protagonist has a promise made to her in a locked room, with no witness, by a man who dies within the hour; its performance is something no one could ever verify, its breach something no one could ever detect, and its benefit accrues to nobody living — and the variant I am considering is that the promiser believes the dead man's spirit observes him. So: on this account, is either promise binding? If the answer is that the general utility of fidelity generates a sentiment that then attaches to the particular promise whether or not that particular instance is useful, tell me where in the passage that attachment is supposed to come from, because he does not mention it here, and tell me what licenses the extension from the practice to the token. If instead the bindingness of each promise tracks the social interest of that very instance, then tell me whether the unobserved promise binds at all, and whether that result is a reductio of the account, a bullet he would cheerfully bite, or something a careful reader can dodge by distinguishing the sentiment of morals from its grounds. And tell me whether this account can even mark the distinction my character cares about — between promises that could in principle be audited by someone and promises that are un-auditable in principle — since if it cannot, I would like to know whether that is a defect in it or a defect in her.
