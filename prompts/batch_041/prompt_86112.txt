I host a small interdisciplinary podcast on philosophy of mind and the ethics of AI, and next month's guest defends an unusual criterion of moral patienthood: a system counts as a patient, she argues, only if some of its internal states are about its own condition and can get that condition wrong — because only then is there a gap between how things are for the system and how they seem to it. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the content of this view rather than inviting her to restate it. I want at least one question each on (a) why self-misrepresentation, rather than world-directed representation, valence, or information integration, should be the mark of mattering morally, and what work that choice is doing; (b) where the criterion draws the line in practice — why she expects it to be met by some very simple organisms yet not by a large language model that can fluently describe its own internal states, and whether the difference is supposed to be one of degree; and (c) how anyone could establish from the outside that the relation obtains, given that she treats it as a fact about the system rather than about our willingness to attribute states to it. For each of those three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with one question about what would count as evidence against the criterion. Keep every question tethered to the view's commitments rather than to her career or influences. Please write in American English.
