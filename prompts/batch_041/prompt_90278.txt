For a graduate seminar on scientific realism I have been working through the literature on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I have ended up defending a fairly strong version of the thesis. The version I hold is this: for any sufficiently mature empirical theory, there exists at least one rival that is empirically equivalent to it -- one entailing all and only the same observational consequences -- while being incompatible with it in what it says about the unobservable; and because every test we could run issues only observational verdicts, no possible evidence could oblige a rational inquirer to prefer either one. Theory choice at that point is driven by considerations that are not evidential at all: simplicity, explanatory power, mathematical convenience, institutional inertia, or sheer accident. I am aware this is a strong claim, and I want to know what would count against it, sorted by how decisive the considerations actually are rather than by how often they get repeated.

Please press on the position from several directions and tell me which of the familiar objections should genuinely move me and which are cheap. Consider at least these: the charge that holism makes the thesis true but trivial, since any theory can be held onto by adjusting auxiliaries; the charge that the allegedly equivalent rivals are never really incompatible once you inspect them, so the thesis trades on a loose notion of empirical equivalence; the charge that underdetermination worth worrying about is a rare curiosity rather than the standing human condition, and that most apparent cases are merely temporary, dissolving once new evidence or new instruments arrive; the charge that a broadly abductive account of theory choice, or a Bayesian story about priors and confirmation, gives evidence a real role after all and so dissolves the problem; and the charge that the history of science displays a convergence the thesis cannot accommodate. I would especially like you to separate the formulations of the thesis that are close to unfalsifiable from the ones that actually stake something, and to name the historical or contemporary episodes that would, if they had turned out one way rather than another, count as evidence for or against the strong thesis rather than merely illustrating it. I also want to know what a committed defender would themselves concede -- which cases they must grant are underdetermined only transiently, or only relative to a fixed stock of auxiliaries, and whether those concessions leave the interesting claim standing. Finally, be candid about whether the thesis has been qualified into something no conceivable evidence could touch, and say whether that insulation is a defect or simply what a claim at this level of abstraction ought to look like. I do not want a reading list; I want the map of where this position is exposed.
