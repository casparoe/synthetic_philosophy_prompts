My reading group has been chewing on this one for two weeks and I've given up on solving it myself. It's an argument that debate-style oversight can't work, and I'm fairly sure it proves too much, but I can't find the bad step.

Setup: there's a question nobody can check directly -- is this plan safe, is this proof actually a proof, did this experiment really work. Two AI systems argue opposite sides of it. A judge, who is weaker than either debater, decides who won. The judge being weaker isn't an incidental detail, it's the whole reason we're doing this: if she could settle the question herself we wouldn't need the debate.

(1) The judge's verdict is a function of the two cases presented to her, and nothing else.
(2) If the judge could reliably tell a true case from a merely persuasive one, she could answer the question herself -- telling true from persuasive just is having the answer.
(3) So a judge who needs the debate is one who can't do that, i.e. one who can be moved by a merely persuasive case.
(4) The debaters are stipulated to be more capable than she is. Not an extra premise, the premise.
(5) A more capable agent can build a persuasive case for either answer.
(6) So for any question, the dishonest debater can assemble something the judge finds at least as convincing as what the honest one assembles, and the verdict is a coin flip with a transcript attached.
(7) So the judge ends up no better off than if she'd answered alone, and worse off in one respect: she now has a confident-sounding record to justify herself with.
(8) Nothing in 1-7 mentions AI. A jury is weaker than the lawyers. A hiring committee is weaker than the candidate. A patient is weaker than the doctor. So the same argument says juries, peer review, medicine and markets don't track truth either.
(9) They definately do track it somewhat. So something above is wrong.

I'd rather give up (8) than (2) or (4), but I don't see how, since (8) looks like it just follows.

Two things I can't place. One, someone in the group says the original debate proposal argues that with optimal play a polynomial-time judge can get answers to anything in PSPACE, whereas asking the judge directly only gets you NP. If that's right, it sounds like the protocol is built precisely around the judge's weakness, which seems to contradict (2) or (3) head on. But I might be mangling it. Two, I can't tell whether (6) is smuggling in a total ordering of persuasiveness, as if a case were convincing simpliciter rather than convincing to this judge, on this question, given what she already knows and what she's allowed to ask for. My money's on (8) being the illegitimate move and my friend thinks it's (3), which probably means we're both looking in the wrong place.

So: which step goes, why did it look fine until I stared at it, and what does dropping it cost me? And if (2) and (4) both survive in some weakened form, tell me what debate actually buys -- does it degrade gracefully into just asking the model and hoping, or is there something left over?
