In a seminar on vagueness, a classmate proposed that for borderline cases — a man one hair away from bald, a legal cutoff set at twenty-four weeks — there is simply no fact of the matter, so a legislature or a committee is free to stipulate one. The professor replied, "vagueness is ignorance," and moved on, as though that settled the matter against him. I want to know what that sentence could actually be claiming, because it seems to do very different work depending on how it is read. Lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, ordered from the trivial to the radical: say for each whether it is a claim about the world, about the meanings of our words, or about the limits of our cognition; whether the sharp boundary it alleges is unknowable in principle or merely unknown in fact; and whether "ignorance" here means ignorance of a fact, ignorance of a meaning, or inability to apply a rule we do grasp. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something close to false, and whether it actually supports the professor's use — that is, whether it blocks the stipulation move or leaves it untouched. Finally, say which reading someone who deploys the line as a conversation-stopper most likely has in mind, and whether any of the readings would have supported my classmate instead.
