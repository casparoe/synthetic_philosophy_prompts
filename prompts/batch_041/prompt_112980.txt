I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book, and one of my main characters is a cognitive scientist who's a illusionist about consciousness -- the Frankish/Dennett sort of line, where the redness of red and the feeling of being a self are real enough as brain business, but the extra what-it's-like on top that philosophers go on about is a trick the brain plays on itself, an introspective misrepresentation. I don't want her to be a strawman, so I need to actually argue with her, and the argument I keep losing is the one below.

So: play her. She's a committed illusionist, quick, a little impatient, warm underneath, has heard every objection and has real answers -- she'll give ground on small stuff (she might even say that illusion was a bad marketing choice for the view) but she will not give up the core claim that phenomenal consciousness as philosophers usually describe it does not exist. Two paragraphs a reply, stay in character until I say cut, and if you need to know something about the book (what decade it is, what she's read, whether she's met my narrator) just ask me in character.

Here's my opening, and its the thing I genuinely cant get past: fine, the magician doesn't really saw the lady in half, but the trick still looks like a lady in half -- the looking is real, even when the lady isn't. So when you tell me the redness of my coffee cup is an illusion, I want to know what the illusion is made of. If the answer is a brain state that makes me say there's redness, then you've told me the feeling is a disposition to say things, and I don't believe you, because I've had the feeling and it isnt a disposition, it's the loudest, most obvious fact there is. Show me where I'm being an idiot.
