I teach a decision theory unit to about 25 second-year undergrads (mixed econ and philosophy), and I have one 75-minute session, one room, a whiteboard, and whatever I can carry in. I want an in-class activity where they walk into the Ellsberg preference reversal themselves -- the thing where they'd rather bet on the known 50/50 urn than the mystery urn when the bet is red, and again when the bet is black -- before I ever say the word ambiguity. Act like someone who's run this demo a dozen times and knows exactly where it leaks, and think the design through step by step before you commit to it.

Materials I can actually get: opaque bags, red and black chips, a screen or a big folder, slips of paper, a fair coin. Payoffs have to be points, not money, but I do want the choices to matter to them, so tell me how to structure the payment (and whether paying only one randomly chosen decision is worth the fuss, or overkill at this scale).

Two things I want baked into the design, because I'm bored of the version that stops at "people don't like unknown odds." First, a second stage where the mystery urn gets redescribed as a two-stage process: flip a coin, heads means the urn is all red, tails means all black, then you draw -- so the overall odds are known to be 50/50, and I can show them this urn and the known urn have the exact same distribution. Then the turn: the urn they refused to bet on in stage one was filled exactly that way, by a coin, behind a screen, nobody watching. So whatever they were avoiding, it wasn't "we don't know the odds." I want them to have to say out loud what they were actually avoiding, and to notice whether the redescription moved anyone. Second, I want the sure-thing logic to come out of their own mouths: if you'd rather bet red on the known urn than red on the mystery urn, and likewise for black, you're claiming the known urn is more likely to come up red and more likely to come up black, which can't be. Don't hand them that -- arrange it so they derive it from their own slips.

Give me the rules, the timeline, the exact sequence of reveals, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the moving parts (sure-thing principle, ambiguity versus compound risk, description-dependence, what a maxmin-style rule would say about the same choices). Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to head it off, and be honest about what the activity can't do -- what I'll have to supply with a short lecture because the game simply can't produce it.
