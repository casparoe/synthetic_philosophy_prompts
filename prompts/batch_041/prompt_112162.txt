Write a pantoum — five quatrains, in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza brings back the opening lines so that the poem ends on its own first line — spoken in turn by a lump of clay and by the statue it has been pressed into, so that lines keep passing from one mouth to the other without their wording changing at all. That transfer is the whole point: the recurring lines must be sentences true of both of them (the weight, the colour, the place, the fact that each is exactly where the other is), while the lines between them are where the two come apart — what each of them would survive, the lump that would still be there after being squashed back into a ball and the statue that would not. Let the statue make the strongest reply rather than a straw man's: that it has no weight of its own but holds the clay's weight derivatively, so that there is one weight here and not two, and that the puzzle is not why they are the same thing but why their distinctness, if they are distinct, seems to need any grounding at all. The clay should not concede. The last line, being the first line in another voice, should leave the reader unsure whether the words have changed their meaning or only their speaker. Nothing in the poem should be false to the metaphysics, and a reader who has never heard of material constitution should still find it a good poem.
