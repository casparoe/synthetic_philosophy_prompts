I am a volunteer trustee of a small family foundation that for eleven years has paid for cataract and corneal surgery in our region for people who were blind from birth or early infancy. I ran a hardware store for thirty years and have no training in medicine or in philosophy, and I am writing because I have to bring a written opinion to the November board meeting and I do not trust my own instincts. What started this was our program officer's follow-up notes. The patients can now see in the ordinary sense: they navigate, they flinch when a hand comes near the face, they sort objects by size and by whether the object would fit in the hand. But they do not reliably tell a cube from a sphere until they have handled it, and one young man took about three weeks before he could name the shape of a cup across a room; he could copy a drawing of a shape he could not name, and he recognized shapes easily when the objects were moving. Our counseling leaflet, meanwhile, tells patients that the world will come into focus and that they will see their families for the first time.

One trustee, a psychology lecturer at the local college, says this is the old Molyneux question — whether a person blind from birth who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere would recognize them on first sight — and she reads our notes as the modern answer: no, because sight and touch are separate channels that must be taught to agree. That is why she wants us to add a six-month visual-rehabilitation component (a trained worker, home visits, exercises with blocks and balls, and a device that paints a low-resolution image on the tongue as a pattern of tingling, which she says some clinics use to give blind people a rough sense of shape) and to rewrite the consent conversation so that seeing is presented as something learned rather than received. A second trustee thinks this is over-engineering: the surgery restores the eye, the brain catches up on its own schedule, patients have waited their whole lives and are not owed a lecture on perception, and the rehab budget is a fifth of what we spend on surgery, which he would rather spend on five more operations.

Please draft the opinion. I want a recommendation the board can vote on — fund the rehab component as proposed, fund a modified version, or decline — with a specific number of months and a specific budget line, and I want the reasoning to be candid about what six patients and no control group can and cannot show. Do not send me a reading list or citations; what I need is for the memo to take a position on the question the two trustees are actually arguing about, namely whether a property like shape is something a person can learn through one sense and then simply recognize through another, or whether perceiving is a separate skill that has to be built by correlating what the eyes deliver with what the hands already know — and then to show how the answer bears on the vote, on what we tell patients before surgery, and on what we owe the patients already operated on who are struggling. Write it as a formal one-page memo to the board: recommendation first, then the argument, then a paragraph that states the dissenting trustee's strongest point in its best form and answers it rather than dismissing it. End with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by one of FUND, FUND-MODIFIED, or DECLINE, a dash, and a single clause naming the operative condition.
