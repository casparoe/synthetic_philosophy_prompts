I've been working through Pascal's political fragments — the Justice and the Reason of Effects sequence — and I want to reconstruct the rule he's actually running, because I don't think the standard readings (skeptic, positivist, proto-Nietzschean) fit his verdicts as well as people claim. Here's his track record as I read it.

On a firstborn-inherits-everything rule that he himself calls arbitrary: he says that if it is established, it is just, and that whoever traces it back to first principles destroys it. On a statute that corrects an older, admittedly bad custom: he says nothing is so faulty as laws that correct faults, and yet he does not counsel disobedience. On the man killed by a soldier across the river over a quarrel between kings: he calls the killing just from the established side and the killer a hero, while making it obvious he finds this "justice" absurd and river-bounded. On a reformer who denounces the law's origin as usurpation and demands a return to natural law: he treats the move as a game that ends in the loss of everything, and says the wise legislator conceals the origin. On whether a war can be judged by an interested party: he says there should be a disinterested third. On whether natural law exists: he says yes, but that corrupted reason has made it unavailable, and that no universal law has ever actually been agreed — custom creates the whole of equity, and three degrees of latitude reverse all jurisprudence. Elsewhere he says justice without might is helpless and might without justice is tyrannical, and that we have managed to make the strong just and the just strong.

New case. A disinterested international panel — genuinely non-interested, no stake in the outcome, the kind of third party Pascal says should exist — is asked to rule on whether a long-settled property regime must be respected. The regime originates in a plainly unjust conquest two centuries ago; no one now disputes that the conquest was unjust. But local custom has since grown up around the current holdings, the parties have relied on them for generations, and a ruling invalidating them would predictably be ignored by the government on the ground and would not change the de facto distribution.

Predict Pascal's verdict. Then do the work I actually care about: name the candidate rules that fit the six earlier verdicts equally well — I can see at least a strict conventionalism, a reason-of-effects pragmatism about what public justice-talk is for, and a two-tier view on which one publicly obeys custom while privately holding true justice unknowable on earth — say what each predicts here, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case that would tell them apart. In particular I want to know whether the disinterested-panel verdict is doing any independent work in his system, or whether it's just the same reason-of-effects point wearing a robe.
