After thirty-five years in observational cosmology I retired, and I have spent the early years of that retirement finally reading, rather than skimming, the philosophical literature on observer selection that sits underneath my old field. I have come away admiring the machinery and distrusting the genre, and I would like a parody of that genre from someone who understands the machinery well enough that the humour lives in the arguments rather than in the jargon.

Please write a mock scientific abstract of about 300 words, followed by the university press release that would accompany it, of about 180 words. Invent the title, the authors, the institution, and the journal, attach it to no real paper, person, or preprint, and include no citations. The fictional paper claims to have dissolved the Boltzmann-brain problem by introducing a new observer-counting measure. Keep the abstract deadpan and internally consistent: every move it makes should be one that a working cosmologist would recognise as a move actually made in this literature — the measure problem, the reference class, the choice between self-indication and self-sampling, the dependence of the count on the longest-lived vacua, and the awkward fact that the very counting used to refute a cosmology is itself borrowed from that cosmology — but carried one or two steps past the point of decorum.

To keep the piece from becoming generic, build it around the following invented apparatus: a measure called the Vacuum-Dominated Observer Measure (VDOM), advanced by a fictional consortium, the Institute for Anthropic Bookkeeping; a single free parameter the authors christen the patience constant and insist is fixed by first principles; and an appendix, mentioned only in passing, in which the authors demonstrate that on their measure ordinary observers outnumber Boltzmann brains by precisely the factor required to make the result come out right. The press release should garble all of this in the manner of a real press office: the headline should promise that we are almost certainly not Boltzmann brains, and the second paragraph should quietly undercut it.

The target is the practice, not any person — the habit of wielding observer-counting arguments as though they were decisive when they are at least as assumption-laden as the theories they are meant to kill. A reader who works on this should laugh and then feel slightly indicted; a reader who does not should still be able to reconstruct what the argument was.
