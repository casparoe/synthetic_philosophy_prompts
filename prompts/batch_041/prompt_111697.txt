I'm putting together a handout on slurs for a final-year semantics and pragmatics option, and I've been going back and forth with a colleague in the law faculty about whether the philosophical literature really supports the line she takes. She's drafted a summary for a joint handout and I'd like an honest audit of it before I put my name to it. Here it is:

'The literature on slurs has by now converged on the view that the harm they do is not a matter of what they mean but of what speakers do with them. On the older truth-conditional analysis, a slur is just its neutral counterpart plus an expression of contempt, so "X is a K" says that X is K and adds the speaker's dislike. But we know this can't be right, because the derogation isn't part of what's asserted: it survives negation and questioning, it can't be cancelled ("X is a K, but I mean nothing derogatory by it" isn't a coherent thing to say), and it never seems to be the issue at hand. Since it's neither asserted nor cancellable, the contempt must be carried by the context and the speaker's intentions rather than by the word itself -- a slur derogates when, and because, the speaker means it derogatorily. Reclamation confirms this: if contempt were built into the word, its inversion by the targets of the slur would be a mystery, yet in-group uses are plainly not slurs at all. What remains is a purely empirical question about how hearers pick up the attitude, which the semanticists have largely handed over to the sociolinguists.'

Two things I want from you. First, a sentence-by-sentence verdict: what's accurate, what's a standard simplification, and what's just wrong, from the point of view of someone who actually works on this. Second, and more useful to me, take each of the main families of views in the area and say how it would handle three cases I use in class, because I've never been sure they cut where I think they cut: (a) a speaker who learned the word in a second language and uses it as the ordinary name for the group, meaning no contempt whatever; (b) a teacher reading the word aloud from a historical text; (c) the word mentioned rather than used, as in scare quotes. Do these cases discriminate between the views, and if so, in whose favour? For what it's worth I've always assumed the speech-act line was basically right, but I'd rather you didn't just agree with me -- and I'd rather you didn't assume the framing of the summary is neutral, because I'm fairly sure it isn't.

You're a philosopher of language who specialises in pejoratives, so bring that expertise to bear, and think carefully before you start -- maybe begin by laying out the families of views and the commitments each one is stuck with, then do the audit. Also, please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and tell me which single point you're least sure of. Oh, and definately flag it if any of the three cases I've chosen are doing less work than I think they are.
