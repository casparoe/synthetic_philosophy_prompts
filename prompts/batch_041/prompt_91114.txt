I hold a thesis about historical analogy in statecraft that I will call the no-transfer thesis: when a decision-maker reasons from a past episode — Munich for Vietnam, Vietnam for Iraq, the July crisis for any great-power standoff, the so-called Thucydides trap for a coming Sino-American war — the analogy does not transfer probabilistic support from source to target. What it transfers is salience, and the argumentative setting then launders salience into apparent evidence. Analogies are legitimate as heuristics, in that they generate hypotheses and candidate options; they are never legitimate as evidence, in that they cannot raise the probability of the outcome they are used to predict. On this view the famous failures are not misapplications of a sound method; they are the method working as it always works.

Two things embarrass me about the thesis. I arrived at it by generalizing from a handful of vivid cases, which is the very inferential move I am accusing the analogizers of. And it may be unfalsifiable: if every apparent instance of transfer can be redescribed as salience plus coincidence, nothing can touch it.

What would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from those that would sink the thesis to those that would merely force qualification, and say of each whether the thesis's own defenders would concede it as a genuine cost or wave it away. Work through at least these: what would have to be true for an analogy to transfer support legitimately — a well-specified reference class, a shared causal mechanism, disanalogies shown to be inert — and whether any actual episode meets that bar in a way we could have recognized at the time rather than in hindsight; what process evidence about how analogies enter deliberation, as framers of the option set or as post-hoc justification, would tell for or against me; whether the alternative, reasoning from general theory or from no prior at all, is any better, since if it is not, the thesis may be true and idle; and whether the hardest cases for me are the vivid single episodes at all, or rather the classes of episodes with measured base rates, which I suspect I can accommodate only by relabeling them as induction and so rescuing the thesis by definition.

Tell me also what the thesis predicts that its rival — analogies as corrigible heuristics that improve under discipline and disanalogy-checking — does not, and where the two diverge observationally. The evidence that would move me most is a case in which an analogy demonstrably changed a decision in the direction it recommended, and the decision turned out well for the reasons the analogy gave, rather than merely turned out well.

Lead with your bottom line in no more than two sentences, then give the ranked defeaters. Seven hundred words maximum.
