I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether moral disagreement between two people is always resolvable in principle, and I lost, which bothers me, because I'm sure his conclusion is too strong. Here is his argument as I wrote it out afterwards.

(1) Two people with conflicting values almost always care much more about some of the things in dispute than about others.
(2) So each can concede the points she cares about less and stand firm on the points she cares about more.
(3) If both do that, each ends up with more of what she cares about than she would have got by refusing to negotiate.
(4) Any two value systems differ in how much weight they give to the disputed items, so some swap of this kind is always available.
(5) Hence for any two conflicting value systems there is a compromise that is better for both parties by their own lights, with neither giving up anything she cares about.
(6) Moral conflict is therefore a coordination problem with gains from trade, and disputes that look intractable are failures of imagination or nerve rather than genuine collisions of value.

I don't accept (6). When I pressed him with a case where both sides hold their position as a requirement rather than a taste — one person who thinks abortion is murder, another who thinks forbidding it violates bodily autonomy — he said I was stacking the deck by choosing values that are held absolutely, and that even there each side has something to concede. I would like to keep the trade picture if I honestly can; it does explain why negotiation so often works. What I can't tell is whether it has quietly stopped doing any work by the time (6) arrives.

So: which step do I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what does giving it up cost? I don't want a survey of the possible positions. Take a side, defend it, and tell me what it commits me to.
