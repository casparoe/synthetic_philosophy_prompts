Here's a tension I keep noticing in rationalist and quant-investing writing. The same people who will tell you that the only coherent theory of choice is expected-utility maximization will also tell you to bet a fixed fraction of your bankroll — full Kelly, or more often half — and to judge a strategy by the rate at which it compounds over a long sequence of bets, treating "go all in on this positive-EV gamble" as obviously wrong. Sometimes Kelly is presented as a theorem of expected utility, just with log wealth plugged in; sometimes it's presented as a rival to expected utility, a matter of time-average rather than ensemble-average growth. What philosophical commitments does the practice of Kelly sizing actually presuppose? Does it require the claim that wealth has logarithmic value, or can a risk-averse utility function of any shape generate the same advice? Does it presuppose that the right unit of rationality is a single agent living through a long sequence of bets rather than an ensemble of agents each facing the bet once — and is the practice committed to that picture, or merely well-suited to it? And if we swapped out the presupposed view for straight expected-value maximization, what concretely would have to change about how these people size bets and what they count as a good decision?
