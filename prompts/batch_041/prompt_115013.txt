I run incident investigations at a chemical plant — process safety, twelve years in, I know my fault trees, bowties, barrier analysis and risk matrices better than I know most of my relatives, and I've read enough Dekker to be insufferable in meetings. Here's what I'm after.

There's a philosophical debate called moral luck, Nagel and Williams. I've never read the originals, only had it described to me, so don't assume I know the literature. As I understand it: we all believe you're only on the hook for what was actually in your control (the control principle), but almost everything about what you did, how it turned out, and who you were when you did it is soaked in stuff you didn't control. Nagel splits the luck into four kinds: the kind of person you turned out to be, the situations you happened to land in, the causes that made you that person, and how things turned out. Williams has the lorry driver who did nothing wrong and killed a child and is eaten up by what he calls agent-regret even though nobody blames him, plus the worry that if you scrub all the luck out of morality there's almost nothing left to assess.

Translate that whole thing into my vocabulary: fault trees and bowties, but-for causation versus NESS (was it a necessary element of a set of conditions sufficient for the harm), probability of causation (the chance that this factor actually made the difference), contribution shares, barrier analysis, reasonably practicable / ALARP, just culture, the substitution test (would a similarly trained and equally motivated person have done the same thing in the same situation), outcome bias, near-miss reporting, latent conditions versus active failures. As faithfully as you can, and don't cheat — if a claim only comes through because you quietly imported an engineering assumption, flag it rather than call it a translation.

Then the part I actually want, as a seperate section: the residue report. What translates cleanly, what only translates with distortion, what has no counterpart at all. Specifically: is there a node in a fault tree for the kind of person you turned out to be, and if not, is that a hole in my tools or is it just not a causal factor of the kind fault trees need? Does desert survive as anything more than contribution share? Is there anything in my world that's the lorry driver — someone who did everything right, whose barriers held, and who still can't come back to work — or is agent-regret a concept my vocabulary simply can't reach? And the one that bugs me: we treat outcome-dependence as a bias to be engineered out, that's the entire point of investigating near misses as hard as we investigate fatalities, whereas Nagel and Williams seem to say you can't get outcome-dependence out of moral judgment without gutting it. Is one of us confused? And wether the answer is yes or no, tell me whether the residue is a hole in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers' claim is mushier than it sounds.
