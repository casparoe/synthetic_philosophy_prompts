I teach a 300-level seminar called Minds, Brains and Machines — 18 students, mostly philosophy majors with two or three from psych — and next week is the session on the timing of experience, which is the one I always flail at. Here's the thing I want them to actually feel rather than just politely nod at. There's an old perceptual demo where you flash a blue dot near the top of a screen and then, a fraction of a second later, a red dot near the bottom, and if the timing is right people don't report two dots — they report a single dot travelling from top to bottom that switches from blue to red somewhere around the halfway point. Which is already strange, but the stranger part is that when you ask them, they say the colour changed in the middle of the trip, i.e. before the second dot was even presented. That can't be right, because the colour information doesn't exist until the red dot shows up. So something clever is going on, and there are two classic stories about what. One is the Orwellian story: the experience happens in real time, in the wrong colour, and then your memory of it is rewritten after the fact, Ministry of Truth style, so that you sincerly remember having seen the change midway. The other is the Stalinesque story: nothing is consciously experienced until slightly later, and by the time the show reaches the audience the editors have already been through it, so the experience itself contains the change. Dennett's point (I think this is in Consciousness Explained, but dont quote me) is that these two stories make exactly the same predictions about every report anyone could ever give — so no experiment can tell you which is true, and if you thought there was a plain fact about when you experienced the colour change, you've got a problem.

What I want is a classroom activity — not a lecture, not a slide deck — where the students run into that wall themselves, and ideally get there by their own route. My instinct is that they should first get the demo to work (I can bring laptops, or even just coloured cards, whatever's most robust), argue about what they saw, and then in the second half split into teams and try to design the experiment that would finally settle Orwellian versus Stalinesque — and discover, to their irritation, that every experiment they propose is one that both hypotheses predict will come out the same way. Please write this as someone who has actually taught this material and knows where it dies on the floor: give me the setup, materials, group sizes and roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief that maps what just happened onto the two hypotheses and then onto the deeper worry about whether "the timing of experience" is even a well-formed question. Tell me the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by telling them. Think it through carefully before you commit to anything — maybe start by brainstorming a few different framings of the activity, say which one you'd pick and why, and then build out that one.
