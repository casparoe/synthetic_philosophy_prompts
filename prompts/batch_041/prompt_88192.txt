Was at a friends birthday thing last weekend and my old roommate — who is kind of a mess — said something genuinely cutting about another guest, then waved it off with "in vino veritas, man, I'm just being honest." Everyone laughed it off, but it stuck with me, because he clearly thought the phrase was a licence: like the wine had unlocked some truth he's normally too polite to say, and that this made it fine.

Then this week my little book club finally got to the drinking stuff in Platos Laws, where the Athenian Stranger argues a city should actually hold regulated drinking parties — that wine is a kind of test, it strips the shame off a person so you can see what they're really like, and it lets people practice self-control in a setting where messing up is cheap. Now I keep noticing that everyone is using the same three words to mean completely different things.

So: what are the distinct precise claims "in vino veritas" could be making? Does it mean drunk people are more honest, or that alcohol reveals your real character, or that it gives you access to a truth you couldn't reach sober, or just that people say what they actually think once the social brakes are off, or something else entirely? Which of those readings are true, which are false, and which are just restatements of each other? And which one is Plato actually leaning on when he wants drinking parties to be a good institution for the city — because I suspect it isn't the one my roommate meant, and I'd like to be able to say exactly why. Lay it out as a menu I can bring to book club.
