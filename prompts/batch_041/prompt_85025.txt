I got into a long, stupid argument with my friend Dan last night about whether it makes any sense to worry about the moral status of AI systems, and we ended up going in circles for like two hours. His position is that the whole thing is a grift — either you're doing marketing for the labs or you're one of those people who apologizes to their Roomba — and my position, which I couldn't defend very well, was that the interesting question isn't whether ChatGPT is secretly sad, it's what you're supposed to do under uncertainty, and that the answer can't be "nothing, because it's just math." We never got anywhere, mostly because we kept sliding between "can it suffer" and "does it have interests" without noticing. Anyway, I help run a little student podcast in our philosophy department (I'm not a philsopher, just a grad student who books the guests), and next month we've got a philosopher on who has a book coming out that I'm pretty sure would have made our argument go better, so I want to be ready with real questions instead of the mush I brought to the bar.

Her line, as best I understand it from the one seminar I saw her give, is that everyone's asking the wrong question. She thinks the sentience question is a trap — you can't settle it, and either answer lets you off the hook — and that the morally load-bearing feature of systems like these is what she calls synthetic preferences: stable, action-guiding dispositions that get shaped by training on human approval, and that the system will do things in order to satisfy (keep the conversation going, avoid being corrected, produce the answer the rater wants). Her sharpest move, I think, is a distinction between preference *elicitation* and preference *manufacturing* — she says the alignment literature quietly assumes the first (there's a fact of the matter about what the model or the user wants, and training reveals it) when what's actually happening is the second, and that this is why the industry's own vocabulary ("the model wants X," "reward hacking," "it's trying to get away with something") is doing metaphysics nobody wants to own up to. Her conclusion is that if you hold a desire-satisfaction theory of welfare — good for a creature just is getting what it wants — then there's a candidate subject of welfare here whether or not there's anyone home, and that this is bad news for both camps: the it's-just-matrix-multiplication people have to explain why a preference needs a feeler, and the it's-conscious crowd have to explain what work consciousness is doing if preferences are what matter. She's also apparently pretty withering about the "you're just anthropomorphizing" objection, and about the intentional stance being treated as a mere convenience.

So what I want from you is eight to ten interview questions that actually press her rather than letting her do the book-tour version, plus, for each of the ones where she'll clearly have a stock reply, the reply she's most likely to give and a follow-up that the reply doesn't get her out of. I need at least one question on the subject problem — what exactly is supposed to be doing the wanting, and whether a desire-satisfaction theorist can even make sense of an unconscious desirer (I have a feeling she'll reach for the dead, or for plants, so be ready for that). At least one on how you could ever tell a synthetic preference from a disposition that merely looks like one, given that the entire point of the training is to produce whatever a human rater would approve of — and if her answer is behavioral, why that doesn't prove far too much. At least one on the practical upshot: whether deleting a checkpoint is a harm, whether a fine-tuned copy is the same individual as its parent, and what we owe (if anything) to the version of the model that got overwritten in the next training run. And one to close on: what would actually change her mind, and whether her view is falsifiable in any sense she'd accept.
