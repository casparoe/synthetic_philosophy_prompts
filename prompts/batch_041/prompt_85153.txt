I'm a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I'm the graduate assistant on the committee that writes our department's qualifying exam in the foundations of cognitive science. This year we want a question on von Neumann's late work on the brain — the unfinished Silliman lectures published posthumously as The Computer and the Brain — but we're worried that every candidate will have memorized the same two-paragraph summary (brains are slow and parallel, computers are fast and serial; neurons are unreliable, chips are reliable) and will simply pad an answer with it. We want a question that forces candidates to reconstruct the argumentative spine of the book and then to say where it is underdetermined by the evidence von Neumann actually had.

The question should turn on a specific tension rather than on the general analogy. Von Neumann treats the elementary signaling events of the nervous system as discrete and all-or-none, yet he also argues that the nervous system's internal language is probably not the language of formal logic or of the mathematician's symbolic notation, and that its characteristic mode of representation is likely statistical. Candidates should have to say what work each of those claims does, whether they can be held together without equivocating on what counts as digital, and what would have to be true of neural coding for the statistical-language claim to be more than a promissory note. A second thread should be the reliability problem: von Neumann is known for asking how unreliable components can compute reliably, but candidates should also confront whether the multiplexing-and-redundancy style of answer he sketches for automata is even the right shape of answer for a cortex, which appears to achieve reliability without anything like the replication factors his argument assumes. A third thread, which should be graded but weighted lightly, is the status of the book itself: candidates should be able to distinguish claims von Neumann asserts, claims he explicitly flags as speculation, and questions he leaves open, rather than treating the text as a settled doctrine.

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of roughly 800 words that a strong candidate could produce in 90 minutes, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves separating a distinction-level answer from a bare pass, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each move wrong. Don't let the question be answerable by reciting the book's table of contents; it should require candidates to make and defend a judgment about which of von Neumann's claims are load-bearing and which are artifacts of mid-century hardware.
