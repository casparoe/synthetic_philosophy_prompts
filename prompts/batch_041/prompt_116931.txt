I am a science journalist who has spent the better part of a year reporting on how climate economics puts a price on the future, and next month I am presenting my argument at a two-day workshop convened by an economics department. What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a survey of the literature.

My thesis is that the consumption discount rate used in integrated assessment models is not the neutral technical parameter its users take it to be, but a moral weight in disguise, and that the near-universal practice of calibrating it against observed returns on capital guarantees a mismatch: the model answers a question about prices while its users believe they have answered a question about whose welfare counts. My proposed remedy is deliberately not to set the rate to zero, which would merely relocate the arbitrary choice. It is to stop treating the market return as evidence about either of the two things the rate is conventionally decomposed into — the pure rate of time preference, and the growth term scaled by the elasticity of marginal utility — and to insist that these answer different questions: how much a person's welfare counts for less merely because they arrive later, and how much a marginal unit of consumption is worth to someone richer. I argue that only the second of these is even in principle observable, and not from interest rates, and I propose as a concrete discipline that every model run report the two components separately rather than only their product.

The room will be roughly two dozen people. Most are applied economists who build or run integrated assessment models and regard the discount rate as an input they can defend by convention. Three or four are philosophers of economics who will press the fact/value distinction and suspect I am doing normative work while claiming to do conceptual work. Two come from an intergenerational-justice organization; they will want me to conclude that the correct rate is very low and will be disappointed when I decline to. One is a senior figure who has spent a career arguing that the market rate is the only non-arbitrary anchor available. They are numerate, allergic to hand-waving, and will not accept "that is an ethical question" as an answer to "so what number should we use?"

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely each is to be raised and how much damage it would do if it lands, each phrased the way this audience would phrase it — in their vocabulary of calibration, opportunity cost, revealed preference, the representative agent, and policy relevance — with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which objection I should stop trying to win, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
