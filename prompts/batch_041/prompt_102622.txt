I have no academic training in this, but for some months I have been turning over a puzzle I cannot put down, and I would rather have the specification before I read any further. The puzzle is this. Two people face a single, one-off exchange in which each does better if they take the selfish option, whatever the other does, and yet both do better if both take the generous option. Each knows the other is the sort of person who will reach the same conclusion as they themselves reach, and neither can communicate or make a binding agreement. Ordinary reasoning says take the selfish option; a stubborn intuition says that two people who know they are alike should both take the generous one. The name I have seen attached to that intuition is superrationality. I have come across several proposals for what it is rational to do here, and I do not want to collect more until I know what any such proposal has to deliver.

So write me the desiderata: what must a theory of what to do in these symmetric situations actually get right? I want the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and I want them sorted into those that are non-negotiable and those that are merely the preferences of one camp. What I am after is a theory good enough to guide an actual decision in such a situation, not merely a tidy formalism.

The cases I most want it to handle are: the one-off exchange between two people known to reason alike; the same exchange against someone known to be selfish; a case where you are only fairly confident the other person reasons as you do; a case where your payoffs differ slightly from theirs, so the symmetry is imperfect; a case where a very reliable predictor has already acted on its forecast of your choice; and a many-person case, such as contributing to a shared resource or refraining from overusing a common one, where those who reason like you are a large and anonymous set.

The distinctions I do not want it to blur are: between what you should do and what you should expect the other to do; between a reason that works because it tracks a real causal connection between the two decisions and one that works because your decision is evidence about theirs; between reasoning that is identical in content and reasoning that merely tends to agree; and between a theory of what it is rational for one person to do and a theory of what a group should do.

The results I do not want it to contradict are: that the selfish option really is right against someone known to be selfish; that no theory may conjure a causal link between two decisions that are causally isolated; and that sometimes the correct advice is to choose at random.

Then tell me which single requirement, if any, rules out the most of the proposals on offer, and whether any pair or larger set of the requirements cannot be satisfied together. That last point is the one I care about most: if the demands are jointly impossible, I would rather learn that than keep hunting for a theory that meets them all.

Finally, briefly, say how the leading proposals fare against your list, without a full scorecard: the view that two identical reasoners must both take the generous option; the view that you should take the option that is best given that your choice is evidence about what others like you will do; the view that you should attend only to the causal consequences of your own choice; and the view that in such situations you should reason as a member of a team rather than as an individual.
