I've been going back and forth on duties to rescue people and I want help figuring out what I actually think. Take the role of a philosopher who works on normative ethics and the ethics of rescue. I don't want a survey of the literature or a history of who said what — I want you to actually reason about it, start by brainstorming a few candidate principles out loud, and only then commit to one. Think it through carefully and don't hedge at the end.

Here are my gut reactions. Treat them as data, not as a theory I'm secretly testing you on.

- Kid drowning in a shallow pond right in front of me. I'd ruin a good suit and probably need stitches from the glass on the bottom. I have to go in. No question.
- Same kid, but the only floaty thing is locked in a boathouse and I'd have to break the lock. Still have to go in. A lock is a lock.
- Same kid, crowded beach, forty people all watching, any of us could go. I still have to go. "Someone else will" isn't a reason for me specifically.
- Faraway kid who dies unless I send $300 to a charity that's about as close to a sure thing as these things get. Honestly? I think I have to send it, and that annoys me, but I can't find the difference from the pond except the distance and the fact that I never see the kid.
- Now the one I flip on. If the only thing that works is me sending money every month for years, I don't think I'm *required* to do it, just that it'd be good of me. And yet by my own logic above I feel like I should be required. This is the one I keep going back and forth on.
- Guy who waded into a flooded river on a dare and is now drowning. If it's cheap for me to save him, I have to. But if saving him means real risk to me, I think I'm allowed to decline — his own stupid choice shifts some of the cost onto him.
- Same setup, but the drowning man is an escaped murderer. I think I still have to save him if it's cheap. Being a bad person doesn't lower my duty. Which is weird sitting next to the dare guy — apparently "you chose this" matters but "you're awful" doesn't?
- Woman on a bridge about to jump, I can grab her. I think I have to, even though she's a competent adult and does not want to be grabbed.
- But a guy about to sell a kidney for money, or about to join some group I think is degrading — I can't just grab him. Not my call. So I override a competent adult's choice in one case and not the other, and I can't quite say why.

What I want: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets all of these, or close? For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed — say, a promise I made to a neighbor to watch their kid, and the kid then starts drowning; a rescue that would require me to admit I was the one who caused the problem; and a case where I could save one stranger only by shoving a second innocent person into danger. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't keep. If I have to choose between a messy principle that saves every verdict and a clean one that loses one, say which you'd take and which verdict it costs me.

End with a final line that begins "Verdict:" and gives, in under 25 words, the principle you'd endorse and the verdict it forces me to give up.
