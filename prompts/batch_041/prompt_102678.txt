I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science — nine students, one 100-minute session — and I want to spend it on Otto Neurath's picture-language program rather than on the better-known slogans of the Vienna Circle. What I want the students to feel in their hands, not hear from a slide, is the friction inside Neurath's ambition: that a deliberately flat, international pictorial notation might serve as a shared and comparatively theory-neutral medium in which the claims of a unified science could be stated and compared, and yet that any such notation, the moment it is asked to carry a proposition rather than a tally, must lean on conventions it cannot itself display as pictures. I do not want to lecture this into them; I want them to collide with it.

Please design the session around a runnable activity, with the following constraints.

The core of it should be a transmission task worked in pairs. Give me a concrete deck of materials: a fixed set of roughly thirty to forty flat pictographic cards, described well enough that I can draw them, with no words, no letters, no numerals, and no perspective. Pairs may use only the cards in the deck and their arrangement; they may not draw new symbols, and they may not fall back on any natural-language text or written number. One pair encodes, another pair decodes, and the encoding pair is scored on whether the decoding pair recovers the intended claim.

Give me a graded sequence of claims for them to transmit, ordered so that the difficulty climbs in a way the students will not anticipate. Start with something like a plain classification, then a comparison of magnitude, then a negative or quantified claim of the not-all kind, then a claim about a trend over time, and end with a claim that is modal, dispositional, or causal. Choose the actual contents yourself, but make each rung isolate a different thing pictures are bad at. I want the pairs to succeed early and then hit the wall, without being told in advance where the wall is.

Then add a second, meta round: once a pair has found some way to make its readings reproducible, it must write down the rules a stranger would need in order to read its cards the way the pair intends. I want the session built so that the moment of writing those rules lands as a discovery — that they have just produced a grammar, that the grammar is doing work no picture could do, and that some of their cards were quietly carrying a classification the picture only appears to display.

Finally, give me the debrief. It should map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of Neurath's position — his anti-metaphysical program and his physicalism, the protocol-sentence dispute with Carnap and what it reveals about the dream of a neutral observation language, and the practical choices behind the Isotype pictorial work, including why it flattened perspective and suppressed detail, and what those choices cost. It should also say plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each failure. And it should be honest about which feature of Neurath's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself — I would rather know in advance which part has to be asserted rather than staged. No reading list, and please keep everything runnable within the single session.
