I've been asked to give the commencement address at the graduation for a college-in-prison program — the students are finishing associate degrees while they're inside, and it's a women's facility, which I keep noticing matters because Foucault's whole story about discipline is told through men's bodies, the soldiers and schoolboys and convicts, and women show up in it mostly as an afterthought. The room is going to be genuinely wierd: the graduates, a lot of their families who've never set foot in the place before, the faculty, and then in the front row, because they helped fund it and one of them is getting a plaque, the state corrections people — the warden, the education-unit guy, a couple of deputies. Everyone is proud and I do not want to be the person who stands up at a graduation and tells these women their diploma is a leash.

Here's my actual problem. I've taught Discipline and Punish enough times that I can't unsee the ceremony I'm about to take part in. The examination, the ranking, the disciplinary writing, the dossier — the file that gets opened about you and then follows you out through the gate — is exactly the machinery he's describing, and this degree is unavoidably one more entry in that file, a credential that also functions as a risk assessment. And then there's the part that's hardest to say in this particular room: his claim that the prison doesn't fail at correcting people, it succeeds at manufacturing a manageable kind of offender, and that the system needs that category — needs the delinquent to keep existing — in order to keep justifying itself. I half believe that. But I also sat in that seminar room and watched these women take the thing seriously, argue with each other, get it wrong and come back the next week and get it right, and a speech that treats them as dupes of power would be false and also just rude.

So write me the five-minute commencement speech, the actual text, not advice about how to write it. Build it around the examination — the idea that it converts a person into a case, that the file ends up standing in front of the person it's supposedly about — and then let the graduates be the ones who come out on top of that, without me having to pretend the institution is neutral or that the degree isn't also a form of surveillance. It has to land for the families, who have no idea who Foucault is and shouldn't need to, and it can't be a lecture aimed at the corrections staff, because they're sitting right there and honestly some of them are the reason the program exists at all. Don't hand me a speech stitched out of Foucault lines — I can't check any quotation while I'm writing this and I don't want the thing leaning on a sentence I can't stand behind. Give me the speech, and then one short paragraph for me alone saying which piece of the Foucault is actually doing the structural work and what I'd have to give up if I cut it.
