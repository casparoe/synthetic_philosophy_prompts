I'm a novelist, and for my current book I've been reading around philosophy of mind so that a character of mine — a cognitive scientist, confident and a little domineering — can deliver a brisk demolition of the idea that we have privileged access to our own inner lives. Rather than invent the philosophy myself, I lifted the gist of a chapter from a well-regarded trade book on consciousness, which runs more or less like this:

Wittgenstein's private language argument is the centrepiece of the Philosophical Investigations. He asks us to imagine a man who jots a mark in his diary whenever a particular sensation comes over him — a sensation that only he can have. Because he must rely on memory to tell whether today's sensation is the same as yesterday's, and memory can deceive him, there is no difference between his using the mark correctly and his merely seeming to; his private definition can never be checked, and so the whole notion of a language whose words refer to the speaker's own private sensations collapses. This is the point of the famous beetle-in-a-box: if each person's box holds something different and no one can look into anyone else's, the beetle drops out of the picture and we are left with the box — that is, with behaviour. Wittgenstein's conclusion, then, is that sensations just are the outward expressions and dispositions we can observe, that the inner private theatre is a myth, and that meaning is ultimately a matter of communal agreement, which is why a word that only one person could in principle use is not a word at all.

I'd rather not put a caricature in my character's mouth, and I'd rather not have my philosopher friends wincing at the novel. So take the role of someone who has read the Investigations closely and taught it for years, and think the passage through carefully before answering — it may help to set out first, in your own words, what the argument is actually doing, before you judge the summary. Then audit it: which sentences would you accept as fair, which are common simplifications a careful reader would let pass with a caveat, and which are misreadings of what Wittgenstein actually says — in particular, where the populariser has run two separate things together, or quietly converted a therapeutic remark into a metaphysical thesis. Finally, tell me whether the concluding claim, that sensations are nothing but observable behaviour and dispositions, follows from anything the argument genuinely establishes. Be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your verdict, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
