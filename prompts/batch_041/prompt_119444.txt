Ok, this is a weird one and I've been putting off writing it for two weeks. My partner's sister and her husband are holding a humanist naming ceremony for their baby daughter in about a month, and because I'm the one in the family doing a PhD in Victorian lit (my thesis is on how the realist novel handles the loss of faith — mostly Eliot, obviously), they've asked me to give the short address, which the celebrant tells me is "the bit that gives the thing its weight." Which is flattering and also a trap, because the room splits cleanly in half. One half is Piotr's family: Catholic, going back generations, who have been genuinely lovely about the whole non-religious thing but who will be quietly watching to see whether we're implying that a life without God is a life with less in it. The other half are the couple's friends from the secular humanist and rationalist circles, who will flinch at anything that smells like a smuggled sacrament — one of them will definately be counting how many times I say the word sacred. The parents' only instruction was: no God, no prayers, and for the love of god nothing about us all being stardust. So I need to make a naming feel solemn in that room, and I've decided the only honest way through is to build the speech on Eliot's actual position rather than gesturing at it. Specifically: the move she takes from Feuerbach, that the attributes we hand to God are really human attributes, so the right response to losing God isn't to subtract the seriousness but to relocate it onto human relations; the poem where she asks to join the choir invisible of the immortal dead who live again in minds made better by their presence, which is what she offered instead of immortality — not survival, but not nothing either, and the speech has to sit exactly in that gap; and the ending of Middlemarch, where the growing good of the world is partly down to unhistoric acts, ordinary things people did that nobody recorded. There's also a strand I want to work in, that for her sympathy is a form of knowledge and not just a warm feeling — that seeing another person accurately is work, and that we mostly fail at it — because that gives me something to actually charge the parents and the godparents with, and it's a charge the Catholic half can nod along to for their own reasons.

Write the address. Four or five minutes, sayable out loud by a person who is nervous and not a performer, and plain — I don't want any words in it that the six-year-olds present don't have. Constraints, and I mean these: no quotations at all, not one line of verse, no Middlemarch sentence copied out — if you want to gesture at a phrase, paraphrase it in your own words, because I will absolutely misquote something and be the guy who botched Eliot at his own niece's naming. Don't explain Feuerbach from the podium, don't name any scholars, and don't turn it into a lecture about secularism; the grandparents should be able to nod at every single sentence, even if they'd give a different reason for nodding. And it shouldn't be mushy — the Eliot material needs to be doing real structural work, deciding what the speech can and can't promise about this child's life, not just decorating it.

After the address, add a short note clearly marked as not to be read aloud. In it: tell me which parts of this reading of Eliot the speech leans on are actually contested among people who know her work better than I do, and where I should expect to be pushed back on. And tell me what would change your mind about the whole approach — what would have to be true about Eliot, or about this room, for the religion-of-humanity scaffolding to be the wrong frame for a naming ceremony, such that I'd have been better off scrapping it and just doing something short and warm about how much everyone loves this baby. If the honest answer is that the frame is doing more for me than for the speech, say so.
