A crisis standard of care is the plan a hospital or a health authority follows when demand for a scarce, life-sustaining resource — a ventilator, an intensive-care bed, a dialysis slot — exceeds the supply. The plans I have been reading contain a feature I cannot make sense of: they describe two allocation regimes rather than one, and they switch between them according to how severe the shortage is.

In the ordinary regime, the resource goes to the patient who needs it when it is free. Clinicians certainly judge whether intensive care would do this particular patient any good — a patient for whom it would be futile does not get the bed — but they do not line the waiting patients up and ask which of them would benefit most from the bed. There is no comparative ranking of candidates, and no one is displaced by a later arrival who would do better. In the crisis regime this changes. The protocols abandon arrival order, adopt an explicit aim stated in outcome terms — save the most lives, or the most life-years — and rank patients against one another by a physiological severity score. The same documents then impose constraints that look as though they belong to the other regime. Patients judged unlikely to survive even with the resource are placed in a category that receives comfort care only and is given no access to it. Patients already on a machine who are not improving are, in some versions, reassessed at fixed intervals and may have it withdrawn in favor of a new arrival — while the documents also insist that no patient is abandoned and that those in the category just described receive full palliative attention. Some versions resolve a tie between identically scored patients by lottery rather than by any further clinical or social consideration. And the protocols characteristically disclaim age, disability, quality of life, and social worth as criteria, even though the scoring system they rely on is a physiological measure that will track some of these things imperfectly.

Set aside the question of which allocation rule is best. I want to know what this practice presupposes. What conception of a patient's claim on the resource does each regime require? Under the ordinary regime, does arrival order presuppose that the claims are equal and that a queue is a fitting way to honor equal claims, or is it merely a coordination device that no one has reason to respect once the stakes rise? What does the crisis regime presuppose about the unit of value — lives, life-years, or something else — and is it committed to aggregation and to a definite form of interpersonal comparability? Does the practice require the allocation question to be comparative (who benefits most) or categorical (will this patient benefit), and can it survive the other answer? What does the categorical classification of the likely-to-die presuppose about the value of the resource — that it has no palliative use, or that its only value is rescue? Does periodic reassessment commit the practice to a purely forward-looking, non-historical view of entitlement, on which having received the resource confers no claim to keep it, and does that conflict with the ordinary medical norm against abandoning a patient once treatment has begun — or with the disclaimer of abandonment itself? Do the disclaimed criteria express a genuine constraint on the objective, or are they only forbidden as explicit tiebreakers, with the physiological score reintroducing them under another name? And finally: can a single theory license both regimes — a threshold deontology, a rule-consequentialism whose rules are selected for different decision situations, a prioritarian view that converges on maximization in emergencies, or a two-level structure in which the ordinary regime is governed by a fairness norm that is itself justified consequentially? Or does the practice require genuinely plural commitments, so that it is underdetermined rather than inconsistent?

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, mark the places where rival theories would license the same conduct, identify the single most load-bearing commitment, and say what would have to change if that commitment were replaced.
