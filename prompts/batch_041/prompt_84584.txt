Here's my opening. You say every contingent fact has a sufficient reason. Take a boring one: there's a mug on my desk. Its reason is either contingent or necessary. If it's contingent then it needs a reason of its own, and so on down the line — fine, you say, the chain terminates somewhere. But wherever it terminates is either contingent, in which case it wasn't a terminus after all, or necessary. And if the reason is necessary then it's the same in every possible world, so there's no world in which it holds and the mug isn't sitting there, which means the mug was never contingent in the first place. So your principle looks like it eats contingency whole: either nothing ever gets explained, or nothing could ever have been otherwise, and you've got a God with no choice about this world. Which is it? And if you do save contingency somewhere, what's left of the argument that's supposed to get you from it to the necessary being?
