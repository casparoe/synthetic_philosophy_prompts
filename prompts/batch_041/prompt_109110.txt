ok so background: I've been hanging around rationalist blogs since like 2010 (LessWrong, SSC and the diaspora that came after), and I also help teach an upper-level formal epistemology seminar on the philosophy of probability. I'm writing the final and I want one 45-minute essay question on the principle of indifference that can't be awnsered by reciting Bertrand's three chords -- half the class would do exactly that and coast to a B.

So I made up my own two cases instead. Case one: a probe picks a random point on the surface of the Earth; what's the probability it lands in the tropics, i.e. within 23.5 degrees of the equator? Case two: a machine outputs a random real number between 1 and 1000; what's the probability it comes out below 10? I want the two cases to pull in different directions and I want the students to have to work out which way and why, instead of just announcing "indifference is inconsistent."

The idea I actually want them to evaluate is the move where you demand invariance under the symmetries of the problem and claim that this pins down a unique prior whenever the symmetry group is rich enough, and that where it isn't, the problem is merely ill-posed rather than genuinely paradoxical. (I think that's a Jaynes thing but I always mangle the attribution, so leave names out of the whole thing -- no citations, no reading lists; my students can't look anything up and neither can you.)
