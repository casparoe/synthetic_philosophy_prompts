Philosophers of biology sometimes speak of a "free-floating rationale": a reason for which a mechanism operates, even though no agent anywhere actually holds that reason. The standard illustrations are a honeybee's dance that conveys the direction and distance of a nectar source, or a bird's broken-wing display that lures a predator away from its nest. I am happy with the idea that such behaviour is adapted, and that there is something correctly called a rationale for it. What I cannot get past is the ownership question. If a rationale is genuinely free-floating, then whose is it? It cannot be the bee's, since the bee presumably has no beliefs about nectar or predators; it cannot be the gene's, since genes do not represent anything; and if we say it belongs to natural selection, we seem merely to have personified a process. So the phrase appears to leave me two options. Either it names a real reason that must after all be lodged in some mind — a designer's, or the organism's own unconscious one — or else it is a convenient façon de parler, and there is no fact of the matter about which rationale a given organism has. I should be grateful if you would tell me which of these it is, and, more usefully, how I ought to decide, for any particular case, which rationale an organism actually possesses.
