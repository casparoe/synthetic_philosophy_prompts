I am a doctoral candidate in bioethics, and I keep running into a puzzle in my work on advance directives. The standard debate pits the "precedent autonomy" of the earlier, competent self who signed the directive against the "contemporaneous autonomy" of the present self who may now be demented and may resist it, and both framings strike me as too coarse to guide an actual bedside decision. So I have been toying with a sharper principle, which I will call the Foreseeability Constraint: an advance directive should be treated as binding only for those decisions whose specific content the earlier self could have reasonably foreseen and endorsed, judged from the standpoint of the values the later self actually holds at the moment the decision is triggered. On this view, where the earlier self could not have foreseen the situation, the directive simply loses its authority, and the decision reverts to some fallback — best-interest judgment, or a reconstructed surrogate judgment about what this particular patient would now want.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of personal identity and a clinical ethicist rolled into one. Before you answer, brainstorm briefly about the concepts genuinely in play here — the nature of autonomy, personal identity over time, the difference between a person's long-range "critical" commitments and their momentary "experiential" wants, and the ways hospitals and statutes actually operationalize directives in practice — and only then give me the most important implications of adopting the Foreseeability Constraint. I am especially interested in the non-obvious ones: what it would mean for a clinician faced with a demented patient who now refuses the very treatment her earlier self demanded; what it does to the supposed authority of the earlier self (does the earlier self ever really bind the later one, or was that always a fiction?); how it would change the drafting of the documents themselves; and whether the principle covertly smuggles in a contested theory of personal identity under the guise of a procedural rule. Please be concrete and well organized, rank the implications by how much they matter, and explicitly flag any implication you think I am likely to be getting wrong.
