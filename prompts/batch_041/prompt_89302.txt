I was a bystander at a two-day workshop on the regulation of aquaculture last month, and I got stuck listening to an argument that ran through both coffee breaks and most of a dinner. The two people involved were a fish-health veterinarian and a philosopher who works on animal ethics—both informed, neither posturing—and the question was whether decapod crustaceans (shrimp, prawns, lobsters, crabs) should be covered by the same welfare rules we apply to the vertebrates we farm, and more specifically whether a shrimp operation ought to be required to stun animals before slaughter. I stayed out of it, and I genuinely could not tell who was winning. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it; I have numbered the distinct moves.

(1) The veterinarian: "The evidence is already good enough to act on. Decapods have opioid receptors, they groom and guard injured limbs, and in controlled experiments they will surrender a preferred shelter or a preferred food to avoid a noxious shock. That is a trade-off, not a reflex. Given the scale—billions of animals a year—and given how lopsided the possible errors are, we should require stunning. If we are wrong to protect them, we waste some money; if we are wrong not to, we inflict suffering on an enormous scale."

(2) The philosopher: "The trade-off data are weaker than they look. A nervous system can detect and avoid damage without any of it being felt; nociception is not pain, and those behaviors are consistent with a purely functional avoidance system. And your asymmetry argument does not get you to a rule—it is a reason to learn more, not to act, because it balances a probability you do not know against a magnitude you are assuming."

(3) Vet: "You do not need a precise probability when the downside is this large; that is the structure of every precautionary regulation we already accept. And 'nociception without pain' is a promissory note—nobody has shown that decapods lack the integrative machinery that would make it felt."

(4) Phil: "Then watch where that takes you. The same argument—small probability, enormous downside—applies to insects, to nematodes, to anything with a nervous system at all. If your principle cannot stop before it reaches the whole animal kingdom, it is not a principle, it is a mood. And the costs are not nothing: stunning at scale is expensive, and money spent here is money not spent on the vertebrates we already know suffer."

(5) Vet: "Proves-too-much is not an objection. Plenty of principles have vague edges and still give clear verdicts in clear cases. We do not reject 'rescue drowning children' because it fails to say what to do about puddles."

(6) Phil: "Except decapods are the contested case, not the clear case—you are using the principle to settle the very question in dispute. And the puddle analogy gives you away: you are treating 'might be sentient' as if it were 'is a child.' Here is a cleaner way to see it. Imagine a machine that detects damage, withdraws from it, and even trades one good off against another to avoid it, but that we would all agree has no inner life. Behavior plus trade-offs do not add up to experience. You need a further fact about the machinery, and you do not have it."

(7) Vet: "The machine example assumes the conclusion. You have built a system with no experience by stipulation and then told me it behaves like a decapod, as though that showed decapods are like the machine. What it shows is that behavior underdetermines the answer—which I grant—and that is exactly why the tie should be broken by the asymmetry, not by your confidence that there is nothing there. And if you demand certainty about a unified subject of experience before extending any protection, you would have failed to protect human infants and half the animals we now protect. The relevant question is whether there is something it is like for the animal, and the behavioral and pharmacological evidence is the best evidence anyone can get."

(8) Phil: "Infants are not the analogy—nobody seriously doubts that an infant has one subject of experience, whereas the whole question here is whether a decapod's nervous system supports one at all. And you keep sliding from 'we cannot rule it out' to 'we should act as if it is true.' If that slide is legitimate, you owe me a threshold—some marker decapods pass and insects fail—and I do not think you have one. Until you do, precaution is just a license to add whatever animals you like."

That was where the dinner ended.

I want a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict in the abstract. For each of the eight moves, rate it on a scale running from clearly mistaken, through defensible-but-overstated, to clearly right, and justify the rating in a few sentences, flagging any premise that is doing more work than it can bear. Then answer these specific questions: Does the asymmetry argument justify a rule, or only further research? Does the "proves too much" objection land, or does the "vague edges, clear cases" reply defuse it? Is the machine thought experiment a fair test or question-begging? Is the infant analogy apt or a category error? And is there a threshold of the kind the philosopher demands—if there is, what would it look like, and if there is not, does that vindicate the philosopher or merely show the question is hard?

Finally, tell me what both of them are missing—something neither said that bears on the dispute—and say which one is closer to right overall, and why. Assume I know the basic vocabulary of the debate; I am after the substance of this disagreement, not a survey of the field.
