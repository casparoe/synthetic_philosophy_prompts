I'm a freelance journo who mostly writes about science and ideas, and I'm halfway through a long read about paying people for bits of their bodies. I've hit a wall on the philosphy and I'd like you to sort me out.

The thing that's bugging me is the patchwork. Selling a kidney is a criminal offence pretty much everywhere — you can give one away, but money turns it into a crime, and it's a crime that's mostly committed by the surgeon doing the cutting rather than by the person on the table. Meanwhile, across the same rich countries: you can get paid for blood plasma in some places and not others; healthy volunteers get a few thousand pounds to swallow an experimental drug in a Phase I trial; egg donors get money (the UK calls it compensation and caps it); paid surrogacy is routine in parts of the US, while here you can have a surrogate but basically can't pay her more than her expenses; and one country runs a legal, state-regulated paid kidney programme, which as far as I can tell hasn't turned into the nightmare everyone predicticted. Oh, and you can be paid to clean windows off a skyscraper, which I'd have thought is a worse bet than donating a kidney.

So: what theory of value, or of the person, or of markets, is that patchwork actually committed to? I want you to reconstruct the moral view that the kidney ban only makes sense under. Is it that some things have a value money can't touch without wrecking it — that a price on a kidney degrades the kidney, the seller, or the relation between them, in a way a price on plasma doesn't? Is it coercion and exploitation, i.e. that consent from someone desperate doesn't really count? Is it consequences — that legal markets crowd out donors, or that the poor get picked off? Or is it something about medicine, i.e. that the ban is really a rule about what doctors may be paid to do, which would explain why it's the surgeon who gets prosecuted? Which of those does the ban actually rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? And where is the whole thing just underdetermined, so that three or four different theories would each license the same law?

Then I want the follow-up, which I suspect is where my piece actually goes: take the bits of the patchwork I listed and tell me which are genuinely inconsistent with the kidney ban and which only look inconsistent once you've assumed which theory is doing the work. If we swapped whatever the ban presupposes for a straight autonomy-and-welfare view, would we have to legalise organ sales, or would consistency force us the other way, to ban paid trials, paid egg donation and paid surrogacy too? And if the answer is that the real line is renewability — plasma grows back, a kidney doesn't — does that survive the fact that the donor of a kidney is also giving up something she may never miss?

Two housekeeping things. I'm in the UK, so if some of my examples don't travel, say so instead of humouring me, and I've definately got some of the details wrong somewhere. And please write it in British English. Don't give me a reading list — I need the argument, not a bibliography.
