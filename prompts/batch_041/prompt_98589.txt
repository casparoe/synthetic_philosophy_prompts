Hey, so I'm putting together handout materials for a grad seminar I'm TAing on the epistemology of testimony, and my undergrads keep collapsing everything into 'reductionist vs anti-reductionist' as if those were two clean camps facing off, which is driving me nuts because alot of the interesting disagreements seem to run orthogonal to that split. What I want from you is a one-page decision tree / taxonomy they can actually work through, something that sorts a reader's view by what they say about cases rather than by which camp they claim to belong to. You're a philosopher of epistemology who's taught this material for years, so please be careful and systematic: before you build anything, brainstorm the real fault lines on paper first (not just a list of famous names), then construct the tree. The deliverable is the tree plus a short note on each leaf.

Every internal node should be a question the reader answers, phrased as a concrete case or a flat-out claim, never as 'are you a reductionist?'. The leaves are the positions, and I need at least these to appear somewhere as leaves: global reductionism, local reductionism, a default-trust / entitlement style anti-reductionism, the view that testimony is a sui generis source of justification, and the outright skeptic who says testimony on its own never justifies belief. Two further things I care about that I don't think the textbook tree captures, so please build them in as branches: (a) whether a view is demanding the hearer's positive evidence or merely the absence of defeaters, since I'm pretty sure that cuts across the reductionism divide and the usual diagrams miss it; and (b) whether the view treats testimony uniformly across domains -- ordinary empirical claims, moral claims, and a person's report of their own current feelings -- because I keep noticing people who are anti-reductionist about scientific hearsay turn into sudden reductionists about moral testimony, and I want that inconsistency to become visible on the tree. It'd be great if one of your concrete cases is the reader trying to decide whether to believe something a chatbot or an unaccountable oracle told them, since that's a live version of the puzzle for them. You can use standard position names where they exist, but every node has to be answerable by someone who's never heard a single one of the labels.

Finally, please flag any combinations of answers the tree can't produce, or that look like nobody would actually hold, and say whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. And if two leaves end up indistinguishable on every case, don't paper over it -- tell me straight that the tree can't seperate them and why. Keep the whole thing to roughly ten to twelve nodes and leaves so it fits on a handout.
