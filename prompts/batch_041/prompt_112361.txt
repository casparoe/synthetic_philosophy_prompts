You are a philosopher of science and a working researcher in causal inference — someone who has spent years on the relationship between association, conditioning, and causation, and who is careful to distinguish what a dataset says from what a causal model says about it. Work in stages. First brainstorm the considerations that bear on the question below, including the ones that cut against the answer you expect to give. Then reason explicitly. Then commit to numbers. Do not hedge by declining to answer; if you think the question is ill-posed, say why and give your credence in the nearest well-posed version instead.

Consider the following dataset, small enough to hold in your head. Patients either receive a drug (X = 1) or do not (X = 0), and either recover (Y = 1) or do not (Y = 0). Some are referred to a specialist (Z = 1); the rest are not (Z = 0). The counts are:

Z = 1, referred:
- drug, recovered: 270
- drug, not recovered: 100
- no drug, recovered: 100
- no drug, not recovered: 30

Z = 0, not referred:
- drug, recovered: 30
- drug, not recovered: 100
- no drug, recovered: 100
- no drug, not recovered: 270

The associations come out as follows. Marginally, 300 of the 500 drug patients recovered, 60%, against 200 of the 500 non-drug patients, 40%: the drug looks beneficial. Within Z = 1, 270 of 370 drug patients recovered, about 73%, against 100 of 130 non-drug patients, about 77%: the drug looks harmful. Within Z = 0, 30 of 130 recovered on the drug, about 23%, against 100 of 370, about 27%: the drug looks harmful again. The marginal association points one way; both stratum-specific associations point the other.

Now suppose the causal structure is as follows: the drug causes specialist referral through a side effect, recovery also causes referral through follow-up care, and the drug genuinely affects recovery. On that reading Z is a common effect — a collider — of X and Y rather than a common cause, the reversal is collider-induced, conditioning on Z opens a path that manufactures a negative association between drug and recovery, and the marginal association is the one that answers the causal question.

But notice where the interest lies. Nothing in the numbers themselves reveals this structure. A table of this shape could equally have been generated by a common-cause story — say, an unrecorded severity variable that made patients both less likely to receive the drug and less likely to recover — in which case the stratum-specific associations would be the causal ones and the drug would be harmful. The data alone cannot tell you whether you are looking at a collider or a confounder. Background knowledge such as temporal order might, if you had it; the table does not supply it.

Here is the thesis I would like you to assess.

Thesis T: For this dataset, once the causal structure is fixed — X causes Y, X and Y both cause Z, and there are no other paths — there is a single determinate fact about which association, the marginal or the stratum-specific, correctly answers the causal question of whether the drug raises the probability of recovery in this population; that fact is fixed by the causal structure alone; and reaching it requires no further choice, convention, or value judgment beyond the specification of that structure.

I put my own credence in T at about 0.85. A colleague who works on causal discovery puts hers at about 0.35.

I want three things.

First, your credence in T, expressed as a number between 0 and 1.

Second, the decomposition. Break T into the sub-claims it actually rests on. Candidate sub-claims, which you may keep, drop, or replace: (i) that the causal structure of this case is determinate and knowable at all, rather than being one model among several that fit the data equally well; (ii) that, given the structure, the causal question has a unique answer, rather than being answerable only relative to a target quantity that has to be selected separately; (iii) that once the structure is fixed, no further convention or judgment enters — choice of population, choice of contrast, what counts as the drug helping, how the numbers are reported. Give each sub-claim a rough probability and one line of justification, say how the sub-claims combine into your overall figure, and check that the combination actually reproduces the number you gave for T.

Third, tell me which sub-claim my colleague at 0.35 and I at 0.85 most likely disagree about; which single sub-claim your own number is most sensitive to; and what evidence or argument would move that sub-claim most. If you think T is not the right thing to have a credence in — for instance because it presupposes that there is a fact about the structure — say so, state the thesis you would substitute, and give a number for that one instead.
