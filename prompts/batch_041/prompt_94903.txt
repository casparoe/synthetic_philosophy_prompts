I spent thirty-one years running an experimental physics group, and since retiring I have been trying, rather late, to learn some philosophy — a continuing-education seminar in the autumn, and now a reading group that has devoted the better part of a year to the surprise examination paradox. You will know the setup: a teacher announces that there will be an exam next week and that the students will not be able to know, in advance, on which day it will fall; the students reason backwards from Friday (if we reach Thursday evening with no exam, we shall know it is Friday, so it cannot be a surprise) and conclude that no day can satisfy the announcement, hence that no exam can occur; the exam is then given on Wednesday and surprises them. What has puzzled me is not the paradox but the group. We have worked through half a dozen treatments, and each strikes me as internally coherent while answering a slightly different question from the last. One holds that the students' backward induction is valid, so the teacher's announcement is simply false — indeed self-defeating, since a teacher cannot know what he announces. Another holds that the induction fails at the last step, because by Thursday evening the students' evidential situation has changed, and that surprise is a claim about what they will in fact believe, not about what follows from the announcement by an ideally rational reasoner. A third says the announcement may not be used as a premise at all, since you cannot reason from a proposition whose truth is the very thing in question — with the corollary, which I find striking, that the students' argument refutes its own premise and so cannot be sound whatever else is true. A fourth treats the whole matter as a demonstration that surprise cannot be known in advance, which is a point about the logic of knowledge rather than about exams. Nobody in the group has moved, and I had assumed that a puzzle this small and this formal would long ago have been settled the way a lemma gets settled.

My own suspicion — offered as a hypothesis, not as a position I can defend — is that the parties are not disagreeing about a single proposition, and perhaps not even asking the same question: one side treats surprise as a predicate of the students' actual psychological state, another as a claim about what is derivable from the announcement, a third as a diagnosis of the announcement's inconsistency, and these cannot be compared until someone says which notion of surprise the teacher meant and by what right the students may treat his words as a premise. I should say plainly that I do not want a survey of who said what, nor a bibliography — I cannot look anything up, and attributions would be wasted on me. What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what surprise or knowledge is to mean, several groups answering different questions under a single heading, or a sign that the puzzle as usually stated is ill-posed? Set out the positions concretely enough that your diagnosis engages them; test it against the degenerate one-day version of the puzzle, where the teacher says the exam is tomorrow and will be a surprise, and tell me whether the diagnosis predicts what the group does there; and say what would count as progress — a formalization that makes the options commensurable, a distinction that dissolves the conflict, a proof that the announcement is inconsistent as stated, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. I am particularly curious whether the students' reasoning is best understood as a self-referential prediction of the kind that is defeated by being believed, and whether that analogy does real work or is merely decorative. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing only a short label for your diagnosis — for example substantive, verbal, different questions, ill-posed, or mixed — with nothing after it.
