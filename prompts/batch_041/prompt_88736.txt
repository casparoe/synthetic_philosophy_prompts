I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on Plato's moral psychology, and I want to end by reading Euripides' Bacchae against the Athenian Stranger's defence of drunkenness in the Laws. What my students tend to miss, and what I want the reading to bring out, is that Plato there does not merely tolerate wine: in the first two books he treats it as an instrument of civic education. Wine is a drug that dissolves shame, makes a grown man childish and fearless, and so exposes what he is really like once his guard is down — which is why a drinking party, properly constituted, can serve as an inexpensive and low-risk test of self-control, in the way that a battlefield tests courage. The test is safe only because it is controlled: the company needs rules and a sober president to keep order, so that the drinker comes to know the shape of his own weakness and is shamed into moderating it. Wine has a second, gentler office in the dialogue as well, as a remedy for the austerity of old age, loosening the old so that they can sing and rejoin the chorus; and the whole discussion is framed by the image of the human being as a puppet of the gods, hauled about by the cords of pleasure and pain, with reason as the single golden cord that ought to govern the rest.

Now place the Bacchae beside this. Dionysus returns to Thebes to punish a house that refuses to acknowledge him, and Pentheus, the young king who tries to police the god, ends by dressing as a woman, spying on the maenads on the mountain, and being torn apart by his own mother. I want a reading that uses the Platonic account as a serious foil rather than as a moral the play merely illustrates. Does Euripides dramatize precisely what the Laws must presuppose away — that the god is not an instrument anyone can administer, and that the sober president is the very man the god destroys? Or can the play be turned the other way, with Pentheus as the pupil who was never educated in pleasure, whose furtive voyeurism is just what a well-run symposium is designed to expose and sublimate, so that his fate confirms Plato's diagnosis while refuting his confidence that the test can be kept harmless?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions. Tell me which scenes carry the argument — the confrontation with the disguised god, the seduction into spying, the messenger's report and Agave's recognition — where Plato's vocabulary of shame and self-control genuinely illuminates the drama, and where the play resists it; and take seriously the possibility that the play is at pains to distinguish the god's possession from mere drunkenness, so that wine may be the wrong lens after all. End by saying what the tragedy adds to the philosophical question that the Laws cannot: what does drama know about the limits of self-knowledge through intoxication that the argument does not?
