Write a parody of a patent office action — the examiner's written rejection of a pending application — in which the applicant is a fictional research group seeking to patent the ontology of a physical theory, and the examiner must decide whether an ontology whose central sector is stipulated to be in principle inaccessible has been identified in the sense the application claims. The piece should run to roughly 800 words and stay in the deadpan register of a real office action: an application number and filing date, a short summary of the claims, then numbered grounds of rejection, a response to the applicant's arguments, objections to the specification, and a closing paragraph that allows one narrow claim while rejecting the rest. Nothing should signal that the author knows the piece is funny.

The following background must be taken seriously, and may be introduced in the examiner's own words without citing any real publication. The application distinguishes an accessible algebra — the coarse-grained statistics that any agent described by the theory could in principle record — from an ontic sector: a set of fine-grained states together with a measure over them, selected so as to reproduce those statistics. The examiner's stated test for identification is that an ontology is identified when every element of the claimed ontic set is in principle discriminable by some procedure available to an agent described by the theory. The applicant concedes that a central subset of the ontic sector is dynamically screened off, so that no procedure available within the theory can discriminate among its elements, but argues that this is a feature of the theory rather than a defect in the identification, and that the ontology is identified up to an equivalence class which the application does specify.

The rejection should get the following right without ever stating them as lessons: that the accessible statistics underdetermine the ontic sector, so the identification is relative to a choice the applicant made rather than derived from anything; that a sector declared inaccessible in advance cannot license any prediction, so the surplus sits idle; that the applicant's equivalence class is defined by the very inaccessibility that is supposed to be incidental to it; and that the examiner's test, applied to the examiner's own evidence, is not obviously satisfiable either — let that asymmetry sit unresolved. Include one amendment in which the applicant narrows a claim and thereby concedes more than intended. Do not let the examiner reach a stable verdict on whether the theory has an ontology at all; the piece should end with the question still open in the paperwork.

Constraints: invent every name, application number, and document, and cite nothing real; name no living person; keep the humor in the substance, so that an examiner and an applicant are each reasoning correctly from premises that cannot both hold, and someone who works on unobservables and underdetermination finds the objections fair rather than merely absurd.
