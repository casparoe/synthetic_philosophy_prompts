I'm putting together the closing piece for a graduate seminar on social epistemology and rational choice, and a few of us have been arguing all term about whether a crowd can ever be epistemically respectable. I've drawn the topic of information cascades, and I'd like to end with a poem rather than a summary. The picture I want rendered is the standard one: each person in a sequence acts on their own private evidence until enough predecessors have acted, at which point it becomes rational to set that evidence aside and do what the person ahead did, so that a run of identical behavior can form with no leader, no conspiracy, and no shared reason — and the particular custom that gets locked in may be an accident of who happened to move first, which is why such runs are both arbitrary and strangely brittle, and why the first movers produce something they never get paid for. What I want is a pantoum, and I'm choosing the form deliberately. Its interlocking repetitions — the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third of the next — are the cascade itself: a sentence handed forward and read differently by the time it arrives, an act that was once evidence becoming precedent and then becoming mere background. Better still, the pantoum's opening first and third lines cannot reappear until the very end, which is exactly the fate of a private signal in a sequence that has already settled. So keep the repetitions exact, and place my one piece of first-hand evidence where it can only come back as the final line, after everyone has committed. Give me a concrete scene rather than an abstraction — a street of shops, a village deciding whether to leave, a jury room, a department hiring — anywhere a sequence of people each sees one thing for themselves and then watches what the person in front of them does. Someone who has taught the theory should be able to point to the first two actors whose genuine evidence is doing the work, the moment the threshold is crossed, the single defection that could shatter the whole thing, and the arbitrariness of where it lands; someone who hasn't should simply find it a good poem. Five or six stanzas is plenty, and rhyme only where it doesn't force a lie.

After the poem, add a short prose note, a few sentences at most, saying where the poem takes liberties with the theory or leans on a contested reading — whether it exaggerates how completely private information is thrown away, whether the sequence I've built is one the formal models would actually license, whether the fragility I've made the emotional center of the piece is a feature of the models or an artistic addition. If the poem's central image is one a careful theorist would reject, I'd rather hear it from you than from my advisor, and I'd like you to say which version of the cascade would force me to rewrite the ending.
