I have to present my term paper in our Limits of Computation seminar next week — it's cross-listed, so the room is about ten CS theory grad students who think P vs NP is the deepest problem there is, three philosophy of science people, and my supervisor, who works on explanation in mathematics and is definately going to ask me something I can't answer. My thesis: P and NP aren't natural kinds, they're artifacts of the polynomial-time yardstick. The argument I'm running: the classes only look robust because every reasonable model of computation can simulate every other with a polynomial blowup, but those blowups hide enormous constants and the definitions are asymptotic, so nothing anyone actually computes ever falls inside a class — the whole edifice of intrinsic difficulty is bookkeeping that pays off in practice and nowhere else. I also say that NP-completeness turning up in things like protein folding and spin glasses doesn't rescue it, since what turns up there is another idealization.
