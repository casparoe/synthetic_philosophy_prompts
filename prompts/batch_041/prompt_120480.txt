I teach a Master's-level seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science, taken jointly by philosophers and by students from a machine-learning background, and I am setting the final assessment for the module on representation and interpretability. My students will have met linear probes (simple classifiers trained on a network's internal activations to predict a labelled property of the input) and sparse autoencoders (which learn a large set of sparsely active directions in activation space). What I do not want is an answer that rehearses the interpretability literature. I want an answer that says what would have to be true for a representational claim about a network to be warranted, and that notices how much of that is a matter of choice rather than discovery.

Please write me one essay question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 25. Candidates have 75 minutes for the question and no access to any literature, so the question must be self-contained: it may name techniques, but it must not require candidates to attribute claims to named authors or to recall published results, and neither the model answer nor the marking scheme may depend on citations or reading lists.

Build the question around the following test, which I have invented for this paper and which no student will have seen:

A state of a network counts as representing a property only if (i) it carries information about that property, in the sense of being reliably correlated with it across the inputs the network is likely to encounter; (ii) it is load-bearing, in the sense that intervening on it alters the network's behaviour in ways that track the property rather than a confound; and (iii) it is non-substitutable, in the sense that no description of the network's computation that omits the property predicts its behaviour as well as a description that mentions it.

The question should ask candidates to assess this test rather than to apply it: whether it returns the verdicts we want in clear cases, where it over-generates (certifying a mere correlate as a representation), where it under-generates (withholding the label where we are inclined to grant it), which of the three clauses is doing the real work, and whether the test is a criterion we could actually apply or a research programme in disguise.

The question must also put this case to them: two networks that agree in their outputs on every input anyone has tried, and that are probed with equal success for the same property, yet whose internal organisation differs — in one, a single direction in activation space tracks the property; in the other, the property is recoverable only by a probe that pools many directions. Candidates should say what, if anything, this shows for the realist who holds that interpretability findings describe the model rather than the analyst's preferred basis, and what it shows for the instrumentalist who denies this.

Write the model answer as finished philosophical prose at the standard of the strongest student in the room, not as a checklist. It should mark explicitly, as it goes, which of its claims are settled among people who work on interpretability and which are live disputes, so that a reader can see where it is taking a side, and it must not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Build the marking scheme around five must-hit points worth four marks each, with the remaining five marks for the overall shape of the argument; give a one-line note for each must-hit point on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a short closing note on what separates a first-class answer from an upper second.
