I teach a 200-level seminar called Knowledge, Media, and Doubt -- 24 students, mostly second- and third-years, a few journalism majors, we meet once a week for 75 minutes and I have one full session I can spend on this. Here's the thing I keep failing to get across. I use "echo chamber" loosely for two situations that I'm now convinced are different animals, and my students nod and then go right on treating them as one thing. Case one: a group just never runs into the other side. Nothing sinister about it -- same three outlets, everyone they know agrees, the counter-evidence simply isn't in the room. Case two: a group runs into the other side constantly, and has been handed a standing reason to wave off every outsider -- funded by the wrong people, no institutional affiliation, probably a bot, has an axe to grind -- so every new piece of outside information lands as more confirmation that the outside is rotten. I want an activity where they feel the difference in their bones instead of hearing me announce it, and in particular I want them to feel that the obvious remedy (give people more access to the other side) does something completely differnt in the two cases -- it fixes the first and can actually make the second worse. Design me something runnable in that 75 minutes.

Concretely, I'm picturing four teams of six, each chasing the same factual question where there's a real, checkable answer we can reveal at the end (I'll take your suggestion on what question works -- it should be the kind of thing where a team can be confident and wrong). Each team gets a packet of source cards I can print and shuffle; two of the teams are in the "never heard it" condition and two are in the "has heard it and has a reason to discount it" condition, with the true answer living on an outsider card that carries a genuine-looking discrediting marker. I want the in-group cards to be actually decent on other questions, so the trust heuristic isn't stupid, and I want a second round where everyone gets more cards, including more outsider cards, so the two conditions diverge. Give me the rules, the materials and how to prepare them (roughly how many cards, what the markers should say), the roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief -- which should map specific moments in the game onto the distinction I'm after, and should tell them what the different remedies actually are in each case, since I think that's the payoff. Don't name either concept or let the labels surface until the debrief.

And please be honest with me about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm already worried the teams will compare packets in the hallway, that someone will smell the rig and refuse to play along, and that the discrediting markers will look so obviously planted that the whole thing reads as a lecture on credulity rather than a demonstration. Second, which features of the real phenomenon the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief: the fact that in real life these things are held up over years by the members themselves rather than by me handing out cards, that the beliefs are usually load-bearing for someone's identity, and that a lot of real-world distrust of outsiders is epistemically warranted rather than a pathology -- I don't want them leaving with a smug "those people are just dumb" takeaway, and I don't want the game to teach them that trusting credentialed insiders is always the rational move.
