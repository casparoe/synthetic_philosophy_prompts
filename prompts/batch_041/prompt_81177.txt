For thirty-five years I worked as a soil ecologist at a regional agricultural research station, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy in a haphazard, self-directed way. I have run into a problem of a kind I suspect philosophers have a name for, though I do not know it. Eight regional stations, mine included until last year, have spent three years debating whether to adopt a new instrument-based soil-mapping protocol in place of a laborious sampling procedure. The way the case for it has grown bothers me. One station adopted it early, and I happen to know their director already had the instrument on hand from an unrelated grant, so the cost of trying it was trivial for them; they reported that it worked well. A second station adopted it citing the first's success, a third citing both, and by now six have adopted it, with the consortium's annual report describing it as the emerging standard and each station's write-up citing the growing number of adopters as further support. What almost nobody says aloud is that each station's own comparison was small, underpowered, and equivocal, and that the equivocal results were never written up, so the public record consists of six endorsements resting on very little independent measurement. I ran a modest comparison of my own before I retired; the differences ran slightly against the new protocol, though the error bars were wide enough that I could not defend the finding to anyone's satisfaction, and when I raised it at the last meeting I was told, more or less kindly, that with six stations reporting success my little sample was noise.

What I want from you is advice about what to do and how to think about it, since I cannot tell whether my unease is scientific scruple or merely vanity at being contradicted. Specifically: can it be reasonable for each station, one after another, to adopt on the strength of others' adoption while the aggregate record remains uninformative about whether the protocol is actually better; what follows from the fact that the later endorsements add no new measurement of their own; how a consensus of this sort typically forms, and what it is vulnerable to; what a single person in my position can realistically do, given that I am retired, hold no formal vote, and dislike making a scene; and whether I should treat the fact that everyone around me regards the question as settled as itself a reason to defer. Please do not send me reading lists or citations — I want the substance, not the bibliography. Give your answer as exactly five numbered pieces of advice, one to two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing paragraph.
