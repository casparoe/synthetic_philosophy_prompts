I'm a fourth-year PhD student in economics, and I've been drafted onto my university's graduate-employee bargaining committee this spring — largely because I TA'd the game-theory sequence and someone on the steering committee remembers that I once explained the Nash solution on a whiteboard. We are about to enter negotiations with the administration over a single package that bundles four issues: a stipend floor, a health-insurance premium subsidy, a childcare allowance, and protections for remote work. Our side needs a "fairness story" to put in front of both the administration and our own membership, and a colleague has proposed anchoring the whole thing to the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution — the bargaining solution that, given a disagreement point and a convex feasible set, returns the point on the Pareto frontier lying on the ray from the disagreement point through the ideal (utopia) point, i.e. the maximal point that preserves the ratio of each side's gains over disagreement, and that is standardly motivated by replacing Nash's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom with a monotonicity requirement. My worry is that we are reaching for a formal object whose practical content none of us has actually thought through.

What I want from you is the most important implications of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution for how we should actually run this negotiation — not a restatement of the solution, and emphatically not an exhaustive list; I want you to exercise judgment about which consequences matter most and defend that ranking. I am especially interested in four things. First, what the solution implies about how we should think about, and possibly try to move, the disagreement point — our strike fund, the administration's reputational costs, the possibility that the current contract simply rolls over. Second, whether it counsels publicizing a specific "fair-split" formula or staying vague, given that the solution makes the agreement depend on the whole feasible set rather than on a pairwise comparison of one proposal against another. Third, how it handles the awkward fact that our ideal points are far more ambitious than anything the administration will concede, and whether the solution's appeal to the utopia point is a feature or a trap in that situation. Fourth, and most important to me, where the solution quietly assumes away the features of bargaining that will actually determine our outcome: that we are trading across four incommensurable issues rather than splitting one divisible sum, that our feasible set and the administration's are not the same object and are not common knowledge, and that neither side's true ideal point is observable. If the honest conclusion is that invoking Kalai–Smorodinsky would do more for our internal morale than for our leverage, say so plainly and explain which implications survive that verdict.
