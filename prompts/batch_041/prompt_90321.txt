The episode. Amsterdam, early 1940s, Nazi occupation. A small group of ordinary people — a couple of office workers, a secretary, some shopkeepers — are hiding Jewish families, the Franks among them, in a canal-side office building. When German police or their Dutch collaborators come around asking whether anyone is hidden there, or whether a particular person has been seen, the helpers lie to their faces, more or less calmly. It's one of the best documented rescue cases we have. (There are messier variants — whole villages that ran a standing system of deception, like the ones on the plateau in southern France — but let's keep the Amsterdam case in the middle of the frame.)

The theory is Kant on lying, and specifically the famous case of the murderer at the door who asks you where your friend is hiding. I want the mapping run hard in both directions.

So: who is the murderer here, and who is the liar? What exactly is the lie — does it even fit Kant's own account of a lie, as an intentional untruthful declaration to another person, with no requirement that it harm anyone in particular? Or could the helpers have done something subtler that isn't a lie at all: refusing to answer, or telling a truth that happens to mislead, or a mental reservation of the sort the casuists allowed? Does the murderer have a right to the truth from you, and if he doesn't, why doesn't that alone settle it in Kant's favour? Kant's own reason for thinking the lie is wrong seems to be that it damages humanity in general and the very ground of right, not merely the person lied to — say what work that idea is doing and whether it survives contact with this case.

Then the hard part. The standard line is that Kant forbids the lie come what may, and everyone since has used that as the reductio. But there's a revisionist reading — I find it the more interesting one — that the essay is really jurisprudence rather than ethics: it's aimed at Constant's claim that there is a right to lie, and its argument is about what a lie does to the possibility of contract and right among people, not about what a person hiding a family ought to do. On that reading Kant never quite said you must tell the murderer the truth. Take the reading seriously, say exactly what it changes and what it leaves untouched, and then tell me whether the rescue case is still a counterexample to Kant once you've adopted it — or whether the absolutist reputation is partly a confusion about what the essay was for.

And the other direction, which I care about most. What does this episode do to the theory? Is it a clean counterexample, an anomaly the theory can absorb with a few epicycles, or a case it never anticipated — one where the neat picture of a duty not to lie colliding with a duty to help falls apart, because the liar and the lied-to stand in no ordinary moral relation at all? Where is Kant just silent, and where is he speaking but saying something we now find incredible? Dont force him to have an answer he doesn't have.
