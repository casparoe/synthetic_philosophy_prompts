I'm a third-year PhD student working on historical method and Christian origins, and I want a number out of you on something I've been stuck on for months.

The background: my whole field sorts sayings and deeds into "probably goes back to Jesus" and "probably comes from the early tradition" using a grab-bag of criteria — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, Semitic flavour, fit with a Galilean Jewish setting, and so on. None of them are stated as anything better than rules of thumb, of the form "if it's embarrassing, it's probably old."

I've been trying to restate each one as an explicit likelihood ratio — P(we'd see this datum if the saying is authentic) over P(we'd see it if the saying is inauthentic) — so the whole thing becomes a Bayesian machine instead of a vibe. My supervisor thinks this is a waste of time and I half think she's right, for two reasons.

First, the denominator. "The saying is inauthentic" isn't a hypothesis, it's a shrug. The early tradition could produce a saying, a parable, a miracle story, a saying that sounds awkwardly Semitic — so for alot of data the denominator is effectively unconstrained, and I can set it wherever I need to in order to get the answer I already wanted. The criteria stop being tests and become a way of laundering priors.

Second, the criteria are one-sided by construction. They were invented to include material, not to exclude it: nobody formulated the criterion of embarrassment so that *failing* it counts against a saying. So the apparatus can only ever pile up confirmations, and it's hard to see how a pile of one-sided tests converges on anything.

But maybe the fix is just to insist on a real generative model of how sayings mutate as they move through oral tradition and then through Greek — once you have that, the denominators are pinned down by the model and everything works.

So here's the number I want. What's your credence that there exists *some* set of criteria — not necessarily the traditional list, and granting unlimited cleverness in constructing them — that could in principle recover, from the evidence we actually have, which sayings are authentic, with at least 0.8 accuracy? Take "authentic" to mean "traceable to a first-century Galilean Jewish teacher who was executed by Rome," so the target at least has a truth value. If 0.8 accuracy is the wrong way to state the bar, say so and restate it, but still give me a number. And please don't just tell me it's a live debate — I know it's a live debate, that's why I'm asking.

Decompose it. I want, at minimum:

- the probability that "authentic," applied to an individual saying, is well-defined enough to be true or false at all (individuation, Greek into Aramaic and back, what counts as the same saying)
- the probability that a generative model of the tradition could be constrained enough, in principle, to fix the inauthentic denominator
- the probability that the criteria could be made evidentially independent of each other, or their correlations modeled (multiple attestation across sources that may not be independent is the obvious case)
- the probability that the surviving evidence base is rich enough that even a perfect method would reach 0.8
- anything else you think belongs in the product

Number each, one line of justification each, then combine them and give me the final figure. For my part I'm at about 0.2, and I'd like to know whether you think I'm low because I'm right about the denominator problem or just because I've been reading too much sceptical stuff.

Also tell me how confident you are in the whole thing, and single out the one sub-probability you're least sure of.

Last line of your answer should be exactly \answer{<your final credence as a decimal>}.
