I'm in a reading group that's spent a term circling AI welfare and we keep talking past each other. I want to build a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually commit to, not by which label they'd claim. The question it sorts on: which computations matter morally, and where the line between sentience-relevant computation and everything else gets drawn.

Each node should be a question someone could answer without knowing where it leads. Roughly these cuts, in whatever order you think is cleanest:

- does moral status attach to a computational role (so any substrate running it counts) or to a particular physical process (so the same computation on silicon doesn't)?
- does mattering need a subject -- someone it's like something for -- or can there be valenced states that nobody undergoes?
- is "this computation is valenced" a fact to be discovered, or a stance we take toward the system for the purposes of predicting and treating it? and if it's a stance, does AI welfare become a policy choice rather than a finding?
- are the units whole systems, subsystems, or brief events, and is status a threshold or a degree?

Leaves should be named positions where there's a name, "unnamed" where there isn't, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please make sure the tree includes at least: a substrate-locked view, a view on which the relevant computation needn't be available to the system's own control or report, one on which valenced states get duplicated across subsystems and we have to say which copy is the bearer, and an attribution-first view on which the question as posed is malformed. Build it from the logical space first and place the positions into it after, then tell me which combinations come out empty and whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree can't really separate. Keep it to about ten nodes and give me something I can hand out.
