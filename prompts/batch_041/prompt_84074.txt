I read an essay a while back (some substack, I dont remember the author) about why policy people keep getting burned by historical analogies, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it. Here's the gist.

Analogies mislead because history doesn't repeat itself — every situation is unique, so when someone says "this is Munich" or "this is Vietnam" they're just dressing up a hunch in borrowed authority. Munich is the showcase example: the lesson everyone drew was that you never appease a dictator, and that lesson got carried straight into Vietnam, where it did real damage. Then there's the so-called Thucydides trap. Thucydides, who invented the idea, argued that when a rising power threatens a ruling power, war is structurally inevitable — structure decides the outcome and the choices of the people involved don't really matter, which the essay says is exactly why the analogy is so seductive, because it lets you skip the messy business of judging intentions and just read the result off the power balance. The fix it proposes is to stop reasoning by analogy at all and lean on general causal theories instead (deterrence theory, bargaining theory, the democratic peace), since those tell you what actually drives outcomes, whereas analogies only tell you what the last crisis looked like. And it offers a rule of thumb it calls "deep structure": if two situations share the same underlying causal mechanism, the analogy is really just an instance of the theory and you didn't need it; if they dont, the analogy is worthless.

I'm about half convinced, but the Thucydides part smells off to me and I have a nagging feeling the essay is doing the exact thing it warns about. I just can't see where the seams are.
