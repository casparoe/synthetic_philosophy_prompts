I keep a positions wiki for a small philosophy-of-mind reading group that meets above a pub, and the consciousness page has metastasised: everyone in the room signs up to the same one-liner — that experience is not a late add-on to an otherwise purely physical world — and then everyone means something different by it, so we spend the hour re-deriving each other's views instead of arguing about them. What I want from you is a decision tree: a classification instrument, not an essay. It should take a reader from that single shared starting commitment to a named position about how the experiences of simple things (or their intrinsic natures, or whatever the reader thinks plays that role) relate to the experience of a whole organism like a person. The whole point is to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like, so every branch node has to be phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing where it leads, and where a node needs a term of art you should gloss it in a parenthesis in plain language. Assume the reader has read no philosophy beyond a couple of popular science books and has no internet access, so the instrument has to be entirely self-contained: define each position by its commitments, supply the standard name where there is one, but never make the tree depend on the reader already recognising it. No reading lists, citations, or bibliographies anywhere — I want the instrument, not a syllabus.

Start from the commitment that micro-level facts are experiential in character, and let the first fork be whether that means tiny subjects or merely qualities that nobody in particular feels. The tree needs to contain at least these distinctions, in whatever order actually makes them diagnostic rather than decorative: whether the bottom level contains subjects at all, or only qualities; whether the experience of the whole has a subject of its own; whether that subject is numerically additional to the micro-subjects, identical with a fusion of them, or nothing over and above them; whether the relation that binds micro-experiences into one is taken as primitive or as something that could in principle be analysed into non-experiential terms; and whether the micro-subjects survive as parts of the whole or are extinguished, absorbed, or replaced when the whole comes into being. That last one matters to us because the group keeps splitting over a case I invented, in which a moment of experience is built out of two micro-experiences that fade out exactly as the built-up experience fades in, so there is never an instant at which all three are present — the question being whether that is combination at all or one experience dying into another. Also give me a fork for combination that is additive (the whole's experience is the union of the parts', nothing lost) versus subtractive (something is lost in the combining), and a fork for views on which no whole ever has an experience, the felt unity being a representational artefact. I want the tree to be able to reach, at minimum, leaves for: subjects combining under a primitive binding relation; subjects whose combination is claimed to be analysable; qualities combining with subjects somehow constructed afterwards; a single cosmic subject with individual subjects as derivative or illusory; the whole's experience as something genuinely new; and the no-whole-experience option. Draw it as an indented outline so I can paste it straight into the wiki.

Give me eight to twelve leaves. Each leaf carries the position's name, or 'unnamed' if nothing fits, plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders have to swallow. Where two leaves would be indistinguishable to a reader by the questions above, say so explicitly instead of pretending the tree separates them, and tell me which extra question, if any, would prise them apart. Where a combination of answers is logically available but has no defenders, include it as an unoccupied leaf and diagnose it in a clause: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finish with a three-line guide to reading a reader's answers off the tree — which pair of answers fixes which leaf — and a note on which single answer, if someone gives it, makes the rest of the tree moot.
