I teach the second half of a graduate seminar on the history of economic thought, and this year I am the one grading the midterm. The last section of the exam asked the students to apply Keynes's account of probability to a case I invented rather than to the standard illustrations, precisely because I wanted to see whether they could put the apparatus to work instead of reciting it. Before I hand the papers back, I would like a second read from you: a grade and, more than the grade, the specific comments that justify it, since I intend to turn your assessment into the written feedback I give the student. Assume the section is worth 20 points and that a competent answer would address each part of the question in proportion to the space it is given.

Here is the question: "Consider a national reinsurance pool that is asked to underwrite a novel class of industrial liability: accidents involving autonomous haulage vehicles at open-pit mines. There is no loss history for the class, no closely comparable line of business, and the actuaries report that the relevant failure modes are not yet settled. Using the account of probability and uncertainty in Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability*, distinguish three positions the pool might occupy, as we set them out in class: (i) it can rank the relevant probabilities against one another although it cannot assign them numerical values; (ii) it cannot rank them, because for some pairs of alternatives it can say neither that one is more likely nor that the two are equally likely; (iii) the difficulty lies not in the probabilities but in the space of outcomes, since the pool cannot claim that its list of possible failure modes is complete. For each position, say what the pool can and cannot claim to know, what work the weight of the argument is doing, if any, and what follows for the decision to underwrite. Then say whether Keynes's apparatus reaches case (iii) or whether the theory runs out there, and defend your answer."

And here is the answer I have set aside to look at again, from a student who is generally strong — which is why I want a careful look rather than a quick impression: "Keynes's central claim is that probability is a logical relation between propositions rather than a fact about anyone's state of mind: to say that a conclusion is probable to a certain degree given the evidence is to say that the evidence stands in a determinate logical relation to the conclusion. This is why he attacks the frequency theory, which identifies probability with the limiting frequency in a series of similar events. So in case (i) the pool can say something genuinely true about its situation even without numbers: it can know that one risk is more probable than another, because the evidence bears a stronger logical relation to the one than to the other, and the absence of a numerical value reflects the comparability of the relations rather than the pool's confidence. The weight of the argument is what carries the pool from case (i) to case (ii). Keynes distinguishes the degree of a probability from the amount of evidence on which it rests, and where the evidence is thin the weight is low. The autonomous haulage class is exactly this: with no history and no settled classification of failure modes, the evidence is too thin to support any ranked probability at all, and we are in the region Keynes calls uncertainty — the same territory Knight marked off as distinct from risk. Case (ii) is therefore just the limiting case of low weight, and case (iii) is that same phenomenon pushed one step further, where the weight has fallen to nothing. What follows for the decision is that in cases (ii) and (iii) Keynes's theory has nothing to offer the pool except the counsel to refrain: if no probability can be assigned, there is no rational basis for action, and the rational underwriter stays out. Keynes's remark that long-term expectation is governed by animal spirits, in the Treatise, is not an exception to this; it is a description of what people do when they act without rational warrant."

Please give me a grade out of 20 and a point-by-point assessment: for each part of the question, say what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or leaves undone, and how much each error should cost. Where you think a slip is serious enough to change the grade substantially, say so explicitly and explain why; where you think an apparent slip is actually defensible on a charitable reading, say that too, and where an error is being compounded by another error, note that the two should not be charged twice.
