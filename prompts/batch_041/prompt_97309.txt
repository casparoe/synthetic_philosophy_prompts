Question 3. Multiple realizability is usually trotted out as the main reason to prefer functionalism over the type-identity theory. (a) Reconstruct that argument in your own words. (b) Does it actually get you all the way to functionalism, or only part of the way? If only part, what else is needed? (c) Now suppose a system reproduces the causal profile of human pain only at a very coarse grain — it avoids and withdraws from damage, but its internal goings-on diverge from ours at every finer grain. And here's what the student wrote:

"Multiple realizability is the claim that a mental state type like pain can be realized by alot of different physical state types. Putnam's version of the argument goes roughly like this. If pain were identical with some physical type, say C-fiber firing, then anything that felt pain would have to have C-fibers. But we have decent reason to think an octopus, or some silicon-based system, could feel pain without any C-fibers. So pain is not identical with C-fiber firing, and by the same reasoning no mental type is identical with any physical type. Since mental states can't be identified with physical states, they have to be identified with something more abstract, namely their functional role — their characteristic causes and effects. And thats just functionalism. So multiple realizability does give you functionalism.

Does it get all the way? I think so. Once you accept that what matters is the role and not the material, your a functionalist. The only thing left would be to say mental states aren't physical at all, which nobody wants.

On the coarse-grain case: I'd say yes, its in pain. Functionalism says all that matters is the role, and the role just is the pattern of causes and effects — if the coarse pattern lines up, the fine details are irrelevant. So the case doesn't change my answer in (b); it's just more multiple realization.

One caveat: I know functionalism gets accused of being behaviorism in disguise. I don't think that's right, but it's true that functionalism is basically behaviorism done more carefully — it says mental states are dispositions to behave, except the dispositions can be internal."
