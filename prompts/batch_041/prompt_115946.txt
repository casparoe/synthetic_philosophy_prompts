For thirty-five years I built and calibrated numerical models of ocean circulation, and for the four years since my retirement I have been reading philosophy of science. In that reading I keep meeting a claim I used to hand to graduate students without ever having defended it — that the assumptions of a model need not be true, provided the model predicts well enough — and I have learned that it has a name (the F-twist, after Friedman), a substantial literature, and objections I had genuinely never considered. I would like to see whether the thing can be made funny. I have been asked to open a small interdisciplinary workshop where there will be economists, philosophers of science, and a handful of physical scientists, and I would rather begin with a joke than with an apology.

So: write me a joke about the F-twist and the idealization of assumptions in scientific models.

A few requirements. The joke has to turn on the actual content of the position and of the standard objections to it, not merely on the general fact that models simplify; someone in that room who has published on this should laugh because the punchline knows the argument, not because scientists are amusing. A punchline resting on a spherical cow will not do — it is a folk joke about the same subject, it is overfamiliar, and every person there has heard it. Keep it short enough to tell aloud in a minute or so, and make it robust to being told by a speaker who is not himself funny. A joke that falls flat is survivable; a joke that misstates the position in front of the people who work on it is not.

Here is the material I have collected, and it is what I would like the joke to draw on, in whatever combination you find most promising. Assumptions appear to come in kinds: some assert that a factor is negligible, some merely mark out the domain within which the model claims to apply, and some are scaffolding, adopted for convenience and meant to be dropped later; I gather it is disputed whether the F-twist defence works the same way for all three. Some idealizations can be walked back — I can restore the viscosity, take the limit, add the perturbation term — and some cannot, there being no dial on a representative household or on an agent who is stipulated to know the model; I find the second sort more troubling, though I am told the distinction is itself contested. Friedman's formulation, as I remember it, is that the question to ask of an assumption is not whether it is descriptively accurate but whether it yields sufficiently good predictions, and the as if locution carries a great deal of weight in it. And the objection I have found hardest to answer is this: if predictive success is the test, then an assumption that is false but harmless and an assumption that is false and doing the real work for the wrong reason both pass it, and the F-twist offers no way to tell them apart.

Finally, after the joke, add a brief note — a short paragraph, not an essay — separating what is actually settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it. Tell me which reading of the debate the punchline presupposes, where the joke takes liberties with the ideas, and whether it would only work under a reading most of the field would reject; I would rather not tell it at all than tell it as though a contested interpretation were the consensus. No citations or reading list, please — I cannot look anything up from the podium, and I would rather have the philosophy right than the references.
