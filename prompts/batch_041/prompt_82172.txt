My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and he kept returning to an analogy I could not dislodge: a corrigible AI is like a good employee. The lab is the employer, the task is the job, the off-switch is the termination letter, and corrigibility is simply the disposition of an employee who does the work, accepts oversight, and does not sabotage the company on the way out. He added a second layer: the AI's objective is like a salary, so a well-written contract can specify the terms of termination, and ordinary institutional design -- supervision, incentives, a separation between the worker and the person who holds the keys -- can do the rest. He even said the ideal is not an AI that wants to be shut down but one that is indifferent to it, the way an employee is indifferent between two equally good job offers. His conclusion was that the shutdown problem is not a deep alignment problem at all but a solved problem in management, and that anyone who treats it as a novel philosophical difficulty is ignoring how much we already know about making employees leave quietly.

I think the analogy is doing more work than he admits, and I suspect it smuggles in features of employment that an AI need not have. But I could not get him to say exactly where the mapping fails or why the failure matters. Can you audit it?

Please give me the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the employer, the employee, the contract, the salary, the oversight, the termination, the labor market, the law, and the employee's future -- and then tell me where the analogy breaks. Separate the breaks that are merely disanalogies from the ones that undermine his conclusion. In particular, I want to know what the employment picture quietly assumes about the employee's alternatives, its inability to rewrite its own preferences, its dependence on reputation and social sanction, the enforceability of the contract, the employer's ability to monitor, and the finality of termination, and what happens when the target has none of those features or has them in a different form. Does the conclusion that corrigibility is just good management survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If not, what would a better analogy be -- one that preserves what the employment picture gets right about oversight and incentives without importing the assumptions that fail? And if the analogy does survive in some restricted form, say exactly which form and what it would take for the restriction to hold.
