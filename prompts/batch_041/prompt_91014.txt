I've been rereading the justice bits of Pascal's Pensées and I can't work out what he's actually claiming. Justice without force is useless, force without justice is tyranny — fine — but then he seems to just concede the first half: since we couldn't make what's just be the strong thing, we made what's strong pass for just, and custom counts as the whole of equity for no reason other than that it's been received. That reads like conventionalism with a shrug. Except he obviously does believe in a real justice, it's just that God has it and we don't, and he also tells himself to keep a private thought behind the public one and talk like the crowd. So which is it — a relativist with a theological escape hatch, or something more interesting?

A friend who does litigation says I'm reading him wrong and gave me a distinction I can't decide wether it's real or a rescue job. She says Pascal isn't offering a theory of what justice is but of what law is for: not to track desert, but to make the question of desert stop coming up. She calls it the silencer view. Her analogy is a triage tie-break rule — any tie-breaker you pick is arbitrary, and the rule only does its job if nobody relitigates the arbitrariness once it's been applied. So on her reading the arbitrariness isn't a residue Pascal forgot to clean up, it's the mechanism.

Write me the essay: is the silencer reading right, and if it is, does it leave Pascal a conventionalist anyway or actually get him out? And tell me whether the silencer view needs his theology at all — because if a thoroughgoing secularist can say the same thing, I want to know whats still distinctively Pascal. Take a side, don't end on "both readings have merit," and say what the verdict costs.

Format: exactly five numbered paragraphs, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no closing summary — if there's a verdict, let it land in the fifth one as a claim rather than a recap.
