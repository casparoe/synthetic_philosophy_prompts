I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of cognitive science seminar, and our unit on collective and group cognition has split the class into two camps over the ant-colony case — the thought experiment in which a colony, viewed at the right scale, seems to be doing something thoughtful rather than merely churning. In place of a term paper, the instructor has asked each pair of us to produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: a document that two people who genuinely disagree could both put their names to. I have drawn the assignment, so please write it.

We are all working from the same stipulated case, which the seminar invented and which is not a description of any real species, so nothing needs to be looked up:

(a) A mature colony of the stipulated species (call it Ant S) has roughly two million workers. Each worker lives about six weeks and interacts with at most a few dozen nestmates; no worker ever encounters more than a vanishing fraction of the colony or its territory.

(b) The colony holds its fungus garden within a narrow band of temperature and humidity across years, adjusting to a slow climatic cycle no worker lives long enough to observe even one full period of.

(c) If the colony is divided by a barrier into two halves, each half rebuilds a functioning colony, and the halves settle into stable but different foraging strategies. The difference persists for many generations and the halves never reconverge.

(d) If a predator removes a random thirty per cent of foragers, colony foraging returns to baseline within a day. If the same number are removed in a structured way — all the ants patrolling one sector — the colony's behaviour shifts permanently.

(e) If two colonies are joined by a bridge they sometimes merge; when they do, which of the two foraging strategies survives cannot be predicted from any property of any individual ant.

(f) Every measure we can apply to a single worker shows something extremely simple: no planning beyond a few seconds, no central pacemaker, no individual that is doing anything we would independently call representing.

The colony-cognition camp holds that the colony is itself a genuine cognitive system: it carries states that stand for conditions in its world (the state of the garden, the presence of a predator), those states can be accurate or inaccurate and are used to control the colony's behaviour, and the colony has interests and can be mistaken about its own situation. On this view, the fact that the representing is spread across ants and structures is no more disqualifying than the fact that a brain's representing is spread across neurons.

The nestmate-minimalist camp holds that everything deserving the name mentality belongs to individual ants, and that colony-level description is a predictive convenience — of the same kind as saying that a market expects a rate cut or that a river is searching for the sea. For a state to represent something, on this view, there must be a subject for whom it is a state; the colony has no unified point of view and no integration that makes a difference to any single locus, so the information it is said to carry is a bookkeeping artifact of the observer.

Please produce the document in three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps can sign, about the stipulated case, about what it would take for something to be a cognitive system at all, and about what the case does and does not show. These must be substantive — a claim either camp could abandon without cost does not belong in the list. Where a formulation is so loose that both sides could sign anything, sharpen it and say what the sharpened version commits them to.

Second, the disagreements, as a table with exactly these columns: Disagreement in neutral terms | Colony-cognition formulation | Nestmate-minimalist formulation | Crux (the sub-question that, if settled, would move at least one camp) | Shared test or case both camps accept in advance as bearing on the crux | Colony-cognition prediction | Nestmate-minimalist prediction | What settling it would move. Each camp's formulation must be phrased the way that camp would phrase it, and no shared test may be one whose outcome is rigged by a camp's own definition of mind. If a crux is idle — settling it would move nobody — say so in the last column.

Third, a JSON object with this schema: {"agreed_claims": [{"id": "A1", "claim": "...", "commits_us_to": "..."}], "cruxes": [{"id": "C1", "question": "...", "shared_test": "...", "colony_cognition_prediction": "...", "nestmate_minimalist_prediction": "...", "moves": "colony-cognition" | "nestmate-minimalism" | "both" | "neither", "kind": "empirical" | "conceptual" | "verbal"}], "verbal_disputes": [{"id": "V1", "surface_disagreement": "...", "shared_commitment_that_dissolves_it": "..."}]}.

If part of the dispute turns out to be about the word mind rather than about the world, say which part, and let the document survive the concession. No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to who said what — argue from the stipulated case and from what each position is already committed to.
