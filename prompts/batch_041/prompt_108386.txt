Quick question about pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. The usual move with asymbolic patients is that someone who says the stimulus hurts, but who doesn't withdraw, doesn't grimace, and otherwise seems untroubled, shows that the sensory side and the can't-stand-it side come apart -- hence pain's badness must be a separate affective/motivational layer sitting on top of the sensation. I want to know exactly what that argument needs to get off the ground, and where it's most likely to go wrong. Here's a twist I keep turning over: suppose a drug made healthy volunteers report the same thing (yes it hurts, no I dont mind), while their flinch and skin-conductance responses stayed completely normal and they still pulled away when they weren't paying attention to the stimulus. Does that case help the seperability claim or undercut it? And if separability is real, what does it actually tell us about why pain is bad -- is the badness just the unpleasantness, or is it also something about being involuntarily occupied by a state you can't negotiate with? Give me the conceptual crux, not a literature review; no citations needed. End with a final line \answer{...} containing just the crux in a short phrase.
