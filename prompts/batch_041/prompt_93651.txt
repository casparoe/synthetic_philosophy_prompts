I've been sitting in a small reading group that has spent the better part of a year on free will and foreknowledge, and we've ground to a halt on Molinism — the view that there are true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, facts about what each possible free creature would freely choose in any circumstances it might be placed in, which God knows prior to any decision to create and uses in order to arrange the world so that his purposes come off. Half of us find the picture elegant; the other half think it is a fiction dressed in a technical vocabulary, and the objection that keeps coming up is that a counterfactual about how someone would freely choose in circumstances that never actually obtain has nothing to be true in virtue of — nobody ever made that choice, and there is no fact about the person's will lying around to settle it. I have noticed that this looks structurally like the arguments we have about predictors and Newcomb-style cases, which is part of why it nags at me, but I don't want to resolve it by analogy; I want a case that makes the dispute concrete instead of turning on how one feels about grounding. I also want to avoid a theological example, because half the group are atheists and the other half will happily spend the evening on predestination rather than on the actual question.

So: please construct a thought experiment to my specifications. First, the scenario itself — ordinary and realistic, one narrative paragraph, a family or a workplace or a rescue or a small town, no angels and no possible-worlds machinery showing on the surface, and no step in which the answer is supplied by anyone's omniscience or by a stipulation that some person simply would have done such-and-such. Second, and this is the part I care about most, the counterfactual must be load-bearing: some plan — and I'd prefer the planner to be an ordinary finite human, so that the theology is doing no work inside the story — must succeed or fail according to what a particular person would freely choose in circumstances that in fact never arise, and the case should let us feel the pull of both answers to whether there is any fact of the matter about that choice. Third, the person who is the subject of the counterfactual should never learn of it and never have any opportunity to falsify it, since the gap between what she actually does and what she merely would have done strikes me as where the real action is. Then do the analysis: state the counterfactual precisely, say what a Molinist is committed to regarding its truth and its truthmaker, say what the critic who presses the grounding objection says instead, and say what a thoroughgoing theological determinist says — the last because I suspect the determinist simply sidesteps the problem, and I want to see whether your case makes that visible or hides it. Close with the honest part: name the single feature of the scenario that is doing the work, say what happens to the case if that feature is removed (does it degrade into an ordinary prediction about someone's character?), and describe how the ablest defender of Molinism would try to show that the case begs the question against her. If the specifications cannot all be satisfied together, tell me which one is the culprit and build the nearest case that meets the rest.
