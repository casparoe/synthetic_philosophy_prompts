I paint signs for a living, mostly by hand, and I've somehow ended up running a two-hour Saturday workshop at the community centre for six or seven adults who can hold a brush but have never once thought about type. The thing I actually want them to walk out with isn't a nice-looking alphabet, it's the answer to a question a friend stuck in my head: what makes every a an a? I can draw you fifty a's that are obviously all the same letter, and I can draw you an a that has stopped being an a, and I cannot tell anyone where the line is. He then pointed out that the Russian a looks exactly like ours and is a totally different letter, which broke my brain a bit, and when I went looking for comfort I found the opposite problem instead, that the two-storey a and the round one-storey a are the same letter even though a kid learning to read might trip over one and not the other.

So dont explain it to me, plan how I should teach it. What has to come first for the rest to land at all, and what depends on what — I have a feeling the order matters more than the content here. What single example should carry the whole session, and I want you to argue with me about this: I keep assuming it's the a itself, but I wonder if the a is actually the worst possible place to start precisely because everyone has seen a million of them and will just copy what's in their head. Tell me what the carrying example has to have and what it has to not have. Then the wrong ideas, in the order they'll actually turn up in the room, and where in the sequence I should head each one off — I'm betting the big one is "a letter is a shape you copy as acurately as you can," but I have no idea what the second one is or when it arrives, and I suspect there's one about style being decoration you add on top at the end. Also one thing I can get them to do in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they actually got it, as opposed to just making six pretty a's. And tell me what I should refuse to go into at this level and say so out loud, so I don't get dragged into an argument I can't win with a brush in my hand.

One more thing, a hunch I want you to either use or kill: I keep telling people a letter is like a face, you'd know your mate across the street no matter what he's wearing. Half of me believes it and half of me thinks it's rubbish, because the hole in the a — the counter, the bit that isn't ink — seems to do as much work as the strokes, and a hole isn't a feature of anyone's face. If that analogy is a trap for a room of beginners, say so and give me something better to hang it on.
