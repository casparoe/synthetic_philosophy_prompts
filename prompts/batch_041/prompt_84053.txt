A colleague in our department's welfare-economics reading group offered the following summary of the debate over interpersonal comparisons: "The problem is that utility is ordinal. Since any increasing transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences, there is no fact of the matter about how much better off one person is than another; the numbers are just bookkeeping. This is why the older utilitarian comparisons were abandoned — they smuggled in a cardinal scale that preference theory does not license. The upshot is that normative economics has to settle for Pareto improvements, plus perhaps comparability-free criteria such as the difference principle; anything beyond that, including money-metric cost-benefit analysis and QALYs, is an arbitrary convention dressed up as measurement."

Another member of the group then mentioned that the literature distinguishes comparability of levels from comparability of differences, and that measurement and comparability are supposed to be separate questions. I suspect the summary is too pessimistic, but I cannot tell whether that distinction rescues anything or is just relabelling. Please audit the summary: which parts would a specialist accept, which conflate claims the literature keeps apart, and which are overstated? I would particularly like to know whether the obstacles here are obstacles to measuring utility or to comparing it, whether the difference principle and money-metric methods really are comparability-free, and whether the pessimistic conclusion follows from whatever survives the correction. Say how confident you are in your verdict, and identify the point you are least sure of.
