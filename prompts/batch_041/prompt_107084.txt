I have been keeping a private ledger of my reactions to people being celebrated and people being cut down, and I want to know what rule, if any, those reactions are following. I am told that some of my reactions sound like the American Dream talking and others like Jantelagen or tall poppy syndrome, but I do not trust either label to capture the pattern, and I would rather derive the pattern from the cases than assume one. Here is the ledger, with my verdicts as I actually have them rather than as I think I ought to have them.

A former classmate of mine founded a company that makes a genuinely useful medical device; she is now rich and does the interview circuit. When people praise her, I join in, and I feel the praise is owed. A different former classmate inherited his father's firm, rode a lucky market upward, and now does the same interview circuit in the same tone. When people praise him I find it grating, and I notice I am pleased when a journalist digs up something unflattering; I think that pleasure is warranted. A colleague was promoted over me on merit I cannot dispute. I feel a strong pull to belittle her in conversation, and I judge that pull to be ugly and unjustified — her success is real and my reaction is mere envy. A neighbor wins a large lottery and immediately begins lecturing the rest of us that anyone could do it who wanted it enough. Cutting him down feels right, and not merely out of envy. The same neighbor, in a variant, wins the lottery and quietly pays off several families' debts without mentioning it; when this comes out, celebrating him feels right even though he did nothing to earn the money. An athlete dominates her sport for a decade, credits only herself, and says in interviews that her rivals simply wanted it less; tearing her down feels right to me even though every win is real and earned. A different athlete dominates for a decade, credits her coaches and her opponents, and calls herself lucky; tearing her down feels wrong, and I want to defend her against those who try. A writer I know publishes a genuinely excellent first novel and announces at her launch that she believes she is better than most of the writers in the room; I feel the takedown coming and I think it is warranted. A close friend is simply better than I am at the thing we both care most about, knows it, and never says it; I feel a tug to diminish her for no reason I can name, and I judge that tug to be the tall poppy in me and to be wrong. Someone who grew up poor in my town becomes rich, moves away, and returns only to tell us what we are doing wrong; cutting her down feels right. In a variant of that same story she returns to fund the school and does not lecture; celebrating her feels right.

Two things puzzle me. First, the pairs that look alike but split: the two lottery neighbors, and the two dominating athletes, get opposite verdicts from me even though each pair seems to differ only in what the person says and how she carries the success, not in what she earned. Second, my tentative principle. I think what I am actually tracking is not whether the success was earned but whether the success is being used as a claim to stand above other people — whether celebrating it is being conscripted into a ranking. Test that against the ledger and tell me how much survives. It seems to explain why the meritorious promotion does not license a takedown (she is not claiming rank) while the lecturing neighbor and the self-crediting athlete do. But it appears to say the two interview-circuit classmates should get the same verdict, since their behavior is identical and neither obviously claims rank, yet I split them. It also struggles with the returned philanthropist, who arguably does position herself above the town and whom I still want to celebrate.

So I am asking for two things. Give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures the ledger, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing and which case forces it. Then tell me, honestly, which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and would require me to abandon, and what it predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular, for someone whose success is wholly unearned but who is genuinely humble about it, and for someone whose success is wholly earned and who quietly believes she deserves more recognition than she gets but never says so. If my tentative principle is right in spirit but wrong in letter, repair it rather than replace it, and mark exactly where the repair is doing work.
