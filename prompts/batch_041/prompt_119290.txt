Hume's economic essays were written for a world of commodity money: gold and silver coin, convertible paper, and states that had to acquire coin before they could spend it. I want a prediction of his verdicts on three features of the present monetary order, with the reasoning traced back to commitments he actually held rather than to whichever later tradition has claimed him.

First. A prominent view holds that money is, at bottom, a creature of the state and of taxation: what makes a token money is that the state accepts it in discharge of taxes and other obligations, and a state that issues its own inconvertible currency can always make any payment denominated in that currency. On this view a sovereign issuer cannot be forced to default for want of its own money; its real constraints are available resources and inflation; and the habit of comparing a state's budget to a household's is a category mistake, since the household must first obtain from someone else the very money in which it owes payment. Hume's treatment of public credit runs the other way. He asks why the case should be so different between the public and an individual as to require different maxims of conduct for each, and he likens a minister empowered to draw bills upon posterity to a prodigal given credit at every banker's shop. Is that disagreement substantive or merely verbal? If the analogy was doing real work in his argument, what work was it doing, and could he keep the argument while conceding the point about the household? Then vary the case: a state that has surrendered its own currency and must obtain its means of payment from others, as members of a monetary union do. Would he place that state in the household's position after all?

Second. Hume is famous for holding that money is not one of the wheels of trade but the oil that eases their motion; he was also, and less famously, doubtful of the benefit of banks and paper credit, on the ground that paper is a money foreigners will not accept and that any great disorder in the state can reduce to nothing. Nearly all money is now exactly that kind of paper, or a ledger entry behind it, and central banks create it at will. Was his objection to paper credit an objection to inconvertibility, to private issuance, or to the dependence of any such claim on the state's continuance? Which of those objections does the modern system answer, and which does it leave untouched?

Third. Hume argued that the rate of interest is governed by the real forces of commerce — the demand for borrowing, the supply of funds, and the profits of trade — and that the quantity of money, once prices have settled, has no manner of influence on it. He also allowed that an increase in money can excite industry during the interval before prices adjust. Modern central banks do little but try to move the rate of interest, and in recent years several have pushed their policy rates to zero or below and bought large quantities of assets to press longer-term rates down. Which part of Hume's account implies that such a policy cannot work? Does he have the resources to explain why it sometimes appears to, and would admitting that amount to conceding that interest is a monetary variable after all?

Close by ranking the three verdicts by how much strain each places on the rest of his system, and by naming the single commitment he would most plausibly abandon. Where his essays underdetermine the answer, give both defensible readings, but still say which one the remainder of his thought favours.
