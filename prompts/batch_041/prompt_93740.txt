Every December I reread A Christmas Carol and this year I'm stuck on it, because I'm also prepping a unit on indicative vs. counterfactual conditionals and the two keep bleeding into eachother. Read the novella through that lens for me. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge the future as if it were already settled -- Tiny Tim dead, Scrooge dead and plundered, nobody grieving -- and Scrooge asks whether these are shadows of things that will be or only of things that may be, and the spirit refuses to give him a straight answer. So is the whole sequence an indicative prophecy that simply gets defeated, or a counterfactual (if Scrooge doesn't change, Tiny Tim dies) wearing the indicative mood as a disguise? Does the story need a semantics in which future contingents have no truth value yet, or can a Stalnaker/Lewis possible-worlds account absorb it without strain? Where does the reading break down -- for instance, the Ignorance and Want children under the Ghost of Christmas Present's robe look like plain present-tense indicative social reportage, not conditional at all, and the Cratchit scene's force depends on our taking the vision as a real warning rather than a mere prediction. And does the book end up dramatizing something about conditionals that the old Oswald/Kennedy contrast doesn't, or is it just exploiting the ambiguity so it can have both foreknowledge and free will at once? Be honest about the places the story resists the reading instead of forcing it. Please write it in American English.
