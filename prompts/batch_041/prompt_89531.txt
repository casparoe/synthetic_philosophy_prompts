I write historical novels, and I have reached the point in a manuscript where the record runs out and invention has to begin. The arguments I make to myself at that point are, I suspect, too comfortable, so I would rather you build me a clean instrument than offer me an opinion. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario. A novelist dramatizes the life of a real person, now long dead, who is stipulated for the purposes of the case — invent them; do not use an actual historical figure, and do not refer to real novels, films, or scholarship. This person was not a public figure: their life mattered chiefly to those who knew them, and there is no public right to know anything about it. No one alive ever met them. There is no estate with a commercial stake, no descendants, no identifiable living party whose feelings or interests the dramatization could injure, and no legal right in play. The invented material must be flattering rather than defamatory: it depicts nothing the subject would have found shameful, and it makes their life come out better than it did. Nothing in the case may turn on an explicit instruction the subject left about how they wished to be portrayed — no wills, no injunctions, no requests to friends. Any surviving testimony of theirs counts only as evidence of what they thought about their own life, never as a directive.

Three views must come out as follows, and you should state each in your own words before applying it:

(1) the view that only the living can be wronged, so that the dead have no claims against us at all — this view licenses the dramatization;
(2) the view that the dead retain an interest in their own story, such that the wrong done is not reducible to any harm to the living — this view forbids it;
(3) the view that a dramatist is bound by the subject's own surviving record of themselves: free to fill silence, but not to contradict what the subject asserted about their own inner life — this view must permit the invention in one version of the case and forbid it in another.

The two versions of the case must differ in exactly one feature, with everything else — subject, documents, invented material, absence of living parties — held fixed. That single feature must be the pivot on which view (3) turns, and it must not be a feature that view (1) or view (2) registers at all. No supernatural access to the dead person's actual preferences; the novelist has only documents and testimony, as I do.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

The Case — the scenario in a single paragraph, no more than about 250 words.
The Variable — one short paragraph, about 80 words, stating precisely the one feature that distinguishes the two versions.
The Verdicts — about 300 words, going view by view through all three, saying what each says of each version and why, derived from the view rather than stipulated.
The Pivot — about 150 words on the feature doing the work, and what happens to view (3) if that feature is removed.
The Rebuttal — about 200 words, in which the ablest defender of view (2) attacks the case rather than conceding it.
The Upshot for My Draft — about 100 words on what this tells a novelist about what a defense of the invented scene has to carry.

Argue entirely in your own words and offer no names, works, or citations; I am not asking for sources and you should not supply any. If the specifications cannot all be met at once, say plainly which one you had to bend and why — do not quietly stipulate a verdict and then present it as a finding.
