I need help settling something, and I'm honestly not sure there's a clean answer. I sit in on a small reading group that's half neuroscience grad students and half philosophy grad students, and last Thursday two of them, Nadia and Bram, went at it for the better part of an hour while the rest of us ate our sandwiches and stayed out of it. I'm supposed to write a summary for next week and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, where each of them is overreaching, and what both are missing? No reading list please, I don't need names of papers or philosophers, just the reasoning.

It started because Nadia raised the possibility that octopuses feel pain using machinery that shares almost nothing with ours: different molecules, different anatomy, but the same behavioural profile, damage detection, avoidance learning, guarding the injured spot. Her take was that if that's true then pain just is the role, not the tissue, and anyone who says only our chemistry counts is being chauvinist about neurons.

Bram: "Fine, but look at the role you listed. A thermostat detects a change and drives a response, a plant curls its leaves. So your list is satisfied by things nobody thinks feel anything. The second you fix that by adding 'and it feels bad,' you've stopped describing a causal role and started describing the very feeling you said we didn't need."

Nadia: "That's only because I gave you a three-item list. The role is the whole position in the economy of the system, how the state interacts with attention, memory, what the animal will trade to get away from it, the way it competes with everything else the animal wants. Specify that fully and thermostats and plants drop out."

Bram: "Then you've bought a disjunction. If our stuff fills the role and their stuff fills the role and silicon could fill the role, then being in pain is being in S1 or S2 or S3 or... No science generalizes over a disjunction like that, and if every effect is already fully explained by whichever disjunct happens to be present, the big disjunctive property isn't doing any causal work. It's a gerrymandered kind, like being either a lorry or a teacup."

Nadia: "Wings. Insect wings, bird wings, bat wings, no shared structure, and nobody says wings aren't real or that the biology of flight is fake. Nature hits the same solution over and over and we name the solution, not the parts."

Bram: "But notice what holds 'wing' together: what wings are for, historically, what they were selected to do. That's not the same thing as a causal role. A causal-role description just says what a device does. A function in the selection sense says what it was built to do, and built-to is a normative notion. So the wing analogy either imports teleology you told me you didn't need, or it collapses."

Nadia: "Then try this. Suppose we find a creature with chemistry just like ours, but the state plays the opposite role, it seeks out the damage signal, doesn't guard, will work to get more of it. Is that creature in pain? If you say no, you've just admitted the role decides. If you say yes, you've admitted the stuff decides. Pick."

Bram: "I'd say the question has no determinate answer yet, and that's not a dodge, it's the same kind of thing as whether Pluto is a planet. We've got two concepts, one tracking the mechanism and one tracking the role, they've never had to come apart before, and this case forces us to decide which one 'pain' is. The decision isn't arbitrary, but it isn't discovered either."

Nadia: "That can't be right. If we can't say whether an animal suffers, that isn't a boundary dispute, that's a scandal. The concept exists to track something that matters morally, and if two lineages both do the damage-detection-and-avoidance thing, I know which way I'd bet."

So, adjudicate. Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, tell me what the strongest point on each side is, and say what both of them keep missing.
