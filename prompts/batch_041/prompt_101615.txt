I'm a second-year PhD student sitting in on a seminar on Marx and the philosophy of technology, and next week I have to lead discussion on a stretch of the Grundrisse — the notebooks on capital, where Marx is working through fixed capital and machinery and starts saying that the creation of wealth comes to depend on science rather than on labor time. I've read the passage maybe a dozen times and I can follow the sentences one at a time, but I can't get the argumentative shape of the whole thing, and I'd like help understanding it rather than a paraphrase. Here is the part I keep circling:

"The exchange of living labour for objectified labour – i.e. the positing of social labour in the form of the contradiction of capital and wage labour – is the ultimate development of the value-relation and of production resting on value. Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

My real question is how these two adjacent sentences are supposed to hang together. The first says direct labor time is the presupposition of the value-relation "and remains" so, and the second says that as industry develops, real wealth depends less and less on labor time. Is Marx simply contradicting himself here, or is "presupposition" doing some technical work — i.e., the value-form still presupposes labor time as its measure even after the actual magnitude of wealth has outrun labor time, so that the two claims are about different things? I'd also like to know what work "real wealth" is doing as opposed to "value," what "the general state of science" refers to and whether it is meant to be the same thing as "the progress of technology" or something distinct from it, and what the scare-quoted "powerful effectiveness" is pointing at. A friend in the seminar keeps insisting that this passage shows Marx believed capitalism would simply collapse on its own once automation advanced far enough, and I suspect that's a misreading, but I can't yet say why in a way that convinces even me.

Please give me a close reading of the passage that resolves the apparent tension, in the order the pieces have to be taken, and tell me plainly which parts of the passage are doing real argumentative work and which are rhetorical.
