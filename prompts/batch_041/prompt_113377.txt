I run a monthly discussion group at a bookshop — smart non-philosophers, mostly people who've absorbed Stoicism secondhand from Ryan Holiday and podcasts. Next month is the dichotomy of control, ninety minutes, and here's what I actually want them to come out with: that the version they've got (focus on what you control, let go of the rest, basically productivity advice) isn't quite the ancient one, which is about what's up to us in the sense of our own judgements and choices rather than what we can causally affect, and that in the old version it only hangs together with the claim that virtue is the only good and everything else is indifferent, plus fate — all of which the modern revival tends to quietly drop. Don't explain the idea to me, plan how I should teach it. Give me five numbered stages in order, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no wrap-up. I need those stages to cover: the dependency order (what has to land before what, and why), the one example or case that should carry the whole session and what it needs to have and lack — I keep reaching for the archer simile, and some of them have definately heard the trichotomy version where you internalize the goal, but I suspect that's doing a different job than the dichotomy, so tell me if I'm wrong — the misconceptions they'll walk in with and the exact stage where each one gets headed off, and in the last stage the question I should ask at the end to tell whether they've actually got the distinction rather than a nicer-sounding tip. Also say how confident you are in your claims about what they'll misunderstand, and flag the single point in the plan you're least sure of.
