Write a short story, of roughly two thousand words, that dramatises the dynamics of satisficing rather than merely illustrating the idea. I will set out the material I want you to work with; treat it as the physics of your invented world, not as a thesis to be stated.

A satisficing agent does not maximise. It searches until it finds an option that clears a threshold, takes that option, and stops. The threshold is the interesting part, and it is not fixed. Call it the agent's aspiration level: what it counts as good enough. Two forces move it. Success raises it, so that yesterday's excellent result becomes today's baseline and an agent that has been doing well becomes progressively harder to please. Failure lowers it, and usually lowers it faster than success raises it, so the aspiration level is path-dependent: two agents in identical present circumstances can want very different things because of what they have been through. A third force works alongside these: shortfall triggers search, and surplus suppresses it. An agent comfortably above its aspiration level has little reason to look around, which means the moment of greatest apparent success is also the moment of least curiosity, while an agent in trouble searches hardest. Note what follows. Satisfaction and performance can move in opposite directions. If the aspiration level falls faster than the agent's results are falling, the agent can feel better and better while doing worse and worse.

Two further distinctions should shape the story. First, separate the level at which the agent stops searching from the level at which it refuses altogether — the standard it will accept, and the floor it will not go below. These can drift independently, and an agent whose floor has begun to move with its aspirations is in a different condition from one whose floor holds while its standards sag. Second, an aspiration level that adapts to what is available is easy to exploit: degrade an agent's environment and its standards will follow the environment down, so that it reports contentment in circumstances it would once have refused. Whether such an agent is being wronged, or is simply revealed to have wanted little all along, is one of the things your story should leave the reader unable to settle.

Requirements for the story:

- Place it wherever you like, but keep it away from the well-worn settings for this material: no boardroom, no research laboratory, no dystopia governed by an algorithm, and no twist in which the agent turns out to be a machine. A person, a household, a village, a school, an orchestra, a lifeboat, a hospital ward — anything whose inner workings a reader can watch from close up.
- Show the aspiration level drifting across at least three episodes, at least one of which is a rise and one a fall.
- Include a stretch in which the agent's satisfaction rises while its performance declines.
- Include an observer — an auditor, a rival, a newcomer, a child — who misreads the satisficing agent, taking it either for lazy or for the servant of some hidden and bizarre goal. Let the misreading be reasonable.
- Include at least one episode in which the apparent complexity of the agent's behaviour is really the complexity of its environment, with a simple rule doing all the work.
- Let the mechanism be visible in the events. Characters may voice it, but only partially and possibly wrongly; nobody should explain it correctly and at length. The word satisficing need not appear.
- Do not resolve the story into a moral. End in a place where the reader cannot tell whether the agent's habit of settling was a discipline or a slow surrender.
