I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm putting together a lecture on the Frege-Geach problem for my metaethics seminar. I've taught it before, but this time I've been reading alot of the recent expressivist stuff and trying to get clear on the negation problem, and now I'm worried I've been misrepresenting the whole thing. Here's my reconstruction: expressivists say that 'lying is wrong' expresses disapproval of lying. But then 'lying is not wrong' can't just be the negation of that, because if the original sentence expresses an attitude rather than states a fact, there's nothing there to negate. So the expressivist has to say that 'not wrong' expresses some second-order attitude, like disapproval of the disapproval of lying. And if they do that, then 'not' in moral contexts doesn't mean what 'not' means in ordinary descriptive contexts. But if 'not' changes meaning, then moral arguments that use negation equivocate, so they're invalid. So doesn't the negation problem show that expressivists can't have a logic of moral language at all? And doesn't that mean the Frege-Geach problem is not just a problem about embedding but a proof that expressivism is inconsistent with classical logic?

A grad student of mine keeps pressing me on whether the expressivist can even define negation for attitude-expressing sentences, and I keep telling her that's seperate from the Frege-Geach problem proper, not a new objection. But now I'm not sure. I want to present the strongest version of the objection, but I also don't want to tell my students something false. So is the standard expressivist reply to say that moral arguments are valid in a non-classical sense, or do they just bite the bullet and admit that moral modus ponens isn't really valid? And if they respond by giving truth conditions for embedded moral sentences, haven't they just become cognitivists with extra steps?
