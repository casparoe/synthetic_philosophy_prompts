I retired a few years back from a career in fisheries ecology — thirty years of stock assessments, quota fights, and a lot of staring at numbers that everyone in the room agreed were too optimistic — and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of social science reading group at the local university, mostly keeping my mouth shut and taking notes. Last month we spent a session on common knowledge, and I walked out of it genuinely unsetteled, in the good way. What got me was the difference between everybody knowing a thing and everybody knowing that everybody knows it, and the fact that this has to keep going up one floor at a time, forever, and that this tower is supposedly what makes coordination possible in the first place. In the fishery it was obvious enough: we all knew the stock was in trouble, and we all knew we all knew, but the moment somebody said it out loud in a public meeting everything shifted, even though not one new fact had entered the room. Still feels like magic to me. Then the group leader mentioned two things that have been rattling around in my head since. First, that if you try to build the tower out of private signals and acknowledgments sent over a channel that might swallow a message, you can exchange confirmations until the heat death of the universe and still not have the thing — each acknowledgment is supposed to be the one that finally seals it, and none of them does. Second, that if you assume the tower actually holds among people who are all rational, whole games come apart from the last move backward, which everyone in the room agreed is not how people play them. I'd like a poem that carries both of those.

I've got a form in mind: a pantoum. I ran into one in an anthology and couldn't get over how the lines come back around. My understanding is that it goes in quatrains, that lines 2 and 4 of each stanza turn up again as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, so a sentence keeps returning in a new spot and doing a new job, and that the last stanza closes the circle by hauling back the opening stanza's lines, so the whole thing loops shut. Five quatrains feels about right. What I want is for the form to be the argument: the recurring lines should be the regress — I know it, you know it, I know that you know it, you know that I know that you know it — coming back one floor higher each time, so that a reader who's never heard of any of this still feels the ladder climbing and the queasy sense that it doesnt land anywhere. Then the closing loop should do the real work, because closing the loop is precisely the difference between an endless regress and a fixed point, and that fixed point is what common knowledge is: the same proposition sitting at every level at once. That's what a public announcement buys you and a private signal doesn't. Somewhere around the fourth stanza, where the ladder should start to creak, I'd love the other thing too — the acknowledgments piling up over a channel that keeps eating them, nobody ever quite home. And if you can get the rational-choice sourness in there — that once we all know that we're all rational, cooperation unwinds from the end backward — do it, but only if the poem can carry it. I'd rather have a real pantoum that's right about the central idea than a checklist with line breaks.

One thing on standards: somebody who's actually done the reading should be able to go through this and see that you've got the distinction right — mutual knowledge versus common knowledge, the regress versus the fixed point — and somebody who's never encountered any of it should come away feeling shown something rather than lectured at. Please don't name theorists or point me at papers; I can't chase that down and it isn't what I'm after. Also keep it under thirty lines and don't knock yourself out making every line rhyme — I gather strict pantoums run ABAB, but I'd rather it breathe.
