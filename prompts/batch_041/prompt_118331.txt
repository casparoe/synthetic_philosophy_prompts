I've been lurking on rationalist blogs for about fifteen years now and I've got a pile of gut verdicts about when a bunch of stuff adds up to one mind, and they won't line up. I don't want a reading list, I want the principle. Here's what I actually think, case by case.

- An ant colony: no. It forages, builds bridges, reallocates workers, and if you squint the whole thing is doing something like what a brain does. Still: nobody home. "The colony decided to attack the nest next door" is loose talk.
- A honeybee swarm picking a new home cavity by scout dances and a quorum: same verdict. The choice is genuinely good, often better than any individual bee's first guess, and I'll happily say the swarm chose the better cavity, but I won't say it believes anything.
- An octopus: one mind. A big fraction of its neurons are out in the arms, the arms keep poking around semi-independently, and I still think there's one animal in there. So "it's decentralized" doesn't kill it for me.
- A split-brain patient: one mind. When one hand does something and the speaking hemisphere then denies it and invents a reason, I still say one person. Two streams of control, one someone.
- Conjoined twins who share everything below the neck: two minds, obviously.
- So "one body, one mind" is out (twins) and "one control stream, one mind" is out (split brain). "One head, one mind" gets all five so far, and I don't trust it at all.
- A corporation: I think it has beliefs, and I think it can deserve blame. "The company knew the brakes were faulty" seems straightforwardly true to me, not a shorthand. But it doesn't feel anything, and I'm not being coy about that — it's not that I'm unsure, it's that I'm sure the belief is real and the experience isn't.
- Prices, the market: represents scarcity better than any trader does, and no beliefs. Ditto a prediction market sitting at 80 cents — "the market thinks there's an 80% chance" is fine as a figure of speech and false as a claim.
- Wikipedia and the scientific literature: no beliefs. It's a repository that happens to track the truth in places. Articles can be wrong and communities can be misled, but nobody's believing.
- Someone with DID: I want to say one mind with badly partitioned access, but if two alters assert contradictory things and each denies the other's memories, that's structurally a lot like the split-brain case, and I said one there, so fine. But then why two for the twins? Two heads, I guess. That's embarrasingly shallow.

The principle I keep reaching for, and keep distrusting, is something like a press-conference test: there's a mind where there's one thing you can address, ask a question of, and hold to the answer. That gives me corporation yes, market no, colony no, split brain one (there's one person on the hook, even when the answers are confabulated), twins two. It has two problems I can't fix.

First, the spokesant problem. Suppose an ant colony evolves a caste of exactly one ant that reads out the colony's state on demand, accurately. Now there's a thing you can address and hold to its answers. Do I really want to say the colony started believing? That feels backwards — a press office shouldn't be able to create a mind. But the split-brain patient's speaking hemisphere is basically a press office that confabulates reasons for actions it didn't order, and I counted that as a mind. So either the press office can be constitutive, or my split-brain verdict is wrong, and I can't tell which.

Second, the intern memo. An intern writes a memo saying the plant is unsafe. Nobody reads it. Ten years later people get hurt, and I think the company knew. But there's no one to ask, no single voice, nothing that would pass a press-conference test. So either "knew" is doing something other than believing here, or the test is wrong.

What I want from you: (a) the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can check it against a new case; (b) which verdict I have to give up, and why that one rather than its neighbour; (c) its verdicts on the spokesant, the intern memo, and on a company that replaces its whole staff one person per month for five years while keeping the rulebook and the spokesperson role unchanged; and (d) whether I'm secretly using "believes" in two or three different senses, in which case tell me which of my disagreements are just verbal. If the honest answer is that belief, blame, and experience need three seperate accounts, say so and keep them apart — I'd rather have three small principles than one that fudges. Also a chatbot: I have no idea what to say about that one and I'm a bit worried your principle will have opinions I don't like.
