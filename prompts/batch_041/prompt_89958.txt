I'm in a philosophy of action readin group and we keep hitting the same picture. Someone will say that when a couple agrees to meet at the station, or a committee votes to hire someone, or a crowd pulls down a statue, the group itself intends and acts, and that this is just like an individual intending and acting -- one big agent, with the members as its parts. Our professor calls it the group-mind-is-like-a-mind picture, but says not to take it literally. I want a propper audit of that analogy. Lay out what is supposed to map onto what: belief, desire, intention, decision, action, responsibility, maybe the unity of consciousness or a single point of view. Then tell me where the mapping actually holds and where it breaks. Does a group have one intention when its members have conflicting reasons, or when membership changes halfway through? Does the one-big-agent picture smuggle in a central controller or a unified perspective that joint action doesn't need?

One person in the group says collective intention is just individual intentions plus common knowledge; another says there's a genuinely joint commitment that can't be reduced that way. Which parts of the analogy are each of them leaning on, and do those parts survive the disanalogies? And when someone says a corporation is a person or that a group can be morally responsible, which parts are they leaning on? I'd like the correspondences set out explicitly -- a table is fine -- then the breaks separated into ones that matter for those conclusions and ones that are just noise, and finally what a better analogy would be if this one is carrying more than it can.
