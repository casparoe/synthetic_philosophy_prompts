I keep ants. Nothing scientific — two small formicaria on a shelf, one colony I have been watching for about four years — and I have a puzzle I have been chewing on alone, with no training in philosophy, so I apologize in advance if I use a word badly.

Years ago I read a book in which two characters discuss an ant colony and it turns out that the colony is a single mind, one you can hold a conversation with. I believe the colony is called Aunt Hillary and I think the book is Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, though I may be misremembering the name or the book. What struck me, and still does, is that I could not tell whether the book was making a claim I could agree or disagree with, or telling a joke that happened also to be an argument.

Here is what actually puzzles me. No individual ant seems to be doing anything like thinking: each one follows local rules and chemical trails and behaves about as intelligently as a small machine. Yet the colony as a whole does things that, if a person did them, I would describe without hesitation as deciding. It settles on one of several candidate nest sites after what looks like a long argument among scouts. It shifts foragers from a dying food source to a fresh one. It holds on to a route for weeks. It throws away a large number of workers for something the colony needs. So I want to know where the actual positions on this question come apart, and where I myself land.

Please build me a sorting instrument: a decision tree, or a taxonomy if you think a tree is the wrong shape, that takes someone from a few plain questions to a position on whether an ant colony can have a mind and, if so, what kind. I want it built out of the questions first and the names second, the way a key to an unfamiliar group of plants is built out of the features and not out of the species list. Every branch should be labeled with the question that separates it from its siblings, and each of those questions should be something I could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, or which position I am supposed to want. Every leaf should carry the name of the position where there is one, and the word unnamed where there is not, plus a single sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. I would like somewhere between six and ten leaves. Where the tree produces a combination of answers that no one actually holds, I want that said outright, with your best guess as to why the slot is empty — because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or simply because nobody has bothered. Where two leaves cannot be told apart by any question you can ask, say so rather than pretending the tree separates them.

I would rather have a few sharp questions than a long questionnaire, so prune anything that only rephrases its parent. The instrument has to survive the following cases, which are the ones my friend and I keep getting stuck on, and I would like each of them to do real work at some node: the workers all die and are replaced within a season, so nothing individual persists except the nest and the trails; the parts are themselves tiny and short-lived and roughly mindless, which is also true of the neurons in my head; the ants are replaced one at a time by little robots that reproduce the same signals and movements exactly, until no ant is left; one nest is divided into two halves that never exchange signals and forage independently; two nests merge so that trails, workers, and food flow freely between them; a parasite makes infected workers climb a blade of grass and get eaten, so the colony now does something that destroys it; and a keeper like me moves the nest, blocks a trail, and puts out sugar, which may mean the mind in question is really the colony plus the keeper. If any of these cases is a bad test, or sneaks in an assumption I have not noticed, tell me that instead of quietly building around it.

I am asking a lot of the shape of the answer and not much of the sources, so do not send me a reading list. I want the structure of the disagreement, not its bibliography. And if my question is really two or three questions wearing one coat — if asking whether the colony has a mind and asking whether anything is going on inside it are different questions that need splitting apart before a tree can be drawn — then say so plainly and split them, or tell me that a tree is the wrong instrument for what I am after. I would rather be told my framing is confused than be handed a tidy diagram that quietly answers a question I did not ask.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would explain it in a long email to a curious stranger: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just sentences and paragraphs, with the branches stated in words.

Please write in American English.
