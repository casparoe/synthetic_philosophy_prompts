I'm putting together a session for a crossover seminar — "Minds, Machines, and Morals" — that mixes advanced undergraduates from philosophy and cognitive science, about eighteen of them, and I have eighty minutes to fill. I want to run an activity rather than lecture, because the one point I keep failing to get across by talking is the gradation thesis: that the boundary between the things whose interests count and the things whose interests don't is not a cliff but a slope, and that our practices nonetheless force us to plant a flag somewhere on that slope. What I want participants to feel is the specific discomfort of that pairing — the ease of every adjacent comparison, the impossibility of a non-arbitrary cutoff, and the fact that they will make the cutoff anyway the moment something is at stake. Please design the activity for me: rules, materials, roles, a timeline for the eighty minutes, and stakes that matter without needing real money. I have a rough shape in mind but I'd rather you work it out — something that begins with a chain of strictly adjacent comparisons (each entity judged only against its neighbour, so that a thermostat and a thermostat-that-learns, or an ant and a beetle, never feel like a hard call), builds all the way from the plainly inert to the plainly sentient, and then hands the group a limited resource they must allocate — protection, funding, a fixed number of tokens — which obliges them to draw and defend an actual line. Whatever the resource is, I'd like a moment where their own earlier, individually reasonable judgments are turned back on them and they discover they cannot keep the line where they first put it. The idea should live in the rules and the incentives, not in anything I say: no lecture up front, and if there's a name for the problem, they shouldn't hear it until the end.

The debrief should map particular moments in what happened onto particular parts of the idea — the gradation itself, the arbitrariness of any threshold, the difference between a cutoff that is metaphysically arbitrary and one that is merely epistemically uncertain, and the reason law, agriculture, and AI policy cannot wait for the metaphysics to settle. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — I expect at least a few participants to try to escape by refusing to draw a line at all, and I expect the thermostat end of the chain to get laughed off as a joke, and I'd like your advice on both. And be honest with me about the feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the closing minutes; if the empirical markers of sentience or the distinction between sentience and moral status have to come from me rather than from the game, say so plainly rather than letting the activity pretend to deliver them.

Finally, since I'll be trusting this in front of a class: say how confident you are in the design choices you're recommending and which single element of the activity you're least sure will land, so I know what to watch for when I first run it.
