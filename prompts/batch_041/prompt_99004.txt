A friend and colleague of mine died last month. He spent his career defending a realist view of dispositions and, in particular, insisting that the difference between a fink and a mask is not a technicality but a real feature of how character works. A fink is a condition that would prevent a disposition from manifesting by removing the disposition itself; a mask prevents the manifestation while leaving the disposition intact. The fragile glass that never breaks because it is swaddled in foam is still fragile. The irascible man who is never provoked into anger, because he has been sedated, is still irascible. He would have wanted the distinction applied to himself, which is why I am asking you to write the eulogy rather than writing it myself.

He died after four years of a degenerative illness. For the last two of them he could not reliably do the things by which everyone knew him: the small generosities, the questions he asked about your life, the way he noticed who was left out of a conversation. Some of the people who will be in the room have quietly concluded that the man they knew was gone before his body was.

Please write the eulogy, roughly seven to nine hundred words, for a mixed congregation. Perhaps half are philosophers who know the finks-and-masks literature and the conditional analysis of dispositions well enough to catch a misuse; the other half are family, neighbors, and former students who have never heard the word disposition. The occasion is a memorial service, not a lecture, and the philosophy has to do structural work in the speech rather than decorate it: it should shape what you are entitled to say and what you are not.

Several constraints. He would have counted as false, and would not have wanted said over him, anything about an afterlife, about his watching over us, or about his character being "really nothing but" a pattern of behavior he happened to display. You may not simply assert that his kindness was masked; you must identify the actual mask and argue that it blocked expression without removing the trait, while taking seriously the obvious objection that this is wishful thinking. Under his own standards, what licenses attributing a masked disposition rather than conceding that the illness removed the trait — that it was a fink? The philosophers should find the reasoning sound and the terminology used correctly; the family should be comforted without being talked down to. No quotations, no citations, no reading lists.
