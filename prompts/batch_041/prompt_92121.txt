For a few years now I have kept an informal notebook of arguments I run into about "Western values," and I have reached the point where I want to know whether what I keep seeing has an established name. The recurring move is this. Someone wants to defend a policy or condemn a practice, and rather than argue for it on its merits they announce that it is, or is not, consistent with "our Western values." Press them on what those values are and the list shifts with the occasion: sometimes it is individual liberty and the rule of law, sometimes tolerance and pluralism, sometimes family and religious inheritance, sometimes science and open inquiry, sometimes a canon of literature and philosophy. The striking part is that the phrase never seems to do independent work: whatever the speaker already approves of turns out to be a Western value, and whatever they already disapprove of turns out to be a betrayal of one. A small example I keep returning to is a campus free-speech dispute in which one side called a speaker's exclusion a betrayal of Western values and the other side called the speaker's views themselves a betrayal of Western values, with both sides invoking the same phrase as though it settled the matter. And the phrase is not the property of one camp: friends and critics of "the West" both lean on it, each filling it with a list that would horrify the other, each speaking as though it named one definite thing we all already understand.

What I am after is not really a label but a carving. I have run across a few terms that seem close, and none that quite fits. I know the idea of an "essentially contested concept," and I can see why someone would reach for it, but that seems to describe a genuine, ongoing dispute over a shared and internally complex ideal, whereas what I am looking at feels more like a container that gets filled only after the verdict has already been reached. I have also come across "persuasive definition," and that is closer, but it suggests a deliberate redefinition engineered to make one's position true by fiat, and the people I have in mind do not appear to be redefining anything on purpose; they seem sincerely to believe the phrase already has a fixed content and that they are merely reporting it. I have even heard it said that phrases like this are simply "empty," which sounds right until I notice that the phrase is not empty at all: it is full of whatever the speaker happens to care about, and that fullness is precisely what makes it useful to them.

So my questions are these. Is there an established concept for a term that (a) purports to name a determinate class, (b) is used by opposing parties to mean opposing things, (c) gets its content filled in only after the speaker has decided what to approve or condemn, and (d) is nonetheless treated by its users as though it named something independent of their own preferences? If such a concept exists, what is it, and how does it differ from the nearest neighbours -- essentially contested concepts, persuasive definitions, family-resemblance terms, thick concepts, and whatever else belongs in that neighbourhood? If no such concept exists, please name and define one, and mark out its edges: is a term of this kind always a mere instrument of rhetoric, or can it name something real while still being used this way, and what would let me tell the two cases apart? I should say plainly that a bare name will not satisfy me; what I want is the phenomenon itself pinned down -- what exactly it is, what it is not, and where it stops.
