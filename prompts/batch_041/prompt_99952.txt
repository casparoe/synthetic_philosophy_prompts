I'm the people analytics lead at a mid-size retail company -- about 4,000 employees, mostly hourly, with a corporate office that's obsessed with generational labels. Our CEO loves decks that say things like Gen Z wants purpose, Millennials want feedback, Boomers won't retire, and our DEI lead thinks the whole thing is astrology. I have to sign off on the numbers in these decks maybe twice a month, and after a Slack fight last week about a chart claiming Gen Z is the most anxious generation, I want a reusable procedure I can run before we put any generational claim in front of the chief people officer. I've got an org psych background but no philosphy, and the HR business partners who'll use this have maybe twenty minutes and zero patience for theory. I read a couple of philosophy blogs that talked about kinds as homeostatic property clusters -- a bunch of properties that hang together because of some causal mechanism, not just because we put a label on them -- and about looping effects, where classifying people changes them, so the label becomes partly self-fulfilling. I also know from methods that age, period, and cohort are tangled up and you usually can't seperate them from one cross-section. Can you turn that into a checklist or decision procedure for deciding when a generational kind is real enough to use for a given claim, and when we should just say younger workers or people with less tenure instead?

I want the procedure to be ordered: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call (like whether celebrating generational identity is good for solidarity even if the causal claims are weak). It should distinguish at least: a demographic cohort (just birth years), a generation in the thicker sense (shared formative experience or self-consciousness), a social kind with feedback loops, and a mere marketing segment. It should tell me how to handle the boundary problem (why 1981-1996 and not 1980-1995?), within-group variance (if the spread inside Gen Z is as big as the gap between Gen Z and Millennials, what then?), and the difference between explaining an individual and explaining an average. Then run it on three cases: (1) our CEO wants a Gen Z retention strategy with targeted benefits; (2) a manager claims Boomers are resisting a new scheduling tool because of generational technophobia, when it might just be tenure or job role; (3) a press release saying Gen Z is the loneliest generation, based on a single survey. At least one of those should come out with an answer I won't like -- probably the retention one -- and I want you to say plainly what the procedure can't settle and where I need a human to decide. No citations or reading list, just the procedure and the worked cases.
