I am working up a paper on rational acceptance, and before I evaluate any particular account of it I want the specification on the table. Fix the target this way: the theory is for a bounded agent, not an ideal one with unlimited time or computational power; what she accepts is what she is prepared to assert, reason from, and act on; and she revises her acceptances as evidence arrives. I do not want a literature review, names, works, or a reading list — I want the requirements themselves.

The two cases that generate the demand are the lottery and the preface. In the lottery, a fair draw with one winning ticket out of a hundred leaves each ticket at 0.99 to lose, so it looks rational to accept of every ticket that it will lose, while the conjunction of those acceptances is something the agent knows to be false. In the preface, an author has strong evidence for each of three hundred claims in a book and strong evidence, from her own track record, that at least one of them is false; she seems entitled to accept each claim and to accept that one of them fails. Take both as fixed phenomena that any adequate theory must accommodate.

What I want from you is the desiderata list: for each requirement, the statement of it, the case or consideration that motivates it, and a ruling on whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp in the debate — I do not want a camp's methodological taste smuggled in as a datum. Cover at least the following ground: which cases must come out right; the demands of consistency and of closure under conjunction and known entailment; the relation between credence and acceptance, including whether a high-probability threshold can do the work; the distinction between belief and assertion, action, or institutional certification; the diachronic requirements governing revision and the withdrawal of an acceptance; and the demand that the theory be neither vacuous nor self-undermining, in the sense that it may not license the acceptance of contradictions on one side nor leave the agent unable to accept almost anything on the other.

State the requirements at the right level of generality, and test that by cases I have constructed, which I want the list to cover:

First, a case with no lottery and no tickets. A diagnostic instrument yields exactly one of ten thousand mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive readings, each at 0.9999 on the evidence. Nobody wins anything and nothing is drawn. If the paradox is about lotteries, the theory should say what distinguishes this; if it is about partitions, the requirements should be stated without lotteries in them at all.

Second, a self-auditing book: its final chapter argues, from the author's track record, that at least one claim in the book is false — and the final chapter is itself one of the claims in the book. Does the theory place the last chapter inside or outside the set of acceptances, and what does it say the author's overall position is?

Third, a serial drawing: losing tickets are announced one at a time over months, so each announcement is genuine evidence about the survivors and credence in each of them rises. What should the agent accept at each stage, and what governs withdrawal of an earlier acceptance? I want an explicit requirement forbidding verdicts that flip back and forth with no change in evidence.

Fourth, a certification case: an auditor must sign one sentence that is the conjunction of a thousand entries, each of which she independently accepts to a high standard. Her institution requires the signature; her epistemology may not permit the belief. The theory must either separate acceptance from certification and assertion, or explain why the distinction is idle here.

Finally, flag any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard positions. A sentence or two per position is enough — I will do the full scorecard myself.
