In the split-brain experiments begun in the 1960s, patients with a severed corpus callosum could act on the basis of a stimulus delivered to the right hemisphere and then, in the left hemisphere's voice, give a fluent reason for the action that the experimenter knew was not its cause. Take this episode and analyze it through Dennett's heterophenomenology — the method that treats a subject's spoken reports not as direct reports of experience but as data about what the subject believes, to be read with the latitude we grant a novelist's invented narrator. Do the mapping carefully: what does the method say the experimenter has, and is entitled to infer, when the left hemisphere offers its confabulated reason; which features of the case does heterophenomenology capture well; and where does it come apart? The method appears to presuppose a single narrator whose beliefs can be catalogued, and the split-brain patient threatens that presupposition — so say whether heterophenomenology can accommodate a subject who may be two sources of report, whether it remains a coherent datum to say that the patient reported such-and-such, and what the episode reveals about the method's reach. Be candid about what the case outruns rather than forcing the theory to speak where it was never built to.
