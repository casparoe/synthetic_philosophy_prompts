I've been going back and forth with two friends about promises and I want to nail down an actual position I can defend instead of vibes. Friend A's line is that a promise is just a prediction with a penalty attached: you announce what you'll do, people start counting on it, and if you flake you eat a reputational cost. On her view the "bond" is a collective fiction we keep because it's handy, and any talk of a special obligation is just that fiction restated with a serious face. Friend B says no, what a promise really does is hand over a piece of your authority over your own future conduct to the other person, like giving someone the remote control for one channel of your life — I honestly can't tell if that's a real theory or a metaphor with a nice bow on it. Please argue for a position on why promises bind, one that isn't reducible to expectation plus sanction, and if you end up defending Friend B's picture then make it do work rather than just sound good. I also don't want "autonomy" or "respect for persons" sprinkled on top as a garnish; if those are doing the work, show me how.

The bit I keep getting stuck on is release. If the promisee says never mind, you're off the hook, instantly, and they don't have to give you any reason why, and they can't hand that power over to some third party — my friend says that's exactly what you'd predict if the whole thing were about expectations and reputational penalties, since the person who'd be disappointed is just waiving the disappointment. I think the asymmetry is a clue to something, but I can't get it to carry wieght, so please try. And then the cases that break everything I attempt: a promise to someone who's now dead; a promise to yourself that nobody else could know about or enforce; a promise the other person has totally forgotten; a promise you made sincerely believing the promisee would never find out; and a promise that's since become pointless because circumstances changed and the person would now prefer you didn't do it. Say which of these are promises at all and which are a different thing that got filed under the same word, and what your answers tell us about the core case.

Last thing, and I care most about this one: what does a promise do that a threat doesn't? Both are ways of changing what someone can count on, both get people to plan around you, both can be perfectly sincere. If your account makes them come out as the same structure with the valence flipped, then say that and defend it, but don't fudge it or wave at "consent" without explaining what consent is doing in the explanation. I'm happy to be told my framing is confused — just don't tell me it's confused and then answer a different question.
