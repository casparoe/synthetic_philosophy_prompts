For most of my working life I have been a site reliability engineer, and I think in a fairly specific vocabulary: services and their users, SLAs and SLOs, uptime and latency, error budgets, incident severity, blameless postmortems, toil, capacity planning, and escalation paths. I am fluent in that vocabulary to the point of pedantry, and I have no training in political theory. Six months ago I moved to Singapore, and the argument I keep meeting here — and in accounts of China — is that a government earns the right to rule by delivering prosperity and order, and that this is a more honest title to authority than a ballot. I would rather understand the claim than merely react to it, so I want to run it through the only analytical vocabulary I actually command.

Please restate the performance-legitimacy argument as faithfully as you can in my terms, treating the state as a service provider and citizens as users. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about: which elements of the argument carry over cleanly, which carry over only under distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I am especially curious whether the error budget — the amount of unreliability a service is allowed before its users lose patience — captures anything real about how much disorder a government can absorb before its legitimacy is spent, or whether the analogy collapses there. And I want your judgement about the residue itself: where the translation fails, is that a limitation of my framework, which cannot represent something the political argument genuinely contains, or is it evidence that the argument is less determinate than it sounds — for example, that it quietly presupposes a user who cannot switch providers?

Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know either subject, and do not present a contested reading of the politics or of the engineering as consensus.
