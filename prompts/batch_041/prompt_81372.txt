In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer sends you a letter demanding $1,000, threatening to burn down your house. The letter is sent if and only if (you would pay the $1,000) XOR (your house has termites causing $1,000,000 in damage). Evidential decision theory says you should pay, because paying is evidence that you don't have termites. Causal decision theory says you should not pay. Explain the conceptual issue: why does the agent's knowledge of its own action not suffice to screen off the evidential correlation between paying and having termites, whereas in Newcomb's problem self-knowledge is often thought to do so? Then compare this to counterfactual mugging, where Omega flips a coin and asks you for $100 only on tails, but on heads pays you $10,000 if it predicted you would have paid on tails. Some decision theories say you should pay on tails; others say you should not. Why does the correlation in counterfactual mugging seem to license payment while the correlation in XOR blackmail does not? In your answer, address whether the difference lies in the causal direction, the nature of the predictor, or the logical structure of the problems.
