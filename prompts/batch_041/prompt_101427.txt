A regional health authority keeps a standing priority-setting panel that each year ranks pairs of proposals for distributing a fixed budget of health benefit between two patient groups of equal size, X and Y. The panel never states its criteria; it only issues rankings. Over the last three cycles its verdicts were these.

Cycle 1. Proposal A: X receives 2 units, Y receives 100. Proposal B: X receives 40, Y receives 50. The panel ranked B above A.
Cycle 2. Proposal A: X receives 10, Y receives 40. Proposal B: X receives 30, Y receives 31. The panel ranked B above A.
Cycle 3. Proposal A: X receives 30, Y receives 50. Proposal B: X receives 40, Y receives 41. The panel ranked B above A.

This cycle the panel must rank a pair in which one proposal levels down: Proposal P gives X 41 and Y 60, while Proposal Q gives X 41 and Y 41. X is identical in both; the only difference is Y's level.

Predict which proposal the panel will rank higher, and give your reasoning. In doing so, please address each of the following.

First, identify the distinct distributive rules that are jointly consistent with all three earlier verdicts, and say which familiar family each belongs to; note also at least one rule that the earlier verdicts appear to exclude, and why.

Second, state what each surviving rule predicts about the leveling-down pair, making clear where they agree and where they come apart.

Third, give your own best guess at the panel's verdict and the considerations that push you toward it, including how much weight you place on the panel's evident pattern of always favoring the more equal of two options.

Fourth, name the further pair of proposals that would most sharply separate the surviving rules from one another, and say how each rule would rank that pair. Explain why that pair does the discriminating work that the leveling-down pair cannot.
