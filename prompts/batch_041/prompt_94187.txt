I've been reading rationalist blogs for way too long and I've got a draft post on futarchy I want shredded before I publish it. It's the standard setup: we vote on the metric we care about, then decision markets bet on whether a given policy would raise that metric, and whatever clears at a positive conditional price gets adopted. My draft's whole point is that this quietly smuggles back in the thing it claims to take off the table. The metric is a value choice, and in the actual proposal the metric is just... picked, by politicians or a constitution or a committee. So we've moved the values fight upstream and dressed it up as a technical parameter.

Then I try a fix and I'd like you to tell me if it works or if I'm fooling myself. Two tiers: a first layer of markets picks the metric from a fixed menu of maybe five indices, second layer runs conditional markets on the winner. I think that collapses, because the stake in a tier-one market is every downstream decision at once, so the manipulation incentive is bigger than in any ordinary decision market. My rescue is the part I'm least sure about. Instead of letting anyone choose the metric, commit in advance to a lottery over the fixed basket, drawn only after the policy markets clear. Then a manipulator distorting the market on any single index only gets paid off with probability 1/n, while someone with genuine information about that index still has to reveal it to bet. So manipulation should be dominated by information, and the market aggregates beliefs about the whole basket rather than one number. It also has a nice side effect, I claim: the old complaint that GDP is a terrible metric stops mattering, because nobody gets to pick. To make it concrete I use the FDA example — drop the efficacy requirement, watch one index jump and another sag — where a single metric gives you a boring yes and the basket gives you something to actually bet on.

What I want from you: (1) is the manipulation argument in paragraph two actually right, or am I overstating how much a manipulator gains relative to what the distortion costs them? (2) does the lottery trick do any real work, or is it defeated by the indices being correlated, so distorting the common factor is cheap and moves everything at once? (3) is there an obvious reason the pre-commitment gets undone by whoever runs the lottery, that I'm not seeing? And (4) just tell me if its boring. I don't need a reading list, I need the objections I'm missing.
