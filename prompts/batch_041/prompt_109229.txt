A colleague of mine in the quant group has circulated a short memo defending Kelly betting as the correct way to size repeated favorable bets, and generalizing it into a broader principle he calls geometric rationality; I'm inclined to accept it, since the mathematics looks clean and he is careful with numbers, but I have a nagging sense that some of the framing is off, and I would like an audit rather than a defense. Here is the substance of the memo, condensed.

"Start with the standard coin: even-money bets, 60% chance of winning. Betting your whole bankroll each round maximizes expected wealth, but it bankrupts you almost surely, so expected wealth is the wrong target. What you should maximize instead is the expected logarithm of wealth, equivalently the long-run geometric growth rate. On this bet that means staking 20% of your bankroll each round; that fraction is the Kelly bet, and it is provably optimal, in the sense that no other strategy ends up with more money than Kelly in the long run. Betting more than Kelly is not aggressive but simply wrong — it lowers your growth rate and risks ruin — and betting less than Kelly is leaving money on the table. Samuelson and others objected that log utility is arbitrary and that maximizing a time average is a fallacy, but these objections miss the point: the law of large numbers is what makes the geometric mean the right quantity, and an agent who cares about the long run has no coherent alternative. Generalizing: whenever you face a sequence of choices whose outcomes compound, you should choose the option that maximizes the expected log of the outcome, and this is what I mean by geometric rationality. The same logic explains diversification in investing, and, I think, why it can be rational to trade a small chance of a huge gain for a large chance of a modest one — Kelly tells you the exchange rate. The only caveat is that you need to know the probabilities, so in practice you should estimate them and then bet full Kelly."

Go claim by claim. Which claims would a specialist in decision theory or information theory accept as stated, which are standard simplifications a careful defender would want to qualify, and which are actually wrong or would be rejected by the people the memo is arguing against — in particular, where the memo mischaracterizes the objection it attributes to Samuelson. I am especially unsure about three things: whether Kelly really maximizes wealth "in the long run" in the sense the memo needs; whether the time-average-versus-ensemble-average issue is a genuine objection or a confusion; and whether geometric rationality, as the memo generalizes it, is a theorem, a heuristic, or a stipulation about what counts as rational.
