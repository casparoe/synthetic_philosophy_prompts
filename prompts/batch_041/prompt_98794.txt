I'm working through Nietzsche's essay on history -- the one with the three ways of doing history, the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical -- and I'm stuck on the critical mode. Here's roughly the bit I mean, in my own words: critical history puts the past on trial, it hauls it in, interrogates it, and passes sentence on it, and this is supposed to be healthy for life because it lets a person or a culture break with what it has inherited. But then right after that he seems to undercut the whole thing by saying that we who sit in judgment are ourselves the product of the very errors and crimes we're condemning, that our existence rests on them, and that condemning the past is therefore a kind of self-condemnation -- we're dragging our own roots into the dock.

What I can't get is whether that's a fatal objection to critical history or the actual point of it. Is he saying critical history is impossible, or that it works precisely by turning the knife on itself? And how does that fit with the other two modes -- is critical history supposed to replace them, or is it just the dangerous one you use when monumental and antiquarian history have gone stale? Help me understand what he's actually claiming here, and what the move is that makes it not a contradiction.
