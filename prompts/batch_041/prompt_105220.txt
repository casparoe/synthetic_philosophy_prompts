Ok so I'm trying to get Hobbes on geometry straight and I keep bouncing off one bit. I don't have the text in front of me, so this is my own mangled paraphrase, but the gist is something like: geometry is the science we can genuinely demonstrate, because the lines and figures we reason about are drawn and described by ourselves, i.e. we make them; and the same holds for civil philosophy, since we make the commonwealth ourselves; whereas with natural bodies we're not the makers, so we can't have that same kind of knowledge of them.

What I can't work out is what the making is actually supposed to be doing. Is it a modest epistemic point — making a thing gives you causal acquaintance with it, so you can see why its properties follow from its construction? Or is it a stronger constructivist point, that geometrical objects are our artifacts through and through and have no nature independent of our drawing them? Hobbes obviously wants the modest version to carry over to politics, but a commonwealth isn't made by one reasoner the way a triangle is, so the analogy feels loose in a way I can't pin down.

And here's what's really bugging me: Hobbes was famously bad at geometry — the circle-squaring business, the long public fight with Wallis, all of it. If the criterion is 'we make it, therefore we know it,' then his own failures look like counterexamples to the sufficiency of the criterion, or at least to the idea that being the constructor guarantees demonstrative knowledge. How would Hobbes, or a sympathetic Hobbesian, deal with that? Does it just show he was a bad geometer, or does it show the criterion was doing less work than he thought?

A related worry: in his own system geometry is supposedly founded on motion — points moving generate lines and so on — but motion is a natural phenomenon, so doesn't that blur the very line he's trying to draw between the demonstrable (made by us) and the non-demonstrable (given to us)? And one more: if the criterion is about what we construct, could a Hobbesian claim demonstrative knowledge of a merely possible commonwealth — the state of nature story, say — but not of any actual historical one, since actual ones weren't constructed by the reasoner? That seems like a weird result and I want to know if it follows.

Can you walk me through the passage and these worries? I'd especially like: the weakest and strongest readings of the we-make-it claim, which reading Hobbes actually needs for the politics to go through, whether the Wallis debacle is a real problem for the view or just for Hobbes's arithmetic, and whether the motion-foundation of geometry undercuts the distinction. Skip the citation stuff, I just want the arguemnt laid out clearly.
