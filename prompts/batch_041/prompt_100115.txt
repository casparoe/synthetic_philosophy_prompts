I am a retired entomologist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of mind, and I would like a consistency audit of a position I have assembled about the Aunt Hillary thought experiment—the proposal that an ant colony might have a mind—before I present it to a seminar. My commitments are as follows.

(1) I am a naturalist: there are no non-physical substances, forces, or properties, and every fact about the world is ultimately a physical fact.

(2) I am a functionalist about mental states: what makes a state a belief, a pain, or a desire is its causal-functional role, not the material it is made of. I therefore accept multiple realizability: minds could in principle be realized in systems other than brains.

(3) I believe consciousness requires a unified subjective perspective. There must be something it is like to be the system, and that requires the system’s states to be integrated into a single point of view rather than merely summed across parts.

(4) An ant colony displays striking adaptive behavior: it forages, chooses nest sites, allocates labor, and responds to threats. These capacities can be modeled as distributed computation, often through stigmergic pheromone trails and local quorum rules. But the colony has no central controller, no neurons, and no obvious place where information is integrated into a single global workspace.

(5) I am inclined to call the colony a cognitive system in a weak sense—it processes information and solves problems—but not a minded system. I do not think there is anything it is like to be the colony, and I do not think it has beliefs or desires.

(6) I reject substrate chauvinism: I do not think we can rule out colony minds simply because a colony is made of ants rather than neurons. If a system realizes the right functional organization, that is enough in principle.

(7) I think emergence is real but epistemic. Higher-level patterns can be irreducible in the sense that they are not predictable or explanatorily tractable from lower-level details alone, but I do not think emergence introduces new fundamental properties or powers.

(8) I am tempted by the idea that a colony is a mind only if there is a colony-level vehicle of representation—some persistent internal state that stands in for features of the environment and guides colony action. The pheromone field strikes me as more like a public workspace than a private representation, and I cannot find anything else that plays the role.

I would like to know whether this package hangs together. Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and which are independent? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would cost me elsewhere. I am especially interested in whether my functionalism plus my rejection of substrate chauvinism already commits me to colony minds, or whether the unity condition in (3) and the representational condition in (8) are doing legitimate work or merely smuggling in a hidden biological prejudice.
