I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I have a slot at a philosophy of mind reading group that meets at the local university: fifteen minutes to present, then the rest of the hour for questions, which I am told is where these sessions are actually decided. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will really get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each tagged with who would raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it, each followed by a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and a one-line note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, and which single reply has to land for the session to go well. Finish with the objection I am hoping nobody raises.

The claim I am defending is that the difference between original and derived intentionality is not a difference in the material a state is made of but in where the error-correction lives. A state counts as a representation for the system that has it only if the system itself treats that state as a claim about the world and revises it when the claim fails: when the action the state drives misfires in a way that traces back to the state, the state gets repaired from the inside. Where the repair has to come from outside the system — the technician who recalibrates the vending machine, the engineer who retrains the model, the interpreter who decides what the detector really tracks — the aboutness is only derived. On this criterion, three consequences follow. First, Dennett's two-bitser, the coin-detecting gadget, stays a merely derived representer however long it runs, because nothing inside it ever treats its own output as something that could be wrong. Second, Searle is right that there is a real gap between a coin detector and a belief, but wrong about where the gap lies, since it is architectural rather than biological. Third, a frozen trained model is derived, while the same model whose own predictions feed back into what it goes on to predict has crossed the line — a far lower bar than being a brain. The consequence I care about most is that a great deal of human subpersonal processing comes out as derived too, so original intentionality belongs to loops and comes in degrees rather than being a property of states.

The room is small and I know its leanings. Two committed Dennettians know the intentional-stance material cold and will suspect I have reinvented it worse. One Searlean holds that nothing short of intrinsic biological content will do. Two cognitive scientists who build predictive, Bayesian models of perception will want to know what my criterion buys them. One eliminativist thinks the whole original/derived vocabulary should be scrapped. Three or four generalists will go straight for the most obvious hole in what I have said.

No citations or reading list, please; I cannot check them and I do not need them. What I need is the arguments in this room's vocabulary, and a clear sense of which ones I should simply give up.
