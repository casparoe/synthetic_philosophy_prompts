In medicine and clinical psychology, a patient's own report of how much pain they are in is treated as the gold standard: pain is whatever the patient says it is, and analgesics are dosed according to the number the patient gives. Yet the same clinicians treat a patient's account of why they feel anxious, what caused a symptom, or whether they are well enough to drive as unreliable and in need of corroboration, and in cases like a stroke patient's denial of their own paralysis the introspective report is discounted entirely. What view of introspection does this pattern presuppose — that it gives us authoritative access to some of our mental states but not others? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival accounts of introspection it quietly rules out, and tell me whether the practice is internally consistent or is really working from two incompatible pictures at once. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say what other views of introspection would license the same pattern of trust and distrust. And if the presupposed view were replaced by the opposite one — that introspection is uniformly trustworthy, or uniformly worthless — what would have to change in how clinicians solicit and use self-report?
