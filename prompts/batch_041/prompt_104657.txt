Two engineers at a robotics company are arguing over whether the lab's text-only language model — the one that narrates what the robots are doing — has grounded symbols. The vision engineer holds that a symbol is grounded only when it is wired to sensorimotor contingencies, to what the agent would see and do, so the narrator's words borrow their meaning from the humans who trained it and would be hollow if the robot itself had to mean them. The language engineer holds that grounding is instead a matter of a symbol's place in a practice of correction: a word is grounded once a community can and does hold its user to account for misusing it, and a model steeped in millions of human corrections is as grounded as any word-user needs to be. Write their dialogue. Neither should be a strawman, and each should have a real reply to the other's central objection. Have them test the disagreement against a single case — the narrator using a word such as fragile or crowded in a situation the robot has never encountered — and have each say what the case shows for their view, where the other's criterion would license an absurd result, and what evidence would actually change their mind.
