Dennett's two-bitser is a vending machine whose internal state is keyed to the coins it accepts; a duplicate of the machine on a twin world whose coins are microstructurally different but functionally identical would accept the twin coins, and the question is whether its state was ever about quarters rather than twin quarters. Searle's answer is that the machine's states have at best derived, observer-relative intentionality, since their aboutness is borrowed from designers and users, while human and animal states have original intentionality. Dennett's answer is that the difference is one of degree and richness, not kind, and that nothing is served by positing a special original variety. Assume both sides have their standard arguments available and do not need to be convinced of them.

I want the dispute broken into a ranked evaluation rather than a summary. First, take the following candidate criteria for marking the original/derived boundary and sort them into tiers (say four tiers, from most to least defensible) according to how well each survives the two-bitser case, twin-world duplication, swampman-style cases in which a duplicate has no design history, and cases in which a device's history underdetermines what it was selected for, plus the ordinary possibility of misrepresentation:

1. The system's states were fixed by natural selection rather than by a designer's intention.
2. The system is made of biological material or has the causal powers of a brain.
3. The system's states admit of misrepresentation, that is, there is a fact about what they are supposed to indicate and a way for them to get it wrong.
4. The content of the states is fixed by the individual system's own history of interaction rather than by an interpreter's interests.
5. The system has conscious experience or a first-person point of view.
6. The system can represent its own representational states.
7. The states are not observer-relative, that is, they would still be about what they are about even if no one had any use for them.
8. Being wrong matters to the system, in the sense that a misrepresentation has consequences the system is organized to avoid.
9. The system's overall function is to produce accurate representations, as opposed to merely producing behaviour that happens to be useful.
10. The system displays means-end flexibility that is not exhausted by a fixed input-output mapping.

For each criterion, say in one or two sentences what it captures and the sharpest counterexample to it, then place it in a tier. Then do a second, separate exercise: rate each of the following on a 0 to 10 scale for how much original intentionality it possesses, where 0 is a rock and 10 is an adult human being — a thermostat, the two-bitser, a counterfeit-detecting coin sorter whose states distinguish genuine from fake coins and can therefore be wrong, a chess engine, a honeybee navigating to a feeder, a corporation's pricing algorithm, a current large language model, a dog, and a human infant. Justify each number in one sentence, and say explicitly where on the scale you would place the cut between derived and original, if you place one at all.

Finally, add one consideration not on my list that you think does more work than any of the ten, and one that you think is a red herring that philosophers keep reaching for. Conclude with your own verdict: is the original/derived distinction principled, merely pragmatic, or a distinction that collapses once the two-bitser and its descendants are taken seriously? Answer the verdict question directly, and make the verdict consistent with the tiers and numbers you gave above; if it is not, say which of your earlier judgments you would revise.
