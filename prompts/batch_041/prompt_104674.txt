I hung up the lab coat a couple of years ago (40 years in a physics lab) and I've been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars since, mostly to annoy the grad students. Here's a thing I keep hitting. Anytime someone floats the idea that there's a general recipe for getting from the past to the future, or that one learning method might be flat-out better than another, somebody in the room says 'ah, but there's no free lunch', and everyone nods like that settles it. The other day a guy used it on me to say my old lab's curve fitting was no better than guessing. I have a nagging feeling that phrase is carrying at least three different loads at once: a budgeting platitude, some formal theorem about averaging over all possible problems, and a Hume-ish point about induction. Can you pull those apart for me -- lay out the distinct precise claims 'no free lunch' could be making, from the trivially true up to the genuinely contestable, say for each whether it's a platitude, a real result, or flat false, and tell me which of the readings is actually doing the work when it's used to shut down the induction talk or to dismiss the curve fitting. A menu of readings with verdicts, not a lecture. And no reading list please -- I'm writing this from a cabin with no internet.
