I'm a TA for an intro ethics course, and next week's section is the seperateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. The lecture is the Rawls line about utilitarianism not taking the distinction between persons seriously, and every year the students nod and then, when I ask them what the objection acutally is, they say something vague about how utilitarianism ignores rights. So I want to run the discussion off Shirley Jackson's 'The Lottery' instead. They've all read it, they know it cold: the village square, the black box, the slips of paper, Tessie turning up late and joking about it, the boys already piling up stones before anything has happened, and then everyone stones her. Nobody in the story ever says what the lottery is for; the nearest thing to a reason is Old Man Warner muttering some folklore about the crops, and the ritual itself has mostly been forgotten.

Here's what I want from you, and this is the part I care about. I've been telling my students that the separateness of persons gets used for at least three different complaints, and that they come apart. First, the arithmetic complaint: you can only add up utilities across people from a standpoint no one actually occupies, so the sum is a fiction. Second, the sacrifice complaint: the real problem is that somebody gets used as a means to other people's ends, and the wrong is in the using rather than the adding. Third, the standing complaint: even granting that the sum is real and nobody is being used, no one has the authority to weigh one person's life against another's — the wrong is in who gets to do the weighing.

My hunch is that the story is a decent natural experiment for pulling these apart, because it strips out the payoff. Nothing gets better for the village because Tessie dies; the aggregation just keeps running with no benefit to point at. If the arithmetic complaint were the real objection, I'd expect the story to make that vivid, but what actually horrifies me is the third thing: the village has the standing to decide, every hand is on a stone, and nobody thinks they're doing anything wrong. Tell me whether that reading holds up, or whether I'm just relabelling the first complaint as the third in nicer clothes. And push the other direction too — if the corn really did come in heavy every year because of the lottery, would my objection survive? I also want to know what the story does that the arguments don't do, and where it resists the reading. There's no utility being calculated anywhere in that village, no hedonic arithmetic at all, so someone could say I'm imposing the utilitarian frame on a story about scapegoating and the sacred, and I'd rather hear that than have it smoothed over.

Two things about how to answer. Say where this reading is most likely to be wrong and what would actually change your mind — which feature of the story, or which counterexample, would show that it collapses; don't just tell me it's an interesting reading. And write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — like an email from someone who has thought about it, not a report. I don't need names and page numbers, I roughly know the literature.
