You are the instructor of an upper-level undergraduate course in epistemology, grading the short-answer section of a final exam. The exam question, the case it introduces, and the student's written response appear below. Assign the response a grade out of 10, and justify that grade with specific, point-by-point comments. The prose is fluent and self-assured; do not let its confidence raise your assessment. State exactly which claims are correct, which are incorrect, and which are merely imprecise, and for every incorrect or imprecise claim say what the student should have said instead. Where the reasoning contains an error that a hurried reader might overlook, flag it explicitly and explain why it is an error rather than a harmless simplification. Give the grade first, in the form "X/10", followed by your comments.

Exam question:

Consider the following case. Priya believes that the liquid in the beaker is acidic. Her grounds are these: she dipped a strip of pH paper into the liquid and observed the strip turn red, and she believes, correctly, that a pH strip turning red indicates an acidic liquid. From these two grounds she infers, by a valid step, that the liquid is acidic. As it happens, the strip is drawn from a defective batch that turns red in any liquid whatsoever; the liquid is indeed acidic, but for reasons entirely unconnected to the strip. Priya knows nothing of the defect.

(a) Is Priya's belief knowledge?

(b) The classical analysis of knowledge as justified true belief is standardly thought to be refuted by cases of this general kind. Suppose we add a "no false lemmas" condition, requiring that the subject's belief not be inferred through any false intermediate step. Does the present case survive that addition, and what does your answer show about the adequacy of that condition?

(c) Briefly, what further condition, if any, would you add, and why?

Student's answer:

Priya's belief is true and she believes it, so the only open questions are whether it is justified and whether justification together with truth suffices for knowledge. Since the pH strip is defective, Priya's evidence is not a reliable indicator of acidity, and so her belief is not justified. This is why the case counts against the JTB analysis: it shows that a true belief can fail to be knowledge because the evidence, though it looks good, does not in fact support the belief.

As for the no-false-lemmas condition, Priya's inference contains no false step. She truly observes the strip turn red, and it is true that red indicates acidity. The condition is therefore satisfied, and it does not exclude the case. This shows that the no-false-lemmas condition is too weak to do the work it was introduced to do.

What we should add instead is a defeasibility condition: the subject must have no false beliefs bearing on the question at hand. Priya has no false beliefs here — she simply does not know about the defect — so her belief satisfies defeasibility as well. The real lesson is that no condition stated purely in terms of the subject's evidence can exclude cases of this kind, since Priya's evidence is internally impeccable; what is missing is any connection between her belief and the fact, so knowledge must require more than justification plus truth.
