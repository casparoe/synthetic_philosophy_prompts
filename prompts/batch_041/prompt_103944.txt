I write the Ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long piece about a constitutional experiment that has been unfolding, mostly unnoticed, in a small parliamentary republic. Two years ago its assembly replaced a century-old constitution with a new charter built around a deliberate design. Every provision, including the ones that govern amendment, can be revised by a two-thirds vote of two successive assemblies. A short list of provisions is declared unamendable, and the amendment clause itself sits on that list. A separate "repair clause" instructs the constitutional court to strike down any amendment that renders the charter internally inconsistent and to supply the minimal correction needed to restore coherence. Finally, the whole charter lapses after twenty years unless it is re-ratified, with the default being a reversion to the prior constitution if re-ratification fails.

The experiment has produced a genuine controversy among the legal scholars who follow the country, and I want the column to take a side rather than survey the field. One camp argues that the design is the only serious answer to the problem every self-amending legal order faces: a rule that authorizes its own revision can always be revised into something unrecognizable, so some entrenched core, policed by an independent court, is what keeps the system from devouring itself. The other camp argues that the repair clause hands the court a roving commission to rewrite the charter whenever it finds the text untidy, that the unamendable core simply relocates sovereignty from the assembly to the bench, and that the twenty-year sunset means the whole structure rests, in the end, on nothing but a recurring political mood rather than on any genuine legal foundation. Beneath both positions sits an older worry, familiar from debates over the basic norm and the rule of recognition: whether a legal order can ground its own authority in itself at all, or whether the foundation always lies outside the law, in a prior political fact that no clause can manufacture.

Write the essay I'll run. Weigh the two camps honestly, engage the deeper self-reference worry rather than gesturing at it, and reach a verdict: can a charter like this one be both coherent and legitimate, or does it collapse into paradox and arbitrary power? Defend whichever conclusion you reach, and be specific about which features of the design survive your argument and which do not. You may bring in the game-theoretic literature on self-amending rule systems if it sharpens the analysis, but the piece has to stand on its own for readers who have never heard of it, and it should not assume access to any library.
