The argument of Borges's "A New Refutation of Time" has convinced me, at least on most days, that time is unreal. The route runs through idealism and then through Hume: if to be is to be perceived, nothing exists but experiences; and if there is no impression of a self over and above the bundle of perceptions, there is equally no impression of time over and above the succession of states. Each state is complete in itself, the past is only a present memory and the future only a present anticipation, so the series is an illusion and the solitary moment is what is real.

What should count against this? I want the defeaters for this particular version of the position, not a general survey of the philosophy of time. Specifically: does the fact that a memory is a memory of something — that it has content which can be correct or incorrect — force the past back in? What becomes of the thought experiment in which the parts of a world freeze and thaw in rotation, so that its inhabitants can apparently deduce that the whole world must once have been frozen for a stretch — an interval in which nothing happened anywhere, and which passed nonetheless? Is Borges's appeal to exact repetition, a state recurring with no discernible difference, a genuine argument, or a confusion between our inability to tell two things apart and there not being two things? And how much should I concede to the charge that the whole case rests on the idealist premise, and shrinks to the harmless observation that we can only ever check the present as soon as that premise is dropped?

Rank these by how decisive they are, tell me which a committed defender can absorb without giving anything up and which should change my mind, and say whether the position is falsifiable at all or has quietly redefined "real" as "given in experience" — and whether that is a defect. If I have muddled the two halves of the view, or if the question as posed cannot be answered cleanly, say so rather than answering it as asked.
