Our reading group has spent a semester on the philosophy of pleasure and we are stuck on the question the literature itself seems stuck on: what do pleasures have in common? One side holds that there is a single phenomenal quality, a hedonic tone, that the taste of sugar, a hot bath, the satisfaction of finishing a proof, and grief at a funeral all share, and that this quality is what hedonism is about. The other side says no such quality can be specified without either triviality or circularity, that the class is held together only by the fact that we want these experiences, or by nothing at all, and that its members differ from one another more than they resemble any common core. Neither side has moved in decades. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a verbal dispute about the word pleasure, a substantive dispute about the same property, or two groups answering different questions under one heading — what makes an experience count as one of these, versus what makes a state good for the person in it? My working hypothesis is that the sensory side needs the word to name one natural kind, while the other side treats it the way we now treat jade, a word that turned out to cover two unrelated minerals, so that the disagreement is about what a kind term is for rather than about pleasure. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says about particular cases — masochism, boredom, the pleasure of solving a problem, the pleasure of a narcotic — and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, whether it could be dissolved by a distinction (treating hedonic tone as a determinable with many determinates rather than a single quality, for instance), and what would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a finding from psychology or neuroscience about a common currency, or an argument that the impasse is permanent.
