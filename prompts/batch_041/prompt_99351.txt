I'm in a little reading group on Buddhist philosophy and we keep circling back to the old chariot simile — the one where the monk grills the king about whether the chariot is the wheels, or the axle, or the frame, and the king keeps saying no, and the monk goes, right, so "chariot" is just a word we hang on the pile of parts, then pulls the same move on the word "person" over against the five aggregates. Everyone in the group treats this as basically a proof of anatta, the no-self teaching, and then we move on to the next reading. But i sat with it afterwards and now I honestly can't tell how much of that conclusion the simile is actually carrying on its own.

What I want from you is a real audit of it. Lay out the mapping explicitly — what in the simile is supposed to line up with what in the person, who's doing the questioning, what the "parts" are, what the move with the "designation" is doing — and then be honest about where it breaks. Couple of worries I can't shake. First, a chariot is an artifact: somebody built it, for a job, and we call it one chariot because it does that job; so this looks like an analogy about functional or conventional identity, same family as the ship of Theseus, and that might only buy you the claim that "person" is a convenient label, which sounds alot weaker than what the Buddhists seem to be after. Second, the simile seems to grant that the parts are perfectly real and just denies there's an extra whole perched on top of them — but I thought the whole point was that the aggregates themselves are not-self and impermanent, so it might be proving a thinner, different thing than the doctrine it keeps getting trotted out for. My friend says I'm overreading and that the simile was never meant as a proof anyway, just a way to loosen the grip of the word "I" — but if that's right then I want to know what's actually doing the work, the argument or the practice.

So: the correspondence table, then the disanalogies that matter for whether it supports no-self versus the ones that are just scenery, then what the simile quietly imports (a designer? a purpose? parts that are somehow more respectable than wholes?), and finally whether the anatta conclusion survives once those imports are stripped out. And if the chariot can't carry the weight, tell me what would — is there a better analogy for what's actually being claimed here, or is the whole analogy route a mistake in this case?
