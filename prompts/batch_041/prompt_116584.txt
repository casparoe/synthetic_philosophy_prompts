I write about science and ideas, and I keep hearing the same sentence at panels on end-of-life medicine: 'death is a process, not an event.' I heard it twice last week in a session on whether brain death or circulatory death is the real criterion, and the two speakers plainly meant different things by it — one used it to argue that the moment of death fixed by statute is a useful fiction, the other to defend a protocol that begins organ retrieval a few minutes after the heart stops. Give me a menu of the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the ones that are almost too obvious to state to the ones that would genuinely upend the debate, and for each say what kind of claim it is — about biology, about what we can know, about vagueness at the boundaries, or about law and convention — whether it is true, false, or a platitude doing no work, and whether it would actually license either speaker's conclusion. I'd also like to know which readings depend on treating the irreversibility of death as a fact of nature rather than a fact about what medicine can currently undo, and which reading, if any, survives the awkward case of a death that looks instantaneous. Lay it out as a menu I can hand to both of them, not a single preferred interpretation.
