I have no background in economics or statistics, but I've spent a few months reading about bet sizing — mostly blog posts and forum threads about sports betting and index investing — and I've arrived at a question I can't settle on my own.

The advice everywhere is to stake some fraction of your bankroll and never to go above what everyone calls the Kelly fraction. When I finally looked up where that number comes from, the answer was that it maximizes the expected value of the logarithm of your wealth. That looks to me like nothing more than choosing a utility function, and not even a natural one, just a curved one that makes you behave like someone who dislikes risk. Choosing a utility function seems like a personal matter, the way some people like spicy food and some don't; it isn't a mathematical fact about betting. I also notice that the word "geometric" keeps turning up in these discussions, and I can't tell whether it's doing any real work or is just a fancy name for a ratio.

So here is my question. If the Kelly rule is just log utility, doesn't the whole recommendation dissolve into a matter of taste? On what grounds does anyone say that staking more than the Kelly fraction is a mistake, rather than simply a different appetite for risk? If I'm comfortable with wild swings and I want the larger expected payout — and the largest expected payout of all comes from staking everything on any favorable bet — why isn't the bigger bet just the one that suits me, with the growth-optimal crowd merely having a different utility curve and no standing to call me wrong?

This is where I get stuck. Someone told me that if you stake a fixed fraction more than twice Kelly, the growth rate actually turns negative and you go broke in the long run no matter how favorable the bets are. That can't be right as stated, since any bet with an edge has positive expected value, and if Kelly is only a preference, then the ruin story must be either false or an exaggeration. I'd like to understand what, if anything, is actually being claimed here, because I suspect the people writing about this mean something more than a preference — and I can't see what it is.
