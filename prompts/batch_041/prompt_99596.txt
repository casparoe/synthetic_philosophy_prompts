A professor of mine summed up Quine's gavagai argument in a lecture roughly like this: 'Quine asks us to imagine a linguist dropped among a tribe whose language is completely unrelated to any he knows. Every time a rabbit scurries past, the locals say gavagai, so he writes down rabbit. But Quine points out the word could equally mean undetached rabbit parts, or a rabbit stage, or the universal rabbithood, and nothing the linguist can observe will ever tell him which. All he has to go on is their behaviour, so he can never be certain he's got the meaning right -- the native's inner mental state is just hidden from him. Translation is therefore guesswork all the way down, and Quine's conclusion is that we can never really understand a foreign culture. It's basically the same point he makes about the underdetermination of theory by evidence.' I don't know Quine well, but the last couple of sentences and the "never really understand" bit feel off to me, even though the gavagai example itself sounds about right. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me whats accurate, what's a common simplification, and what's just wrong -- and whether the conclusion about understanding other cultures follows from the parts that are right?
