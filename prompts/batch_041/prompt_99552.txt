I run a small philosophy reading group and next week we're doing the statue and the clay stuff — is the lump the same thing as the sculpture, can you squash one without squashing the other, all that. Instead of a reading I want to open the session with a poem, so I'm hoping you can write me one.

Here's the concrete thing I keep coming back to. There's a bell in my hometown that cracked twice. Both times they hauled it down, melted it, and recast it out of the same bronze, and both times everybody went on calling it the same bell — same name, same slot in the tower, the plaque still shows the original date. People swear it rings a little different after, but it's the same metal, so who knows. I've never been able to decide wether they replaced the bell or repaired it.

So: write me a poem, somewhere around 16 to 20 lines, where the same sentence comes back more than once but means a different thing each time it lands — the way "the same bronze" is one thing when you're talking about the metal and another thing when you're talking about the bell. I want that repetition to be doing the actual philosophical work, not just being pretty. By the end I should feel why someone could insist there's only one object here and someone else could insist there's two, and why they'd both be pointing at the same words. Bonus if the last line is the first line again but by then it's clearly about the other thing.

Form is up to you — blank verse is fine, rhyme is fine if you can keep it from getting sing-songy — but it needs to read well out loud, since I'll be reading it to the group. Someone who's spent real time on this problem shouldn't wince, and someone who's never heard of it should still get a good poem out of it. Definately no citations or reading lists needed, just the poem.

American English please.
