For a few months now I've been keeping a private list of judgments about a family of cases that I can't get to line up, and I've decided to hand the list to someone else and ask what principle, if any, covers it. The cases all turn on the distance between the words a speaker used and the thing the speaker meant, and this is what I find myself saying about them. When a colleague reports that some of the applicants have doctorates and it later emerges that all of them do, I don't feel she misled me in what she said; she said something true and left the rest to be inferred. When the barista asks what I'd like and I answer that I have a meeting in ten minutes, I take myself to have told her I'm in a hurry, though I'd hesitate to say I had asserted anything about my hurry. When a restaurant critic describes the soup as a liquid that was once on speaking terms with a chicken, I feel the point has been made but not said, and I notice the critic could add, without contradiction, that she means no disrespect to the kitchen. When a friend says the table is flat, or that the fish is fresh, or that she has told me something a million times, I don't think she has spoken falsely even where the literal words are false — and yet, unlike the barista case, I do think she has made a claim that could be right or wrong, one I could fairly hold her to. When a poet tells me the moon is a coin nailed to the sky, I don't think she has said anything that could be true or false at all, and I also don't think she has merely implied something the way my colleague implied that not every applicant had a doctorate. And when someone remarks that her brother is a poet but punctual, I feel the contrast has been conveyed in a way I cannot cancel, and yet I resist saying it was said. I've read some Grice and a little of what came after him, and none of it has made these line up for me.

The principle I keep reaching for — that a speaker means whatever the hearer must add to the literal content in order to make the utterance worth making — captures the colleague, the barista, and the critic, but it seems to get the poet wrong (it would have her meaning something assessable), and it gets the table and the fish wrong in the other direction (it would have my friend meaning something non-literal, hence not assessable, when I want to hold her to what she said). So either the principle is missing a clause I haven't found, or one of my judgments has to go, and I'd rather know which.

What I want from you is a principle, or a short list of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without coming back to you: which clause of it each of my verdicts is doing the work for; what it predicts about cases I haven't mentioned — a doctor who tells a patient her scan was clear when it was merely unchanged, a realtor who says the roof is in good shape when it is merely not yet leaking, someone who answers a compliment with "I've been lucky"; and, honestly, which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, so that I know what I'd be giving up. I would rather have a simple principle and one verdict overturned than an elaborate one that keeps everything.
