The idea is about the intent-alignment vs value-alignment split, specifically the "narrow" definition of the alignment problem, where you say: the alignment problem is just getting the system to do what the operator actually intends, and we bracket the question of whether that intent is any good, because that's the "value" part and someone else's problem.

I keep feeling like this framing is doing something sneaky with the word "intent." The way it gets operationalized, "intent" ends up meaning the operator's preferences over outcomes — basically a utility function, possibly implicit, that we're trying to infer from behavior. But thats a desire model of intent, not an intention model. In the philosophy-of-action tradition, intentions aren't just strong desires: they're settled, they close off deliberation, they're the thing you're committed to and that structures everything else you're planning, and they can come apart from what you'd most prefer (you can intend to finish the draft even while, at every moment, preferring to do something else). If that's right, then "align to the operator's intent" and "align to the operator's preferences" are two different targets, and the narrow framing quietly swapped one for the other without anyone noticing.

So the question I want the proposal to nail down: does the intent/value distinction survive once you stop treating intent as preference-satisfaction? And once you do, does the narrow definition of the problem (a) collapse back into the value question, (b) become a different and harder problem, or (c) stay tractable only because it's now aligning the system to something thinner than anyone actually cares about?
