I'm a third-year student double-majoring in philosophy and drama, and I'm writing a final essay for a philosophy of language seminar on Grice — we've worked through his account of meaning, the Cooperative Principle, the maxims, flouting, and conversational implicature — and I've chosen Edward Albee's *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?* as my text. I'm using the 1962 play as primary and Mike Nichols's 1966 film where staging choices seem to matter, though I want the reading to stand on the play. My starting hunch is that the play is not an illustration of Grice but a stress test of him. The obvious hook is that the whole night is built from named conversational games — Fun and Games in the first act, then Get the Guests and the other rounds later, and finally the third act's exorcism — and that nearly every exchange between Martha and George runs by flouting a maxim in a way the other decodes instantly while Nick and Honey sit there missing it. That asymmetry, and the fact that the title itself is a joke with no literal content, makes me think Albee is dramatizing exactly the gap between what a sentence means and what a speaker means. But two things seem to escape the Gricean apparatus as I understand it. First, implicature here is not a cooperative efficiency device; it is a weapon and a form of intimacy at once, and the same mechanism that lets Martha or George land a devastating point while keeping deniability is the mechanism by which they recognize each other as partners — the play seems to suggest that the possibility of cruelty is not a failure of the system but a consequence of it. Second, the gin-mill story about the boy mocked for a private word for bourbon has always struck me as a case of something that never had a community to mean anything to, and the son — the fiction Martha and George jointly maintain — raises a question an individualistic account of speaker meaning doesn't obviously answer: when Martha tells Honey about their son, who is the speaker, what is meant, and can there be meaning without any intention to inform or deceive?

I'd like a committed reading, not a survey of options. Please structure it as follows. First, a section titled The Gricean machinery (roughly 400 words) that works through the play concretely in Grice's terms: which maxims get flouted in which games, how the older couple's implicatures are recovered effortlessly while the younger couple's failures show how much mutual knowledge is presupposed, and what the private word in the gin-mill story reveals about meaning without a linguistic community. Second, Where the play resists Grice (roughly 450 words) arguing that weaponized and intimacy-constituting implicature is not captured by the Cooperative Principle as usually read, and taking a definite position on whether this is a genuine counterexample to Grice or something he could absorb with a patch. Third, The son as a hard case (roughly 300 words) treating the child as a sustained joint pretense: what speaker meaning can attach to statements about him, whether the pretense was ever "meant" in Grice's sense, and what the final movement — George declaring the son dead and Martha's response — shows about a fiction becoming real, being exposed as never believed, or something else. Fourth, My verdict (roughly 200 words) stating plainly what the play adds to the philosophy of language that the Gricean apparatus alone does not, and what a Gricean should concede. Please don't hedge: I would rather have a reading I can argue with than a balanced list of interpretations, and if you think the Gricean framing is simply the wrong lens for this play, say so and say why.
