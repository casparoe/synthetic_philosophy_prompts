I'm a first-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I share an office with Rosa, a fourth-year who's writing her dissertation on demarcation. Since my seminar paper is basically about how people actually draw the science/pseudoscience line as opposed to how they say they draw it, I've been keeping a note on my phone of the verdicts she drops over lunch. So far: astrology is pseudoscience, but not because it's unfalsifiable, she thinks that's the lazy version and her line is that astrologers have an answer for every result and never run a study that could embarrass them; creation science, pseudoscience; homeopathy, pseudoscience; Fomenko's new chronology, pseudohistory, and she treats that as the same verdict in a different costume; Atlantis and the ancient-aliens stuff, also pseudohistory; the multiverse and many-worlds, not pseudoscience, because it's metaphysics riding along with a healthy physics and the people doing it argue in good faith and would drop it if it stopped paying rent; cold fusion, not pseudoscience, just a claim that got tested and lost; the whole social-priming and ego-depletion mess, where the effect keeps failing to replicate and the authors keep saying it only shows up with the right moderator or the right experimenter, she says is not pseudoscience either, just a subfield that is probably wrong, because they submit to review and eventually eat the nulls; and Popper on Freud, she thinks he was just wrong, analysis isn't pseudoscience, it's mostly dead but it was a real research tradition. She also said once that she'd rather say a claim isn't even wrong than call it pseudoscience, which I wrote down and then couldn't get her to explain.

The reason I actually care: my older brother has an MSc in physics, works as a data analyst, and for the last two years he's been building a theory that consciousness is a field and that the field shows up as tiny correlations between human attention and the output of hardware random number generators. He's got about forty people in a Discord running web-based runs against a public RNG service, a Substack, a little wiki of results. Credit where it's due: he preregisters each run before he looks, he posts his nulls publicly, he dropped two sub-claims last year after a guy in the server found a bug in his analysis code, and he isn't selling courses or supplements or anything. But he won't submit to a journal, he says the referees in this area are hostile and anyway the effect is too small for that kind of venue, and when a run comes out flat he says the effect is fragile and he's still hunting for the conditions it needs, and he also thinks that skeptics watching the data live can suppress it, though he says he isn't sure about that last one. My parents have started calling it his hobby, which is its own kind of verdict I guess.

I'm presenting this at the grad workshop Thursday as my case study and Rosa will be in the room. Will she call my brother a pseudoscientist? I don't want it depends, I want to know which rule she's actually running, what the other rules are that fit everything she's said so far just as well, what each of them predicts about my brother specifically, which one I should bet on and why, and the one extra case I could describe to her that would make her show her hand. Also, off the record, if the answer is that she would call him one, is there anything in his setup that a reasonable person would say is the thing doing the damage, because I have to sit across from him at Thanksgiving.
