I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about how the brain manufactures the feeling of the present moment. While reporting it I sat in on a conversation between two people I'd interviewed separately — a vision researcher who studies apparent motion and an independent philosopher of mind who has written skeptically about "neural editing" — and they ended up in a genuinely substantive fight that I couldn't referee on the spot, in part because I couldn't tell whether they were disagreeing about the mind or just about words. The setup they were arguing over is the color phi phenomenon: two dots are flashed in quick succession at different spots on a screen, roughly a tenth of a second apart, and the second dot is a different color from the first. Observers don't report two dots; they report a single dot gliding from the first position to the second. The strange part is the color: they report seeing the dot change color partway along its path — apparently before the second dot was ever shown. The researcher's framing is that the brain must be reaching backward to insert a color change into a stretch of motion that, at the moment it supposedly occurred, hadn't been delivered yet. The philosopher kept using the labels that have attached to this debate — the Orwellian reading, on which an experience really happens and is then rewritten in memory, like a ministry of truth airbrushing a photograph; and the Stalinesque reading, on which nothing is rewritten because nothing was ever shown in the first place, the whole coherent percept being staged after the fact and back-dated to look as though it unfolded in real time. Both kept insisting the other's label was doing illegitimate work.

The exchange went roughly like this. The researcher: the phenomenon is proof that experience is edited after the fact; you cannot report a color change at a point on the trajectory before the second flash unless the percept is stitched together retroactively, so the now of experience is partly written in the past tense. The philosopher: that is too quick, because all the data deliver is the final report, and there need never have been an experience of a plain single-colored dot that got overwritten; the simpler story is that the percept isn't handed over until both flashes have arrived, and then a single moving, changing dot is presented to you as if it had unfolded in real time, with no earlier true record falsified. The researcher: but that isn't a real difference — both stories say the content you end up with doesn't match what the stimulus delivered at the time, so calling one revision and the other delay just relocates the editing without removing it; either way you never experience the present, only a past assembled to look present. The philosopher: relocating it changes everything, because on the delay reading there is no moment at which you genuinely experienced the wrong color, so experience remains a real if slightly late event, whereas the revision reading saddles you with a genuine experience that your mind then falsified — a much stronger and stranger claim, which the phenomenon gives no independent reason to accept, and parsimony favors the weaker one. The researcher: and I say the two are indistinguishable in principle, since every test ends in a report and both hypotheses predict the same reports; when two stories are empirically equivalent, calling one more parsimonious is a metaphysical preference dressed up as a finding, so the philosopher has shown only that he'd rather not believe it.

I'd like you to adjudicate this as a bystander would — I'm not on either side and I genuinely don't know who has the better of it. Go through the exchange move by move, rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then tell me where the real crux is: whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, whether parsimony is being used legitimately or as a substitute for an argument, and whether the researcher is right that the two readings are empirically equivalent in principle. Finally, say what each of them is missing — what a third party would want to point out that neither raised. Please state explicitly how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of.
