My verdicts on which systems have reasons and which merely behave in reason-like ways are these. A thermostat switching on the furnace has no reason to do anything; it simply does it. A bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient seems to me to respond to something reason-like — its movement is about the sugar and is corrected when the gradient misleads it — yet nothing there is thinking. A spider spinning its first web acts for a purpose, but the purpose is not the spider's in any sense I can make out. My immune system raising antibodies against a pathogen it has never encountered is not reasoning, even though it looks uncannily as though it has drawn a conclusion. A chess engine that searches lines and discards the bad ones has reasons, and I say so without hesitation, though I doubt there is anything it is like to be it. A network trained by reinforcement to play a game, when it exploits a shortcut no one taught it, strikes me as acting for a reason. And natural selection producing the vertebrate eye is the clearest case I know of a rationale that floats free of any reasoner.

What I cannot reconcile is this: I deny reasons to the immune system and to natural selection, but grant them to the trained network, even though all three are blind variation plus retention, and none of the three appreciates its own grounds. Is there a principle that yields these verdicts, or close to them? State it precisely, name the case that motivates each clause, and say honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then apply it to two cases I have not sorted: a slime mold that finds its way through a maze to food, and a market whose clearing price induces suppliers to ship goods where they are wanted.
