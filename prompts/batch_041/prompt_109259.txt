I sit on the governance committee of a member-governed professional association — about 6,000 members, an elected twelve-member board. At its meeting on 9 October the board must decide how to allocate an unbudgeted surplus of roughly $1.4 million, and the membership is genuinely split. Four proposals are on the table:

A. Cut dues by 15 percent for two years.
B. Launch an open-access peer-reviewed journal.
C. Create a member travel-grant program.
D. Place the entire sum in the operating reserve and endowment.

The board commissioned a ranked straw poll of a representative sample of the membership (n = 300, weighted to the membership profile). Every respondent ranked all four proposals; the poll reports the sample in four groups by ranking:

30%: A, then B, then C, then D
27%: B, then C, then A, then D
23%: C, then A, then B, then D
20%: D, then C, then B, then A

Two further facts. Our chair has announced that the board will settle the matter by pairwise votes taken in this order: A against C, the survivor against D, the survivor against B. She describes this as nothing more than the order in which the proposals were submitted. And Article IX permits the board to adopt internal voting procedures by simple majority of the board, subject to ratification by the membership at the annual ballot in April; last April's ballot drew 11.4 percent turnout, and any single member's vote has effectively no chance of being decisive.

Last year, after a disputed election, the committee adopted a three-part test that any procedure we recommend must pass: the outcome must not depend on the order in which proposals are considered; no member's ballot may count for more than another's, and no proposal may receive special treatment; and a member must be able to have the procedure explained in two sentences. Two colleagues object. One says the order-independence condition is a trap, because any procedure that satisfies it can be gamed by members who misreport their rankings, and that we should therefore keep the familiar rule. The other says that on a simple yes-or-no question — as April's ballot will be — nothing beats majority rule, since it is decisive, treats every voter alike, and treats both answers alike, so the board should not depart from majority rule anywhere in this process.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the 9 October meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action, with a time frame. State what the chair's announced order would produce and whether it can be defended. State what your recommended procedure would produce on this profile, and if it produces a tie, say so and specify how the tie should be broken. Address directly whether the board may adopt a procedure and then use it to decide the very question that prompted the change, or whether that is self-serving. Say plainly whether, on this profile, there is any fact of the matter about what the members want, and what follows from your answer for how the board should describe its decision in public. Engage the strongest argument for the position you reject: a dissenting board member should be able to read your memo without feeling caricatured. Two pages at most; I do not need citations.
