I'm writing a long feature on tacit skill — the people who can do things they cannot explain — and before I file I want to state the strongest philosophical objection to my subject's own self-description, then answer it. A researcher I interviewed, who regards the distinction between knowing how and knowing that as a piece of folklore, handed me the argument below and invited me to break it. I have not managed to, which is why I'm asking someone else to.

(1) When we say that someone knows how to do something, we credit her with knowledge, not merely with a knack. If the phrase named nothing but an ability, it would be a euphemism, and we could not say that she had forgotten how, or that she knew how but could not bring herself to do it.
(2) Knowledge is knowledge of truths, and a truth that is known is a proposition that is known.
(3) So if someone knows how to do something, there is some proposition she knows in virtue of which she can do it.
(4) Maya grades the ripeness of a mango by touch with almost perfect reliability, on a scale finer than any instrument she has been shown, and she is not deluded about her own competence.
(5) So Maya knows how to grade mangoes.
(6) By (3), there is a proposition p that she knows and that accounts for her reliability.
(7) A proposition is the content of a possible statement. Whatever no finite statement could capture is therefore not a proposition, since there would be nothing for a thinker to grasp and nothing for a sentence to mean.
(8) Maya is fluent, cooperative, and has spent years with teachers who tried to make her trade explicit. Every rule she has been offered — including rules that reproduce her verdicts on every fruit she has ever seen — she rejects as not what she goes by. What she tracks, she says, is not any single feature but the whole way the fruit sits in the hand, and no list of features she has heard comes close.
(9) If p were a proposition and Maya knew it, then, being a fluent speaker who has tried, she could state it.
(10) She cannot. So either p is not a proposition, against (2) and (6); or she does not know it, against (5); or knowing it is not what accounts for her reliability, against (3).

He draws from the last two steps the conclusion that the distinction is a fiction: what we call knowing how is either a proposition that does no explanatory work or a non-propositional capacity that is not knowledge at all. He anticipates the obvious reply — that one can know a proposition without being able to state it — and calls it too cheap, on the ground that whatever she knows must be the very thing she acts on. I would rather not put his conclusion in print, and I suspect a step is doing something quietly. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up commit me to elsewhere? I would most like to keep (4) and (5) — I have watched her work — and I am attached to (1), because without it I am not sure what I am praising when I praise her.
