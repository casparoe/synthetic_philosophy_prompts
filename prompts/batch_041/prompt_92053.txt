1. Age bucket. Gen Z is just a birth-year bucket; everything you notice about them is an age effect that'll wash out as they age. The label is a filing convenience, nothing more.
2. Formative imprint. A generation is a real peer personality forged by whatever was happening in your teens and early twenties -- a war, a crash, a new technology -- and that stamp is permanent, it doesn't wash out.
3. Period effect in disguise. What looks generational is really everyone alive being reshaped at once by the same thing, and we're just watching different age groups react to it. Nobody is "a generation"; we're all in the same weather.
4. Looping label. The category makes itself real: tell a fourteen-year-old they're Gen Z, sell them Gen Z stuff, get them to sort themselves into it, and they become more Gen Z over time. The label has causal feedback.
5. Measurement artifact. The birth-year cutoffs are arbitrary, but once surveys, marketers and the cohort itself all start reporting by them, the cutoff becomes a real sorting device. Fake boundary, real category.
6. Bundling artifact. The traits pinned on a cohort (broke, anxious, online) aren't actually correlated with each other; they got narrativized into one character with no common cause underneath.
