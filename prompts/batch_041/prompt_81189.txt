I have no training in philosophy, but over the past few months I have read a good deal about how we should talk about what AI systems think, and I have reached a disagreement with a friend that I cannot settle on my own. My friend's position is that Dennett's intentional stance is the right guide for deciding whether a language model believes anything: you attribute beliefs when treating the system as a believer predicts its behaviour better than treating it as a mere mechanism, and the further question of whether it really believes, over and above the usefulness of the attribution, is either empty or answered by whether the pattern you are tracking is a real one rather than a convenient fiction. I hold something more demanding, closer to what I take the mechanistic interpretability literature to assume, and which I would call a representational criterion: a system believes that P only if it carries some internal state that tracks P and that actually does work in producing its behaviour, so that the attribution has to be earned by locating the state, not by the fact that belief-talk happens to summarise the system's behaviour efficiently.

Please compare the two as carefully as you can, and in particular tell me what each criterion counts as evidence for a belief attribution; whether they can come apart, with cases in both directions, such as a system whose internal states let you read off some fact about the world that it nevertheless never uses, and a system whose behaviour is best predicted by belief-talk even though nothing inside it resembles a representation; whether the appeal to real patterns quietly collapses the intentional stance into my representational criterion or leaves a genuine gap between them; and what the intentional stance can do that my criterion cannot, since I suspect there is something it captures about attributions that are useful before any internal state has been found.

Two things push me around here. One is the probing evidence I have read about, where a transformer trained only to predict legal moves in a board game turns out to have internal states from which you can decode the board position, which seems to favour my side. The other is the reply that probing finds whatever is linearly decodable, and decodability is not use, which I find harder to answer than I expected. I would like a verdict on which criterion should guide our practice of saying that a system believes things, along with the observation or argument that would most move you from one to the other; and if you think part of the dispute between my friend and me is verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
