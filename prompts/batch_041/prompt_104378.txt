I write about science and its disputed edges for a general-interest magazine, and over the years I've kept a private ledger of gut verdicts about which fringe claims deserve the label pseudoscience and which don't. I'd like a principle that captures the pattern, if there is one, and I'm ready to be told that some of my verdicts have to go. None of this is a request for reading; I just want the reasoning.

Here is the ledger. (1) A retired mathematician's multi-volume re-dating of ancient history, which uses statistical patterns in old chronicles and astronomical references in ancient texts to argue that most recorded history before roughly the eleventh century was fabricated or duplicated by later scribes, so that, say, ancient Egypt and medieval Europe are the same civilization shifted in time. My verdict: pseudoscience, despite the quantitative apparatus. (2) Claims that ancient monuments required extraterrestrial help to build. Verdict: pseudoscience. (3) A lone early-twentieth-century scientist's proposal that the continents drift — ridiculed for decades, then absorbed into the modern picture. Verdict: not pseudoscience, not even at the height of its rejection. (4) Homeopathy. Verdict: pseudoscience. (5) Certain unobservable speculations in mainstream theoretical physics — extra universes, or interpretations of quantum mechanics that make no distinctive prediction anyone could test. Verdict: not pseudoscience, though I'll admit these are about as untestable as (1). (6) Cold fusion: a startling result announced to the press before peer review, never replicated, defended for decades. Verdict: I keep going back and forth — I want to say the first report was just bad science and only the later defense became pseudoscience, but I can't find the line where it flips. (7) A dietary theory that makes checkable predictions, fails them, and explains each failure away ("the trials were too short," "the wrong population"). Verdict: pseudoscience, I think, though it never stops making predictions. (8) A historian who uses standard archival methods but a controversial statistical technique to argue that a famous document is a forgery; the field rejects the conclusion but engages with the argument. Verdict: not pseudoscience.

Two of these pairs are the ones that keep me up at night. (2) and (3) were both rejected by the experts of their day, yet I call one pseudoscience and not the other. (1) and (5) are both, on their face, unfalsifiable, yet I call one pseudoscience and not the other.

My tentative principle: a claim is pseudoscientific to the degree that its defenders make it unfalsifiable in practice — arrange things so no conceivable observation could count against it — whereas a claim is merely wrong, or unorthodox, or speculative when its defenders stick their necks out and can lose. I suspect this is close, but it seems to make (5) a problem (its defenders arguably do the same immunizing), and it doesn't obviously separate (6) from (7).

Please test and repair this. If one principle can't save every verdict, give me the smallest set of candidates and be explicit about which of my verdicts each one overturns. Then run the repaired account on a case I haven't listed, which I'll state here: a well-funded group claims a new fundamental particle at a specific mass; the collider finds nothing; the group argues the detector wasn't sensitive enough, nudges the mass, and carries on, decade after decade. Pseudoscience or normal science, and why.

Structure your reply as follows. First, "The principle," stated precisely, in roughly 150 to 250 words. Second, "What each clause is for," roughly 300 words, tying every clause to the specific case that earns it. Third, "Verdicts on the case I didn't give you," roughly 200 words, plus a second case of your own invention that the principle is strained by. Fourth, "Which of my verdicts I have to give up," roughly 200 words, naming them and defending the trade.
