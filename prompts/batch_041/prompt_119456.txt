I teach a seminar on death, meaning, and the human future, and we spend the middle of the term on Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife, which argues that a great deal of what we value rests on an assumption we rarely notice: that human life will go on long after we ourselves are dead. Scheffler calls that continuing human life the collective afterlife, and he tests our attachment to it with a pair of thought experiments — a doomsday in which the earth is destroyed a few decades after our own generation is gone, and a case of universal infertility in which no further children are ever born and the species simply runs out with the last of us — arguing that both prospects would drain our projects of their point in a way that the bare fact of our own mortality does not. His conclusion, that our own survival matters to us less than the survival of the human race, is what has come to be known as the afterlife conjecture, and my students find it either thrilling or preposterous; I want to close the term with something that forces the issue rather than letting them restate it.

So for the final session I intend to pair the lectures with Lars von Trier's Melancholia, and I would like you to write the reading I will hand them. What I want is genuinely two-way criticism, not a plot summary with the conjecture pasted over it. Take seriously what the film shows about the conjecture: the wedding in the first half as a value-laden project that only makes sense against a future — the marriage, the promotion, the campaign slogan Justine is expected to compose during her own reception — and Justine's withdrawal from all of it, which can be read as the conjecture's prediction arriving in a single person ahead of schedule; then the second half's pairing of Claire, who looks up the planet's trajectory on the internet and comes apart, with Justine, who has been unable to rise from her bed or eat, who declares that all life on earth is evil and deserves to die, and who is calm. Press equally on where the film refuses the conjecture. Its apocalypse is simultaneous, so that unlike Scheffler's scenarios it leaves no one occupying the position of the survivor who learns that the human future is gone; Claire's terror therefore has no subject left to be a terror about, and I want you to say whether that makes her the film's Schefflerian and Justine its clear-eyed denier, or whether it exposes something the thought experiments quietly presuppose. Note too that the film gives Justine knowledge rather than arguments — she turns out to have known, without guessing, how many beans were in the bottle at her wedding, and she asserts with the same authority that the universe holds no other life — and say what it means for a film to settle a philosophical question by fiat of character.

The last ten minutes are what I care most about. Justine, who has been unable to rise from her bed, takes charge and builds a shelter of branches for her nephew, telling him it is a magic cave that will keep them safe; the three of them sit inside it holding hands as the planet fills the sky, and Claire lets go. I will be showing that sequence and then asking the class what it means. Is it a counterexample to Scheffler — a value that is entirely present-tense, undertaken with no future to sustain it, and treated by the film as the one moment of grace available? Or is it his view confirmed, since the shelter is a consoling lie told to a child, and since the small human community the three of them briefly form is annihilated in the very instant it is formed? I would like a reading that neither reduces the film to an illustration of the philosophy nor trims the philosophy to fit the film, that says plainly where the reading strains, and that ends by telling me what the film adds to the debate that the lectures on their own cannot — what it shows about the texture of the loss, or about whether the conjecture can even be stated without the standpoint of a survivor. A colleague in film studies insists the film is a study of depression that happens to have a planet in it, and that any Schefflerian reading of it is a category error; I think she is wrong, but I would like the reading to be the sort that could answer her.
