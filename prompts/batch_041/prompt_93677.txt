For most of my career I have covered physics from the outside, and I have arrived at a position I did not expect to hold: that the multiverse — the ensemble of regions or vacua that inflationary cosmology and string theory appear to generate — is a legitimate scientific hypothesis, even though it may never be testable in any direct, decisive way. My reasoning is not aesthetic. It is that a theory can earn rational credence through explanatory unification, consistency with well-established physics, and the failure of every competitor, rather than through a unique prediction; that the demand for decisive empirical testability is a positivist inheritance we would not impose on historical geology, or on the atomic hypothesis before it could be probed directly; and that taking that demand seriously would have ruled out much of what we now accept. I want to know what would count against this, and I want it specific rather than a restatement of the falsifiability charge, which I already know. Which developments, arguments, or conceivable results would genuinely count against the view, and which would be merely awkward? Which of them would the people who actually work in this area — the ones who think it is science — concede as counting against them? I am interested in three things in particular. First, whether the argument from the absence of alternatives is a claim about the world or only about our collective imagination, and what becomes of my view if it is the latter; relatedly, what my argument says if someone constructs a rival that is empirically indistinguishable from the multiverse but conceptually different — does it give me any reason to prefer one over the other, and if not, what exactly is being confirmed? Second, whether a framework that can accommodate any observation is thereby supported or thereby exposed, and whether the probabilistic version of the case — that the world is more likely given the hypothesis — can survive the measure problem, or whether it quietly collapses back into the non-probabilistic version. Third, whether the multiverse that eternal inflation seems to predict and the landscape that string theory seems to imply should be assessed together at all, or whether my view is borrowing the credibility of one while inheriting the liabilities of the other. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what I would personally find most persuasive, and tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is fatal here or simply the price of doing business in this corner of physics.
