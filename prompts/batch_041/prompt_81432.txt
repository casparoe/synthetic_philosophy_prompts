I keep running into the statue-and-the-clay problem, the one where a lump of clay gets shaped into a statue and everyone asks whether the statue and the lump are the same object, on the grounds that the lump but not the statue survives being squashed back into a ball.

Restate that debate for me in the vocabulary I actually think in: phases and phase changes, microstructure, allotropy, composition versus structure, annealing versus quenching, mixtures versus compounds, the fact that the same atoms in the same proportions can give you two materials with completely different properties. Go position by position — the identity view, the constitution view (statue constituted by but not identical to the lump), and whatever the eliminativist or four-dimensionalist alternatives are, if they'll go through the translation at all.

Then give me the residue report. What comes across cleanly, what I have to fudge, and what has no expression whatsoever in my vocabulary. I especially want to know what happens to the modal part — the clay could have been a bowl, the statue couldnt have been — because my whole framework is about what actually happens at a given temperature, pressure and cooling rate, and "could have" looks like it's doing work I have no machinery for. Does the residue mean my way of talking about materials is missing something real, or that the philosophers' question was less well-formed than it sounds?

And a related thing I've never been sure about: when I quench a steel and it comes out hard instead of soft, is that a change of substance or not? It feels like the same question and I'd like to know if it is.
