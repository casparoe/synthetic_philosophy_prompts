I've spent fifteen years as a reporter and editor covering science and ideas, and I have lately been reading my way into a philosophical debate whose name I didn't know but whose shape I have apparently been living inside the whole time. As I understand it, the question is what justifies a belief, and the three classical answers are foundationalism, on which some beliefs are warranted without support from other beliefs and the rest rest on those; coherentism, on which beliefs are warranted by supporting one another, with no bottom and no top; and infinitism, on which every belief needs a further reason and the chain never ends and isn't supposed to. I would like you to restate each of the three as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: sourcing and attribution, on the record and on background, corroboration, chain of custody for a document, the practice of requiring a second independent source before a claim runs, the editor who asks how I know, the correction we publish when we turn out to be wrong, the reader we are asserting to rather than merely informing, and the deadline that ends the argument whether or not anyone has settled it. Take me through the translation position by position, and resist the temptation to make it tidier than it is.

Assume I know my own trade cold and have only a lay reader's acquaintance with the philosophy, so lean on the former and define the latter's terms as you go. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. A newsroom foundationalism sounds like having the document, or having seen it myself, but I cannot tell whether a primary document is a foundation in your sense or merely a place where inquiry stops for practical reasons, and I would like the difference made precise, including whether it matters. Our coherentism sounds like corroboration, except that ours is social and can be counterfeit: three sources who turn out to have heard it from the same person, which has burned me before. Tell me whether that is a flaw in my translation or a real difficulty for the view itself. Our infinitism sounds like the editor who can always demand one more layer of verification, which every newsroom I know treats as a vice rather than a virtue; tell me what the infinitist says about stopping, and whether stopping is ever justified or only unavoidable.

The part I most want is this. Our real stopping rule is, I think, none of the three. We publish when the expected cost of being wrong has fallen below the expected cost of staying silent, which sounds like a decision rule rather than a theory of justification, and I want to know whether that is a fourth position the debate has overlooked, a disguised foundationalism about practical reasons, or a confession that the whole dispute is about where to stop rather than about what justification is. End with your verdict on which of the three our practice most resembles, and whether the resemblance is a vindication or an embarrassment.
