i'm in a reading group thats doing Leibniz next week and i've got into an arguement with a friend that i keep losing. his move is always the same: fine, keep your earthquakes, keep the wars, just take out childhood leukaemia. one item. the world is obviously better without it, an all-powerful god could have done it and lost nothing, and anyone who says otherwise is doing PR for cancer. in prose i always end up sounding like i'm making excuses for tumours. so i want a poem instead.

give me a sestina, six six-line stanzas and the three-line envoi. voice: someone defending the line — could be Leibniz, could be a modern defender, could be god if you can pull that off without it getting gross, your call. it's addressed to the friend who keeps proposing one surgical improvement at a time, and it has to be an actual reply, meaning the answer isnt 'its a mystery, trust the plan' but the structure: a world isnt a bag of parts you can swap in and out, its a whole series of things that can coexist, and whats best is the best whole, not the sum of the best bits. leibniz even thinks god wills every good thing taken on its own and only the best whole taken all together, and that the best is whatever gets the most variety out of the fewest laws — happiness isnt the metric. and he'll say the fall was worth it, which is the part nobody wants to hear.

the form should be doing the arguing, not decorating it. six end-words, the things being bargained over — pick them yourself so they can carry a poem (pain, time, plague, whatever) — and the whole point is that every stanza has to carry all six, so you never get to hold one good and drop the rest. then the envoi, where the form crams all six into three lines with three of them buried mid-line: that compression should feel like the thesis, nothing thrown away, everything still there, but only as a whole. the last line should land it rather than trail off.

commit to it — pick your end-words, pick which Leibniz you're writing (the compossibility one, the variety-and-order one, the fall-was-worth-it one, or a mix), and dont hand me alternatives or a menu of forms. after the poem give me a short note: where you bent the doctrine, and which bits a Leibniz scholar would fight you about. and no Pangloss — thats the version everyone already has memorized, and the whole reason i'm asking is that it isnt that.
