So I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background and I've fell down a rabbit hole on Molinism and middle knowledge. The picture I get is that before God decides what to create, he already knows what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible situation, and this knowledge is supposed to be seperate both from knowing what's logically necessary and from knowing what he's actually going to will into existence. That middle bucket is middle knowledge, right?

Here's my problem. I keep modeling the whole thing like a lookup table. God has a giant table: rows are (creature, exact circumstances), columns are the free choice the creature would make. Before creation he consults the table, finds the row he likes, and actualizes that world. It's basically memoization, or a precomputed pure function. But then my inner engineer starts asking: who populated the table? A cache doesn't generate its own entries. And I think the critic's move here, the grounding objection, is exactly that: a counterfactual about what a free creature would do has to be true or false, and if it's true then something has to make it true, and if the creature never exists and never chooses then there's nothing there to do the making. So is it just a brute fact? Is that allowed?

I want a dialogue between two people who actually know this stuff, both competent, neither one a punching bag:

- Person A: a Molinist. Thinks middle knowledge is real, thinks counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have truth values, thinks this is how God providentially orders a world containing genuinely free creatures, and thinks he can handle the grounding objection.
- Person B: a theist who thinks the grounding objection is fatal. Not an atheist dunking on theism, but someone who wants divine sovereignty and creaturely freedom both fully intact and thinks Molinism buys one at the price of the other.

Let them go at it. Specifically I want them fighting about:

1. Whether something like "Dana would freely hand a stranger a $20 if she were in exactly situation S" is true before Dana exists, and what, if anything, makes it true. Have them use a concrete case like that instead of only abstractions.

2. My lookup-table analogy. Have one of them actually pick it up and either run with it or tear it apart, and have the other push back. I want to know if the analogy is a help or a trap. My hunch is that a table has to be filled in by somebody, so it either begs the question or smuggles in determinism, but I might be wrong about that.

3. A wrinkle I haven't seen anyone address: what if the table is sparse? Like, for some creature/situation pairs there's simply no fact about what they'd freely do, not because nobody can know it but because there isn't anything there to know. Person B should push this and Person A should have to say what it costs him. If the move is incoherent, one of them should say why.

Rules for the dialogue: real back-and-forth, each responds to the other's actual last point, no talking past each other. No strawmen, no cheap gotchas, and don't have them agree at the end just to be nice or converge on "it's a mystery" -- one of them should come out ahead, or at least they should be precise about exactly where the live disagreement sits. Don't worry about citing papers or naming who said what; I don't know the literature and can't look it up, so just have them argue from the ideas themselves.

Also: American English please. And at the end, tack on a short note in your own voice, a few sentences, telling me how confident you are that you've represented both positions fairly and which single point you're least sure about.
