I lead a small analytics team at a health insurer and we've quietly started running basically everything through AI assistants — writing SQL for the monthly claims pulls, drafting the plain-language reason on denial letters, sizing samples for outreach A/B tests, summarizing clinical literature when our medical director asks. I dont want to ban it, and I don't want a blanket "always verify" rule, because that isn't a procedure, it's a shrug. What I want is a decision procedure the team can actually run in the two minutes before they paste something in, that sorts each task into one of three buckets: take it and spot-check; take it but you personally have to be able to walk me through the reasoning; don't use the AI for this one at all. And I want it grounded in the real distinction between getting the right answer and actually understanding the thing, not vibes.

Two ideas I want you to build it around, which may or may not survive contact with the cases:

1. Treat understanding as the ability to be wrong on purpose. You understand a result if you can say what evidence would overturn it and how the answer would move if one of the inputs moved. If you can't do either, you have the output but not the thing.

2. Explanation debt. When the AI's answer is going to become a premise somebody else relies on — a number that goes into a filing, a rule that goes into code someone else maintains, a claim that reaches a member — someone on my team has to be able to discharge that debt by explaining it, and the debt does not get discharged by pointing at the model.

Please build the procedure from those, and be honest about the parts that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is really one of values rather than facts.

Context to respect: the call gets made dozens of times a day across a team of six, nobody on the team has formal stats training, two minutes is about all the patience anyone has, and anything that is member-facing or that touches pricing has to be signed off by compliance, who will not read a paragraph of philosophy. So the procedure has to say which bucket feeds into that sign-off and which doesn't.

Then run it on three cases and show your work: (a) generating the SQL for a routine monthly claims extract; (b) drafting the denial reason that a member will read; (c) choosing the method for a new risk-adjustment model that will eventually feed premiums and gets looked at by a regulator. I expect at least one of these to come out in a way the team won't like — if the procedure tells me the thing everyone most wants to offload is the thing we can't, say so plainly and don't soften it. Finish by telling me what this procedure can't do, and the specific situation where it will misfire and I'll need to override it.
