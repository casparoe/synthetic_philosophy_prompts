I'm TAing a second-year ethics unit and marking the scripts on consent. I've been going back and forth on this one all evening and its doing my head in, so I'd like a second read before I commit to a mark.

The question was:

Q3 (30 marks). 'Consent obtained by deception and consent obtained by coercion are both invalid, but only the second is not consent at all.' Discuss, using the three conditions for valid consent covered in the lectures — voluntariness, understanding and capacity — and explain what work each condition does.

And here's what the student wrote:

Consent is valid when it is given voluntarily, by someone who understands what she is agreeing to, and who has the capacity to make that decision. Coercion and deception each attack one of these conditions, which is why both invalidate consent, and the quotation is right to treat them differently.

Coercion attacks voluntariness. If I agree only because you threaten me, my agreement does not express my will; it is your threat doing the agreeing. So it is not consent at all. Deception attacks understanding. If I agree because you told me the operation was routine when it was not, I do agree — I consent, and I consent to the operation — but my consent rests on a false belief, so it is defective rather than absent. The distinction in the quotation is therefore sound: one is no consent, the other is bad consent.

This also tells us when deceptive consent is invalid. The deception has to be material: if the patient would have consented anyway, the lie made no difference to her decision, so the consent stands. Only lies that would have changed the decision defeat it.

Capacity, finally, is not really a third condition alongside the other two. To understand what you are consenting to you need the capacity to understand it, so capacity is the precondition of understanding; someone who lacks capacity fails the understanding condition rather than a separate one.

Voluntariness is a matter of how much pressure there is. A threat of something trivial does not undermine consent, because it does not move the will; a serious threat does.
