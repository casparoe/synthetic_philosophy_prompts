I write backend code for a living, no philosophy degree, and I've been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty on the train because a freind wouldn't shut up about it. There's a thing in it I can't get past, and I want an essay that actually takes a side rather than laying out the menu.

Here's the setup. At my last job there was an assumption everyone just operated on — that our metrics pipeline wasn't silently dropping events. Not a belief anybody argued for. Every performance debate, every rollback call, every postmortem ran on top of it. Nobody had proven it, nobody had a dashboard for it, and if you asked how we knew, the honest answer was that the question didn't really have a place in how we worked. Then it turned out to be false, had been for about two years, and the way we argued about performance changed wholesale. Not because someone won an argument. Because someone finally instrumented the thing and the floor moved under us.

As I understand him, Wittgenstein says our questions and our doubts depend on some propositions being exempt from doubt, like hinges a door turns on, and that what counts as bedrock is held up by our acting rather than by any proof. He also has the bit about thoughts having a river-bed that can shift — where something that was a settled rule becomes a flowing empirical proposition again, or the reverse.

So: is a hinge flip like the one above ever rational? Or is it just something that happens to a community, with the rationality only kicking in afterwards, once the new floor is already in place? Please weigh the main options and land on a verdict. Some positions to either take or reject along the way: (1) hinge revision is never justified by argument, only caused, and that isn't a defect of the view; (2) there is a coherent sense in which a hinge can be rationally abandoned, say when it stops paying its way across the whole practice; (3) hinge talk is just a fancy way of saying people can't be argued out of their frameworks, which is either true and bad or false and reassuring. Also please deal with the obvious cheap objection, which is that if you can't tell a hinge from a very confident ordinary belief, the whole framework is unfalsifiable — I want to know whether that objection has teeth or not.

Ground rules: no citations, no reading list, I won't check them. If you need an example, make one up. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would push you to the opposite verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think it's most likely to break down.
