I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for going on fifteen years, and the argument I keep returning to is Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the claim that we are all quietly banking on the survival of people who will come after us, and that a great deal of what we care about would lose its point if we learned that humanity would end shortly after our own deaths — even though no living person would be harmed by that ending, and even though we would all be dead by then regardless. Discussions of it usually go one of two ways: either people nod along and conclude that extinction must be bad for reasons beyond the deaths involved, or they object that the thought experiment only reveals an irrational attachment to our own projects. Both moves feel too quick to me, and neither is a story.

So: write a short story that takes the conjecture seriously as worldbuilding and stress-tests it rather than illustrating it.

Setting and constraints:

- The premise is public, precise, and undisputed: the human species will end in roughly eighty years, for reasons the story need not explain and should not dramatize. Everyone now alive will die of ordinary causes first, so no one alive is harmed by the ending. There is no ambiguity to hedge with and no last-minute reprieve.
- Tell it from the point of view of someone whose work faces backward rather than forward — a conservator, archivist, translator, restorer, or something in that family — so that "who is this for?" is a live question for a project that was never aimed at the future to begin with.
- Put at least three responses to the premise into the story, embodied in characters and concrete decisions rather than announced: one who finds their work emptied of point, one who finds it entirely unaffected, and one whose sense of mattering is attached to something that is not future people at all (a standard, a practice, a discipline, an institution that will outlive them by a month).
- Include at least one attempt to substitute a smaller afterlife for the collective one — a single reader, a family line, a footnote, a record — and let it hold or fail on its own terms.
- Include one character or faction who tries to widen the conjecture, arguing that the survival of something successor-ish (an engineered species, a machine, a language, a text, a distant intelligence) can do the work that future generations were doing, and one who insists that only our own kind, remembering us, can. Do not adjudicate between them in the narration.
- No character may state the thesis, and no ending may deliver a verdict. End on an action, an image, or a decision with costs.

Form: prose fiction, roughly 1,500–2,500 words, no exposition dumps, no named philosophers, no lecture delivered by a wise elder. Assume the reader already knows the conjecture; do not summarize it for them. The reader should be able to reconstruct the positions from what the characters do and from what they cannot bring themselves to say.
