I'm partway through a graduate seminar on philosophical logic, and we have just finished several weeks on Aristotle's sea-battle argument for fatalism. I can recite the argument's steps in my sleep, so that is not what I am writing about. What I cannot sort out is a genuine disagreement among the treatments we have read, and I would like you to explain the conceptual issue to me rather than hand me a summary. Roughly half of the discussions treat the argument as resting on a scope error: it is said to slide illicitly from the claim that a future event is truly predicted to the claim that it is necessary, and once the modal operator is kept in its proper place the fatalist conclusion simply evaporates. The other half treat the argument as a serious problem that compels a substantive revision of our logic, most often the abandonment of bivalence for statements about contingent future events, so that a prediction of a sea battle tomorrow comes out neither true nor false today.

Please explain whether these two diagnoses are actually in conflict or whether they are answering different questions, and in particular what the scope-error diagnosis is supposed to establish. Does it really dissolve the puzzle, or does it merely relocate the difficulty into the question of why we were ever tempted to think that the mere truth of a prediction threatens the openness of the future? If the diagnosis is correct, why does it seem that something more was needed than a distinction of scope?

I would also like you to address whether the godless version of the argument and the theological version are the same problem in different dress. The standard reply to the theological version denies that an infallible knower's present beliefs are causally prior to the events known, and concludes that they therefore do not necessitate those events; I want to know whether that reply has any analogue once no knower is in the picture, or whether the godless version remains exposed to the argument in a way the theological version does not. A further wrinkle that I suspect is doing hidden work: whether the future tense in these statements is functioning as a mere tense or as a modal notion, and whether the whole puzzle looks different once that ambiguity is noticed. Throughout, be explicit about which step of the argument each proposed fix rejects, and say whether any of them can preserve bivalence while still blocking the fatalist conclusion. I am after the conceptual lay of the land, not a survey of who said what.
