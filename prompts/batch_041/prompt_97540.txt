There is a move I keep encountering in rationalist-adjacent discussions of consciousness, and I would like it audited rather than applauded. The move runs roughly as follows. Before Darwin and the biochemists, the story goes, people took life to be a special something over and above the ordinary physical processes — an élan vital, a life-force — and took there to be an unbridgeable explanatory gap between dead chemistry and living things. Once we understood life as a particular kind of organized process, the gap turned out to be an artifact of our ignorance, and vitalism was exposed as a pseudo-problem rather than a deep mystery. The hard problem of consciousness, on this telling, has exactly the same shape: the felt explanatory gap between brain processes and experience is a gap in our imagination rather than in nature, and a mature science of information processing will show that experience just is a certain functional organization, at which point the residual sense of mystery will look as quaint as asking where the life-force goes when an organism dies. The practical conclusion people draw is that one should not be a mysterian, should not treat consciousness as a special barrier to explanation, and should simply fund the neuroscience and the computational work and wait.

I want the correspondence laid out explicitly — a table is fine — with each slot of the vitalism story paired to its alleged counterpart: what plays the role of the life-force, of the pre-Darwinian explanatory gap, of the dissolving explanation, and of the people who insisted the gap was real. Then I want the places where the mapping breaks, and I want them sorted. In particular, I suspect the analogy quietly imports several features of the vitalism case that may not carry over: that the disputed entity was posited to do causal work which a mechanism could then take over; that both ends of the alleged gap could be independently identified (chemistry on one side, the observable functions of life on the other); and that the dispute was empirical, so that further research could settle it. Tell me which of these survive translation to the consciousness case and which do not, and which of the resulting disanalogies actually bear on the deflationary conclusion rather than merely decorating it.

The interlocutor I have in mind goes one step further: he says the analogy also settles the question of machine consciousness, because if experience is nothing over and above functional organization then a system implementing the right organization is conscious, full stop, and there is no further fact to wait for. I want to know whether that extension is supported by the analogy itself or is an independent premise riding along. And finally: does the deflationary conclusion — that the hard problem will dissolve as vitalism did — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If it is a bad analogy, what would a better one be for the deflationist, and what would a good analogy be for the other side?
