I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and my city has asked me to give the opening remarks at a citizens' assembly it has convened to decide how to spend a fixed capital fund — sixty residents chosen by lot, three Saturdays of deliberation, a few councillors sitting in, and a panel of engineers and economists presenting evidence in separate sessions. I have the first ten minutes, before any of the evidence is presented, to say what kind of occasion this is.

I want to build the remarks on Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, because the room holds two kinds of resistance at once. The councillors suspect the whole exercise is a glorified opinion poll, and that deliberation is just where the quiet get talked round by the confident; they will be listening for anything that sounds like an argument for handing public decisions to a random sample. Most of the sixty have never read a word of philosophy and will switch off the moment I use a term like inquiry or warranted assertibility. Two of the academics in the room will wince at any version of Dewey loose enough to be said out loud.

So the ideas need to do structural work rather than decorate the speech. I want three of them to carry it. First, that Dewey treats inquiry as beginning from a situation that is genuinely unsettled, not from a menu of options already on the table — which means I have to say, plainly and early, that the question the assembly has been handed may itself be rewritten. (The remit asks for the best use of the fund over thirty years, and I want to exploit how much is packed into the word best.) Second, that a public, on his account, is not a group of people who already share interests but one that comes into being when people become aware of consequences they hold in common — that is how I want to answer the councillors without flattering them. Third, that he took the outcome of inquiry to be a warranted and revisable conclusion rather than a certainty, which is why the remarks cannot end by promising that sixty strangers will get this right.

One local detail worth weaving in: the organisers have adopted a house rule of their own — at the close of each Saturday, every participant has to name one thing that would change their mind. I think that rule is the most Deweyan object in the building, and I would like the speech to notice it without turning it into a slogan. I should also be honest that I have written sceptically about deliberative exercises before; I don't want the ten minutes to read as a conversion.

Please write the remarks: spoken prose a non-philosopher can follow at one hearing, Dewey named at most once and only if it earns its place, nothing the academics would call a misreading, and no ending that tells the room it is about to make history.
