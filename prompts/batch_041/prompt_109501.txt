I'm a second-year PhD student (decision theory / phil of action) and I need help with something. Two people in my cohort have been arguing about money pumps for about a week and have now recruited me as referee, which I did not sign up for. I'll lay the exchange out as faithfully as I can.

The case that started it: an agent — call her S — has preferences that cycle over three items. She prefers the apple to the banana, the banana to the cherry, and the cherry to the apple. She's holding the apple and has a small pile of coins. A trader offers to swap her apple for a cherry and sweetens it with a penny. Since she prefers the cherry to the apple, she takes it. Then the trader offers the banana for the cherry plus a penny; she prefers the banana, so she takes it. Then the apple for the banana plus a penny. She's back where she started, minus three pennies, and the trader can run it again.

Move 1 (Jules): this proves cyclic preferences are irrational. No information, no skill, no risk on the trader's side — the trap is built entirely out of S's own rankings. A transitive agent is immune to exactly this. So transitivity isn't a convenience for building models, it's a requirement of rationality.

Move 2 (Ren): it only works if she says yes at every step. She can see the sequence coming and just keep the apple; she's lost nothing, and nothing about her preferences obliges her to accept any particular offered trade. So the pump shows that myopically grabbing every preferred option is bad, not that the cycle is. And transitive agents get pumped constantly — offer someone a series of bets, each of which she'd take, that jointly guarantee a loss. Nobody concludes her transitive preferences are irrational. Being exploitable isn't a fingerprint of intransitivity.

Move 3 (Jules): if she refuses, she isn't choosing by her preferences — she prefers the cherry to the apple and can have it for a penny. So she's either pumped (if she follows her rankings) or overriding them (if she commits to refusing). A transitive agent never faces that fork: she can follow her rankings at every step and stay safe. That asymmetry is the whole arguement and it survives your objection.

Move 4 (Ren): sure, but 'follow your rankings at every step' isn't the only standard of rational choice. She can have a policy — don't enter chains of trades that leave me somewhere I'd rather not be. That isn't overriding her preferences, it's having preferences over trajectories rather than over individual swaps. Once you allow that, the cycle over the three items doesn't settle what she should do, and the pump dissolves. Jules: then tell me what her trajectory-preferences are. If she prefers apple-plus-coins to cherry-minus-a-penny, you've replaced the cyclic agent with a transitive one and stipulated the cycle away.

Move 5 (Jules, changing targets): anyway, the same pump works against someone whose preferences merely change over time. Suppose at t1 she prefers to be a non-smoker at t2, but at t2 she prefers a cigarette. Offer her, at each moment, a small swap that's an improvement by her lights at that moment; she ends up somewhere she'd have rejected at t1, and out of pocket. If the pump shows cyclic preferences are irrational, it shows time-inconsistent ones are too. Which is a lot of people.

Move 6 (Ren): no, these come apart. In the diachronic case there's a determinate answer to 'what does she want' at each time, and the conflict is between selves; we handle that with a theory of prudence, commitment, self-binding, whatever, without calling anyone incoherent. In the cyclic case there isn't even a determinate answer at a single time — no best option, no faithful ordering. Different kind of problem, and the pump is doing different work in the two cases.

So: please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right and give me a sentence or two on each. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and who's more right overall. I'd also like to know whether the pump really does anything against cyclic preferences once the foresight point is on the table, or whether it just tells us not to be myopic.
