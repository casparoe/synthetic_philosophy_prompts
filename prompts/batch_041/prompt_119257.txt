I am a software engineer — firmware for coin-operated and ticket-dispensing equipment — with no formal background in philosophy, and a running argument with a colleague has pushed me into the literature on intentionality further than I can comfortably go on my own. The dispute, as I now understand it, has two sides. On one side is the thought that a machine like ours has aboutness only by courtesy: our controller's state machine has states named for the world — coin_present, jam_suspected, expects_change — and whatever aboutness those states carry is borrowed from the people who wrote the firmware and the documentation, whereas a person's aboutness is not borrowed from anyone. On the other side is Dennett's two-bitser: a coin machine that has been fooled by a slug can be described either as believing that a slug is a quarter or as simply malfunctioning, and there appears to be no fact of the matter that settles which description is correct. If there is no fact of the matter there, then the borrowed-versus-genuine distinction may not be doing any real work.

What I would like is an essay that takes both sides seriously instead of settling the matter with a slogan, and that reaches a verdict. Two test cases should carry the argument, because they are the ones that seem to break the standard formulations. First, our controller runs unattended in a car park: the designer is long gone, the firmware is frozen, no human being is present at runtime, and yet the machine keeps behaving as though it were tracking coins. Second, a growing share of systems like ours are not written by people at all — a learned component or a code generator produces the coin detector, and in principle whatever produced it could itself be another machine, and so on upward without a human link. If intentionality is always derived from something else, does the chain require a non-machine terminus, and if so, on what grounds — and if not, what remains of the difference between the thermostat and the person?

I would like the essay to set out the strongest version of each position, use my two cases as the test cases rather than restating the usual textbook examples, and then commit to an answer: state whether derived intentionality names a real distinction or merely a place where we stop asking questions, and say what follows for how I ought to talk about my own machine and about the code generators I increasingly rely on. Please make the argument within the essay itself — I do not want a reading list, bibliography, or citations, since I would have no way to check them; I want the case made, and a conclusion defended.
