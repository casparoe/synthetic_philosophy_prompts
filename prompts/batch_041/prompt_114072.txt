The question (20 marks; partial credit available):

Q3. Read the exchange, then answer (a)–(c).

Context: A hiring committee meeting. The chair asks Dana, "Can we count on Priya to have the shortlist ready by Friday?" Dana replies:

"Priya has read some of the files, but she hasn't booked any interview slots."

(a) [10 marks] Each of the following is something Dana's reply might be taken to communicate. For each, say which category it falls into — entailment, conventional implicature, generalized conversational implicature, or particularized conversational implicature — and name the test that justifies the classification (cancelability, detachability, calculability, or the contradiction test for entailment). Be explicit about what the test shows.

(i) Priya has read at least one of the files.
(ii) Priya has not read all of the files.
(iii) There is a contrast of some kind between Priya's having read files and her not having booked slots.
(iv) Priya will not have the shortlist ready by Friday.

(b) [4 marks] Explain Grice's distinction between what is said and what is implicated, and say which of (i)–(iv), if any, belong to what is said.

(c) [6 marks] A classmate objects: "If implicatures are just things the hearer infers, then Gricean implicature can't explain how we communicate — anything could be implicated." Assess this objection.

The student's answer:

(a)(i) Entailment. If Priya has read some of the files then it simply follows, in virtue of what "some" means, that she has read at least one. You can't say "Priya has read some of the files but she hasn't read any of them" without contradicting yourself, which is the contradiction test.

(ii) Conventional implicature. When someone says "some," part of the conventional meaning of the word is that it's not all — that's just what "some" means as opposed to "all." It isn't something the hearer has to work out from the context, so it's not conversational; and you can't cancel it, since "Priya has read some of the files, in fact all of them" comes out contradictory. Detachability confirms it: swap in a synonym for "some" and the not-all reading stays put.

(iii) Conventional implicature. "But" does two jobs: it commits the speaker to both clauses being true (same as "and"), and it signals a contrast between them. The contrast is a conventional implicature and not part of what's said. It's detachable — replace "but" with "and" and the contrast disappears — which is exactly what you'd expect of a conventional implicature rather than a conversational one.

(iv) Particularized conversational implicature. Dana's reply would be pointless as an answer to the chair's question unless she meant to convey that Priya won't be ready. The hearer calculates this from the Maxim of Relation plus the context. It's cancelable ("...but I'm sure she'll pull it together by Friday") and it's particularized because it depends on this specific question having been asked.

(b) For Grice, what is said is the content fixed by the conventional meaning of the sentence plus the resolution of any ambiguity or reference, and it's the thing that's straightforwardly true or false. What is implicated is everything else the speaker means to convey without saying it. Of the items above, only (i) belongs to what is said; (ii)–(iv) are implicated rather than said. The point of the distinction is that you can be committed to an implicature without its being part of the truth-conditional content of your utterance.

(c) The objection is confused. Gricean implicatures aren't free-floating guesses: they're calculable, which means the hearer can derive them from the Cooperative Principle and the maxims together with the context, and a rational speaker can predict that the hearer will derive them. So the space of things that could be implicated is tightly constrained. The decisive feature is cancelability: because the speaker can always add a rider that cancels the implicature, an implicature can't be part of what the speaker actually meant — it's just an inference the hearer draws, which the speaker can disown. That's why Grice can explain communication while still allowing that what's implicated goes beyond what's said.
