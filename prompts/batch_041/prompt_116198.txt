You are a philosopher working at the intersection of the metaphysics of mind and applied ethics, with a track record of drafting advisory opinions for bodies that must decide under deep uncertainty. Take your time with this one; the quality of the reasoning matters more than the polish.

I chair the ethics advisory panel of a national physics facility. The panel reviews proposed experimental campaigns and the standing conditions attached to them, and publishes its opinions. The directorate has never overridden us, but it has no patience for open-ended inquiry, and our members are mostly experimentalists. I need a document they can actually vote on.

Two proposals are before us. The first is a decade-long quantum-optics campaign that will repeatedly prepare, entangle, and decohere very large lattices of cold atoms — on the order of ten trillion decoherence events per run, and something like 10^21 across the campaign. The second is a computational proposal to run a long-duration simulation of a simple artificial chemistry in which transient, agent-like structures form and dissolve; the group is asking for 10^18 simulated steps on dedicated hardware. Neither group is trying to make anything conscious, and both have told us the suggestion is absurd.

Our staff philosopher has filed a memo arguing the opposite. If experience is a widespread and graded feature of the physical world rather than a special achievement of certain biological systems, he says, then both campaigns would involve producing and destroying astronomically many dim, momentary experiences, and a very small credence multiplied by a very large number of subjects may swamp every other ethical consideration the facility has ever faced. Two of my members call this a category error wearing the costume of caution. A third wants a moratorium on the whole campaign until the question is settled — which in practice means forever.

At the last meeting, half-seriously, I floated a four-level screening ladder and was asked to put it in writing so the panel can vote on it. Level 0: no review. Level 1: a one-page declaration from the principal investigator stating whether any current physical theory assigns nonzero credence to experience in the systems involved, and if so which theory. Level 2: independent review by someone outside the collaboration, requiring a stated credence and a stated stopping condition. Level 3: a moratorium pending a named research milestone.

Please write the opinion I will circulate before the March meeting. It should:

- give a clear disposition for each proposal — approve, approve with conditions, defer to a stated date, or reject — with your reasons, and for every condition name who owns it and when it must be satisfied;
- tell me whether the panel should adopt the ladder, amend it, or drop it, and if you amend it, give me the amended version;
- meet the strongest case for the two members who think the question is a category error, and for the member who wants the moratorium — including the objection that multiplying a small credence by a large number of subjects is a machine for generating whatever conclusion you want;
- say whether the facility owes a positive showing that a campaign is safe, or whether the objector owes a positive showing that it is harmful, and defend that allocation of the burden;
- address whether a momentary, unremembered, non-unified experience can wrong anyone at all, and whether your answer changes your recommendation;
- say whether this is a permission decision at all, or instead a bounded research programme the directorate should fund, and if the latter, what it should buy and by when;
- say whether this review belongs in the same family as the environmental and biosafety screening we already run, or is different in kind;
- state plainly what evidence or argument would change your recommendation.

Assume the directorate will not fund an open-ended philosophical inquiry, and that any review step you invent must cost less than one person-week. Write for a mixed-expertise audience: define any term you introduce. Do not give me a literature review or a reading list; the panel needs an argument it can act on, not a survey of who has said what. Keep it under about 1,400 words.

Before you write, spend a few minutes mapping the space of positions a reasonable panel member could hold here and the strongest argument for each; then set that map aside and write only the opinion itself.
