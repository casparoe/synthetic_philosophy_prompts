I watched two colleagues, Nadia and Owen, argue about cognitive penetration and I could not referee it. They agreed on all the empirical starting material: that what a person expects, wants, or already believes can alter how things look and sound to them rather than only how they are inclined to describe or judge them; that these effects are small, automatic, and not under deliberate control; and that they tend to concern the very feature at stake, as when the pitch looks like a ball to the batter who wants it to be one. Neither cited studies and neither needed to. Everything they disagreed about was the epistemic upshot.

(1) Owen: This is not a defect at all; it is a design feature. Perception is inference from ambiguous input, and a system that refused to use prior knowledge would simply be worse at its job. Using priors makes perception more accurate on the whole, so top-down influence is on balance good, and the misleading cases are the price of a mechanism that usually helps.

(2) Nadia: That defense works only if the priors being used are good ones. The cases anyone actually worries about are cases where the prior is a wish or a stereotype. The mechanism does not sort priors by quality; it uses whatever is at hand. So the fact that using priors can be good in general says nothing about whether using these priors is good here.

(3) Owen: Granted, but then the penetration is an epiphenomenon. A distorted percept traces back to a distorted prior, and a distorted prior is a problem whether or not it reaches perception. Fix the prior and the perceptual effect disappears. The effect itself adds nothing.

(4) Nadia: It adds the loss of an error signal. If my bias distorted only my judgment, my experience could still conflict with my judgment, and that conflict is a chance to catch myself. If the bias reaches the experience, the world itself appears to agree with me and there is nothing to catch. The bias becomes self-sustaining in a way it is not when the evidence can still push back.

(5) Owen: There was never a pristine check of that kind. Experience is already the product of inference from noisy input, shaped by attention, adaptation, and memory, and testimony and disagreement supply error signals from outside your head, which your perceptual priors do not touch. Notice the demand underneath your position: that perception be independent of belief. That demand is unsatisfiable, and if it were met, perception would be worse, because independence from your priors means ignoring information you legitimately have. The right ideal is not independence but tracking: a perceptual system that gets the world right as often as it can. If priors help it do that, they are epistemically good.

(6) Nadia: Tracking measured how, and over which cases? Testimony is not untouched either: if what I keep seeing conflicts with what I am told, the percept is more vivid, so I will treat the report as the error. And the cases in dispute are ones where the prior is not a learned regularity but a wish or a prejudice I would disown if I thought about it. A system can be well calibrated on average and still fail exactly where I need it, on the ambiguous cases, which are the cases where perception is doing real work.

(7) Owen: Then that is an argument for better priors, not for counting penetration as a harm in its own right.

At that point they had reached the same question from opposite ends — whether the verdict on penetration depends entirely on the quality of the prior, or whether something is bad about the mechanism itself — and neither could say.

Please adjudicate move by move: which claims are right, which are too quick, and where the two of them are talking past each other. I am especially interested in (a) whether penetration is an epistemic harm in its own right or only inherited from the priors, (b) whether the independence ideal Owen attacks is the one Nadia needs, and (c) whether they are even evaluating the same thing at the same level, since one of them seems to be assessing a perceptual system's overall reliability while the other is assessing what a particular person is entitled to believe on a particular occasion — and whether that difference changes who is right. End with a verdict on which of them is more nearly right overall, and say what would have to be true of a perceptual system for top-down influence to count as epistemically good, and whether that condition holds in the cases they were arguing about. Do not worry about citations; I will add them. Treat the phenomena as stipulated, as they did.
