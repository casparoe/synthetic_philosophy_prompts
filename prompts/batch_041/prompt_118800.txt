I am writing a novel, and I have come to a scene I cannot yet manage: a dinner party at which one of the guests, a retired solicitor of mild and punctilious manner, is asked to tell a joke. I need the joke itself — not the scene, not a story, just the thing he says — and I should be grateful if you would write it for me.

Here is what the joke must be. It has to turn on the structure of escalation of commitment: a contest that two parties enter freely, each step of which is defensible on its own, and from which neither can withdraw without paying the whole of what he has already spent and receiving nothing in return. The specific features I want it to exploit are these. First, that the cost of stopping is everything already committed, whereas the cost of one more step is only the small increment that step requires; so the trap closes without anyone having made a mistake. Second, that the party who drops out pays nearly as much as the winner and gets nothing, so that losing and quitting are indistinguishable in cost — and the winner, if he goes on long enough, ends by paying more than the thing was ever worth, so that winning rescues no one either.

Some constraints, if you would be so good.

- The stakes must be trivial, and both parties must know from the outset that they are trivial: the whole of what is at stake should be worth less than the first step each of them takes towards it.
- No auctions, and the word "auction" should not appear; no sums of money that signify. Let the contest be domestic or neighbourly — a boundary hedge, a parking space, a chair by the fire, the last slice of something, who is to leave a party first.
- Nobody in the joke may behave stupidly. Each party's move must be the reasonable move from where he now stands, and the comedy should come from the inescapability of the structure rather than from the participants' folly. A listener should come away thinking "yes, that is exactly how it goes", not "how absurd of them".
- It must be tellable aloud in well under a minute — a set-up and a punchline, no more than about a hundred and fifty words — and it should amuse a listener who has never heard of escalation of commitment while being distinctly funnier to one who has.
- I should like it in British English: the idiom, the rhythm of the sentences and the social detail should all be his, not an American's.

After the joke, give me no more than three sentences on which feature of the escalation the punchline actually turns on, so that I can judge whether it is doing the work I need. Nothing else — no commentary, no variants, no apology for the joke.
