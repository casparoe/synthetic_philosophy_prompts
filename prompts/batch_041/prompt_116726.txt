I have no academic background. I belong to a small group of hobbyists who build and test an open-source language model, and within the group one member — call him the reviewer — has ended up as the person whose judgment we follow about which safety measures are worth adopting. He has no formal training either, but he is remarkably consistent, and over the past year he has ruled on a long series of proposals. A new one comes up at our next meeting, and I would like to predict his ruling; but more than the ruling itself, I want to understand the rule he is actually running, because I suspect more than one rule would fit everything he has said so far.

Here is his record as best I can reconstruct it.

He approved confining the model to a sandbox with no network access and no way to affect anything outside it, on the ground that a model cannot damage what it cannot reach.

He approved restricting the model to answering only from a fixed, pre-approved set of documents, with no tools and no general-purpose abilities — same reasoning: there is no track for it to go off.

He approved a hard cap on how many actions the model may take per hour, even though it costs us a great deal of usefulness, because a model that can do only so much per hour can do only so much harm per hour.

He rejected a blocklist that would suppress any output containing one of a list of dangerous phrases, saying such a list works only until the model learns to avoid the phrases, and that in the meantime it makes us feel protected when we are not.

He rejected fine-tuning the weights to erase a specific dangerous capability and then deploying the resulting model, because we cannot tell whether the erasure took, and a model we believe to be clean will be trusted more than it has earned.

He rejected having a monitor read the model's step-by-step reasoning and flag any reasoning that looks like it is working against us, because a model that is actually scheming will simply produce reassuring reasoning, and the human watching will believe it.

He rejected asking the model to rate its own riskiness before each action, on the ground that the model's account of itself is precisely the thing we cannot trust.

The proposal for next time is this: we would install a tamper-proof recorder that logs everything the model does, in a form the model cannot disable, alter, or even detect, so that if it ever does something harmful we could see exactly what happened and unwind it. Those in favor say this finally gives us ground truth about the model's behavior; those against say it does nothing to stop the harm in the first place, and that we would be reading the log after the fact.

What will the reviewer say? Give me the rules that fit his record so far, tell me which one I should bet on and why, and tell me what single further case, if he ever ruled on it, would tell those rules apart.
