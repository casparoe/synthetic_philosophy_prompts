Ok so I need help settling an argument I had last night with my friend Devi that went on way longer than it should have, and honestly I'm still not sure I lost it on the merits or just ran out of gas. It started as a fight about why the world loses its mind over some disasters and shrugs at others. Her example was 2010: the earthquake in Haiti, which pulled in an avalanche of pledges and wall-to-wall coverage, versus the floods in Pakistan that same summer, which hit and displaced way more people and got a fraction of the attention and the money. Her read is that this is just rank bias -- proximity bias, race bias, whatever -- and that anyone with a functioning conscience should be able to override it. My read was Adam Smith, and I thought I had it nailed: sympathy for Smith isn't a searchlight that falls equally on everyone, it runs on imagination, and you only really feel for someone once you've done the work of imaginatively trading places with them, which is vastly easier when the sufferer is vivid and close and looks like you. I brought up his example of the humane European who gets word that China has been swallowed by an earthquake, feels a pang, and then goes back to his dinner, because the twinge in his own finger means more to him in the moment than the ruin of a whole empire. I said look, Smith called it, this isn't a moral failing, its definately the machinery of sympathy doing exactly what it does.

But here's where I got stuck, and this is what I actually need from you. Smith's whole corrective for our selfish and partial feelings is the impartial spectator -- the internalized fair judge we consult, the one who isn't us and who's supposed to level things out. So what I want is a real case analysis, running both directions, and please don't just tell me the obvious thing about sympathy fading with distance, I already know that part. First: map his machinery onto the Haiti/Pakistan case honestly. What's actually doing the causal work here -- the vividness of the imagined particular, the nearness, the resemblance to the sympathizer, the sheer availability of images -- and does the theory genuinely predict the gap, or does it only rationalize it after the fact? Second, the thing that actually broke my argument: Devi said the impartial spectator is itself something we build out of our own imagination, so if our imagination is the very thing that's skewed by nearness and vividness, then the spectator just inherits the skew and rubber-stamps it -- in effect we get an impartial spectator for people like us, and its verdicts are only as universal as our sympathies already were. Is she right? Does Smith have a real resource against this -- the internal spectator being stricter and cooler than any actual audience, the contrast between the gentler amiable virtues and the sterner respectable ones, the thought that the spectator's judgments are meant to hold regardless of who's watching -- or does his own China example quietly give the game away, i.e. is he describing the man of humanity's reaction or lamenting it? Third, turn it around on the theory: if the impartial spectator can't close this kind of gap, what does the case show about Smith's account that he didn't see -- for instance that sympathy is a scarce resource allocated by attention rather than by desert, and that the impartial spectator is a normative ideal bolted onto a descriptive psychology that never promised to support it? I'd much rather have an honest verdict about where Smith runs out of road than a defense that patches the hole with "well, you just ought to feel more."
