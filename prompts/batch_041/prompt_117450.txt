I'm a third-year psychology student currently taking a course on behaviour genetics, and I've spent the past few weeks trying to explain to my flatmates why a heritability coefficient of 0.7 does not mean that 70% of a given child's outcome is genetic. The lecture I keep coming back to concerned what a heritability estimate actually is: a ratio of variance components, computed for a specified population at a specified time, in a specified range of environments. It is not a fixed property of a trait, not a statement about any individual, and not a ranking of causes. The consequence I found genuinely startling, and which I would like to see put to work, is that a successful environmental intervention can raise the heritability of a trait rather than lower it, because removing environmental variance from the mixture leaves proportionally more of what remains attributable to differences in genes; our professor illustrated the point with the old thought experiment in which the same varieties of seed are planted in a fertile plot and a barren one, so that within each plot the variation looks entirely genetic while across the two plots it looks entirely environmental. Meanwhile I keep encountering the word heritability in education marketing, where it appears to be doing precisely the thing the lecture warned against, and I would like to write something satirical about that.

Please write a launch announcement for a fictional education-analytics company bringing a product to market: the Heritability Index, a per-school score licensed to parents choosing schools and to districts deciding where to spend money. Use the deadpan register of a corporate press release — a headline and dateline, two or three paragraphs of announcement copy, a methodology section that a behaviour geneticist would recognise as a real design (a twin or sibling comparison, say) which the company then reinterprets, a pull-quote from a named consulting scientist that is technically accurate but framed in the most flattering possible way, an FAQ of four or five entries (including whether a high score means the teachers don't matter, and whether the index measures a child's genetic potential), a parent testimonial, and a closing line about the company's mission. Around 650 words. The comedy should come from getting the variance decomposition right rather than from invented nonsense: every claim the company makes should be a recognisable misreading of what a heritability coefficient is, the kind of misreading a specialist would wince at and then concede is very common. I'd like the piece to imply, without ever stating, what such a metric would actually reward given what it measures — a careful reader should be able to derive it. It should be funny to someone who has taken the course and still followable by someone who hasn't.

After the piece, in a short note clearly separated from it of about 150 words, say where the parody takes liberties with the science or leans on a contested reading of heritability, and what would change your mind about whether the joke is fair — for instance, what would have to be true about how such indices are actually computed, marketed and explained to buyers for the satire to be attacking a straw man rather than a live practice.
