Question

Consider an election over three alternatives, a, b, and d, where d is a designated default. Each voter submits a strict ranking of the three alternatives. Rule R is: if some alternative is ranked first by an absolute majority of the voters, that alternative is elected; otherwise d is elected.

(a) State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem precisely enough to determine whether R falls within its scope, and determine whether R is strategy-proof. If R is manipulable, exhibit a profile and a profitable misreport; if it is not, argue that it is not. (12 points)

(b) The theorem is sometimes glossed as showing that any voting rule can be gamed, and restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences is the most familiar way of escaping it. Is that a genuine escape? (8 points)

Student's answer

(a) The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that any strategy-proof social choice function must be dictatorial. It is the strategic counterpart of Arrow's theorem: Arrow shows that no aggregation rule can satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives along with the other reasonable conditions, and Gibbard and Satterthwaite show that the same impossibility resurfaces one level up, at the point where voters decide what to report. In both cases the culprit is the demand that the rule treat each voter's ranking as a complete ordering and respond to it independently of the other alternatives on offer. So the theorem's hypotheses are unrestricted rankings, at least three alternatives, and independence.

R clearly falls within the theorem's scope: there are three alternatives, every strict ranking is admissible, and the rule's range is all of a, b, and d, since any of them can be ranked first by a majority. R is also plainly not dictatorial, since no single voter's first choice is always elected. The interesting question is whether it is strategy-proof, and I think it is. Only first-place rankings enter the majority test, so a voter's dominant strategy is to rank their true favourite first. Ranking it lower can only help some other alternative reach the majority threshold, and since the voter ranks that alternative below their favourite, this cannot be an improvement; and if the voter's favourite cannot reach a majority, nothing they report will put it there. So R is strategy-proof, and since it is also onto and non-dictatorial, the theorem's hypotheses must be doing more work than they appear to.

(b) Yes, this is a genuine escape. With single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional ordering of the alternatives, the median voter rule is strategy-proof and not dictatorial, and the reason it does not contradict the theorem is that the theorem assumes every ranking is admissible, whereas the median rule is only required to work on the single-peaked domain. So the impossibility is an artefact of the unrestricted domain. On a left–right electorate, then, the theorem loses most of its force, and it is best read as a warning about exotic preference profiles rather than a limit on real elections.
