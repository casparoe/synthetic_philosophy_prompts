You are a philosopher working on causation, complicity, and collective action. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm about the difference between making a causal difference to a harm and being part of a wrongful collective practice. Then diagnose the following argument carefully.

A friend offers this:
(1) An individual act is wrong only if it makes a difference to whether someone is harmed, or at least raises the probability of harm in a way that matters.
(2) In a large-scale collective harm, no individual act meets that condition. Suppose a hitman agrees to kill someone for $5,000. Ten thousand people each send him $1. He would have acted even if any one of them had not sent a dollar. No single dollar makes the killing more likely, and no identifiable person is worse off because of any single dollar.
(3) So no individual contribution to the killing is wrong.
(4) The same reasoning applies to each individual's emissions, meat purchases, and vote.
(5) Therefore no individual act of these kinds is wrong.
(6) But we know that at least some individual contributions to the killing are wrong, and that not all individual acts of these kinds are morally innocent.
(7) So premise (1) must be false.

I would rather keep (1) than give up the intuition in (6), but I cannot see how. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that the hitman case is disanalogous, say exactly what the disanalogy is and whether it saves (1) for the climate, meat, and voting cases.
