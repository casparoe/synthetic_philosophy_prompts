Write a dialogue between two senior statisticians — one a frequentist of the strict long-run school, the other a subjectivist who identifies probability with rational degree of belief — convened, alongside a chair who says almost nothing, to advise a government on a decision that will be taken exactly once and can never be repeated. The case is as follows. A newly engineered bacterium, which we may call strain K, has been proposed for release at a single coastal site to digest an oil spill. It has never been released anywhere; there is one site, one release, and no second trial. The minister will approve or refuse the release on the strength of a single number — the probability that the release leads to an uncontrolled bloom — and both statisticians are to supply that number. They accept the same microbiology and the same engineering, and the only evidence either can cite is a handful of sealed microcosm experiments that both agree bear on the question but neither regards as decisive. Their disagreement is not about the facts but about what such a number could mean and where it could legitimately come from.

The exchange should be a genuine back-and-forth rather than two adjacent monologues. Let the frequentist attempt a constructive answer instead of merely refusing to speak — by constructing a reference class, or by redescribing the question as a hypothetical long run, or by some other route — and let the subjectivist press on where that class comes from and why it, rather than some competing class, is the relevant one. Let the subjectivist, in turn, be made to say where the prior comes from and to answer the frequentist's charge that a degree of belief is a fact about the believer rather than about the world, so that two equally rational people may assign different numbers to the same event without either being mistaken. Bring in, wherever they arise naturally, the reference-class problem, the difference between calibration and coherence, and the question of whether propensity is a genuine third option or merely a relabeling of one of theirs.

Neither figure should be a strawman: each should be given the strongest version of their position, each should concede a real difficulty when it is pressed, and each should land at least one point the other cannot simply wave away. End with the two of them giving the minister their number — the same number or different ones — and with a clear-eyed account of what exactly they still disagree about, and whether that disagreement bears on the decision itself or only on how the number ought to be described.
