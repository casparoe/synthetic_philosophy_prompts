I'm an economist by training, working mostly on decision theory, so the vocabulary I actually think in is this: a set of alternatives, a preference relation or choice function defined on it, a state space with a prior and likelihoods, an updating rule, and — once more than one agent is in the picture — aggregation rules that turn individual orderings into a collective one. I've been reading metaethics on the side, and I keep colliding with a family of views that I can only half-translate. The shape of the family is this: you do not first fix a substantive standard of what is good or right and then apply it; instead you specify a procedure — what some improved version of you would choose, where "improved" means better informed, more coherent, undistracted, reasoning without error, perhaps deliberating with others under conditions that strip away your particular position — and the output of that procedure is what you ought to do. Sometimes the improved version is a single ideal counterpart of you, sometimes a whole idealized community, sometimes a hypothetical agreement among people who don't know where they will end up. I understand why the indirection is attractive, since you never have to write down the answer, only the machine that produces it. But I have a nagging suspicion that the machine, once I translate it into my own terms, does less than advertised.

Restate the family in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and keep the main variants separate rather than collapsing them: the ideal-counterpart version, the idealized-agreement version, the view that a principle is justified when it survives unforced deliberation among people reasoning well, and whatever other joints you think the family has. I want the translation in my notation, not a popular exposition — I am comfortable with choice functions, aggregation, fixed points, limits of iterative processes, and the difference between what a counterfactual agent prefers and what a theory licenses, so do not soften it on my account. Where a variant translates cleanly, say so and give me the object it becomes; where it translates only under a distortion, tell me exactly what the distortion is — for instance, what happens when the idealization changes the alternative set rather than merely re-ranking it, or when the ideal deliberator's ordering turns out not to be representable by anything I would call a utility function.

The part I actually care about is the residue report. My suspicion, which I would like you to confirm or kill, is that every version of the view I can state in my vocabulary comes out either descriptive or covertly normative. Descriptive, because a claim about what my idealized counterpart would choose is a claim about a counterfactual agent — my machinery predicts it and says nothing about whether I ought to conform to it, and the gap opens exactly where I expect the is/ought gap to open, between a model and a norm. Covertly normative, because the idealization only yields an ought if I have already fixed the idealizing conditions in a way that encodes a substantive theory of what counts as a reason, in which case the indirection was decorative. So: what in this debate has no counterpart at all in my framework? My guess is something like the authority of the ideal — the first-person "and therefore I should," which is not the same as a prediction about a better-informed me — and I want to know whether that residue is a defect of my vocabulary, which can represent belief-improvement but not value-improvement, or a sign that the philosophers are smuggling in the very content the indirection was supposed to avoid. One subsidiary matter I would like settled along the way: is the coherence demanded of the idealized deliberator the same thing as the coherence my axioms demand of a preference relation — transitivity, completeness, no cyclic choice — or is that a pun that conceals a substantive requirement?
