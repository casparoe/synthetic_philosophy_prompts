I've been using LLMs a lot for work over the last few months — mostly drafting and summarizing — and it's turned me into the annoying person at dinner who keeps arguing about whether the thing "really believes" anything. My buddy from college (who did a philosophy minor and never lets anyone forget it) sent me a blog post by some tech writer he follows, told me it was the last word on the subject, and honestly I read it and it sounded super convincing. Here's the gist of what it said:

"Dennett's intentional stance is basically instrumentalism. When you predict a system by treating it as having beliefs and desires, you're not claiming it actually has them — you're adopting a useful fiction that makes its behavior easier to forecast. So when someone says ChatGPT believes it's Tuesday, that isn't a factual claim that could be true or false; it's just a convenient way of talking. And since the whole point of the stance is predictive usefulness, Dennett's view actually lines up with the eliminativists: there's no fact of the matter about beliefs, they're just tools we deploy. Applied to AI, this kind of settles things — asking whether a model really believes anything is a category mistake, because the stance tells us belief-talk was never about what's really going on inside the system to begin with. It's the least committal of Dennett's three stances, which is why we reach for it whenever the physical description gets too complicated."

Something nags at me though — I have a vague memory from a class years ago that Dennett was defending a kind of realism about beliefs, not just saying they're handy fictions, and that he spent a lot of energy distancing himself from the eliminativists rather than agreeing with them. But I couldn't tell you where the line is, and the guy who wrote the post clearly knows the material better than I do. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of Dennett would nod along with, what they'd wince at, and what's just flat-out wrong? I especially want to know whether the conclusion about AI — that it's a category mistake to ask if a model really believes something — actually follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether it's riding on the shaky parts. And if the summary is unfair, what would the defensible version of the AI point even look like?
