I have taught Newcomb's problem for the better part of a decade, and I have settled into a reading of it that I now suspect may be built on a mistake. The reading is this. Causal and evidential decision theory agree wherever the state of the world on which the payoffs depend is probabilistically independent of the agent's act; they come apart only when the act carries information about the state. Outside of Newcomb-style cases, the only way an act can carry information about a state it does not cause is for the state to be a forecast of the act, since nothing else could tie the two together. So the divergence between the two theories is confined to cases in which some predictor forecasts the agent's choice with something close to perfect accuracy; and since a predictor of that sort would have to be a Laplacean demon or a time traveller, no deliberator will ever be in such a case. It follows, I have been telling my students, that the choice between the theories is empirically idle: a debate about an impossible being, useful only for making a conceptual distinction visible. I have accordingly stopped presenting one-boxing as a live recommendation and treat it as the eccentric answer to a puzzle.

A colleague in my department insists this is wrong — she says the two theories come apart in perfectly ordinary cases in which nothing predicts anything, and that the perfect predictor is a distraction. I have tried to reconstruct the objection and cannot: if no predictor exists, the state is independent of my act, both theories condition on the same probabilities, and they must prescribe the same act. I have also been tinkering with a case of my own that I think shows the shape of the difficulty. Suppose a research group publishes a scoring model, validated on a large sample, that forecasts from a short interview which box a person will take, with about eighty-five per cent accuracy; the scoring key is published, and the subject in question has read it before playing. My instinct is that the correlation must collapse here, precisely because he can read the key and choose against the prediction — which would mean that even this case is not a genuine Newcomb case. My colleague says the opposite: that the correlation survives his having read the key, and that this is a Newcomb case with no impossible predictor anywhere in it. So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does, and what the actual structure of the disagreement between the two theories is. In particular: is there a case with no predictor at all in which the two theories prescribe different acts, and if so, what feature of the case does the work? Does the Newcomb case require perfect prediction, or does some weaker assumption suffice — and if weaker, what does the answer turn on? And does anything follow about the practical import of the dispute for someone who will never meet a predictor? Please be blunt; I would rather be corrected than reassured.
