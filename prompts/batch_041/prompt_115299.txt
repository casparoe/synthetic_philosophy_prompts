I'm a barrister in London — commercial and public law — and I did a philosophy minor at university, which means I know enough of the names to get myself into arguments I can't finish. Here's the one that keeps nagging at me.

People are always invoking Gödel's incompleteness theorems for things that have nothing to do with arithmetic. Someone will announce that the theorems prove no system of rules can be both complete and consistent, or that a human mind can't be a machine, on the grounds that a mathematician can see the truth of the sentence the system itself can't prove. I dont think anyone's ever given me a straight answer about how much of that is real and how much is a slogan.

So: translate the theorems, and the main claims people make about what they show outside maths, into the vocabulary I actually work in. That's a body of statutes and case law; precedent, ratio and obiter; a hierarchy of courts and appeals; distinguishing and overruling; implied repeal, where two provisions genuinely conflict and we argue about which one gives way; a written constitution with an entrenched amendment clause and a court that can strike down legislation; and the familiar feeling that some questions are for Parliament and not for us.

I've been at the bar twelve years, so be as technical as you like on the legal side — I know that machinery cold. The logic is where I'm shaky, so dont skip steps there.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue. Which claims carry over cleanly, which only fit if you twist them, and which can't be said in my vocabulary at all? Don't be generous with the analogy — if something is only a loose metaphor, say so, rather than dressing it up as a translation. In particular, is a hard case, or a gap in the statute book, or a real conflict between two provisions, or an amendment clause that changes the rules for amending the constitution, the same kind of thing the theorems are about, or does it just look similar from a distance? And is whatever is left over a hole in my framework, or a sign that the people making these claims outside maths were never saying anything very clear in the first place?

No reading lists or case names, please. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. British spelling throughout.
