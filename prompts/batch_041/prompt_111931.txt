Ok, I run a small reading group and we've spent about three months going round in circles on the demandingness objection to consequentialism — two of my members are basically not on speaking terms. What I want from you is the document an adverserial collaboration between them would produce: a joint statement that a committed act-consequentialist and a committed demandingness objector could both genuinely put their names to. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right, not another essay about why the debate is stuck. An agreement text, with whatever disagreement survives stated exactly.

Before anything else, have both sides jointly fix the target. Which consequentialism are we talking about, and with which theory of the good and which rule for distributing it? A lot of our arguements have been about a moving target and I suspect some of them are verbal for that reason. Say so explicitly if they are, and say which bits aren't.

Then the agreed claims, numbered. What do both sides accept about what the theory requires in a world like ours, about what compliance costs the agent, about why the objection has real force, and about which facts about the world are common ground (that there are people in desperate need who could be helped cheaply, and so on).

Then the disagreements. Each one stated twice — once in wording the objector would accept as her own position, once in wording the consequentialist would accept as his — and note whether each side accepts the other's phrasing as at least a fair statement of the view, even while rejecting it.

Then the cruxes, and for each one: what both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction, written down in advance. I've got three suspicions I'd like tested as cruxes, though add others if they're real:

1. Height versus shape. Is the complaint that the theory asks *too much*, or that it leaves *nothing exempt* — that no project, relationship or commitment is ever off the table, so the agent's life has no protected interior? Test it with a pair of cases: a theory that demands a lot in aggregate but reserves a genuine sphere the agent may never be required to sacrifice, versus a theory with the same aggregate cost of compliance and no reserved sphere at all. Do the sides agree this pair bears on the crux, and what does each predict the objection does when you hold height fixed and vary only the shape?

2. Size versus allocation. Is the objection really about how much is demanded, or about the fact that the demand falls unevenly and the theory has no resources to object to that unevenness as such? Would the objection survive a theory with the same total demand but a principled allocation of burdens between agents?

3. Truth versus believability. Is this an objection to the theory being true, or only to its being believed, taught and adopted as a public morality? Would either side accept a theory it thought true but unfit to believe — and does anything follow for the other side if it would?

Also cover: whether the objector can run the objection without assuming the very permissions and prerogatives that are in dispute (and what would show that she's begging the question); whether 'ought implies can' is doing conceptual work here or just psychological work, i.e. whether the complaint is about human motivational limits as an empirical matter or about what it takes to be a morality at all; whether the agent-centred prerogative can be justified from inside the theory or has to be imported (and whether the theory is incomplete if it must be imported); and whether the objection survives being redirected from individuals to institutions and collectives, or just reappears there.

Finish with a short section on what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there's anything both agree would actually move the debate.

No citations, no reading list, no 'as X argues' — I can't look anything up and I don't care who said it. Write it in British English please (organise, behaviour, that sort of thing).
