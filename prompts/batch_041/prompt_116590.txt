I have spent the term in a formal epistemology seminar arguing for a position that I can now state cleanly but cannot defend as cleanly, and I would like to know exactly where it is exposed. The position is this: in a sufficiently liquid market, the price of a claim is a better guide to the truth about that claim than my own considered judgment, so that when I find myself disagreeing with a well-functioning prediction market, or with the price of a deep and actively traded contract, the rational response is to defer — to treat the price as a compressed summary of dispersed private information that I could not hope to assemble on my own, and to update toward it unless I can point to some specific reason why my information is not already in it. Behind this is a picture of the price as a statistic produced by a mechanism, one that encodes what a great many people are willing to stake on a question, and I take the view to carry several commitments: that my disagreement with the market is itself evidence against me; that the epistemic value of a price is not merely a matter of its being arbitrage-free; and that a market's verdict on a factual question has genuine evidential weight rather than merely registering the consensus of the credulous.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Which results, arguments, or empirical patterns would count against it, and how decisive would each be? Please rank them, drawing a firm line between the ones that would genuinely damage the view and the ones that are merely awkward for it, and say which of them the view's own defenders would concede as real costs rather than wave away. Several candidate defeaters occur to me, and I want you to tell me which of them actually bite and which are red herrings. First, the no-trade results in the economics of information, which appear to show that differences in private information alone cannot generate trade at all, because any offer reveals the offeror's information before it can be accepted. Second, the paradox that if prices already reflected everything knowable, no one would have an incentive to gather information, so prices can at best reflect it partially. Third, the fact that those who trade are a selected subset of the population, so that the price may encode the beliefs of the wealthy, the risk-tolerant, or the systematically mistaken rather than the informed. Fourth, the worry that a price tracks what people believe other people will believe rather than what is true. And fifth, the thought that prices do not merely record facts but change them, so that a market's verdict is partly about the world it has helped to make. I suspect that at least one of these is a red herring — that a theorem about the impossibility of trade is not yet a theorem about the informativeness of prices — but I do not trust my own reading of it, and I would rather be told plainly than reassured.

Two further things. I am troubled by a circularity that I cannot dissolve: if the market's authority is what underwrites my deference, then the market cannot be the thing that certifies its own authority, and yet I have no non-market standard by which to check it; I would like to know whether this is a genuine defect of the view or a confusion on my part. And I would like a candid assessment of whether the position, as its defenders usually state it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether the claim that prices reflect information can be tested at all without already assuming a model of what the price ought to have been, and if it cannot, whether that is fatal for a thesis of this kind or merely the ordinary condition of any claim about a complex social mechanism. The single consideration that would move me most is a concrete, non-hypothetical case in which a liquid market's price was confidently wrong about a matter of fact, and in which the disagreement was, in hindsight, resolvable in favour of the dissenter.
