I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar in decision theory — 14 students, mostly philosophy graduate students — and I want to run an activity that makes them feel the pull of the coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization rather than just hear them. The activity should keep two arguments that are usually blurred together distinct, because I want students to feel that they are different arguments with different conclusions: the representational argument (incoherent preferences cannot be assigned utilities and probabilities that reproduce them — no utility function unique up to a positive affine transformation will fit) and the pragmatic argument (incoherent preferences can be drained by a sequence of trades). Please design it so the first half makes the representational failure tangible: students rank a fixed menu of lotteries, then have to fill in numbers that reproduce their own rankings, and discover that they cannot without fudging. The second half should stage a trade sequence in which a broker, working from a schedule printed in advance and taking swaps one at a time, drains tokens from anyone whose ranking violates the relevant structure, with no axiom named until the debrief. A student can play the broker.

Give me the materials (the lotteries, the token payoff scheme with a small real prize at the end, the broker's schedule), a timeline, and the debrief. In the debrief I want to draw out what is usually glossed: why the representational and the pragmatic arguments support different conclusions (one is about how a preference can be interpreted, the other about how it can be exploited), and why the trade sequence only works if the agent takes the trades one at a time rather than judging the package, and cannot see the whole sequence in advance — so what the students have felt is that incoherence is exploitable, not yet that it is irrational. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the coherence requirement the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself; my guess is the completeness assumption over a fully enumerated state space, which no classroom menu can really stage.
