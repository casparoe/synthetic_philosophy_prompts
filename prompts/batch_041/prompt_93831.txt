I'm in a playwriting workshop that meets on Thursday evenings, and last week's session ended in an argument I have been chewing over since. One of us, R., has finished a full-length play about a real woman — a district administrator in the 1890s, of whom almost nothing survives except a bundle of letters, a few official notices, and a two-line obituary. In the second act R. has her authorize a clearance order that ruined a neighbouring village. The surviving letters never mention anything of the kind, and in two of them she seems to be arguing against precisely that sort of order. R. has also given her a private motive for signing it: she was protecting her brother. The play is billed as fiction, and nothing in it pretends to be documented.

Afterwards two other people in the workshop went at it, and I stayed out of it, because I honestly could not decide who had the better case. (1) M., who is writing a biography of a different nineteenth-century figure, said R. had wronged the woman: he had made her do the thing she spent years opposing, and you do not get to write lies into a real person's life merely because the person is dead. (2) R. said there is no one left to wrong — she is dead, there is no subject left to be harmed — and that since the play is announced as fiction nobody is deceived, so the falsehood harms no one either. (3) M. said that missed the point twice over. First, the wrong is not the deception but the circulation: the invented version is the one that will travel, the vivid scene crowds out the thin record, and in fifty years the play is what people will think they know about her. Second, and more deeply, a person's interest in how she is remembered is an interest she held while she was alive, and it can be set back after her death — the way a promise can be broken to someone who has since died. (4) R. said the first reply defeats itself: if the harm is a false picture in circulation, then the harm is to the living who are misled, and it grows with the size of the audience, so that a flop wrongs nobody and a hit wrongs thousands — and a wrong you could have avoided by writing a worse play is no wrong at all. The second reply, he said, proves far too much: any invented detail, an accent, an argument with a clerk, sets back some imagined interest in accuracy, so on M.'s reasoning nobody may write historical drama at all. He asked where the line falls between what he may invent and what he may not. (5) M. said the line is the difference between filling a gap and overwriting a fact: you may invent what the record leaves open, you may not invent what the record contradicts, so the accent is permissible and the ruinous order is not. He added that R. was treating this as a question about harm when it is a question about what one may do with a person — the two come apart, since a promise broken to a man who dies the next day harms nobody and is still a wrong. (6) R. said that rule cannot survive contact with his own play, because the most damning thing he invented is the motive, and motives are never in the record; they are always inferred. So either the rule forbids all historical drama or it permits everything he has done. And the distinction between wronging and harming merely restates the problem: if writing about a person can be a wrong in itself, then his play is condemned before any question of truth arises — which cannot be M.'s view, since M. writes biographies.

I would like you to take these six moves one at a time and say which of them stand and which do not, and why, rather than simply declaring a winner. Then tell me who has the better of the exchange overall. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — in particular, whether there is a distinction neither of them has drawn that would settle the question, and whether they share an assumption they should not.
