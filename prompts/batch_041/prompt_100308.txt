I run a fortnightly reading group out of my living room — twelve or so regulars, a mix of people who arrived via the Sequences and a couple of graduate students, plus one retired linguist who keeps us honest — and we're closing out a term on metaethics. I want the last session, seventy-five minutes, to be on the Frege–Geach problem, and I want it to land as a discovery rather than a doctrine; the last time we covered it, the conversation died into everyone agreeing with a summary and then disagreeing about what the word 'expresses' was doing. So please design an activity for it, not a lecture with a game bolted on. My own sketch, which you should feel free to improve or replace: the participants are cast as a small community that has adopted an expressivist semantics for some vocabulary, so that a sentence's meaning just is the attitude it expresses; they keep a public ledger in which every sentence that gets uttered has to be assigned exactly one meaning; and they score points by winning arguments that the group itself accepts as valid, with the rules of composition and the rules of validity fixed in advance. The hope is that the two demands — one meaning per sentence, and validity by the group's own lights — start to grind against each other at some point in the play, and that the grinding is something they can point at before anyone has said the word 'embedding' or the name of the problem. I'm aware that 'the meaning just is the attitude' is a stronger premise than any actual expressivist needs; if that turns out to make the game unfair, tell me, and tell me how you'd weaken it without losing the pressure.

Two things I'd like built into the staging. First, I don't want the pieces of the problem collapsed into one another: the case where the same sentence turns up in an asserted and an unasserted position feels different in the hand from the negation case, and both feel different from the conditional whose antecedent nobody is endorsing, and all three feel different again from the moment the group has to say why modus ponens still goes through, or what 'valid' could mean if it isn't truth-preservation. If the activity can be arranged so that these arrive as separate, locally resolvable difficulties and only later turn out to be the same difficulty wearing different clothes, that's the version I want. Second, I'd rather the participants acquire the vocabulary's semantics on a case they have no stake in — I'm thinking of running the first half on a vocabulary that is obviously just a matter of taste or attitude, where nobody minds conceding that the meaning is the attitude — and then, without announcement, swapping in the moral vocabulary for the second half and watching whether the same machinery still runs. Assume nobody in the room has read anything: I don't need a reading list, and I'd rather not have names in the design at all. What I do want is a debrief that walks back through the moments where the machinery jammed and says, for each, which piece of the problem it was, and that then lays out the escape routes the group might reach for — the one where the expressivist earns the right to the realist's vocabulary after all, the one where the attitude turns out not to be the meaning, the one where the meaning is the sentence's inferential role — with an honest accounting of what each costs and buys, including the worry that one of them buys so much that the view stops being distinguishable from the realism it was supposed to replace.

Finally, the practical bits. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I'd bet on at least one participant quietly refusing to play the expressivist and arguing from outside the game, and on the ledger becoming a source of bookkeeping confusion rather than pressure — and how to handle each failure without rescuing the group by simply explaining the answer. And be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: I assume no game can make anyone feel the pull of the realist alternative, or show that expressivism is false rather than merely under-described, but say which of those it is, and what the facilitator has to supply in the last ten minutes.
