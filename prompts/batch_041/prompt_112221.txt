I'm writing a paper for a Machiavelli seminar and I want a consistency check on my reading before I commit to it. The thesis is that Machiavelli has a coherent account of political agency that survives the standard reduction of virtù to "whatever happened to work." Here is where I've landed, stated as flatly as I can:

(1) Virtù names a genuine causal power — the capacity to impose form on the matter that fortune supplies — and not merely a retrospective label for whoever succeeded.
(2) Fortune is the arbiter of roughly half our actions and virtù of the other half, or a little less. I take that estimate as a substantive claim about political life rather than a rhetorical flourish, and I take seriously his image of fortune as a flood: virtù builds the dikes, which bound what fortune takes without abolishing floods.
(3) There is no generally best way to act. The same conduct that preserves a prince in one situation ruins him in another, so the exercise of virtù consists in acting as the times require.
(4) Machiavelli also holds that men do not change their natures — the cautious man cannot turn impetuous when the times turn — so whether a man's temperament fits his times is a matter of luck.
(5) When a man of virtù loses his state, the loss is charged to fortune's share rather than to a defect in him; a lost state does not refute his virtù.
(6) Virtù is nonetheless acquirable to some degree: imitating great men and studying history are supposed to leave a man more capable than he would otherwise have been.

I don't know which of these are pulling against each other, and I would rather find out now than in the seminar. Please audit the set.
