I run a reading group on philosophy of mind and AI — 12 people, mostly grad students plus two faculty who wont stop talking, 75 minutes. I want a session where they feel the force of the intentional stance rather than just nodding at it. So: design me a runnable game. Dont give me options, pick one design and commit, with real numbers, timings, materials, and a debrief, and tell me why you made the calls you did.

Here's the rough shape I'm after, but improve it where it's weak. Split them into three bureaus — call them the Physical Bureau, the Design Bureau, and the Intentional Bureau — and give them a black box to predict. The black box can be a laptop running a script I control, or a stack of sealed input-output transcripts if that's easier to run. Each round they see a batch of the box's outputs and have to submit (a) a prediction for the next batch and (b) a one-line theory of what the box is doing. Points for correct predictions; but also a bonus for the shortest theory that still predicts, because I want them to notice that ascribing beliefs and desires is a cheap way to compress behaviour. That compression angle is the bit I care about — I want them to feel the temptation of the intentional stance as an efficient coding scheme, not just as a folksy habit.

Then two twists. First, a counterfactual round: I change a hidden parameter or feed a novel input and ask each bureau what the box would do. The intentional theories tend to go vague here while the design theories stay sharp, and I want them to feel that as a genuine loss — the stance that predicted best is the one that can't say what would happen if things were different. Second, a contrarian round: the box is set up to predict what the intentional bureau will guess and then do the opposite. The intentional stance collapses; they have to fall back on mechanism. I want them to discover that the stance only works under an assumption of something like rationality or optimality, and that adversarial systems break it.

Debrief should map each round onto the three stances, then push on the question the game makes urgent: does predictive success under the intentional stance license any realism about the beliefs it ascribes, or is it just instrumental? And what's the difference between the shallow belief-talk the game rewards and the deeper notion of belief that actually figures in explanation and prediction of agents over time? Bring it home to LLMs — we ascribe beliefs to them all the time, so what does the game's lesson transfer to, and what doesnt it?

Also give me the predictable failure modes — teams reverse-engineering the script, the compression bonus rewarding trivial theories, someone anthropomorphizing early and derailing the counterfactual round — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity cant reproduce: I suspect it cant reproduce the involuntary pull of the stance (the fact that you cant help seeing the box as a believer), it cant settle the metaphysics, and it strips out the normative/social web in which we hold believers accountable. Say what I have to supply as facilitator for those.

Give me the committed design, not a menu.
