Novelist here, not a philosopher, so go easy — but I need this straight.

I'm writing a book with a character who's been in and out of sex work since she was nineteen, and I've been reading everything I can get: policy papers, advocacy sites, a documentary, a couple of memoirs. There's one sentence that keeps turning up almost word for word — on a poster at a fundraiser, in an op-ed, and out of the mouth of an academic on the doco: 'prostitution is violence against women.' Everyone around me nods at it like it's a settled fact, and then uses it to argue for completley different things. One friend says it's why you charge the buyers and leave the women alone (the Nordic thing, which is roughly what Sweden did and a few others followed). A woman I know who does outreach out of a van says the same sentence is exactly why nobody should be arrested at all, and that the real story is poverty and housing. And I've read people arguing that if it's violence then Germany and the Netherlands legalising and regulating it is basically the state handing out licences for it, while New Zealand decriminalising is a third thing again that doesn't follow from either.

I'm fairly sure that's not one claim, it's about six, and I keep nodding along to all of them and then noticing they can't all be true at once.

So could you pull the sentence apart? What are the actual distinct claims someone could be making when they say it — the claim about the act itself, the claim about the institution, the claim about what causes someone to end up there, the claim about 'violence' being used as a term of art in feminist theory rather than the way a cop means it, whatever else is in there — and for each one, say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false. Then, and this is the bit I really need: which of those readings actually gets each person the conclusion they want? Which ones really do support charging the buyers, which support full decriminalisation, which prove too much (or nothing at all), and which only work if you smuggle in an empirical premise that I've got no way of checking. I'm especially interested in the move where the sentence is doing the work of saying her consent doesn't count — is that a separate claim, and does it follow from any of the others, or is it the other way round?

Lay it out as a list I can stick above my desk. Cheers.
