I am putting together a teaching exercise for a history of philosophy module on happiness, and I want to ask students to extrapolate Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness into three debates she could not have known. Take her view to be roughly this: happiness is not a passive state but something one must work at; the passions are not to be extinguished but regulated and directed toward worthy objects; love and the pursuit of truth are among the great goods; and a certain self-esteem, an opinion of oneself as estimable, is essential to contentment. The three developments are: first, the experience machine thought experiment and the wider question whether a life of pleasant illusion could be as good as a life of reality; second, algorithmic social media and engagement-optimized feeds that seem to intensify comparison, envy, and status anxiety; third, political proposals to measure national well-being directly—well-being budgets, happiness indices, and taxes on conspicuous consumption—rather than relying on GDP. What would Du Châtelet likely say about each? Would she treat the experience machine as a threat to her view or as a distraction from it? Would she see social media as just another passion to be regulated, or as a structurally different kind of threat? Would she support well-being metrics as a political project, or regard them as a category mistake? Reconstruct her likely verdicts from her own commitments, flag the places where her eighteenth-century assumptions make the extrapolation genuinely uncertain, and say which of the three she would find most and least congenial. Do not give me citations or a reading list; I want the argument. Answer in no more than 600 words.
