I'm a third-year undergraduate writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind course whose second half is devoted to personal identity, and I've been trying to get Dennett's 'Where Am I?' out of the seminar room and into something concrete. What prompted this was a long conversation with my aunt, who works as a transplant coordinator at a large teaching hospital. She described the ordinary working procedure there as follows. A patient whose entire brain has irreversibly ceased to function is declared dead and a time of death is recorded, even though the heart is still beating under mechanical ventilation, the body is warm, and it continues to digest, heal wounds, mount immune responses, and regulate its own temperature and hormones; if the family consents, the ventilator is kept running for a while longer so that organs can be recovered, on the understanding that the donor must already be dead before anything is removed. Meanwhile the same unit treats a patient with locked-in syndrome, who may be fully conscious and able to communicate only by blinking, as unambiguously alive, and treats a patient in a persistent vegetative state, whose cortex is destroyed but whose brainstem keeps the body going, as alive too, sometimes for years. My aunt also mentioned that some jurisdictions let families block the neurological diagnosis on religious grounds, that she has heard of protocols in which the donor is given anaesthetic before the organs are taken, and that she believes there have been cases where a woman declared brain dead was kept on a ventilator for weeks or months so that the pregnancy could continue. I'd like to know what this whole apparatus is committed to.

Reconstruct, as carefully as you can, the theory of personal identity that this practice presupposes. Does it commit itself to the view that the person is located in the brain — so that when the brain stops, the person is gone and what remains is a body, however lively — or to a biological view on which the patient just is the living organism and brain death is therefore not death at all, or to a psychological-continuity view on which what matters is the persistence of consciousness and memory? Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit: tell me which parts of it would survive under each of the rival views and which would collapse, whether its various components presuppose consistent things (it seems to treat the brain as the seat of the person when deciding that someone has died, but the whole body as the patient when deciding what to treat), and where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same bedside behaviour. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with one of its rivals — what happens to organ procurement under a biological view, to the status of vegetative patients under a consciousness-based view, to the religious exemption and to the pregnant-patient case under either — and whether the changes would be legal, procedural, or merely rhetorical. I'm aiming for a section of the paper, so a structured reconstruction with explicit rival hypotheses would suit me better than a general survey.
