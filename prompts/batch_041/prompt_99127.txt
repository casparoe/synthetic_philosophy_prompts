I have been trying to work out, on my own, why the argument about whether conscious experience is causally idle never settles. The view in question is that experiences themselves do no work — the underlying brain processes do all of it — and the standard objection is that this refutes itself: if an experience never causes anything, then my belief that I am having a red experience, and my saying so, cannot be caused by the experience, so it cannot be the evidence for itself that it appears to be. The reply I keep running into is that the belief and the experience could both be effects of a common brain cause, so the belief reliably tracks the experience without being caused by it; the objection then returns in a new form, and the two sides circle. I want a diagnosis rather than another round. Is this a real disagreement about whether reliable correlation without causation can ground a judgment that is genuinely about an experience, a merely verbal quarrel over how loosely "cause" and "explain" are being used, or two parties answering different questions — one about what produced the judgment, the other about what makes it warranted or what its content refers to? My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that the standoff persists because one side is asking about the causal history of the judgment while the other is asking about the aboutness of its content, and that neither question can be answered in the other's terms. Tell me whether that is right, and given your answer, what would actually count as progress: a distinction that separates the two questions, a case that forces one side to concede, or an argument that the deadlock is a sign the whole question is badly posed.
