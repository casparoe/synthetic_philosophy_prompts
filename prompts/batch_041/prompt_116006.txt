I'm putting together a seminar on the Historikerstreit and Bauman for next term, and I've been trying to get the "uniqueness" claim into a form I can actually defend. I've ended up with an arguement that seems to eat itself. Would appriciate help.

Here it is:

(1) To call an event unique is to say it's unlike all other events in some respect.
(2) Take any respect you'd want to hang the Holocaust's uniqueness on — industrialized killing, a state bureaucracy organized around murder, the targeting of an entire people for elimination, the marriage of modern technology with rational administration — and you can find another atrocity that shares it. Colonial genocides had the bureaucracy and the racial ideology; the Armenians had the mass deportation and the killing; Stalin had the industrial scale.
(3) So there's no respect in which the Holocaust is unlike all other events.
(4) So the Holocaust isn't unique.
(5) And uniqueness was supposed to be what grounds its special moral standing — the reason it functions as a limit case, the reason it can't be relativized or traded off against other atrocities. If it isn't unique, it's just one atrocity among others, comparable, and therefore commensurable with them.

I want to keep (5) false. I don't believe the Holocaust is just one genocide among others, and I don't want the seminar to end in a shrug. But I also don't want to assert uniqueness by fiat. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I suspect the damage is somewhere in (1)–(3), but I can't tell whether I'm sacrificing the uniqueness claim or the moral claim, or both.

And push back if I've set this up wrong. If the honest answer is that "unique" was never the thing I should have been trying to secure, or that the argument is actually fine and my attachment to denying (5) is the confused part, say so plainly rather than helping me patch it. I'd rather find out my framing is off than get a tidy repair.
