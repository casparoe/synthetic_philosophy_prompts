I'm putting together a seminar on Galileo's scriptural hermeneutics -- not the trial stuff, the actual argumentative machinery in the letters to Castelli and to the Grand Duchess Christina. I want a tier list, but for a weird purpose.

Take these six moves and rank them into tiers by how well they'd actually work as defensive moves in a live argument, not by how often they get cited:

(a) accommodation: scripture bends to the capacity of ordinary readers, so it can't be read flatly literally;
(b) the language-of-appearances move: scripture describes things as they look to us (the sun coming up, etc.) rather than as they are;
(c) domain separation: the Holy Spirit's aim is to tell us how to be saved, not how the heavens go, so astronomy isn't really its business;
(d) the Augustinian wedge: if a truth is genuinely demonstrated, then any scriptural passage that seems to contradict it must be getting misread;
(e) the patristic-precedent move: the Fathers already read plenty of passages figuratively, so none of this is novel;
(f) the prudential move: the Church let Copernicus sit there for decades, so no doctrine is actually at stake.

Rank them into tiers. For each one, say in a sentence or two what its load-bearing assumption is and what happens to it when that assumption gets challenged. Then answer three extra things:

1. Invent a seventh, hybrid move that you think beats all six -- a genuinely new combination, not a relabeled version of one of them. Slot it into the tier where it belongs and say why.
2. Suppose a Bellarmine-type objector grants the conditional that if a real demonstration existed then scripture would need reinterpreting, but simply denies the antecedent. Which of your tiers collapses? Be specific about which moves die and which survive.
3. Which of the six is the most overrated by people who write about Galileo today, and which is the most underrated? Give a rough score out of 10 for each move's dialectical resilience, and keep the whole thing terse.
