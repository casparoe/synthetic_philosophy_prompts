I'm in a second-year epistemology seminar and we've just spent three weeks on the accuracy-first programme — the Joyce/Pettigrew sort of line, where accuracy is the sole epistemic good, credences get scored by a strictly proper rule, dominance arguments give you probabilism and expected-accuracy gives you conditionalization. I got into an argument about it with my flatmate, who does formal epistemology and thinks the whole thing is settled business, and I ended up banging out a short draft of an essay taking a deflationary line on it. I'd like you to go at it hard. Not in the "here are some things you should read" way — I'll find the literature myself, and the point is whether the arguemnt stands up on its own. What I most want: (a) does the central move actually work or have I smuggled something in; (b) which of my three worries would an accuracy-firster find easiest to shrug off, and which would actually bother them; (c) is this just one of the standard objections in a new coat, and if so which one; and (d) is there a version of this worth growing into something longer, or should I drop it before I sink more time in.

Here's the draft, such as it is. Thesis: accuracy-first epistemology is best read as a representation theorem about what it is for one credence function to be more accurate than another, and its normative conclusions are bought with three assumptions that are doing far more work than the programme lets on. (1) The dominance argument. Fix an algebra and a scoring rule S. If your credence function is incoherent, some coherent function scores at least as well in every world and better in some, so incoherence is irrational. My complaint: the quantifier ranges over the worlds of the theorist's model, and the alternatives compared are credence functions on that same algebra. The agent never chose the algebra or the rule. So what's shown is that if you accept a certain representation of the agent's state, then the represented state had better be a probability function — a constraint on the model, not a norm on the agent. It's like a representation theorem in decision theory: it tells you what a preference ordering must look like to be representable, and only becomes a norm once you add the premise that this is the right representation. Accuracy-firsters seem to treat the theorem as self-standing. (2) Additivity. Total inaccuracy is normally the sum of local inaccuracies per proposition, and that's what makes the dominance results go through. But seperability across propositions is a substantive assumption with no non-question-begging motivation that I can see. It isn't that I have a worked-out non-separable measure; it's that once you take seriously that the epistemic value of a credence in P can depend on your credences elsewhere — because you care about an integrated picture rather than a bag of unrelated opinions, or because the propositions are vague or self-referential in ways that make local scoring artificial — the theorems either fail or need patching, and the patching is where all the philosophy would be. The programme treats the additive form as default and non-additive cases as curiosities; I think that's backwards. (3) This is the one I care about. Accuracy is relative to a fixed stock of propositions, so it can't register a real epistemic virtue: getting the distinctions right. Two agents with the same credences on the same coarse algebra can be equally accurate at every world, but if one works with a finer partition that carves the subject matter at its joints and the other doesn't, we want to say the first is epistemically better off — and accuracy-first epistemology is silent, because accuracy is defined only downstream of the question set. Choosing your ideology is part of inquiry, not prior to it, which suggests accuracy is a proxy for something that can't be identified with accuracy itself. So: beautiful modeling, and I don't doubt the theorems; I doubt they're normative in the way advertised. The honest position is that the programme is conditional — it tells you what to do given an algebra, a rule, and a separability assumption, and the interesting action is all in those givens.

(short — is (1) or (3) already well-known and named, and does that matter); and "What I'd do with it" (a few sentences — develop, cut, or abandon, and what the one thing I should change is).
