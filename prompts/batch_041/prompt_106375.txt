I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about philosophical arguments that feel like conjuring tricks — you follow every step, nod along, and still suspect the deck was stacked. The one I keep circling is the paradox of knowability: from the apparently modest anti-realist thesis that every truth is knowable in principle, it seems to follow that every truth is in fact known, which is plainly false. I'd rather open the column with an original thought experiment than with the usual dry derivation, and I've been arguing about it with a colleague who insists the whole thing is a verbal trick about self-reference, so I want a case sturdy enough to survive that complaint.

Construct that thought experiment for me. It should be realistic and no longer than a paragraph: a community of ordinary inquirers — a lab group, a newsroom, an audit team — brought up against a truth that none of them can know, even though the truth is in the relevant sense available to them. The unknowability must not be stipulated; it has to fall out of the structure of the proposition itself, and the scenario must not lean on demons, brains in vats, or an omniscient being, and should stay free of possible-worlds jargon. Then run the checks: state the proposition at issue, give the Fitch-style derivation showing why it cannot be known, and identify precisely which assumption is doing the work — the factivity of knowledge, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, or the existence of a truth about someone's own ignorance. Set out what the anti-realist is committed to saying about the case and what the realist says, and show what happens to the argument if factivity is dropped or if the knowability principle is restricted to basic truths. Finish with the strongest way a defender of the knowability principle would attack the case, and tell me honestly which feature of the scenario, if removed, would let the anti-realist escape.
