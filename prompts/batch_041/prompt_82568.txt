I am designing a small theory programme on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — enough to fund roughly four researchers for two years, all of it for proofs and conceptual work rather than audits or empirical benchmarking. The canonical target already feels worked over: the trade-off among within-group calibration, equal false-positive rates, and equal false-negative rates, given unequal base rates in a fixed population, with a binary label and a single thresholded score. I want the agenda to begin where that framing runs out, and I want your judgement about which questions are upstream of the others. Candidates I would consider, offered as material rather than as a list to work through: what the impossibility becomes when the ground-truth label is not exogenous but produced by the very decisions the classifier informs, so that base rates are an output of the system rather than a fact about the population; what survives when the decision is a randomized allocation of a fixed budget of scarce goods, or a ranking, or a choice among many groups rather than two; what happens when the score is calibrated to a population that moves because individuals respond strategically to it; whether causal or counterfactual criteria for fairness generate impossibility results of their own or dissolve the statistical ones; whether the desiderata should be stated over individual decisions or over long-run distributions, and what turns on that choice; and whether there is any general theorem here at all — an Arrow-style result covering fair decision rules as such — or whether the apparent impossibility is an artifact of one particular three-criterion formulation that a better formalization would escape.

Tell me which of these, or something adjacent I have not named, is load-bearing, and defend the ordering. For each, say what it would have to deliver to count as settled, how many other questions in the area depend on its answer, whether it is tractable with the formal tools people already have, and what would make it more or less urgent over the next two years. Say explicitly which of my candidates are the same question wearing different clothes, and which you would deprioritize.

Finally, be candid about the epistemic status of your recommendations: mark what you take yourself to know, what you are extrapolating, and what is guesswork. State what would change your mind — which argument or theorem, coming out one way rather than the other, would move a question up or down the list — and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
