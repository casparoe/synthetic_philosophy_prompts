I have no academic training in this area. What follows is a puzzle I have assembled over years of reading newspapers and having lived in several countries, and what I want from you is help naming a thing, not a summary of the usual argument about separating church and state.

The puzzle is that a country's formal position on religion and its practical entanglement with religion do not seem to move together, and in some cases appear to run in opposite directions.

In England the church is established in the fullest legal sense: the head of state is its head, its bishops sit in the upper house of the legislature as of right, and a substantial share of the schools paid for out of the public purse are run by religious bodies, the established church among them. Regular attendance, meanwhile, is a very small fraction of the population, and the whole arrangement seems to cost dissenters almost nothing.

In France, separation is asserted more loudly than anywhere else in Europe, yet one region still lives under the arrangement that predates the separation law, with priests, pastors and rabbis paid out of a government ministry's budget and religious instruction on the public school timetable.

In Germany there is no established church at all, but the ordinary revenue office collects a church tax calculated as a percentage of your income tax; whether you are a member is a matter of the revenue office's records; and ceasing to be a member is a declaration to a state office rather than to the church.

In Denmark the church is established, its tax is collected by the tax authority, and the state also supports it out of general taxation, so non-members pay for it as well.

In Sweden the church was disestablished at the turn of the millennium, yet the tax agency still collects its membership fee, the same channel is open to a list of other religious bodies, and everyone, member or not, pays a burial fee whose collection is largely in the hands of the church's own parishes.

In the United States establishment is constitutionally forbidden, and yet churches are exempt from taxation as a matter of course and legislative and military chaplains are paid out of public funds.

What I want is a name, if there is one, for this specific thing, and I will not be satisfied with a bare label. I suspect I am in fact looking at three different phenomena and would like them separated. The first is the divergence itself, between legal status and practical entanglement. The second is the move by which an entanglement survives because it has been made even-handed, extended to every faith in turn, so that fairness becomes the mechanism by which religion stays on the state's payroll. The third is the re-description by which a religious arrangement comes to look like neutral administration: the collection of a tax, the keeping of population records, the upkeep of old buildings, the burial of the dead.

I have been told the phrase de facto establishment, which seems to cover part of the first phenomenon but is too coarse to be useful, and I know that civil religion means something different, a shared civic symbolism rather than a fiscal or administrative channel. So: does each of these three have an established name, and if not, can you coin one and define it precisely enough that I can tell what falls inside and outside it? Where are the edges — is a state that pays for the upkeep of historic church buildings doing the same thing as one that collects a church tax, and is a public holiday a form of establishment or something else entirely? Which of the three distinctions is the most useful to have, and which of my examples actually belongs to a different phenomenon than the one I have filed it under?
