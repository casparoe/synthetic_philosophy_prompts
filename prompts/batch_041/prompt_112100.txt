I got into a long, slightly heated argument with a friend last week about revealed preference, and I can't stop turning it over. She's a behavioral economist; her line was that "revealed preference" is basically a bookkeeping device dressed up as a theory of what people want -- once you take framing effects, menu effects, preference reversals and all the rest seriously, the idea that choices reveal a stable underlying ranking falls apart, and welfare economics should just admit it has no subject matter. I think that's way too quick. I know the original program was supposed to be austere: derive demand from choice data alone and leave "utility" as a summary of behavior, nothing more. But "utility" has always been doing at least three jobs at once -- representing choice, standing in for welfare, and grounding the expected-utility machinery -- and I don't see why those have to stand or fall together. So now I'm trying to figure out whether there's a real research program here or whether she's right and it's just nostalgia.

Here's what I need from you. Put on your philosopher-of-economics hat -- someone who's thought hard about measurement and welfare, not just about empirical behavioral econ -- and give me a prioritized agenda of the open conceptual questions about what choice reveals and what "utility" is supposed to mean. Candidates I've been chewing on, but don't feel bound by them: whether the choice-based approach can survive the behavioral evidence by moving to a richer domain (say, defining welfare over choice sets or over the "ancillary conditions" a chooser faces, the way some behavioral welfare economists do); whether the real diagnosis is that welfare was never the same thing as preference satisfaction in the first place, so the empirical stuff is a red herring; whether the ordinal-versus-cardinal question is still live once you care about interpersonal comparison and policy; whether the measurement-theoretic apparatus around representation theorems is genuinely settled or is quietly doing normative work; and whatever I'm obviously missing. What I want is the map, not a reading list -- assume I can find the literature myself and don't give me citations.

Lead with a two-sentence bottom line naming your single top pick, then the ranked list with a short paragraph each, and keep the whole thing under 800 words.
