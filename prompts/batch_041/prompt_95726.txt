I help run a small grantmaking operation, and like everyone else in this corner of the world we rank candidate interventions with the standard three-factor rubric: score each one out of ten for importance, out of ten for tractability, and out of ten for neglectedness, multiply the three, and sort the pile by the product. It is an innocuous-looking piece of arithmetic, and I have come to suspect it carries an entire theory of value in its luggage. I would like you to unpack it.

Start with what the multiplication itself commits us to. Does treating the three dimensions as factors presuppose that they are commensurable — that a point of importance and a point of tractability trade against one another at a fixed rate — and hence that the worth of a cause is a single cardinal quantity? Does it presuppose consequentialism, or could a deontological constraint survive inside a rubric of this shape? Which rival pictures of value does the product quietly rule out: lexical priorities, incommensurable goods, value pluralism, the possibility that some causes are simply not rankable against others?

Then the dimensions one at a time, because I think at least one of them is doing something the rubric cannot admit. Neglectedness is scored as though it were a property of a cause, like its budget or its death toll; but the work it does in the ranking is as a proxy for the marginal value of the next dollar, which is a claim about the slope of a returns curve rather than a property of the cause. Notice the mismatch: importance measures a level, neglectedness measures a derivative, and the rubric multiplies them as if they were the same kind of thing. Is that a category error, or a defensible heuristic that works because the two happen to be correlated? And what does the rubric assume about the shape of returns — that a fully funded field returns nothing at the margin, a hard ceiling, or merely that returns diminish? Under a log-shaped curve, would the neglectedness factor still be the right correction? Which assumptions about crowding between causes, and about the mutual independence of the three factors, does the ranking silently require?

Importance looks like it presupposes a common currency in which lives saved, animal welfare, the interests of future generations, and non-welfarist goods can all be priced. Which aggregation rule does that need — totalism, prioritarianism, something person-affecting — and which does the rubric thereby assume without argument? Tractability looks like it presupposes that difficulty is independent of the other two factors, when the selection effects of the field suggest the opposite, and that whoever assigns the score can state a probability. And what counts as one cause? The rubric presupposes that causes come in well-defined units that can be scored and compared; tell me where that breaks down.

Then give me the analysis the practice deserves: what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the very same rubric. Close by telling me what the rubric would have to become if the contested presuppositions were dropped — if neglectedness were replaced by a direct estimate of marginal returns, or if values were treated as incommensurable. What would a scoring practice look like then, and would it still be a scoring practice?
