Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mathematics about Skolem's paradox. The first takes the paradox at face value: since the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable model, and set theory is such a theory, the claim that the real numbers are uncountable cannot state a fact about the universe rather than a fact relative to a model, and countability and uncountability are therefore not absolute notions. The second insists that the paradox trades on a confusion between truth in a model and truth in the universe: the reals really are uncountable, and a countable model merely lacks the bijection that would count them. Both should grant for the sake of argument that set theory has a countable transitive model, so that the second cannot dismiss the problem by claiming that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem yields only models with a nonstandard membership relation. Have the second give a careful account of what is actually going on when such a model — countable, and interpreting membership as the real relation — satisfies "there exist uncountable sets," and have the first press whether that account does any explanatory work: if "countable" is applied to the model's sets from the outside, what privileges the outside? Have the first then extend the argument to finiteness, well-ordering, and the power set operation and ask what is left of the intended interpretation beyond a regulative ideal, while the second concedes what should be conceded and identifies exactly where the extension overreaches. Neither party may be a strawman, and each must answer the other's actual point rather than talking past it. Close by stating what the two agree the dispute turns on: whether any further mathematical or philosophical consideration could settle it, or whether part of it is merely verbal. I plan to use this in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, so it should be self-contained and accessible to students who know the theorem but have not thought much about its interpretation.
