I direct a writing center, and next month I have to run a single ninety-minute training session for a new cohort of undergraduate peer tutors, none of whom has any linguistics. The topic is how default assumptions get built into language — how an unmarked term quietly names a default while a marked term names everyone else — and I want to build the session on Douglas Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," the satire (written in the voice of a mock usage columnist) that transposes the vocabulary of racial hierarchy into the slots where our language marks sex, so that the ordinary, apparently neutral words are made to look as loaded as they really are. Do not deliver the explanation to me; design the session I should deliver.

Tell me the order in which the ideas must go, and why each has to wait for its predecessor: what the tutors must already hold before the satire can function as a demonstration rather than as a gotcha or a scolding, and in particular whether the semantic question (does the generic masculine actually denote women?) and the pragmatic one (whom does it presume as the default?) have to be pulled apart, and if so, which must come first. Then tell me which single case should carry the session — the satire itself, or the bland control sentence I have drafted, "Each student should hand in his assignment by Friday" — and what that case must have and must lack if it is to bear the weight; if you would rather I use a different carrying example, say what it must do that neither of these can. Set out the misconceptions the tutors will bring or form — I expect at least "this is just a matter of politeness," "the generic he really does cover everyone," and "the satire cheats, because race and sex are not analogous" — and mark the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Give me one question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether the idea landed, rather than whether they can now paraphrase the satire. And tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and to say so in the room.

End the whole answer with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing, in at most fifteen words, the one misconception the entire sequence is built to prevent.
