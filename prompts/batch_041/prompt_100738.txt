I'm a philosopher who has been drafted onto a small interdisciplinary project on the conceptual foundations of psychiatric classification — the group is two philosophers, two clinicians, a historian of medicine, and a patient advocate, and we have roughly a year of part-time effort and a modest budget to divide among ourselves. The funders have been explicit that they do not want a literature review or a reading list; they want us to commit to a short, ordered list of open questions and to defend the ordering. I would like your help working out what that list should be, and I care far more about the reasoning than about the list itself.

The organizing theme is looping effects in Hacking's sense — the way a classification can change the people classified, and changed people can change the classification in turn. But I want to push past the standard summary of that idea, because I suspect the interesting work lies in the distinctions the slogan conceals. Consider, for instance: (a) whether we should separate loops that make a category more homogeneous (people converging on the stereotype) from loops that make it more heterogeneous (people developing and reporting new symptoms, or the category proliferating into rival subtypes), and whether those two directions carry different implications for whether the category is tracking anything real; (b) whether looping runs only through the classified or also through the classifiers — clinicians and researchers being themselves reshaped by the categories they use, so that a generation trained on dimensional instruments may simply perceive and describe patients differently; (c) what to make of deliberate resistance, where the people classified reject, revise, or campaign against a category (the removal of homosexuality from the diagnostic manuals, or the reframing of autism by neurodiversity advocates), and whether such resistance is evidence against a category's validity, evidence of a different kind, or neither; (d) whether the transient or niche disorders of earlier eras are genuinely the same phenomenon as contemporary candidates such as a proposed gaming disorder or prolonged grief disorder, or whether the resemblance is superficial; and (e) whether the current shift toward dimensional and transdiagnostic frameworks — a general factor of psychopathology, hierarchical spectra, symptom-network models — dampens looping, intensifies it, or merely relocates it.

Given all that, which open conceptual questions should this particular group prioritize, and in what order? Please defend the ordering rather than merely listing items: for each, say why it is upstream of the others (what work it would unblock or render moot), how tractable it is for the philosophers in the room rather than for the clinicians and empiricists, how neglected it currently is, and what a result or development over the next year or two would move it up or down the list. I am also interested in the trade-offs between those criteria — where you would sacrifice tractability for upstreamness, say, or tolerate a crowded topic because it is load-bearing. No citations are needed; I want the agenda and the argument for it.
