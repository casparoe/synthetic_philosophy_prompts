I am a high school student who has been reading ahead in a philosophy of science course, and I have gotten stuck on the propensity interpretation of probability. I understand the standard contrast with the other two familiar views: the frequentist says a probability is a long-run relative frequency in a sequence of repetitions, and the subjectivist says it is a degree of belief subject to coherence constraints. The propensity view, as I understand it, says instead that a probability is a tendency or disposition of a particular physical setup — the coin, the radium atom, the loaded die — to produce a given outcome in a single trial, and that this tendency is a real feature of the world rather than a fact about our beliefs or about what happens in the long run. What attracts me to it is that it seems to give the right answer in the single case: even if a particular atom decays only once and never again, it seems meaningful to say that it had a certain tendency to decay within the hour.

My confusion concerns what becomes of a propensity once the outcome is settled but unknown, and how propensities are supposed to behave when we condition on things. Suppose I have a coin that I know has a strong tendency to land heads — say, 0.7 — and I toss it, catch it, and cover it with my hand, so that the outcome has already been physically determined but neither of us has seen it. Is the propensity of that toss to have landed heads still 0.7, or is it now 1? If a propensity is a physical feature of the situation, it seems to have been consumed or resolved by the toss itself; yet it would be very strange to say that the probability for me has jumped from 0.7 to 1, since nothing about the physical setup changed between the moment before the coin landed and the moment after. Relatedly, I have read in passing that some philosophers object that propensities cannot satisfy the axioms of probability, because conditional propensities run into trouble: the propensity of a setup to produce heads given that it has already produced heads seems to be either 1 or undefined, and neither answer looks like a genuine tendency of a chance setup. I gather that feeding propensities into Bayes's theorem produces similarly awkward results, such as something that would have to be described as a propensity belonging to the setup given the outcome, which sounds like backwards causation.

Could you explain, in an intuitive rather than technical way, precisely what the difficulty is and what it shows about the propensity interpretation? I would especially like to understand three things. First, whether the tension I have described is a genuine problem for the interpretation or merely a confusion between the propensity itself and my information about it. Second, how a defender of the propensity view would answer the objection about conditional propensities, and whether that answer requires giving up anything important — for instance, whether propensities can still do the work of the probability calculus, or whether they end up as a metaphysical gloss on a notion that has to be cashed out some other way. Third, what the propensity interpretation explains that the frequentist and subjectivist interpretations cannot, particularly in the single case. I would also appreciate your saying where the most natural line between the three interpretations should be drawn, and whether the worry I have raised would equally afflict a view on which objective chances are simply taken to be primitive.
