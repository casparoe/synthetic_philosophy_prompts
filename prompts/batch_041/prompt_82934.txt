You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive science with expertise in the Darwin/Turing inversion of reasoning—the explanatory move of accounting for a capacity by a process that lacks that capacity, as natural selection accounts for design without a designer and computation accounts for thought without a mind—and in the foundations of artificial intelligence. I would like you to adjudicate a disagreement between two colleagues, neither of whom I am party to. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the criteria one might use to distinguish competence from comprehension and reliability from reason-tracking.

Background: A machine-learning system was trained only to predict the next step in human-written mathematical proofs. It has now produced a proof of a new theorem, and human mathematicians have verified every step. The proof is not a trivial recombination; it uses a known lemma in a way no human had used it before. The system cannot explain its proof, answer questions about it, or recognize that a step is wrong when the surrounding context is altered. I have been calling the crux the inversion gap: the gap between a mechanism that reliably produces correct outputs and a mechanism that is responsive to the reasons that make those outputs correct.

A says: This is a clean case of competence without comprehension. The system has the competence to produce a valid proof, but it does not comprehend the mathematics, because its mechanism is prediction rather than reason-tracking. The proof's validity is a fact about the proof, not about the system's mind. A calculator can be reliable at arithmetic without understanding it, and natural selection can produce design without a designer. The Darwin/Turing inversion just extends this: you explain the competence by a process that lacks the comprehension.

B says: That conflates reliability with responsiveness to reasons. The system is not merely reliable; its output is novel and sensitive to the specific inferential context. If it reliably produces steps that are correct for the reasons that make them correct, then it is tracking those reasons, even if it cannot articulate them. A calculator only follows an algorithm; this system is more like a scientist's intuition. And if you insist that comprehension requires articulation, you make comprehension a mysterious extra ingredient that does no explanatory work.

A replies: Novelty and sensitivity are not comprehension. Natural selection produced novel solutions without any representational states, and this system's internal states represent a proof space only in the way a map represents a city—representation is not understanding. What matters is whether the process is answerable to reasons, not whether it covaries with them.

B replies: Then your distinction between answerability and covariance is itself doing the mysterious work. Either you can give a test for it that does not presuppose human-style articulation, or you are just labeling whatever humans do as comprehension and whatever machines do as mere reliability. The inversion shows that comprehension is not needed to explain the competence; it does not show that there is a further fact about comprehension that the system lacks.

A replies: The further fact is normative. The system's states have no correctness conditions of their own; they are correct only relative to the training data and the verifier. A human mathematician's states are correct or incorrect for reasons she can be criticized for missing. That is the difference between a process that is answerable to reasons and one that merely produces outputs we happen to endorse.

B replies: But you have now moved from comprehension to accountability. A mathematician can be answerable to reasons without being able to articulate them, and a system can be answerable to reasons if its design makes it sensitive to counterevidence—say, if it revises its proof when a lemma is shown false. If it does not, that is a limitation of this system, not of the inversion.

Now adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say which side, if either, has the better overall position, and what both are missing. In particular, address: (1) whether the distinction between reliability and reason-tracking can be made precise without begging the question; (2) whether the Darwin/Turing inversion supports A's or B's reading; and (3) whether the case would change if the system revised its proof after a lemma was shown false, or if it could answer a single counterfactual question about why a step is valid. End with the strongest version of the view you reject.
