I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book about a chemist who gets appointed to a government safety committee, and I've realized the book is really about the precautionary principle, except I can't tell you what my own position is. So here's what I actually think, gut level, case by case. I want to know what principle, if any, holds these together — and I want the ones that don't fit pointed out, not smoothed over with an extra clause for every case.

- The morning-sickness sedative back in the late 50s that turned out to deform babies. The agency that just kept stalling the application instead of approving it: right, and I don't think they were merely lucky.
- A vaccine for an outbreak that's killing people, approved on thin safety data because waiting means more funerals: also right. I don't feel like that contradicts the one above, but maybe it does.
- Phasing out the spray chemicals that were eating the ozone layer before anyone had the chemistry nailed down: right, and sooner would've been better.
- Gene-drive mosquitoes to wipe out malaria: no. Even though malaria kills a lot of kids and the mosquitoes would save them. Once those things are out there they don't come back, and the whole ecology is a different thing forever.
- The moratorium on making bird flu more transmissible in labs: right, and the researchers' own consent doesn't settle it, because a lab leak doesn't stay in the lab.
- My neighbor's herbicide. He sprays it, he's the one breathing it, he's fine with it, and I still think it should be restricted, because it ends up in the creek and in everybody's wells downstream.
- Cheap generic drug, saves a lot of lives in poor countries, held up for years over a cancer signal in rats at doses no human would ever see: I think holding it up was a mistake.
- And the embarrassing one. I won't let my kid on a trampoline, but I drive her to school every morning on a road where, statistically, she's in more danger. I'm not going to change the driving.

The half-formed rule I catch myself using: the burden of proof should fall on whoever stands to profit, and when the people who profit aren't the people who bear the risk, I want a moratorium until somebody else does the proving. My friend who does environmental law says the real distinction is what she calls baseline-rewriting harm — harm that doesn't just add a bad thing to the world but changes the default that everything else gets measured against, like an invasive species or a permanent change to the atmosphere. Is that a real distinction or is it just "irreversible" wearing a nicer coat? (I'm also not sure my profit rule survives the trampoline, honestly.)

And the case I keep going back and forth on, which is basically the climax of the book: a scientist releases something self-spreading — not a weapon, something meant to clean up a mess — without asking anyone, because asking would take ten years and the mess is getting worse. Half the time I think he's a hero and half the time I think he's the villain, and I need to know which my own principles make him.

What I want back, please:

1. A table with these columns, one row per case above: case | my verdict | what your principle says about it | agrees? (yes/no) | which clause of the principle that row is evidence for.
2. Then a JSON object with exactly these fields: principle (one paragraph, stated precisely enough that I could argue against it), clauses (a list, each with clause and case_that_forces_it), verdicts_you_cant_keep (the ones of mine you're overturning, one line each on why), predictions (two or three cases I haven't listed, each with case, verdict, why), confidence (a number from 0 to 1 for your whole answer), least_sure (the single claim you're least sure of, and what would change your mind about it).

Don't add a clause just to save a verdict. If something has to go, say so.
