I need a two-page proposal for a three-month working paper, and I want to pin the framing down before drafting. The target phenomenon: an agent whose reasoning is captured by a consistent, arithmetic-strength formal theory T cannot, from inside T, certify a successor agent that reasons in T. The obstruction is structural rather than practical. If T could prove the reflection schema — for every sentence φ, if T proves φ then φ — then by Löb's theorem T would prove φ for every φ and so be inconsistent; the instance φ = falsity is exactly T's unprovability of its own consistency. An agent that must prove it is entitled to trust its own future conclusions therefore faces a dilemma: strengthen T, and the stronger theory must be trusted in turn; weaken T, and the power one wanted to preserve is lost. My half-formed idea is that this dilemma rests on an unexamined assumption — that the justification of self-trust has to be a theorem of the trusting theory — and that the way out is to separate grades of self-trust: (i) the full reflection schema, (ii) consistency of the trusted theory, (iii) a rule-level commitment to act on the successor's proofs without certifying the rule. Since no consistent theory can prove the soundness of its own rules of inference in any case, (i) is not merely hard but impossible, and the live question becomes whether self-improving agency needs (ii), (iii), or something weaker still — and whether an evidence-based rather than proof-based successor-licensing rule dissolves the obstacle or merely relocates it one level up.
