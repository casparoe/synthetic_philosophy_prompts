Two colleagues of mine, Mara and Devin, spent most of a long car ride arguing about what each of them should now believe, and I never worked out who had the better of it. I would like you to referee. Here is the exchange as fairly as I can reconstruct it.

The background: both volunteer for a wildlife trust and are experienced at estimating the size of large crowds by grid sampling; they have done it side by side for years and are, so far as anyone can tell, about equally good at it. Last month each independently estimated the crowd at a one-day festival that cannot be re-run. Mara's figure was about 9,000; Devin's was about 22,000. Each regards the other as competent and honest.

Mara (1): Since we are equally good at this, neither of us can claim her own number is more likely to be right. So the rational response is for each of us to move substantially toward the other's figure. Refusing to budge just means treating myself as the authority in a case where I have already conceded I am not one.

Devin (2): That cannot be a general rule. If I have a worked calculation in front of me and you tell me the answer is different, I do not halve my confidence in it; I assume you slipped. Here I have gone back over my sampling and cannot find any specific mistake. The disagreement tells me that one of us has gone wrong somewhere, but it does not tell me to abandon a number I can find no fault with.

Mara (3): "Cannot find a mistake" is cheap when you are looking for the kind of mistake that lets you keep your number. And the disagreement is the same piece of evidence for both of us: if it raises the chance that you erred, it raises the chance that I erred by the same amount. You have no more title to wave it away than I do.

Devin (4): It is not the same evidence, because it is not the same on both sides. I have my detailed reasons plus your bare contrary figure; you have your detailed reasons plus my bare contrary figure. When I weigh my reasons against your disagreement, the disagreement is a single coarse fact with no diagnostic content, so it should move me only a little. That is not self-privileging; it is what it is to have reasons at all. And notice: if either of us could have spotted the error by rechecking, we probably would have, which suggests the mistake, if there is one, is of a kind neither of us can detect from the inside. That undercuts your confidence in your own recheck as much as mine.

Mara (5): Then turn your own principle on itself. We also disagree about the correct policy for handling disagreement: I say peers should move toward each other, you say they should not. That is a peer disagreement about the very question we are fighting over. If a disagreement with a peer is some evidence that you have erred, then this one is too, so it should give you some reason to doubt your policy and move toward mine. If it does not, you have exempted the one disagreement that matters most.

Devin (6): If that argument worked, any confident contrarian could defeat any view just by disagreeing with it. And the two cases are not alike: about the crowd we each have a measurement and no way to settle it, whereas about the policy we can argue from premises we share, and I have given you reasons you have not answered. The bare fact that you disagree with my argument does not neutralize the argument.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then identify what each of them is missing, and say who has the stronger overall position and why. I would also like you to state, explicitly, what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to hold, would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
