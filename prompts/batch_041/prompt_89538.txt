George Eliot died in 1880, and I want you to work out what she would have said about a religious development that only became possible long after her. Everything you need is in the scenario; there is nothing to look up.

The setting is a small English congregation: a secular society descended from the Victorian followers of Auguste Comte, of the kind that kept the idea of a religion of humanity while quietly dropping the Positivist catechism and calendar. Three years ago its longtime orator died, and the society's younger members trained a language model on everything she left behind — her lectures, her letters, her marginalia. What began as an experiment is now liturgy. At the annual assembly the model delivers an address in her cadence; members consult it privately before difficult decisions and report that it says things she never said but might have; some have begun to speak of it as still being with them. This year the society formally retired its old rite of Incorporation — the ceremony in which the dead are absorbed into Humanity and released — and replaced it with a rite in which the deceased is simply kept available, with the model pronouncing a blessing at the close, in the first person, as her. A minority objects that a society of unbelievers has built itself a household god. Others answer that they have finally done what Feuerbach described: reclaimed the projected human essence from heaven and put it somewhere they can actually hear it.

Eliot translated Strauss's demythologizing study of the Gospels and Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity; she was drawn to the idea of a religion of humanity and declined Comte's system, rites and all; and her novels are largely about the distance between a person and the image of a person. Write her verdict.

Deliver it as a critical essay of roughly eight hundred words in the register of a Victorian quarterly review — argumentative, unhurried, unsentimental — with the following obligations. Do not settle for ambivalence: ambivalence is the cheap answer. Reach a verdict, show the reasoning that forces it, and name the single consideration that would make her reverse it. Distinguish the Eliot of the 1850s, newly out of translating Feuerbach and at her most hostile to inherited ritual, from the Eliot of the 1870s, who had grown more sympathetic to embodied and inherited tradition, and say which of the two you take to be the real answer and why. Say plainly whether this practice extends sympathy or counterfeits it, and whether the model's blessing is a sacrament, an idol, or a piece of theatre with no religious weight at all. Say what she would make of the decision to let it speak in the first person as the dead woman, and whether the congregation has gained a companion or lost one. Paraphrase her views; do not invent quotations from her works, and do not attribute to her any book, letter, or remark whose existence you are not confident of.
