I've been chewing on this idea that what's really good for someone is whatever they'd want if they were smarter, better informed, and less of a mess internally -- that your real preferances are the ones you'd have if you were idealized somehow. I think the term I've seen floating around for it is coherent extrapolated volition, and some people stretch it to whole groups, so the good for all of us is what we'd all want if we were idealized together.

I mostly buy it. It feels like it has to be true, because who else gets to say what's good for me except the best version of me? But I want to streess test it. What would actually count against it? Not the boring stuff like "people are irrational," I get that. I mean the real defeators, ranked by how bad they'd be.

Two things I keep bumping into that I don't know what to do with.

First, does it matter whether you idealize the person's actual choices, or the procedure they use to make choices? Because those come apart. A more informed, more consistent me might settle on a rule for living that leads to outcomes he himself would say are worse, just because he thinks he ought to tie his own hands. So which one is the real idealized preference? And if there's no fact of the matter, is that a problem for the view or just for me?

Second, the idealized version of me -- is he even me? If you improve a person enough, at some point you've just built a different person who happens to share my memories. Then "what he'd want" starts to sound like asking what a stranger wants, and the whole thing stops being about my good at all. Does that kill it, or is there some line you can draw that keeps the idealized guy connected to the actual me?

And for the group version: even if each person's own idealization is fine, throwing a bunch of idealized people together might not give you one coherent answer. They could disagree about the rule for combining their wants, and that could go around in circles with no stopping point. Does that sink the collective version specifically, or is it just the same problem as the individual one wearing a hat?

Be honest with me too -- has this view been qualified so much that nothing could count against it anymore? If someone says "well, the extrapolation might not converge, and it might not even be well defined, but it's still the right ideal," is that a real position or are they just refusing to lose? The thing that would move me most is a case where the idealized self and the actual self come apart in a way we'd all agree is bad -- like if the idealized me genuinely wouldn't care about my actual life, or would be fine trading it away. If you can make that case, I think it's the strongest thing against the view. Sort everything from "yeah, that's fatal" down to "awkward but survivable," and tell me which of these the view's own fans would already admit counts against them.
