I am a teaching assistant for a 300-level undergraduate course on death, dying, and medical ethics, and I am working through the midterm essays before the professor moderates the marks. The question asked students to work through the two criteria for death recognized in American law and to say whether they can be held together in a single standard. Before I commit to a mark on this one, I would like an independent read of it: I have been grading for two days straight and no longer trust my own calibration. The professor's rubric puts most of the weight on accurate exposition and comparatively little on the student's own conclusion, so the mark should turn mainly on whether the student has the material right.

The question was: the operative sentence of the Uniform Determination of Death Act reads, "An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead." Explain what each of these two criteria is meant to capture, why the neurological criterion came to be thought necessary, and how each criterion is applied in practice. Then assess whether the two criteria can be reconciled within a single coherent standard of death.

The essay reads as follows.

Both criteria are attempts to answer the same question: when has a human organism ceased to exist as an integrated whole, rather than as a collection of parts kept going from outside? The circulatory criterion is the older answer. For most of the history of medicine, death simply was the cessation of heartbeat and breathing, because without them the rest of the organism disintegrates within minutes. Its inadequacy became visible only once we could ventilate and perfuse a body mechanically: a patient whose brain has been destroyed can be kept warm, oxygenated, and circulating indefinitely, so the traditional signs no longer tell us whether the organism is still there. The neurological criterion was introduced to close that gap. On the account the Harvard committee set out in 1968 and the President's Commission later took up, the brain — and in particular the brain stem — is what integrates the organism's subsystems into a working whole, so the irreversible loss of brain function is the loss of the organism itself rather than the loss of one organ among others.

It matters which version of the neurological criterion one adopts. The President's Commission endorsed the higher-brain criterion, which identifies death with the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness and of integrated cerebral function, rather than with the failure of every brain function including the brain stem's. On that reading a patient in a persistent vegetative state has lost what is essential to being a living person, and the statutory reference to the brain stem is best explained as a practical marker of the underlying loss rather than as part of the definition. Those who insist that the whole brain, stem included, must have failed are defending a different criterion, not a different reading of the same one.

In practice the two criteria are applied quite differently. A neurological determination is made at the bedside: unresponsive coma, absent brain-stem reflexes, and no respiratory drive, confirmed by an apnea test; where the clinical examination cannot be completed reliably — because of sedatives, say, or facial trauma — an ancillary study such as a cerebral blood-flow scan or an EEG is used to support the diagnosis. A circulatory determination is what is used when life-sustaining treatment is being withdrawn but the patient does not meet the neurological criteria. In donation-after-circulatory-death protocols the heart is allowed to stop and the team then waits out an observation interval of a few minutes before declaring death. That interval is what establishes the irreversibility of the cessation: once it has elapsed, spontaneous circulation cannot return, and the patient therefore satisfies the statutory standard of irreversible cessation of circulatory function before the organs are retrieved. The usual objection — that the donor's circulation could have been restarted had anyone tried — is therefore beside the point.

Can the two be reconciled? I do not think so, and I think the statute papers over the difficulty. The neurological criterion asks whether brain function can be restored; the circulatory criterion, as it is actually applied, asks only whether circulation will be restored. Those are different questions, and a patient can pass the second test while failing the first, which is precisely the situation in DCD. Since the two criteria are not measuring the same thing, treating them as two doors into a single room is a mistake, and the circulatory criterion is the one that should be surrendered, because the neurological criterion is the one that tracks the death of the organism.
