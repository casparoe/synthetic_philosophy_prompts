I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice and its critics, and I am assembling a diagnostic handout for the first meeting: a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on bounded rationality and satisficing according to what she is actually committed to, rather than according to the label she would apply to herself. Most of my students arrive able to say that Herbert Simon thought people satisfice and almost none can say what follows from that, or where they themselves stand. The tree should close that gap. Each internal node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf must carry a descriptive name for the position together with a single sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Build the tree around three distinctions, taken in this order.

First, what the stopping rule ranges over. Does it govern the options considered (take the first alternative that clears a bar, without surveying the field) or the deliberation performed (stop thinking once further computation would not change the choice, even though the field has been surveyed)? Some positions are only about options, some only about computation, and running the two together is the most common confusion in the area. Every reader should have to commit to one or the other, or to both with an explanation of how they interact.

Second, where the threshold comes from. Is the bar fixed in advance by the agent, imposed by the task or the environment, or does it drift upward after success and downward after failure? A view that fixes the threshold and a view on which the threshold is itself revised are different positions, and the second is easy to misclassify as the first.

Third, the status of the claim. Descriptive (this is how agents in fact proceed), normative (this is how they ought to proceed), conceptual (this is what rationality amounts to, with the maximizer as the special case), or deflationary (the normative question should be abandoned rather than answered). A reader may hold any of these while denying the others, so the tree must ask them separately rather than bundling them into a single branch.

The following positions must appear as leaves: optimization with deliberation costs folded in, so that there is no separate phenomenon of satisficing once the cost of thinking is included among the things being maximized over; satisficing as an instrumentally superior heuristic, better in the right environments precisely because it does not maximize; satisficing as a stopping rule with a fixed threshold; adaptive satisficing, in which the threshold moves with experience; the divorce view, on which we do satisfice and ought not to, and the two claims need no reconciliation; bounded rationality as a research program with no normative pretensions whatever; the redefinition on which rationality just is what bounded agents do well; and the meliorating position, on which one chooses what improves on the status quo rather than what clears an absolute bar.

At least four nodes must turn on concrete cases rather than on doctrine. Use these: a tenure committee that reads files until one clears the bar and never sees the rest; a chess engine that prunes a line because it is already lost; a house hunter who raises her price ceiling after six months of failed searches; a firm that reaches its target profit and stops looking for further efficiencies; and a doctor who stops testing once the probability of serious disease falls below a threshold.

Finally, two housekeeping requirements. If two leaves cannot be separated by any case I have listed or any you can supply, say so explicitly and explain why the distinction collapses behaviorally, rather than papering over it. And for each combination of answers that no position occupies, note it and say briefly whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. Aim for roughly a dozen internal nodes; where a case leaves the reader genuinely indeterminate, record that rather than forcing a branch. The intended audience is graduate students in philosophy and social science, so no question may presuppose training in economics, and no node should be answerable by recalling a textbook definition. Do not include citations or reading suggestions; if you name a position after a person, do so only where the attribution is secure.
