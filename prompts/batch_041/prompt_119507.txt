So I'm writing a long feature about the fight over discounting the future — the rate you use to weigh a benefit now against a benefit in a hundred or five hundred years — and I've got two people who've been going at it for years. On one side, a philosopher: pure time preference is just a moral mistake, she says; caring less about a harm because it lands later is like caring less because it lands farther away, and once you subtract the fact that future people will probably be richer, there's nothing left to justify a positive rate. On the other side, an economist: she's confusing an ethical parameter with a fact about opportunity costs, he says; any finance ministry that tried to run a zero pure rate would be told by its own treasury to sit down, and her whole program quietly assumes we can forecast five centuries out.

They agree on the arithmetic and disagree about what it means, and they talk past each other in a way that would make a bad piece. So I want the document they should have written together — the kind of joint statement that comes out of an adversarial collaboration in psychology. Claims both of them can sign. Then the disagreements stated so precisely that each of them would accept the sentence describing their own side. Then the cruxes. Then, for each crux, a case or argument both agree bears on it, with what each one predicts about it written down before anyone runs it. Numbered list of agreed claims first, then a table for the disagreements. And dont let the agreed claims get so vague they're unprintable — if the only thing they agree on is "discounting is a modeling choice," that's not a document.

Two things to be careful about. First, a lot of this is settled and a lot of it isnt, and I need those kept apart — I don't want a contested reading of what the standard formula means (the one that splits the rate into a pure time preference term, a term for how fast future consumption grows, and the curvature of the utility function) handed to me as if it's just what economists think. Second, they've both told me that half the fight is verbal — she means something different by "time preference" than he does, something about utility versus consumption — so if part of this is a word fight, say which part and how you'd fix the wording.

Specifics my editor will ask about: how much of the disagreement is about discounting one person's own future welfare versus discounting across generations; whether uncertainty about the right rate really does make the far future dominate everything (one of them keeps pulling this out and I can never tell if it's a trick or a theorem); whether a zero pure rate commits you to an absurd amount of saving; and whether the fact that different policies change who gets born matters here at all. Also give me one concrete spend-now-or-not case with real numbers — something like a project that costs a lot today to prevent something bad in 300 years — that both of them would agree is a fair test.

Im not looking for a transcript. I don't need them talking. I need the document.
