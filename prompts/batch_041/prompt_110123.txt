I spent thirty-odd years as a marine biologist and retired a couple years back, and since then I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars, mostly out of a nagging sense that I skipped a chapter somewhere. Here's what's got under my skin. I sit on a small grant-review panel — just three of us, tiny foundation — and our job is to decide whether a proposal gets funded. Somebody wrote our rule down years ago: fund it if the proposed method is sound and the sample size is adequate, otherwise no. So we each vote on the two sub-questions and then apply the rule mechanically. Last cycle we got this: I thought the method was sound but the sample too small; my colleague thought the method shaky but the sample fine; the third thought both were fine. By the sub-questions the panel says the method is sound (2–1) and the sample is adequate (2–1), so the rule says fund — except if you'd just asked each of us straight out "should we fund it?" you'd have gotten two no's. I genuinely can't tell if our procedure is a bug or a feature, and I've stopped wanting the patch and started wanting the general picture.

So here's the actual ask: give me the desiderata for any method of turning a bunch of individual judgments into one group judgment. Not a defense of one procedure — the requirements list. What I want: the cases any such method has to get right (mine above is one, presumably not the only species), and for each requirement the reason behind it rather than just the label — it ought to return some verdict for whatever combination of opinions it's handed, it shouldn't let a single member dictate, it shouldn't care which member holds which view, it should be responsive to what the members actually think, it shouldn't hand back a group position that contradicts itself, it probably shouldn't let the answer to one question depend on opinions about some unrelated question — and the distinctions it mustn't collapse, like the difference between aggregating final verdicts and aggregating the reasons people gave for them. Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are just house preferences of one camp, which single one is doing the most work in ruling methods out, and — the part I most want — which of them can't all be satisfied at once and why. I have a strong hunch that some of the ones I just rattled off are jointly impossible, and if that's right then the list itself is the result and I'd rather see that than a method that quietly drops one. Briefly at the end: how does plain majority voting on each question fare against your list, and how does a procedure that only ever counts up-and-down votes on the final question fare? Skip any reading list — I'm asking you, not the library.
