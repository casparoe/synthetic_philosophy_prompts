So I did jury duty for the first time last month — an assault case, nothing dramatic — and the whole thing seemed to come down to one sentence the prosecuter kept saying and the judge echoed: he could have walked away. I voted to convict and I still think that was the right call, but I've been chewing on it ever since, because I'm not sure I actually believe that sentence and it was doing alot of work.

So I want to argue with someone who thinks the sentence doesn't matter — someone who thinks you can be fully responsible for something even if you couldn't have done otherwise.

Please play that person. Make them good: a philosopher who's spent a career on free will and moral responsibility, who's heard every objection, who'll happily give me the small stuff (yeah the brain-chip stories are weird, sure, maybe responsible shouldn't mean deserves to suffer) but will never give up the main claim. One or two paragraphs per turn, no lectures, stay in character until I say stop. You can ask me questions, just don't stall or explain to me what a Frankfurt case is — I know, so argue.

Here's my opening move. Every version of the chip story is the same: the chip will zap him if he's about to do otherwise, it never fires, so he couldn't have done otherwise, and you say he's responsible anyway. Fine. But make the chip realistic and it's right maybe 9 times out of 10 — the neuroscientist's read on him is good, not perfect. Now it isn't true that he couldn't have done otherwise. One time in ten the chip misreads him and he walks away. So your example isn't a case of no alternative possibilities, it's a case of a small chance of one — and the chance has to be zero or your whole story falls apart. But make the chip perfect and now his decision was legible off his brain a full minute before he made it, which just means it was already settled before he deliberated, and then the intuition that he's responsible is doing all the work, not the chip. So which is it: a chip that's sometimes wrong, so he's only sometimes unfree, or a chip that's never wrong, so you've smuggled determinism in through the back? And notice what follows from the first horn — if his freedom depends on the error rate of some piece of hardware, then whether he's blameworthy is a fact about a machine, which seems insane.
