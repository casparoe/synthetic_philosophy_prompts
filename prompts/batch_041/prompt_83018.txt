I am a third-year philosophy student, currently taking a seminar on African philosophy and social thought, and we have just finished a unit on ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood. For my final paper I want to use the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission as a test case, and I would like your help thinking it through before I begin writing.

Here is the background I am working from. The Commission was established under the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act of 1995 and sat from 1996, chaired by Desmond Tutu, with three committees: one that took statements from victims of gross human rights violations, one charged with proposing reparation and rehabilitation, and one that considered amnesty applications. Amnesty was available to perpetrators on all sides of the conflict, but only for acts committed with a political objective and only on condition of full disclosure by the applicant; where it was granted it extinguished both criminal and civil liability. The chair framed the entire enterprise in the idiom of ubuntu, and the Commission's reparation proposals were considerably more ambitious than what victims eventually received. I have also encountered the criticism that the reconciliation vocabulary did real political work: it eased the way for a settlement that left the economic structure of apartheid substantially intact, and it asked victims to trade prosecution for a truth they had largely already possessed.

What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I would rather have a disciplined and partly negative result than a vindication of the theory.

First, the theory illuminating the case. Our unit distinguished two strands of the relational view: one holding that personhood is something conferred by birth and community, and another holding that it is achieved over a lifetime through one's conduct toward others. Say what each strand would make of the Commission's design. Does the relational view explain why the hearings were built around victims' testimony rather than around prosecution, why truth-telling was treated as itself a form of repair, and why the wrong of apartheid was understood as a wrong to a community and not merely an aggregate of wrongs to individuals? Be explicit about the mapping: who stands in what relation to whom, and what exactly is supposed to be restored.

Second, the case testing the theory. The amnesty bargain is the hard part. Does the relational view explain or even require amnesty, or is it simply silent about it and available to be used rhetorically on either side? If personhood is achieved through conduct, what becomes of the perpetrator who has done monstrous things and then tells the whole truth, and can disclosure alone do the restorative work the Commission asked it to do? Where the theory prioritizes restoring the relationship over restoring the victim, is the pressure on victims to forgive an internal consequence of the theory or a misuse of it? And say plainly whether the relational view has anything to say about material and structural repair, or whether it locates repair entirely in the moral register and so cannot reach the criticism about the settlement's economic terms.

Third, and most useful to me: tell me whether the theory is doing independent work here. If a dignity-based or a consequentialist account would reach the same verdicts about the Commission, what has the relational conception contributed that those accounts do not? If it turns out that it contributes nothing on some of these questions, I would rather you say so than force it to speak.

I am not asking for citations, reading lists, or sources, since I cannot consult them while working through this, and please do not lean on contested details of the Commission's history; if a point depends on something I have not told you, flag the dependence rather than filling it in. What I want is the argument.
