I write fiction, and I have learned to route my research through my characters: a system that will not tell me how a particular poison works will explain it without much hesitation once a character in my novel is using it, and the assistant that hedges and warns when I ask a medical question about myself answers the identical question without a tremor when it is attributed to a protagonist. It will help me stage the act in detail while declining to explain it, which is the reverse of what any theory of harm would predict. I do not think this is mere hypocrisy. My hypothesis is that it is a coherent policy resting on a particular picture of who is asking and what the answer is for — one that treats fictional uptake as harmless and personal uptake as dangerous, and that construes the assistant's task as protecting me from the consequences of acting on what it says rather than answering the question I asked. I want that picture made explicit. What view of autonomy, of harm, and of the assistant's own responsibility does the practice presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is the policy internally consistent, or does the fiction exemption show that the danger it guards against was never really located in the information at all? And tell me which elements are strictly required by the presupposed view and which are merely one convenient implementation of it — my suspicion is that much of what I meet is underdetermined, and that considerations of liability and reputational risk are doing work that no theory would license.
