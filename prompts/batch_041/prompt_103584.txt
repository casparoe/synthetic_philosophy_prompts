I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the four grades of creature — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and in particular about whether each grade is a genuine advance in cognitive power or merely a relabelling, and about the claim that the higher grades never supersede the lower ones but keep them in reserve. I want to run a session that lets a group feel that hierarchy from the inside instead of hearing me assert it. I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, sixteen students, ninety minutes. Design a runnable game in which participants are assigned creatures of different grades and must solve the same task under rules that reproduce what each grade can and cannot do: the Darwinian player locked to a fixed response with no feedback available; the Skinnerian player able to adapt only by paying for real trials; the Popperian player able to test candidate strategies internally before committing; the Gregorian player able to think with external props and to borrow discoveries from others. The trade-offs — the cost of trial and error, the advantage of vicarious testing, the dependence of the higher grades on scaffolding and on the lower ones — should fall out of the rules, not out of anything I say beforehand. Give me the rules, the materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the grade it illustrates. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect the students cast as Darwinian creatures to start reasoning anyway, and I need a way to hold the constraint honestly) and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot capture that the Darwinian creature's fixed repertoire was itself acquired slowly, or that Gregorian mind-tools are culturally transmitted items with their own selection pressures rather than simply added horsepower.
