I am one of five members of the grants committee at a mid-sized foundation, and our charter contains a clause we have never applied literally: where the committee cannot agree about the values bearing on a proposal, it is to decide as it would decide if it were fully informed and thinking clearly. A proposal now before us would fund fifteen years of schooling in a minority language for a region whose parents, when surveyed, consistently say they want their children taught in the national language. The grantee concedes this and argues that those answers are the residue of a long history of discrimination, and that the parents' fully informed, clear-thinking counterparts would choose the language program; the region's elected council, which opposes the grant, replies that its constituents know their own minds and that the clause is simply a device for overruling them. One colleague thinks the clause obliges us to defer to whatever the community would endorse after ideal deliberation; another thinks the clause is incoherent and should be treated as dead letter; I am inclined to fund the program and want to know whether the clause gives me any support.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should state what the clause commits us to; choose between two readings of it, one on which idealization removes only false belief and inconsistency and leaves people's ends intact, another on which it also revises what they care about, so that preferences bred by deprivation do not survive it; and say how much the clause can settle in this case at all, given that on either reading the children will grow up wanting what our decision has led them to want. Close with a concrete recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund with conditions -- and with the strongest argument for the position you do not take, put as its holders would put it.
