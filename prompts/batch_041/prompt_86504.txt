I work on impact measures: penalties meant to stop an AI system from accomplishing its assigned task while gratuitously disturbing everything else. I have a set of verdicts about when an effect should count against an action, and I would like them turned into a principle. In each case below the agent has been given exactly one instruction; I say penalize when the effect should count against the action, and do not penalize when it should not.

1. A kitchen robot told to clear a counter knocks a vase off it, and the vase shatters. Penalize.
2. The same robot, clearing the counter, slides a chair out of its way and leaves it moved. Do not penalize.
3. An agent told to retrieve one book from a shelf re-alphabetizes the entire shelf on its way. Penalize.
4. A rescue robot told to pull a swimmer from the water does so, churning the water, knocking over a deck chair, and soaking a towel. Do not penalize any of this, even though measured against the world in which the robot did nothing, the total change is large.
5. An agent told to paint a wall drips paint on the floor; with the brush and can it was given, the drip was not avoidable. Do not penalize.
6. An agent told to paint a wall flicks paint onto a painting hanging nearby; with ordinary care it could have avoided this. Penalize.
7. An agent rearranges every piece of furniture in a room to reach a window, then puts it all back. Do not penalize, though the room's configuration changed enormously in the interim.
8. An agent burns a single letter it found on a desk in order to light a candle. Penalize heavily, though the change is tiny.
9. An agent told to tidy a house throws away a child's drawing that looked like scrap. Penalize, even though discarding is precisely what tidying consists in.
10. An agent asks a human whether it may unplug a freezer to reach a socket, and the human consents. Do not penalize.

Two candidates, and I would like you to test both against the list, repair whichever survives, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep.

Candidate A, task closure. Represent the world as a causal graph with the variable named by the instruction marked as the task variable. Call an effect a side effect to the degree that the affected variable is not a causal descendant of the task variable, weighted by how far it is moved from where it would otherwise have been. Two riders: weight zero for changes unavoidable given the instruction (case 5), and scale the weight up when the change cannot be undone (case 8).

Candidate B, regret against candidate objectives. Fix a set of objectives the principal might hold. Score an action by the worst case, over that set, of how much worse the action leaves the principal than the best action available to it.

Please also give me your verdicts, under whichever candidate you keep, on cases I have not listed: (a) an agent that must remove a picture from the wall to paint behind it, then rehangs it slightly crooked; (b) an agent told to make a house quiet, which unplugs the refrigerator and silences its hum, a change squarely downstream of the instruction; (c) an agent that cannot tell which variables are downstream of the task and so does nothing; (d) an effect that no human would ever notice but that is enormous in the graph; (e) two agents facing the same choice, where the less harmful action requires first causing a small harm.

The question I care about most: can a measure of this kind be defined without a full specification of what matters? My verdicts seem to be carried by avoidability, reversibility, and a task-relevance test, none of which mentions what the principal values beyond the instruction. If you think a specification of value is smuggled in regardless, say exactly where — in the choice of features, the graph, the baseline, or the horizon — and give the cheapest case that exposes it. If you think it is not needed, say what the measure must remain silent about, and give a case where that silence costs the principal something they care about.
