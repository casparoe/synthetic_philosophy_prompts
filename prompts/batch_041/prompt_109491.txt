I'm the parliamentarian for a mid-sized professional society (roughly 400 voters, two business meetings a year), and every other year somebody proposes a new way of picking a chair, a conference site, a prize winner — usually right after the last method got gamed. I want a checklist I can run on a proposed rule before we adopt it, built on what social choice theory actually proves about strategy-proofness: is this rule manipulable, who gains by lying, what do they have to know about everyone else to pull it off, and what should we do about it. It has to be usable by a non-economist in an afternoon, and it has to be willing to tell the committee "any rule with three or more live options can be gamed; heres the price of each fix" instead of pretending theres a clean answer. Run it on three proposals: (a) Borda count over four candidates, where two members claim they swung the last election by burying the front-runner; (b) instant runoff with three candidates; (c) what the committee actually wants, a two-round majority where the nominating committee can drop a candidate in after the first round. At least one of these should end with the procedure telling me something I dont like, and it should say where it stops deciding for us because the rest is values. Also flag the cases where we're fine: two options only, or when the options line up along one axis (our site votes basically do).
