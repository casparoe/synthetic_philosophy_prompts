I am preparing a long-form interview for a political theory series, and my guest's recent book defends an interpretive thesis about John Rawls that I want the conversation to test rather than publicise. The thesis is a continuity claim: that the political liberalism of Rawls's maturity is a descendant of the intensely religious outlook of his student years — the undergraduate thesis in theology, the serious prospect of a religious vocation — and that the familiar picture of a young believer who grew into a secular liberal overstates the break. On this reading, Rawls's insistence that his account is political rather than metaphysical is an architectonic and strategic move rather than a renunciation of the earlier outlook.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. They should be pitched at someone who knows the corpus well, but nothing should turn on page references or on tracking down a passage, since the audience will not have the texts at hand. For every question, add the answer the guest is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer, so that the exchange has somewhere to go; each question should be long enough to carry its presuppositions, and answerable in a few minutes of speech. No question should merely invite him to summarise the book.

I want at least one question on each of the following:

- the parallels he treats as load-bearing between the early theology and the later theory, with pressure on whether these are resemblances of substance or merely of inherited vocabulary, and on what would settle the difference;
- the requirement that a religious argument eventually be backed by a public justification — what exactly it demands of believers, whether it falls on them more heavily than on secular citizens, and why it is religious speech above all that gets policed in practice;
- whether political liberalism is itself a comprehensive doctrine in disguise, or at least committed to a contested conception of the person and of reasonableness, and what the reply that the view is freestanding really secures;
- the standing of religious doctrines inside an overlapping consensus: must a believer be able to affirm the political conception from within her own tradition, and what becomes of those whose tradition cannot;
- the evidential weight of biography — what follows, if anything, for the truth of the political philosophy from the fact that its author lost his faith during the war;
- and, to close, what evidence could count against the continuity thesis, so that he cannot answer by restating it more emphatically.

If it suits the guest's work, work in one question about the alternative that assigns the burden of translation to the state rather than to citizens, and ask him to say what his own placement of that burden buys.
