In a seminar on distributive justice, a colleague of mine keeps making a move I would like to see examined properly rather than merely denied. His claim is that Rawls's veil of ignorance is, at bottom, a lottery: the parties behind the veil are best modelled as choosing under risk, each with an equal chance of turning out to be any member of the society. Grant that, he says, and the rest follows. The rational choice is the one that maximizes expected welfare, and Harsanyi's aggregation theorem supplies the formal result, on which the social welfare function is a sum of individual utilities with equal weights. The difference principle, on this view, is what one gets only if the parties are irrationally timid about a lottery whose odds are equal by construction.

What I want is an audit of that move, not a restatement of the Rawls-Harsanyi dispute, and I would like both positions stated at their strongest, since the audit is only worth anything if the analogy is given its best form. First, give me the correspondence: what plays the role of the randomizing device, the prizes, the probabilities, the chooser, and the decision rule, and what in Harsanyi's derivation answers to each of these. Then say where the mapping breaks, and, more importantly, sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion he wants and those that are merely decorative, so that I can see whether the argument survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

Three things seem to be carrying the weight, and I would like each pressed: the equal-probability assumption (what becomes of the result when the probabilities are unequal, or simply undefined, as they would be if the parties faced uncertainty rather than risk); the assumption that a single chooser's preferences over lotteries can stand in for the social preference, which is what makes the aggregation go through at all; and the fact that the lottery is drawn before anyone knows who they are, so that what gets aggregated is expected rather than realized welfare. If there is a better way to state what the veil does for Harsanyi -- one that preserves whatever is defensible in the lottery picture and discards what is not -- I would like that as well. No citations are needed; I am after the structure of the argument.
