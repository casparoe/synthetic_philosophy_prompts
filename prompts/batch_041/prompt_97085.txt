My book club is reading Rousseaus attack on the theatre — the letter where he begs Geneva not to build a playhouse — and I made the mistake of defending the gist of it. Now I'm stuck with an argument I can't fault and can't accept. Here it is, as I laid it out for them.

(1) A play works by making you feel things for people who aren't there: pity, contempt, love, outrage.
(2) The feeling you spend on a character is feeling you don't spend on anyone real, so you leave the theatre morally moved and morally spent, having actually done nothing.
(3) And the playwright shapes those feelings to please the crowd that's paying, so the stage always flatters its audience — it trains you to admire the charming and laugh at the awkward, which is the reverse of what a decent person needs.
(4) Anything that spends your moral feeling on fictions and flatters the tastes you already have is bad for you.
(5) So the theatre is bad for you, and a city that cares about its people shouldn't have one.

I can see (5) is where I'm meant to flinch, but every time I try to reject a premise the trouble just moves. Reject (2) and I've also condemned the novel, the film, the bedtime story. Reject (4) and I've let in history, biography, and any sermon that works by making you picture a scene. And if the real complaint is just that the playwright flatters the crowd, then so does the novelist, the newspaper, and the man in the pub with a full room and a good story. Rousseau says: have festivals and public dancing instead of a theatre. But a festival is a spectacle built to move a crowd too, and it flatters the crowd just as much — so by his own lights he should be banning that as well.

So where does it go wrong? Which step do I give up, and what do I pay for giving it up? I'd sooner keep (1) and (2) then (4) if that helps, but I want to know what that costs me.
