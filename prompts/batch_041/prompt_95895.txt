I'm writing a dissertation prospectus (philosophy, so call it a year of full-time work) and I want to pitch something on internal vs. external reasons, but not the usual "does the sound-deliberation test rule out too much" argument that everyone runs. Here's my half-formed idea and I'd like you to turn it into the prospectus. Almost the whole debate is about a reason someone has *right now*, relative to the motivational set they already have. I think the interesting cases are ones where the reason is a reason to *become a different agent* — to acquire a whole new motivational set. Getting sober, falling in love, converting to a religion, deciding to become a parent, taking up a vocation. Call these conversion reasons, for now. My hunch is they don't fit the dichotomy cleanly. If the reason has to be internal, it looks like it has to be grounded in the very set you're trying to leave, which is bizarre (the drinker's reason to stop grounded in his wanting to drink?); if it's external, the internalist loses a big class of cases she wanted, and the externalist owes us an account of how an external reason could even move someone whose motivational set is exactly what stands in the way. I want the prospetus to (1) state the question so it has a determinate answer — something like, is there a class of reasons that is neither internal nor external in the standard senses, and if so what is it? — (2) explain why the existing positions leave this open rather than settling it, without a literature review by citation (I'll fill in citations myself), (3) say what the method actually is, chapter by chapter, (4) say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and (5) be honest about scope: is this a year or is it a career? Also flag the one or two spots where I might just be redescribing akrasia or the transformative-experience stuff, and say what would actually be new there. American English please.
