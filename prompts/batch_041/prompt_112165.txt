Write an in-universe formal document — a notice of non-compliance issued by a fictional regulatory office to two parents who, in selecting among their embryos, did not select the one whose life was expected to go best — of roughly 750–900 words, formatted as real administrative correspondence: a letterhead, a file reference, a salutation, numbered paragraphs, one appended schedule, a closing and a signature. You may invent the office, its jurisdiction, the parents, the child, and the index the office uses. The register should be precise, faintly stilted, and unfailingly polite.

The office administers the following position and takes it to be both true and humane: when prospective parents have decided to have a child, and can select among the children they could have had, they have a significant moral reason — the office's own regulations treat it as a duty — to select the child expected to have the best life, in light of the relevant available information. Draw the office as neither villainous nor stupid. It is patient, well-informed, courteous, and, from its own premises, right. It never winks at the reader, never moralises, and never doubts its mandate. The discomfort must be produced by the reader's inference, not by the document's rhetoric.

The piece should be deadpan throughout, funny to someone who knows this literature and legible to someone who does not. Do not mention real philosophers, works, studies, or institutions; do not compare anyone to twentieth-century eugenics programmes; do not reach for Huxley or Orwell or any dystopian novel. The target is a position and a style of reasoning, not a person.

Structural requirements:

1. The finding. State what the parents did and the ground on which they did it. Their reason should be one a reader will find decent, and the office should restate it in its strongest form, weigh it respectfully, and set it aside.

2. The spine. The office should locate the hedge in the principle — that it applies only to the options the reproducers actually have, only on the information actually available, and only on the assumption that other considerations do not outweigh it — and then argue, step by step and without fallacy, that the hedge cannot be cashed out: either it does no work at all, or it swallows the whole. This argument should carry the piece. A specialist should be unable to convict the office of a mistake; that is the joke.

3. The schedule. Append a list of the traits the office's index scores, running from serious disease at one end to entries at the other end that the reader will find, in turn, uncomfortable and absurd. The office must treat every entry in the same tone, giving no sign that it knows one is different from another. At least one entry should be defended on the ground that the trait is a general-purpose means, useful in the pursuit of whatever plan of life the child turns out to have, and that defence should receive the office's usual scrupulous care.

4. The parents' defence, quoted. Quote a passage from the parents' own written submission. It must be the strongest version of the objection available: that the child they had could not have existed otherwise, that it therefore cannot have been made worse off, and that no one was harmed by the choice. The office should grant every step of this and show why it strengthens rather than weakens the case for the finding. The reader should feel the machinery turn at exactly this point.

5. The child. A short paragraph on the interest the office takes the child to have in a future that has not already been settled for it, and on why the office believes its own intervention is compatible with that interest. Leave this uncomfortable rather than resolved.

6. The remedy. Set out an appeal mechanism fairly and accurately — a deadline, the permitted grounds, a fee — and let the reader see, without the document's ever acknowledging it, that no appeal can succeed.

7. The expansion. Close by announcing an extension of the office's remit in the same even tone as the rest: a new class of traits, or a new population of reproducers, or a lower evidential threshold for acting.

Constraints: any figures the document gives — index scores, probabilities, differences between embryos — must be internally consistent if a reader checks them. The office must never misstate a philosophical point, only follow one. The reader should finish believing that the office is right about everything except the premise it never examines, and should not be told which premise that is.
