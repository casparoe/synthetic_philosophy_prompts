I'm a novelist — historical fiction, mostly — and I've gotten into a running argument with a couple of historian friends that I can't seem to win or lose, and it's starting to bug me more than it should. Maybe you can tell me what kind of fight it actually is.

It started because I got excited about the cliodynamics stuff — Peter Turchin and the secular cycle idea, where societies run through long integrative phases and then disintegrative ones, driven by things like elite overproduction (too many credentialed people chasing too few elite slots) and popular immiseration, and you supposedly get a rough 200-300 year rhythm with a crisis at the end. I thought great, this gives me a spine for a novel: I can set my characters at the top of a disintegrative phase and let the structure do some of the work. Then I mentioned it to my friends and got the eye-roll. One of them said it's just drawing the target around the arrow after it lands. Another said sure, if your theory is vague enough to cover Rome, Song China, and the US in the 2020s, it's too vague to say anything about my decade.

So here's roughly where the two sides sit, as I understand it. The quantifiers say: we build models, we fit them to lots of societies, we do out-of-sample checks and retrodiction, and we've flagged real instability in specific places before it happened, so the naysayers are just doing anecdote. The historians say: your variables are fuzzy (who exactly counts as an elite? how do you measure immiseration across a thousand years?), the coding calls are subjective, the cycles only look clean after you've smoothed and binned the data, and a model that fits everything explains nothing. Neither side seems to be lying about the facts. They just keep talking past each other.

My hunch — and I want you to tell me if it's wrong — is that this isn't really a disagreement about the evidence at all. I suspect they're using different standards for what counts as a test: the quants want an ensemble-level statistical fit, the historians want a mechanism that would convince you in a single case you can walk through step by step. If that's right, then they're not even answering the same question — one is asking whether history has a shape you can predict forward, the other whether you can explain a particular collapse convincingly in hindsight — and the word "predict" is doing a lot of quiet work.

So: diagnose it for me. Is this a substantive dispute, a standards-of-evidence dispute, two different questions wearing one name, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a formal result, a pre-registered prediction, a distinction between predicting the frequency of crises and predicting a particular one — or should I just accept that "is history predictable" is a badly posed question and go back to writing my book? I'd rather have the diagnosis than a reading list, by the way; I'm not going to read the whole field, I just want to understand the shape of the thing.
