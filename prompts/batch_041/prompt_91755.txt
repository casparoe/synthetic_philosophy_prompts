I sit on the content committee for a mid-sized science museum that's building a permanent exhibit on thinking machines — a Babbage engine, a small room on neural nets, the usual can-machines-think payoff at the end. Our exhibit developer has drafted a wall panel that more or less declares the question settled by Gödel: for any consistent formal system there's a true statement it can't prove, a human mathematician can nonetheless see that the statement is true, so the human mind isn't that system, and since every computer is some such system, no computer will ever do everything a mind does. She loves it because visitors walk away with a clean, dramatic answer, and honestly it reads well on the wall. But I'm uneasy about putting a contested philosophical thesis up as if it were a theorem, and two colleagues agree — one of them wants to cut the panel entirely and not touch the question at all, on the grounds that we'll mislead people no matter which way we go. I have to write the committee's decision memo before we meet Friday, and the label text has to be locked by the end of the month because the fabricator is already on a schedule.

I should admit I'm not a philosopher — a couple of undergrad courses and I keep getting tangled in the details. When I push back, the developer says the human just sees that the Gödel statement is true, and that's exactly the step I don't trust; a friend who teaches philosophy of mind told me the argument quietly assumes the human knows the system is consistent, and that it only ever rules out one machine at a time rather than machines in general. But she also said there's a serious version of the argument and that I shouldn't just wave it away, and I don't want to be the person who kills a good exhibit because I couldn't follow the logic. I gather the whole line is associated with Lucas and Penrose, though I couldn't tell you where one stops and the other starts.

So please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before the meeting: recommend a concrete course of action — keep the panel as drafted, cut it, or run it only under conditions you spell out — with a timeframe that fits our deadline, and reasons my colleagues could actually respond to rather than just object to. It has to engage the strongest version of the anti-mechanist argument seriously, not the strawman, and it has to give the developer's side its due even though I'm asking you to land somewhere. Take a definite position; don't just lay out the options and leave the call to me, I need a bottom line I can defend in the room. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
