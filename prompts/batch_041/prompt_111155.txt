I'm a TA for a third-year course on free will and the criminal law, and I've got to build a one-page decision tree the students can use to figure out where they actually stand on just deserts. The two obvious poles are Dennett and Caruso: Dennett, the compatibilist, thinks a robust notion of desert survives once we stop hankering after libertarian free will; Caruso, the hard incompatibilist, thinks nobody ever deserves to suffer for what they've done, though he's perfectly happy with forward-looking sanctions that reduce crime. I don't want the handout to be a two-hander, though — I want the tree built from the logical space first and the names second, so a student who turns out to be a semi-compatibilist (responsibility doesn't need alternate possibilities), an illusionist (the retributive beliefs are false but worth keeping), a revisionist (the concept of responsibility should be reformed rather than junked), or something with no name yet can still find themselves on it. So please don't list the famous positions and hang questions off them; go the other way round, questions first, then see which combinations of answers correspond to which actual views, and where the empty cells are.

The distinctions I want the branches to turn on, roughly in the order I think they come apart: whether basic desert is even a coherent notion, or whether every desert claim is really a claim about entitlement, forfeiture, or convention; whether moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise or only reasons-responsiveness of the right kind; whether the justification of a punishment practice is exhausted by its consequences; whether the fittingness of suffering to wrongdoing adds anything over and above deterrence and incapacitation; whether the distinction between blaming someone and merely holding them responsible does any real work here; and whether any of this should change what we do on Monday morning, or is just a fact about how we ought to talk. I'm especially keen on the desert-as-fittingness versus desert-as-forfeiture split, because I think a lot of students collapse those two, and on the question whether an error theory about desert leaves our punishment practices untouched. Every branch node has to be phrased as something a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to — no question whose answer is just the name of a position.

Give me eight-ish branch nodes and somewhere between eight and twelve leaves, each leaf carrying a name (or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Two cases I want the tree to handle: the offender whose childhood was so awful that it's hard to see what else he could have become, and the victim who genuinely wants the offender to suffer and couldn't care less about deterrence. Also flag any leaves the tree can't seperate — I suspect hard incompatibilism and Caruso's own version are going to be hard to pull apart, and I'd rather you say so than fake a distinction — and diagnose the empty cells: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to explore it?
