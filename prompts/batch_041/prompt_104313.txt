I've been reading around antinatalism lately, and a friend of mine who's finishing a master's in philosophy offered me a summary of David Benatar's asymmetry that I've been turning over ever since, because I can't tell whether it's a fair account or a garbled one. Her version runs roughly like this. Benatar's central claim, she says, is that pleasure and pain are asymmetric — that the bad in a life simply outweighs the good, that pain is heavier than pleasure — and this is the asymmetry he's famous for. On that basis he lays out four claims: that the presence of pain is bad, that the absence of pain is good, that the presence of pleasure is good, and that the absence of pleasure is not bad. Because the absence of pain counts as good even when there is no one around to enjoy it, whereas the absence of pleasure only counts as bad if there is someone who is being deprived of it, it follows that non-existence is a condition a person can be in, and that it is better for that person than existence. So the asymmetry, on her reading, amounts to the claim that each of us would have been better off never having been born; and since Benatar is supposed to regard the asymmetry as a conceptual truth that no reasonable person can deny, the whole position stands or falls on whether you can stomach where it leads — which, she adds, is why anyone who accepts it should also conclude that their own life is not worth living and should be indifferent to their own death.

I suspect this is a garbled rendering — I have a vague memory that Benatar is careful to separate the question whether a life is worth starting from the question whether it is worth continuing, and that he is wary of treating non-existence as a state anyone is in — but she is a much more careful reader than I am and I don't trust my memory against hers. Could you take her summary apart sentence by sentence? I'd like to know which parts Benatar would accept, which are the standard simplifications a popularizer makes, and which are flatly wrong. In particular, I want to understand whether the asymmetry is even meant as a claim about the relative weight of pleasure and pain, whether the move to "non-existence is better for the person" is licensed by the argument or is exactly what the argument is constructed to avoid, and whether the conclusion about suicide and a life not worth living follows from anything Benatar actually holds. Please be explicit about which of her sentences survive and which don't, and tell me what would change your mind: if there is a defensible reading on which her summary comes out substantially correct after all, I'd want to know which textual or philosophical consideration would make you revise the audit you give me.
