ok so my bioethics seminar has this final project where we have to take one of the course units and turn it into something a real practitioner could actually use, and I got the unit on whether genuine moral dilemmas are even possible. I picked it because it wound me up — half the class kept saying "obviously dilemmas exist, look at this case" and the prof kept going "not so fast, you might just not know which one wins." By the end I couldn't tell if they were disagreeing about ethics or about what the word even means, and I still can't, so now I have to build something out of it.

What I have to hand in is a decision procedure that a hospital ethics consult service could actually run when someone pages them and says "we have a dilemma." Not an essay — an intake triage thing. The people running it are clinicians with zero philosophy training plus maybe one ethicist who is not always around, they get maybe three to five of these a week, and usually someone needs an answer within a few hours, sometimes minutes. So it has to be runnable by a smart non-philosopher, and for every step it has to say what counts as a good enough answer before you're allowed to move on.

The whole point of it is to seperate real dilemmas from the stuff that just looks like one. So the first thing it should do is figure out whether the "dilemma" is a problem of not knowing which option is right (which is solvable in principle, so go get the info) or a problem of there being no right option (which isn't). Then it should test whether both options are actually required of you or only permitted, whether the situation could have been avoided upstream by better planning, and what is still owed afterward — because even when you pick the lesser evil I think something is still owed to whoever got the short end, and the procedure should not pretend the decision settled that. I also want it to force you to state whether you could actually have intended the bad outcome on the losing side, and not just tolerated it, because that feels like the line between doing something wrong and merely letting it happen.

Where it can't get an answer, I want it to say so and hand the thing to a person, not fudge it.

Then run it on two cases. Case one: an ICU has one ECMO circuit and two patients who both need it, one young and previously healthy, one older with other conditions, and the team genuinely can't find a rule that doesn't feel like it's just picking. Case two: a ward is short a nurse, a patient gets a med late and is harmed, and everyone is calling it a tragic unavoidable dilemma, but I suspect your procedure will say it isn't a dilemma at all — it's a staffing failure somebody could have prevented. That second one is the answer I don't want, so please don't soften it for me.

Format it as:

"What this is and isn't for" — about 100 words, just framing.
"The procedure, step by step" — about 700 words, numbered steps, and under each step one line on what counts as an answer good enough to move on.
"Two cases run through" — about 400 words.
"Where it stops, and where I'm fooling myself" — about 150 words.

And in that last section, be straight with me about what a procedure like this definately can't do, because I have to present this and I'd rather know the holes now than get eaten alive in Q&A. Also no reading list or citations please — the prof said she doesn't want us hiding behind other people's names, I just need the procedure itself.
