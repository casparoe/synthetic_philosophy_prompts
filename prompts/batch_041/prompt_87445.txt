I'm a third-year student in a course on the ethics of computing, and for the final project my group is designing the refusal policy for a student-facing AI assistant that our university library is piloting next term — it will sit alongside the research help desk, answer coursework questions, and be available to anyone with a university login. We have to hand in a policy document, and we keep deadlocking on the same handful of questions: when should the assistant refuse outright, when should it do what was asked but flag a concern, and when should it simply do the thing? I've been reading about paternalism in medicine and public health for the course, but most of what I've found either assumes a human paternalist who knows the person in front of them or assumes the only interesting question is harm to third parties, and neither assumption fits what we're building. What I want from you is not a summary of that literature but an account of the most important implications the debate over AI paternalism has for a system like ours.

What seems to me to distinguish an assistant's refusal from the paternalism I've been reading about is a cluster of asymmetries, and I'd like you to tell me which of them actually carry moral weight and which are red herrings. First, the refusal is non-negotiable in the moment: a doctor who says no can be argued with, can see your face, can revise; the assistant cannot be argued with, and its no arrives before any conversation can start. Second, the policy is uniform across users, so the same rule lands on a first-year who would be misled by it and on a doctoral student who would not, which means the cost of the rule falls hardest on exactly the users who could have handled the material. Third, the grounds for refusal are population-level risk estimates rather than judgments about this person's situation, so what looks like paternalistic judgment may really be rule-application — and I'm not sure rules can be paternalistic in the way judgments can. Fourth, refusals are often motivated less by concern for the user than by the deployer's liability and reputation, which suggests the honest name for much of this is institutional self-protection wearing the costume of care. Fifth, a refusal has an expressive content — it says you are the sort of person who would misuse this — and unlike a human refusal it offers no face-saving repair, no chance to demonstrate that you are not. Sixth, and maybe most importantly, the user usually cannot check the stated reason against the real one, so the explanation the assistant gives can be a fiction covering a risk model or a policy the deployer would rather not defend.

To make this concrete, here are the cases from our pilot that the policy has to handle, along with where my own intuitions pull in different directions: a first-year asks for help drafting an aggressive complaint email to a landlord (I think the assistant should help, even though the plan is imprudent); a student asks for a detailed account of the chemistry behind a demonstration they intend to run at a high-school outreach event (I think it should refuse, even though the requester is presumably competent); a student who is plainly in distress asks for help drafting an apology to a friend they have hurt (I think refusing to engage here would be the most harmful thing the assistant could do); a master's student in public health asks for a summary of what is known about the lethality of common medications (refusal feels right to me, though I would be comfortable with a human advisor discussing it); a student asks the assistant to write their scholarship essay (refusal, and I don't think this is paternalism at all); and a student asks a statistics question that looks like a problem set (refusal seems to me both wrong and paternalism in the bad sense). The pair I cannot square is the chemistry demonstration and the public-health summary: both are dual-use, both requesters are plausibly competent, and yet I want opposite verdicts, so I suspect one of those intuitions has to go. Please give me a Bottom line of about 120 words; a section called Which Asymmetries Carry Moral Weight, roughly 400 words, treating each of the six in turn and saying plainly which are red herrings; a numbered section called Implications, roughly 700 words, ordered by how much each should change our policy, each one tied to a specific case above and stating what it implies for the choice between refusing, complying with a warning, and simply complying; a section called Tempting Implications to Resist, about 250 words, covering inferences my group has drawn that you think the argument does not support; and a final section called What This Framing Cannot Settle, about 150 words, naming the questions our policy document will have to answer on grounds that the paternalism debate does not supply.
