Here's the half-baked idea. The necessity-of-origin thesis — the Kripke-ish line that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg, that an individual couldn't have had a different origin — gets tested almost entirely on cases like you and me and the Queen: organisms with a tidy geneological tree, one sperm, one egg, a determinate parent or two. But the thesis is supposed to be completely general — every individual has its actual origin essentially. Now take something that reproduces by splitting in two: an amoeba, or the fission case from the personal-identity literature. When it divides, there's famously no fact of the matter about which of the two resulting cells is "the same one" continuing and which is the offshoot — the standard move is to say identity just goes indeterminate or doesn't survive. But if there's no fact about which product is the continuer, then what exactly is the origin of the two products? Their origin looks like a shared, symmetric blob, not a determinate individual. So either necessity of origin quietly presupposes a determinate genealogical tree and isn't the fully general thesis it's advertised as, or we owe a principled story about when an origin is determinate enough to be essential — and I suspect that second option is a lot harder than it sounds.
