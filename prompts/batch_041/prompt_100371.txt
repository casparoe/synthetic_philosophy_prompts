I have no training in philosophy, but I've spent a lot of time thinking about how online communities go wrong, and I keep arguing about it with a friend of mine, Dana, who moderates a large discussion site. Dana is unusually consistent and has firm verdicts on which online spaces count as epistemic bubbles, which count as echo chambers, and which count as neither. Before I argue with them again, I'd like to figure out the rule Dana is actually running.

Here are the verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them.

- A model-railway forum where everyone shares the hobby and there is no real politics: the members mostly don't know that rival forums exist, there is no figure whose word settles disputes, and nobody suspects outsiders of lying. Dana calls it a bubble, and a harmless one, but insists it is not an echo chamber.
- A neighborhood chat group whose members all take their news from one small newsletter and are only dimly aware that other outlets exist; again there is no leader and no hostility toward outsiders. Dana calls it a bubble.
- A forum called Wake Up, whose members are told that the entire mainstream press is a coordinated deception and that anyone who cites it is a paid plant; the founder's posts are treated as the last word on any dispute. Dana calls it a textbook echo chamber.
- A rowdy debate forum where people hold a wide range of views, argue hard, defer to no shared authority, and assume their opponents are sincere. Dana says it is neither a bubble nor a chamber, just disagreement.

Next week Dana will visit a group I belong to, The Reading Room. We read across the whole political spectrum, so nobody could call us uninformed, and we have no suspicion that outsiders are lying or bought. But we do have a moderator whose readings of whatever we're discussing are treated as essentially settled; once he posts, the argument is over.

I want you to take the role of an expert philosopher of social epistemology and testimony, and to reason carefully and explicitly rather than giving me a quick answer. Begin by brainstorming the various rules that could have produced Dana's four verdicts — I would rather see several than one — and only then settle on a view.

Then tell me: what is Dana most likely to say about The Reading Room, and why? Which of the candidate rules is Dana actually running, which of the other rules fit the four earlier verdicts just as well, and which single further case would best tell those rules apart? If the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly, and tell me what principle Dana would be committing to under each reading, since I would rather know what I am agreeing to than win the argument.
