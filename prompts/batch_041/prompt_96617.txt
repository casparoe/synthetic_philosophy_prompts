I've been going back and forth with a friend in a reading group on ancient political philosophy, and we've gotten stuck on a question that is narrower than the usual one about Aristotle's defense of natural slavery. Neither of us is interested in whether he was right — we both assume he was not — nor in cataloguing the ways his views reflected the prejudices of his time. What we want is a verdict on an internal question: on Aristotle's own premises, is the thesis that some human beings are slaves by nature a genuine implication of his system, or is it a piece of fourth-century common sense that his metaphysics and psychology happen to tolerate rather than require? My friend's line is that it is entailed, and that the entailment runs through his teleological biology together with his faculty psychology: once you accept that natural kinds have fixed functions, that the deliberative faculty admits of degrees, and that ruling and being ruled is a natural relation ordered to the good of the ruled, you already have everything you need. I think there is more slack in the system than that — that a charitable reconstruction could keep the teleology and the hierarchy of faculties while treating the assignment of particular human beings to the slave class as a contingent (and false) empirical premise, and that his own concessions about the virtue of slaves and about friendship between master and slave pull against reading the doctrine as tightly entailed.
