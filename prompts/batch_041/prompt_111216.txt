I'm a software engineer by trade, with no training in linguistics or philosophy, but I write constrained verse as a hobby, and I've just worked my way through Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, a book that is largely dozens of English attempts at a single short Renaissance French poem. It sent me back to a project of my own: putting into English a tiny poem I'm fond of that has four constraints running at once. It rhymes, it scans, the first letters of its lines spell the name of the person it addresses, and it runs to an exact number of lines. I've made about twenty English versions by now, and the same thing keeps happening, and I don't have a word for it — I'm not even certain it's one thing rather than two.

Here is the pattern. The rhyme and the scansion are audible; anyone who hears my English version can tell that they're there. The acrostic is invisible unless you know to look down the left margin, and it is the constraint I keep losing. I have not managed to keep it and the sense at the same time, and in the versions I actually like it is simply gone. Worse, holding the rhyme forces a particular word, the word drags its associations in with it, those associations pull the following lines, and by the end my English poem is quietly asserting something the French does not — a small image, a faint note of reproach, that is now doing real work in the poem. The odd part is what happens when I show people. They prefer the drifted version, because it reads like a poem rather than a gloss, and nothing in it announces that a constraint has been dropped or a claim added; they take the added image to be the poet's, and I have caught myself taking it that way too. When I showed an early draft to a friend who then made her own translation, the image came back — she had inherited it from me while believing she was inheriting it from the French. I've since read about the famous French novel written entirely without the letter e, and the English version that also avoids it, where the constraint is preserved at the price of rewriting the whole book. That looks like the same difficulty approached from the other end, and it makes me suspect the thing I'm circling has to do with which constraints a reader can check for free.

What I want is not so much a name as a carving, and I don't need a reading list — just the analysis. Is there an established term for this: a translation's most visible formal success being precisely what conceals the loss, and the loss then being read back into the original as a feature of it? If there is, I'd like to know how it differs from the nearby ideas I do know — the general complaint that something always gets lost in translation, deliberate adaptation or imitation, and the thought that a translator makes up elsewhere for whatever was dropped. If there is no term, please coin one, define it, and mark its edges. In particular: is the dropping of the unseen constraint the same phenomenon as the adding of the unearned image, or are they two? Is it still the same thing when a translator drops a constraint on purpose and flags it in a note, or when the added image flatly contradicts the original? And does it extend to prose whose constraint is stylistic rather than countable?
