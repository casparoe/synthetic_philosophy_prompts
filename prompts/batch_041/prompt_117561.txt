My wife and I run a Sunday reading group — four retired couples, two engineers, one ex-physician, and me, a geologist who spent forty years squinting at seismic lines — and since January we've been grinding through Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia. I'd never read political philosophy before and I keep trying to turn it into a measurement problem, which doesnt really work. The Chamberlain example I get: fans drop money in a box at every game, he ends up with a fortune, and Nozick says the resulting distribution is just and nobody may confiscate it, because it came out of voluntary choices from a starting point that was itself legitimate. Fine. And I can get his other rulings off the same line — a tax that takes part of a person's earnings to fund redistribution he treats as morally on the same footing as making someone work for you; any principle that says the distribution has to match a pattern, equality or the difference principle, he says would have to be enforced by continuous meddling, so that transactions between consenting adults that spoil the pattern would have to be forbidden; on the proviso that appropriation is only legitimate when there's enough and as good left over for others, he treats it as a real constraint on how you may acquire a thing in the first place, though he's noticeably vague about how strong it is and seems unsure whether it's violated in particular cases; and he concedes that past injustice generates some claim to rectification, admits he has no theory of how to compute it, and hints it might license a good deal more redistribution then he'd be comfortable with. So that's five verdicts and I can produce all five from one sentence — whether the holdings came about by voluntary steps from holdings that were themselves legitimately acquired, with the resulting shape of the distribution counting for nothing. Whether that sentence is the whole theory or just a cheap summary of it, I can't tell.

Here's the case that's been keeping me up. A young man with no prospects and a sick mother signs a contract with a firm: the firm pays him a large lump sum up front, enough to clear the family's debts and then some, and in exchange he binds himself to work for the firm for the next twenty-five years. No force, no fraud, he read every clause, the deal is genuinely his best option, he's better off than he would be without it, and he says so. The clause that bothers me is the exit clause: if he stops showing up, the firm is entitled to compel him back to work, and there's no buy-out price. His labor, for twenty-five years, is the firm's to use, and it can use the law to make that stick. Is that a just holding on Nozick's account, and may the firm enforce it?

I can see two readings that fit everything in the first paragraph and split on this one. Reading A: justice is just the history. If he legitimately owns his own labor, then a voluntary transfer of it is a just transfer, full stop, and the state has no business voiding the contract or declining to enforce it — the pattern that results is nobody's business but the parties'. Reading B: the whole apparatus is built on the idea that people can't be used as means, and that's a side-constraint on what other people may do to you, not a thing you own and can hand over; so no contract can authorize the firm to treat him as property, the enforcement clause is itself the violation, and the deal is void no matter how cheerfully he signed it. What I'd like from you: your best guess at Nozick's verdict here and the reason for it; the candidate rules laid out, with what each predicts about this contract; which one you think fits his other verdicts better, and where the other one has to bend to fit them; and then the case that would actually settle it between them — one he'd plausibly have an opinion about, not a science-fiction hypo. And if the honest answer is that his track record genuinely underdetermines this, please say that instead of picking a side, and tell me which way the choice pushes the rest of the theory, because my hunch is that it decides whether he can condemn chattel slavery without quietly smuggling a pattern back in.
