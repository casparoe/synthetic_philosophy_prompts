I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've run into a problem I'd like your help with. The unit I'm preparing covers what's usually described as the strange inversion that Darwin and Turing each worked on the ordinary order of explanation — the move from supposing that comprehension is what produces competence to the thought that competence can come apart from comprehension altogether, so that a system can reliably do the right thing without anything anywhere understanding why. My students are past the stage of needing a tour of famous positions; they already accept the broad evolutionary and computational picture and now want to know where they themselves actually stand. So what I need from you is a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey: a decision tree that starts from a commitment nearly all of them will grant and leads, through a series of questions, to a definite verdict about which position they hold.

Build the tree from the logical space first and only then locate the positions inside it. The root should be something like "a system reliably produces competent outputs across a range of situations, and nothing in the system understands why" — phrased so a student can assent or dissent without yet knowing which label it commits them to. Every subsequent node should be a single question on which the candidate positions genuinely diverge, expressed as a concrete case or claim rather than as a doctrinal label. Three distinctions should do most of the work, because they are the ones that matter for my purposes: whether the difference between competence and comprehension is located in the system's causal history (how it was selected, trained, or learned) or in its functional organization (the algorithm or machine table it implements); whether understanding is a matter of degree or is all-or-nothing, and whether attributions of understanding report a fact about the system or express a stance we adopt toward it; and whether there is a fact of the matter about what the system is really doing or whether that is observer-relative. At least these positions should appear as leaves — the view that comprehension is itself just a further layer of competence, so the inversion runs all the way down; the view that formal symbol manipulation, however competent, can never amount to understanding; the instrumentalist or stance-dependent view; the embodied or enactive view on which genuine understanding requires a certain kind of bodily coping; the classical representationalist view; the connectionist view; and the deflationary view that the whole distinction is one of degree with no sharp line. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag the pair rather than inventing a distinction, and where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

I'd like a tree of roughly a dozen nodes and eight to twelve leaves, plus a short closing note. Keep the questions answerable by someone who has the intuitions but not the vocabulary, and don't hedge: commit to one tree with definite branch points and definite leaf assignments, and give your reasons wherever a branch is contestable. In the closing note, tell me where the instrument leans on a contested reading — for instance, if it assumes that understanding names a natural kind, or that reasons must in principle be representable — so that I can warn the class before they fill it in.
