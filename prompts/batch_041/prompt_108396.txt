I am writing a seminar paper on the metaphysics of chance, and I have talked myself into thinking that the Principal Principle — the one that is supposed to tell us to defer to objective chance — cannot be right. As I understand it, the principle says that a rational agent's credence in a proposition ought to equal that proposition's objective chance. But credence and chance come apart constantly. Suppose a friend shuffles a standard deck and deals me a single card face down. The objective chance that my card is a heart is one in four; that is a fact about the shuffle, not about me. I then lift the corner and see that it is the seven of hearts, and my credence that the card is a heart becomes one. The shuffle has not changed, so by my reckoning the chance is still one in four. The principle, though, tells me to match my credence to the chance, which leaves me with two options I cannot accept: either I am irrational for believing what is plainly in front of me, or the chance itself shifted the instant I looked, in which case chance is a fact about my information rather than about the world.

Someone in the seminar suggested that the principle is only meant to govern "admissible" evidence, and that peeking has given me inadmissible evidence. That strikes me as a dodge. As far as I can make out, admissible evidence is characterized by the very role it plays in the principle — it is the evidence that leaves the chance-credence link intact — so the restriction is defined in a circle, and any case that threatens the principle is simply relabelled and set aside. A friend then proposed that I read the principle as concerning the chance of the outcome given my evidence rather than the chance fixed by the setup, but I cannot see how that reading does not simply identify the chance with whatever it is rational for me to believe. If the principle is either empty or false, and if the only thing left for "chance" to name is our credence under another description, I am inclined to conclude that there is no such thing as objective chance at all.

I would like someone to sort out where, if anywhere, I have gone wrong. I want to know whether the card case is a genuine counterexample to the principle or a case it was never intended to cover, what work the notion of admissibility does that is not circular, and whether the move from "my credence came apart from the chance" to "there is no objective chance" is legitimate. If the principle does survive, I would also like to understand what it actually constrains, since it plainly does not tell me to disbelieve my own eyes.
