You are a professional philosopher working in ethics and the philosophy of the family, and you are also an experienced writer of occasional speeches. I would like you to write me a wedding toast. Please think carefully before you begin, and start by brainstorming on the page: what this particular room needs, which one or two concrete images could carry the idea sketched below, and which of the standard wedding-toast moves you intend to avoid.

The occasion: my oldest friend, Nadia, whom I met in graduate school, is marrying her partner, Robin. I am giving one of the toasts. The room is about eighty people and genuinely mixed. One side's parents are devout and were opposed to the marriage when it was announced; they are present, and I do not want them handled, managed, or addressed as a problem to be solved. A table of our former colleagues are professional philosophers and will notice any argument I fumble. Everyone else — the rest of both families, Robin's colleagues from the ambulance service, the couple's friends — is not academic at all and has no interest in being lectured. The marriage is possible because the law changed fairly recently; that fact should be present in the room's awareness without being argued, since no one here needs persuading.

Here is the view I would like the toast to embody, and I would rather it never be stated as a thesis:

I think we routinely conflate two different questions. One is why these two particular people are marrying; the other is what the institution of marriage is *for*. Most toasts answer the first and quietly pretend they have answered the second. My own answer to the second: what marriage is for is the creation of a promise whose content is deliberately left blank — a promise made not only to the person standing next to you, but to a person who does not exist yet, namely whoever each of you will have become in twenty years. On this view the openness of the marital promise is not a defect to be patched with vows that spell everything out; the openness is the point. Marriage is the device by which two people are made answerable to futures they cannot yet describe. And I take it that this is precisely why widening the institution to couples like Nadia and Robin is not a change in what marriage is for but the clearest possible demonstration of it: an institution whose content is made rather than found can be made again.

Constraints, all of which matter to me:

- Do not name or allude to any philosopher or text, and do not cite anything; I will handle any of that myself if it is ever needed.
- Keep the vocabulary ordinary. No jargon — nothing like teleology, social construction, speech act, performative, constitutive. If an idea cannot survive in plain words it does not belong in a toast.
- Let the view do structural work rather than appear as decoration: it should determine what the toast can and cannot say about the couple, and it should shape the ending.
- Warm, with one or two genuine laughs, and it must land as affection rather than as an argument won. Avoid the stock moves: the journey, the two halves, the "I knew from the moment," the dictionary definition.
- It has to be sayable by me — a philosopher — without my wincing at anything false or saccharine, and it has to hold the attention of a table of paramedics.
- About four minutes spoken, ending in the toast proper.

If you think my view is confused, overstated, or would make this room bristle for reasons I have not anticipated, say so briefly and adjust rather than forcing it. After the toast, add two or three sentences telling me what you would cut first if it runs long, and what you deliberately left out.
