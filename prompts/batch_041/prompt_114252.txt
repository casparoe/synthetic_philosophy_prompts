I'm a second-year PhD student in a classics-adjacent philosophy programme, currently trying to write a chapter on the consolatory argument in Cicero's Cato Maior — mostly the opening stretch where Cato has to explain to Laelius and Scipio why old age isn't a burden for him. I'm stuck on one sentence in ch. 5 and I'd rather have it unpacked properly than keep nodding along at it in the Loeb. The run-up, as I take it: Cato has said that people who have nothing in themselves for living well find every age heavy; that everyone longs to arrive at old age and then complains once they've got there; and that the wise man follows nature as a guide. Then comes the bit that won't sit still for me. I'm copying off the Latin Library page because I haven't got an edition to hand, so the spelling is theirs: "... in hoc sumus sapientes, quod naturam optimam ducem tamquam deum sequimur eique paremus; a qua non veri simile est, cum ceterae partes aetatis bene descriptae sint, extremum actum tamquam ab inerti poeta esse neglectum. Sed tamen necesse fuit esse aliquid extremum et, tamquam in arborum bacis terraeque fructibus maturitate tempestiva quasi vietum et caducum, quod ferundum est molliter sapienti. Quid est enim aliud Gigantum modo bellare cum dis nisi naturae repugnare?" One more thing that may or may not matter: back in ch. 3 he tells us he didn't put the speech in Tithonus's mouth because "parum enim esset auctoritatis in fabula", so the theatre language here looks like it's doing something, though I can't see what.

What I actually want is the argument reconstructed, not just the construal — though I want the construal too. How is "the other parts of life are well ordered" supposed to get you to "the final act hasn't been neglected"? Is that an inference from design (nature is a competent author, so no botched ending), or just a rhetorical figure that borrows the authority of an argument? And then the next sentence feels like it pulls the other way: the end of life is a fruit shrivelled and ready to drop, and the upshot is that the wise man has to bear it gently — which sounds more like resignation than like the claim that the ending is well made. Are the two sentences meant to sit together, or is there a slide from "nature is a good guide" to "whatever nature does must be accepted"? Is the Giants line the conclusion of the argument or a separate moral aside? And how far should I read "extremum actum" as old age rather than death (or both), given "inerti" — lazy, or unskilful? Flag anything you're genuinely unsure about or think is a stretch rather than smoothing it over, and skip the reading list, I've got a commentary and what I need is the joints of the thing.
