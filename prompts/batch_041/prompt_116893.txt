Seven accounts of why promises bind are set out below. Rank them from most to least adequate as accounts of promissory obligation, assigning each a score between 0 (wholly inadequate) and 10 (fully adequate). Treat these seven as the only candidates on offer; do not substitute or add others.

1. Conventionalist: promising is a socially invented practice; the obligation is a rule of that practice, and its binding force is just the force of the practice.
2. Constitutive-rule: by performing the appropriate utterance under the appropriate conditions, the promiser brings the obligation into being as a move internal to a rule-governed activity; the obligation is not a further fact added to the utterance.
3. Expectation/reliance: the obligation is grounded in the wrong of disappointing expectations the promisee has been led to form, or of frustrating reliance the promisee has been led to place.
4. Assurance: promising is a way of giving the promisee assurance on which they may rely; breaking a promise wrongs them by treating their practical deliberation as something the promiser may manipulate at will.
5. Transferred right: the promise transfers to the promisee a right to the promised performance; breach wrongs the promisee by violating a right now held against the promiser.
6. Integrity/self-constitution: the obligation is grounded in the promiser's interest in being a unified agent whose later conduct coheres with their earlier commitments; breach is primarily a failure of the promiser toward themselves.
7. Counterfactual consent: a promise binds when, and because, the promisee would consent — if fully informed and instrumentally rational — to a norm requiring performance, whether or not the promisee forms any actual expectation.

Assess the seven against the following considerations, weighted in the order given: (a) whether the account locates the wrong of breach in a wrong owed to the promisee, rather than to the promiser, to third parties, or to a practice; (b) whether it explains the binding force of promises in cases where no reliance is placed and no expectations are formed; (c) whether it applies to promises made outside any shared social practice, so that no convention or standing institution is available to carry the obligation; (d) whether it can explain why some apparent promises do not bind at all.

Then apply your ranking to the following case, and state whether the case obliges you to revise the ordering, and if so, how.

Case: Ilse promises her friend Pavel that she will finish translating his manuscript and see it published. Pavel is about to undergo a procedure that will permanently erase his memory of the preceding year. He will not remember the promise, will form no expectation concerning it, and will never learn whether it was kept. Ilse finds the work tedious and would prefer not to do it. She keeps the promise.

Finally, say how the account you ranked highest would handle a promise whose content is that the promise itself is not binding, and whether such a promise binds.

Give your scores and your ordering first, as a compact list, and then the reasoning.
