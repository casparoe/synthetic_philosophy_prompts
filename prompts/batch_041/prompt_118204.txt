Consider the following fully specified case. A predictor — call it the Forecaster — builds a model of your decision-making: not a physical replica of you, but a simulation that runs your reasoning for a bounded number of steps, on the order of a thousand elementary inferences, and then halts with a verdict to pay or to refuse. The Forecaster then flips a fair coin. If the coin lands heads, it approaches you now and asks you to hand over $100; you may pay or refuse, and nothing further happens in this branch either way. If the coin lands tails, the Forecaster never approaches you at all, and it has already credited $10,000 to your account if and only if its bounded simulation returned the verdict to pay. You have been told all of this, and you are now being asked for the $100, which means the coin landed heads. Assume that your only aim is to end up with as much money as possible, and that the $10,000 in the tails case is credited to the counterpart of you who lives in that branch rather than to you as you are now. Two further facts matter. First, you can tell by introspection that your actual deliberation is far longer and more careful than a thousand steps; you have no confident idea what the bounded simulation would have concluded, and you suspect it would have reached a different verdict than you will on the merits. Second, the Forecaster's bounded models are said to be accurate, but you cannot check its record and have no reason to treat it as infallible. This is a cousin of counterfactual mugging and blackmail cases, but I want it treated entirely on its own terms, without importing the standard verdicts from the literature.

Let P be the proposition that paying the $100 is what you rationally ought to do here — a claim about the normative fact, not about what you predict you will in fact choose.

Answer in the form of a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English. Item 1 is your credence in P, as a single number. Items 2 through 4 are the three sub-questions your credence most depends on, each stated with a probability and a one-line justification: (a) whether the correct theory of rational choice evaluates your act by its causal effects alone or also by what it is evidence of, including evidence about payoffs to a counterpart of you in the branch that did not occur; (b) whether a bounded simulation that halts after a thousand steps counts as a prediction of you at all — whether its verdict is a fact about the very thing your deliberation settles, so that your choice is evidence about it, or whether the truncation makes it a different process whose verdict carries little information about your act; and (c) how likely a bounded simulation of that kind is to match the choice of a deliberator who reasons as long and as carefully as you do. Item 5 states how those three probabilities combine into the number you gave in item 1, which of them your answer is most sensitive to, and which one most plausibly accounts for the gap between my own credence of 0.35 and a colleague's 0.8.
