My friend and I argued well past midnight about whether Dennett's heterophenomenology is a genuine method for a science of consciousness or an elaborate way of refusing to study the thing itself, and we never settled it. Because the question is still live for me, I want to use it to calibrate how carefully I read student work, so I would like your help grading an exam answer I am about to hand back.

The exam question was:

Dennett proposes heterophenomenology as the method for a science of consciousness.
(a) Describe the method as he sets it out, including the role of the intentional stance and the construction of the heterophenomenological world, and explain how the theorist is meant to move from the subject's reports to explanations of them.
(b) Distinguish heterophenomenology from classical introspectionism and from behaviorism.
(c) A critic objects that the method can study only what subjects say and believe about their experiences, never the experiences themselves, and therefore cannot be a method for studying consciousness. State Dennett's reply and assess whether it succeeds.

The student wrote:

Heterophenomenology is Dennett's proposal for making the study of consciousness scientific by treating subjects' reports as data rather than as a direct window onto experience. The theorist begins by recording the subject's utterances under controlled conditions and adopting the intentional stance toward her, interpreting those utterances as speech acts that express beliefs and desires. From this material the theorist constructs the heterophenomenological world: the world as the subject takes it to be. This world is a construction of the theorist, not a transcript of the subject's inner life, and the theorist need not accept its claims; where a report conflicts with what we know about the perceptual system, the theorist may treat the report as an illusion to be explained.

This is what distinguishes heterophenomenology from classical introspectionism, which treats the subject's reports as authoritative descriptions of experience, and from behaviorism, which refuses to posit inner states at all. Heterophenomenology is willing to attribute beliefs and to model the subject's point of view; it merely refuses to treat that point of view as automatically true. In this respect heterophenomenology is best understood as a sophisticated form of behaviorism about consciousness: it holds that the only evidence we can have about experience is what subjects are disposed to say, and so it reduces consciousness to verbal behavior.

(c) The critic's objection is that the method studies only what subjects believe about their experiences, never the experiences themselves, and therefore cannot be a method for studying consciousness. Dennett's reply is that the objection begs the question. Everything the subject says about her experience is included in the heterophenomenological world, so nothing is omitted; what is set aside is only the further assumption that her reports are infallible. If a completed heterophenomenology left a residue that no third-person account could capture, that residue would be exactly what Dennett calls qualia, and he regards the absence of any such residue as a virtue of the method rather than a defect.
