I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend who works on formal epistemology about whether an agent can coherently trust its own reasoning. We kept circling the Löbian obstacle: if an agent is a formal system strong enough for the usual incompleteness phenomena, and its self-trust is represented by a provability predicate, then endorsing the schema that whenever it proves a sentence it may conclude that sentence makes the trust collapse into triviality or inconsistency. My friend thinks the obstacle is real but local, and proposed a distinction I had not heard before: an agent's trusting a particular conclusion it has drawn is one thing, and its trusting the mechanism by which it draws conclusions is another; on his view the Löbian obstacle only bites at the second level, and a theory of self-trusting agents should be built to keep the first while disowning the second. I am not convinced, but I now want the full specification before we argue further.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of self-trusting agents must meet. I mean the requirements in the fullest sense: the cases it must get right, including the agent that trusts itself and thereby proves its own consistency, the agent that refuses all self-trust and is paralyzed, the bounded agent that cannot complete the relevant proof search, the agent that trusts a stronger external system but not itself, and the agent that modifies its own code and must decide whether to trust the modified system. The distinctions it must respect, including trust versus proof, local versus global self-trust, consistency versus soundness, self-trust versus self-knowledge, and reflective consistency versus logical omniscience. The formal results it must be consistent with, including Gödel's second incompleteness theorem and Löb's theorem, and any no-go results you take to bear on self-trust. And the failure modes it must avoid, including vacuity, inconsistency, paralysis, and the collapse of trust into a triviality. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp.

The part I most want is your judgment about joint satisfiability. Is some subset of these requirements unsatisfiable together, and if so which one? In particular, does my friend's distinction between trusting a conclusion and trusting the mechanism actually carve the obstacle at a joint, or does it merely relabel it? If you think it fails, say which requirement it violates. If it succeeds, say what else has to be given up to make it work. Then, briefly, assess the main families of response, such as hierarchical reflection principles, trust in a stronger external system, bounded proof search with explicit consistency assumptions, and pragmatic or fallible self-trust, against your list, marking which requirement each one fails.
