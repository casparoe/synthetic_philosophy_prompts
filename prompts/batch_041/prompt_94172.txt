I was at a graduate reading group last week when two of the other participants, both of whom work on the history and philosophy of science, fell into a long argument about Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. I stayed out of it, and I have to admit I could not tell who had the better of it. Below I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can. Neither was setting up a strawman, and each conceded real ground along the way. What I want from you is not a verdict on who won, but a point-by-point adjudication: which moves are clearly right, which are clearly mistaken, which are defensible but overstated, and what both of them failed to see.

Round one. Nadia: Lakatos's framework is the most serious attempt we have to make theory change rational without pretending that a single failed prediction refutes a theory. A research programme has a hard core that its practitioners are methodologically forbidden to abandon, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorbs the anomalies, and a positive heuristic that tells them which problems to attack next. On this picture, scientists who refuse to give up on a programme with a growing list of anomalies, or who keep working within it while a rival waits in the wings, are not being irrational; they are giving the programme the run that any programme deserves. The rationality shows up over the long haul, in whether the programme is progressive — whether it keeps generating novel predictions that then get corroborated — or degenerating, merely mopping up anomalies after the fact. That, she said, is how Lakatos answers Kuhn: theory change is not a conversion experience but a rational transition to a programme that is doing better.

Round two. Owen: The trouble is that progressive and degenerating are retrospective verdicts. At the moment a scientist has to decide whether to keep going, she cannot know whether the programme's novel predictions will pan out; if she could, there would be no need for the criterion. So the distinction cannot guide anyone's decision in real time. It only lets the historian sort programmes into winners and losers after the fact, once we already know which ones bore fruit. What presents itself as a normative methodology is really a rational reconstruction of history — and a reconstruction guaranteed to succeed, since we can always find a progressive phase somewhere in a programme's past if we look hard enough.

Round three. Nadia: That is a feature of the account, not a defect, and it is a deliberate inheritance from Popper's insistence that there is no such thing as instant rationality. Demanding a rule that tells you today which programme will win is demanding prophecy, and no methodology of science has ever supplied that, including the ones that pretend to. The rationality of science is fallible and diachronic; it consists in the pattern of choices over time, not in an algorithm for each choice. And the framework does guide in the present: the negative heuristic tells you what not to abandon, and the positive heuristic tells you what to try next, which is exactly the kind of guidance working scientists need.

Round four. Owen: But that guidance is nearly empty. To tell someone not to abandon the hard core is to describe what a committed practitioner already does, not to give her a reason; and to tell her to follow the positive heuristic is to tell her to keep doing what the programme says to do, which is circular as advice. Worse, the progressive/degenerating test is circular in a deeper way. Whether a fact counts as novel depends on whether the theory was built to accommodate it or predicted it in advance, and in real episodes the two are almost never cleanly separable — programmes routinely relabel accommodations as predictions, and their defenders are the ones who get to do the relabeling. And because the hard core is methodologically protected from refutation, the empirical record alone can never discipline a programme; only the appearance of a rival can, and whether a rival appears is a matter of sociology, not methodology. So Lakatos either collapses back into Kuhn with extra machinery, or he offers a standard that no actual scientific practice meets.

Round five. Nadia: All right, let me try to meet you. Suppose we index the verdict to an agent and a time. Call a programme progressive for a scientist at a given moment if it has at least one novel prediction corroborated so far and no rival programme is doing better on the evidence she actually has. That is not hindsight; it is a judgment she can make today. Owen: But that is a different criterion from Lakatos's, and it now licenses persistence with a programme whose only novel successes are long in the past, as long as no rival has yet caught up — which is precisely the situation Lakatos wanted to call degenerating. And if you add a condition about the rate of novel predictions, you owe an account of how much is enough, and any threshold you pick will look arbitrary.

Please go through their exchange move by move and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then step back and tell me: is there a version of the framework that survives both Owen's objections and Nadia's amendment, and if so, what does it cost — does anything recognizably Lakatosian remain? Finally, what are both of them missing about the relationship between a normative methodology and the historical record it is supposed to illuminate?
