I spent 35 years as a physiologist poking electrodes into various unfortunate animals, and since I retired I've been working through philosophy of mind, which is alot harder than it looks. I want a short story I can hand to my reading group. Give yourself the role of a genuinely good philosophical novelist -- think carefully about what the concepts actually commit you to before you write a word -- and start by brainstorming: what does global workspace theory really claim, where exactly does the line fall between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and what's the sharpest concrete scenario that would put pressure on the claim that a mental state only counts as conscious if it gets broadcast to the workspace.

Then write the story. Build it on this premise, which is mine, so please don't just reach for the stock examples everyone uses: a lab has a rig that can detect and momentarily suppress the broadcast -- the ignition event where a representation goes global -- so that a subject keeps walking, answering questions, doing arithmetic, but afterwards reports experiencing nothing. The standard reading is that suppression means no consciousness. But one subject, a retired cellist, insists the silenced intervals are the only times she feels anything like herself, a kind of wordless inner weather that never gets to be reported and leaves no trace in the readout. The tension I want in the scene: if it can't be accessed, reported, or used to guide anything, is there anything it is like to be her then, and how would anyone, including her, ever know?

Aim for maybe 1,500-2,000 words. I don't want a lecture with characters bolted on -- let the disagreement live in the scene, in what each of the two researchers thinks they're actually measuring. No name-dropping studies or papers, I don't care about citations, and don't resolve it with a tidy moral at the end. One more thing: make the cellist's claim specific enough that a skeptic could say exactly what would have to be true of her brain for her to be right.
