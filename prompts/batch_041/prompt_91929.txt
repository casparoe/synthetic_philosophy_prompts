You are an expert in computational complexity theory and in the philosophy of computational complexity, and you are advising a software engineering team. Before you write anything, think carefully about the problem, and open with a short brainstorming section: list the distinctions that actually matter for the question below, mark which of them are theorems, which are conjectures, and which are habits of thought, and say which you intend to keep and which you intend to drop when you turn them into a procedure.

Context. I am a software engineer on a platform team. Roughly once a quarter, someone proposes that we stop trying to solve a problem exactly and ship a heuristic or an approximation instead, on the grounds that the problem is intractable. The arguments are always the same shape and are never written down: someone has read that the problem is NP-hard, or that a special case is, or that a reduction exists from something known to be hard, and concludes that further effort is wasted. Sometimes they are right and we save a quarter of engineering time. Sometimes they are wrong and we ship an approximation to a problem whose exact version would have been fine at our scale, or we accept a quality guarantee that was never necessary. I want to stop adjudicating these one at a time.

I have no formal philosophy or theory background, so I am relying on you to keep the real distinctions intact while making the thing runnable by an engineer in about a week, with no access to a complexity theorist. The output goes to a director who has to sign off on narrowing the scope of a project, so the procedure has to produce a defensible written verdict, not a vibe.

The deliverable: a decision procedure, in words, that a single engineer can run on a proposal to give up on exactness. It should be a checklist of questions in order; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer; which question usually decides the case; where the procedure stops with a no, meaning the proposal has not earned its conclusion; and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is about what we are willing to accept rather than about what is true. Give the procedure a small set of named verdicts rather than a yes/no.

Some things I want it to get right, because our current arguments keep getting them wrong:

- The gap between a problem being hard in the worst case and being hard on the instances we actually see, and what would have to be true for the worst case to be the relevant one.
- The difference between a problem being hard to solve exactly, hard to solve well approximately, and hard to solve in a way that would tell us anything. Do not let the procedure treat these as one thing.
- What a reduction actually buys. When we show our problem is as hard as some known-hard problem, we typically prove it in one direction and then quietly act as if the two problems are interchangeable. Ask what the reduction preserves: the size of a solution, the quality guarantee, the parameter we care about, or nothing.
- The status of the hardness claim itself. Separate a problem that is provably unsolvable or carries an unconditional lower bound, from one that is hard only if a famous conjecture is true, from one where we simply do not know and are generalizing from our own failures. The procedure should not let the third masquerade as the first.
- The model-relativity and the asymptotic framing. Hardness claims are made relative to a model of computation and to a limit our n never reaches; ask what our actual n is, and whether a bad constant could make the supposedly efficient algorithm the slower one.
- Solver-relative hardness: what is hard for the model in the textbook is not necessarily hard for the tool we would actually point at it.

Then run the procedure, showing your work at each step, on three proposals:

I expect this one to be the case the procedure hands back to a human; make sure it is, and say exactly what the human is being asked to decide.

Include one case where the procedure tells me something I will not want to hear, and do not soften it.

Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the steps that are judgment dressed up as procedure, the ways it will misfire, and what it would take for me to be wrong to trust it.
