I belong to a small reading group on the philosophy of history, and we have just finished a stretch on cyclical theories — the rotation of constitutions in Polybius, the rise and decay of dynasties in Ibn Khaldun, the morphology of cultures in Spengler — before turning to the linear, cumulative pictures one finds in providential history and in the Enlightenment idea of progress. One member of the group, who has become a convinced Spenglerian, has a single move he makes whenever the rest of us press him, and it is an analogy rather than an argument. A culture, he says, is an organism: it is born, it grows, it flowers, and it dies, and you can no more argue a culture out of its old age than you can argue a body out of senescence. On that picture, the late, sceptical, cosmopolitan phase of our own civilization is simply what the autumn of an organism looks like from the inside, and reformist politics is the illusion that a dying body can be talked back into youth. The conclusion he draws is strong: that our decline is not a contingent predicament we might have avoided or might still mitigate, but a natural terminus, so that the only honest responses left are diagnosis and acceptance rather than repair. I find the analogy seductive and I suspect it is carrying more weight than he admits, but I cannot say precisely where his argument hangs.

I would like you to take the role of a philosopher of history with a specialist interest in how metaphors and models do argumentative work in the discipline, and to think carefully and at some length before you commit to a verdict. Begin by working out for yourself the correspondence the analogy asks us to accept — what in the target is supposed to answer to the organism, to birth, to a germ line or genome, to nourishment and environment, to maturation, to senescence, to death, and to reproduction and inheritance — and set that mapping out explicitly. Then say where it breaks, and, importantly, sort those breaks into the ones that matter for the conclusion he wants and the ones that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in whether the analogy quietly supplies premises the historical record does not: that the unit called a culture has determinate boundaries and a determinate lifespan, that its ageing has an internal cause rather than a contingent external one, that its end is death rather than transformation, and that age is decline. Once the disanalogies are on the table, tell me whether his conclusion survives in any form, and if it does, what the strongest version of his position is that does not lean on the organism picture at all. If you think a different analogy would do better, or that this whole genre of analogy is a liability in this area, say so and explain why.

Please write the whole answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a long, careful letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics for emphasis, and no tables. If you want to give me a mapping, unfold it in sentences.
