I'm slowly working through Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics (the old Montgomery translation, if that matters) and I've hit a stretch early on that I can't get past. The gist, as far as I can make it out, is that every individual substance has a notion so complete that it contains all of its predicates — so the complete notion of Alexander contains everything that will ever be true of him, including that he conquers Darius, and God, looking at that notion on its own, can read off his entire career without waiting for anything to happen. Leibniz then adds that if a different predicate had been true of him, it would have been a different notion, and so a different subject — you can't swap one predicate out and still have Alexander.

What I can't sort out is what this commits him to. Is Leibniz a believer in transworld identity, or is he closer to something like Lewis's counterpart theory? It looks to me like he can't have the same Alexander in two worlds at all: if the notion is complete, then any world containing an Alexander who doesn't conquer Darius contains somebody else. But he still talks as if God surveys possible worlds containing possible Adams and possible Sextuses, so are those worlds populated by near-copies of actual people rather than the people themselves? And if so, is his view just counterpart theory before the fact, or is there a real dissagreement underneath? Does it matter that for Leibniz the notion is, I think, supposed to be in God's understanding, whereas a counterpart relation is just a similarity relation between things?

Also, two knock-on puzzles. First, if no alternative to my actual life is a life of me, should I stop saying things like if I'd left five minutes earlier I'd have caught the train, or is that just loose talk that his view can accommodate? Second, what about fission-type cases — if one person somehow divides into two, the counterpart theorist can give each branch its own counterpart in another world, but Leibniz seems stuck, because two things can't both answer to one complete notion. Can you walk me through the passage and tell me which bits are doing the work, and where I'm reading modern debates back into him?
