Below is a short exam question from an undergraduate course on the philosophy of mind, together with one student's answer to it. I am grading the answer and would like your assessment.

Question: Explain Dennett's distinction among Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, and say how the four kinds are related to one another.

Student's answer:

Dennett's four kinds of creatures are distinguished by how they cope with the world, and each one is a stage in the growth of mind.

Darwinian creatures are the products of natural selection. They know what to do because individuals whose hardwired responses were poorly suited to their surroundings tended not to survive and reproduce, so the better-suited responses spread through the population. Their competence is written into the genes and is fixed for the life of the individual.

Skinnerian creatures are one step up: they can learn within their own lifetime. Rather than being stuck with a fixed repertoire, they try things out, keep the actions that are reinforced, and drop those that are punished. This is trial-and-error learning within the lifetime, and it lets a creature adjust to local conditions that its genes never anticipated.

Popperian creatures go further, because they can try things out in the head before acting. Instead of risking a fatal trial in the world, they run a simulation of the consequences internally and let the bad option be discarded without ever being carried out. Because this inner rehearsal works on representations of the world, on propositions about what will happen if I do X, the Popperian stage is where language first becomes essential.

Gregorian creatures are the fourth kind, and here Dennett's label reflects the human case. What distinguishes us is that we do not merely simulate; we equip ourselves with thinking tools that we inherit from our culture, such as words, numbers, and maps, which restructure the problems we face. These tools are not in our genes, and we acquire them by learning from other people, so the mind reaches out into the world and borrows structure from it.

The four kinds form a ladder: each stage supersedes the one below it, so that a creature that has reached a higher rung no longer relies on the lower ones. A Gregorian creature such as an adult human is therefore not really a Skinnerian creature anymore, since language and culture have taken over the job that conditioning once did; and it is only in the creatures below us that we still see the bare Darwinian and Skinnerian machinery at work.
