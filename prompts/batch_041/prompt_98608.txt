I have been reading Camus's 1957 essay on the guillotine, and I find myself less interested in his conclusion than in his diagnosis. He treats the death penalty as resting on a picture of the condemned person — as irremediable, past any possibility of change or atonement — and on a picture of the state as occupying the seat of an absolute judge, delivering a verdict no human being is entitled to deliver. He also argues that once executions were moved behind prison walls, whatever deterrent work they had once done was gone, and what remained was the bureaucratic disposal of people the state had written off. I want to test that diagnosis against the practice as it actually exists now, rather than against the guillotine.

So take the contemporary American death-penalty regime, with these features among others: a bifurcated trial in which guilt and sentence are decided separately; a sentencing phase in which the jury must weigh aggravating factors against mitigating evidence about the defendant's history and circumstances; a "worst of the worst" threshold meant to select the small subset of murderers who deserve death; a requirement that the condemned be competent to be executed, so that a prisoner who has become psychotic cannot be put to death until he can understand why he is being killed; a right to a last meal and a final statement; a choice, in some states, of the method of execution; lethal injection defended precisely as the most humane method available; and years or decades on death row while appeals run, with the courts themselves sometimes conceding that the delay has become a punishment in its own right.

What theory of the person, of culpability, of the state, and of what makes killing wrong does this package presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a defense or an attack on the death penalty as such.

Give me the answer in these sections, in this order:

1. The implicit theory (~250 words): the commitments, stated positively, that the practice only makes sense under.

2. Commitments the practice strictly requires (~600 words): each commitment tied to the specific feature that forces it, and for each, the rival view it quietly rules out. Be exact about which feature carries which commitment — I want to see, for instance, what the competence requirement commits you to that the mitigation phase does not.

3. Commitments it merely accommodates (~350 words): where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same regime, and what would distinguish among them.

4. The inconsistency I cannot resolve (~350 words): the practice treats the condemned at once as a responsible agent who must understand his death, choose his method, and be heard, and as a thing to be disposed of; and it treats culpability as a matter of degree while the penalty is all-or-nothing. Say whether these are real contradictions or only tensions, and which reading of the practice dissolves them.

5. Camus's diagnosis, tested (~500 words): which of his charges the practice genuinely presupposes, which it does not, and which he gets backwards. In particular, say whether the practice really needs the picture of the condemned as irremediable, or whether that is only how the practice looks from the outside.

6. What would change (~250 words): if we replaced the presupposed view of the person with one that granted the condemned an open future, which features would have to go and which could stay.
