A colleague of mine over in the law faculty keeps saying a constitution is just a Ulysses contract — the polity ties itself to the mast so it can't chase impulses it'll regret. His version has a twist I'm not sure I buy: he says this isnt paternalism at all, because Ulysses actually wants to hear the song and the ropes are what make the voyage possible, so entrenchment is enabling rather than restricting. He also insists the ropes only count if nobody can slip them, i.e. a commitment is only real insofar as it's costly to undo, and he uses that to argue a constitution needs a core that's practically unamendable, with judges holding any passing majority to it. Can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table first — who's Ulysses, who's the crew, what's the mast, what are the ropes, what's the wax, what's the song — then tell me where the mapping breaks, which breaks actually matter for the work he's making it do, and what the analogy is quietly importing that the polity doesn't have. I'm especially bothered that Ulysses only ever binds himself, and he's standing right there while the knots are tied, whereas a constitution binds people who weren't born yet; does that sink it or can it be patched? And once the disanalogies are on the table, do the enabling-not-paternalistic move and the unamendable-core conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying them the whole time? If it can't carry that weight, whats a better comparison he should be reaching for?
