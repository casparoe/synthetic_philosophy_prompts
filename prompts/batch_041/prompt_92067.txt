I run a 90-min session on social choice in our phil/econ crossover seminar, about 16 people, and I want to build an activity where they walk straight into the discursive dilemma instead of me announcing it. The tricky bit is I don't want them to just recognize the textbook three-judge example and feel clever — I want them to actually get stuck. There's alot riding on getting the rules right. Here's my sketch; tell me whether it'll land, and fix the parts that won't.

The setup is a disciplinary committee deciding whether to sack a professor. Three propositions on a card: (1) what he did counts as serious misconduct; (2) serious misconduct is grounds for dismissal; (3) he should be dismissed. Everyone fills out the card and I insist their answers be internally consistent — if you tick 1 and 2 you have to tick 3. Easy, nobody objects.

Then we vote, proposition by proposition, plain majority, and I tally on the board. If it works out the way I think, we end up with the group "accepting" 1 and 2 but rejecting 3 — flatly incoherent as a set, even though every single person was coherent. That's the moment I want. Then the real work: I hand them a list of things we'd want any decision rule to do — the group's final set is consistent; if everyone agrees the group agrees; the rule treats all three propositions the same way rather than deciding some by vote and deducing others; no one person's vote decides everything — and they have to actually try to build a rule that satisfies all of them. They'll fail, and the way they fail is the lesson. If they settle on "vote on the premises, then deduce the conclusion," I want a follow-up that makes them uneasy about it — something where the deduced conclusion contradicts what a majority would say, or where someone can game the thing by adding or rephrasing a proposition.

I definately want a round two where people can lobby each other and change their votes after seeing the tally, so they feel the outcome move depending on procedure and timing.

Tell me: the exact rules and roles (is there a chair? do I need a fourth proposition to make round two bite?), what to hand out, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps each stage onto the actual pieces of the problem. Be honest about what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — I assume someone spots the trick immediately and spoils it — and about which part of the dilemma the activity can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud.

Also: do I need a scenario people will actually disagree about, or is it better if the content is boring so the structure does all the work?
