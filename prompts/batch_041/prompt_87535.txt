I've been working through the predictive-processing material for a seminar — the free-energy formulation, active inference, the machinery of priors, precisions, and prediction error — and there's a pattern in how the framework gets deployed that I can't find a name for, and that I suspect isn't quite any of the standard accusations. Take two outcomes of the same perceptual system. In the first, the system holds a strong prior and the world happens to match it, and the theorist says: good — that's a well-learned generative model, the prior is doing its job, the precision is appropriately weighted. In the second, the system holds an equally strong prior and the world doesn't match it, and the theorist says: that's a failure — an over-weighted prior, a rigidity, the sensory evidence wasn't granted enough precision; that's how you get hallucination or delusion. But the numbers in the two stories are the same; what changes is the evaluative label attached to them once the outcome is known. The same happens in the other direction: heavy precision on sensory input is responsiveness when it helps and noise-chasing or distractibility when it hurts. And there seems to be no way, from inside the framework, to say in advance how strong a prior or how heavy a precision is the correct one — the calibration is always read off the outcome, so every result, success or failure alike, comes out as the framework functioning as intended, with the parameters merely relabelled.

What I want to know is whether this has a name. I know it sits somewhere near the charge that such theories are unfalsifiable, and I know the standard reply that any theory can be rescued by adjusting auxiliary commitments, but this feels more specific than that: the issue isn't that a claim is shielded by an extra hypothesis, it's that one and the same parameter gets described with opposite evaluative valence depending on what happened, so that the theory can never be the thing at fault. Is there an established term for this — the relabelling of a free parameter by its outcome, or the absence of any outcome-independent standard for setting the parameter? If so, I'd like to know how it differs from plain ad hocness and from the general underdetermination point, since neither seems to capture the reversal. If not, I'd like a term coined and defined, with boundary cases: in particular, how to distinguish this from a theory that simply has free parameters and honestly estimates them from data, and whether the same structure shows up elsewhere — I have a suspicion it does in evolutionary just-so stories and in the looser reaches of Bayesian epistemology, but I can't tell whether those are the same phenomenon or merely cousins. I'm not after a bare label; I want the phenomenon itself pinned down, its edges marked, and the nearest neighbours ruled in or out.
