I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on an argument I want to render fairly rather than merely plausibly. One of its two central figures is an actuary who administers a charitable trust with an indefinite horizon; she holds that the trust is right to value a pound devoted to people a century hence at a small fraction of a pound spent on the living, and she defends the practice with a comparison she treats as self-evident: moral concern thins out with distance in time exactly as it thins out with distance in space. She does not deny that a stranger's suffering is as bad for him as a neighbour's is for the neighbour; she says only that our practical concern, and therefore the weight we are obliged to give, declines with distance, in time as in space, and that a discount rate is simply the price we put on that distance. She has read, and cites, the finding that giving after a disaster falls off with the distance between donor and catastrophe, and takes this to be a fact about the shape of human concern that any ethics must accommodate. The other figure, a young trustee, replies that this comparison is precisely what condemns her: if distance in space does not diminish a person's claim on us, then distance in time cannot either, and pure time preference — a preference for an earlier benefit merely because it is earlier — is indefensible. I have since met versions of the comparison in several places, made by people on both sides of the question, and I cannot tell how much of the argument the comparison itself is carrying.

I am not asking for a verdict on whether discounting is defensible in general. I am asking for an audit of the analogy, so that I can see what each character is entitled to. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of distance, of the person at the far end, of the neighbour and the traveller, of the discount rate, of the moral claim that supposedly weakens. Then the places where the mapping breaks — I suspect the direction of time is one, since space runs both ways and the past is closed to us, but I want the rest — and I want you to separate the breaks that actually matter for the use each character makes of the comparison from those that are merely decorative. Does the comparison, followed consistently, cut symmetrically, so that it must debunk spatial partiality and temporal partiality together or license them together? If it licenses both, what work is left for the discount rate to do, and what would the actuary have to say instead? What does the analogy quietly import: that distance is a fact about the world rather than about the observer, that the decay of sympathy and the decay of obligation run at the same rate, that the far end is occupied by people who will exist whatever we do? And does it reach the whole discount rate, or only the part of it that is pure time preference, as distinct from what we owe to the uncertainty of the future and to the likelihood that later people will be richer?

Finally, and this is what I would most like: a sentence I can put in the actuary's mouth that states the strongest version of her position once the disanalogies have been conceded, and a sentence for the young trustee that answers that repaired version rather than the original. If the comparison is beyond repair, give me a better one — some image of the relation between the present and the further future that carries what she needs carried.
