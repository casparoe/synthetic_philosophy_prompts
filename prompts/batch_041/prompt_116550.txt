I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy and I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on punishment and responsibility this fall — 16 students, mostly seniors, and I get one 90-minute block to run however I want because the prof is traveling. I want to use it to make them feel a specific problem in retributive theory that I don't think they'll have run into before, and I'd rather they hit it with their faces than hear me explain it up front.

Here's the thing I want them to feel. Retributivism is supposed to be about desert, and desert seems to give you a ranking: everyone can agree some crimes deserve more punishment than others. But the second you ask "more than what, and how much more," desert goes quiet — it gives you the order of the cases but not the units. There's no independent yardstick for how much suffering equals how much wrongdoing, so a pure desert theory can rank cases but can't calibrate them; any actual number you write down has to come from somewhere else. Consequentialism has the opposite problem: it does give you a way to fix the numbers (crank the deterrence/incapacitation lever until expected harm stops going down), but then the numbers it spits out start violating the desert ranking everyone agreed on a minute earlier — the highly deterrable offender gets hammered, the barely-culpable-but-dangerous guy gets warehoused, etc. I want them to build a sentencing grid from scratch and walk straight into both walls.

So: design me that activity. My rough thought is a mock sentencing commission. They get a deck of maybe 12-14 case cards (crime + aggravating/mitigating facts + a couple of offender facts), and there are phases. First they just rank the cards from least to most deserving — I expect that to be easy and for them to mostly agree. Then they have to assign actual years to each card, and I want the wheels to come off there. Then a third phase where I impose some consequentialist constraint (a prison-capacity budget, a deterrence number, a recidivism figure I hand them) and make them redo the grid under it, so they have to watch their own ranking get violated by numbers they can't argue with. I want the two walls to be something they did to themselves, not something I told them about.

Give me: materials, the actual case cards or at least the design principle for them, a phase-by-phase timeline with rough minutes, what I say at each transition (as little as possible), and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the concepts — the ranking-vs-calibration thing, why mixed theories tend to use desert as a side constraint rather than as the whole story, and the difference between desert doing limiting work (this is too much) and desert doing positive work (this is exactly what he deserves). Don't let them hear the technical terms until the debrief.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm betting someone tries to dodge by refusing to put numbers on the cards at all, someone else imports real-world sentencing law and derails the room, and at least one person short-circuits the whole thing with a crude equal-harm intuition. How do I handle each without rescuing them too fast?

And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce — I know it can't give them the felt weight of actually imposing a sentence, and I know the real anchor in actual systems comes from history and politics rather than from theory, which I'll have to just tell them at the end.
