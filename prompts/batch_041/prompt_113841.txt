My friend Dan did a masters in econ and now works in policy, and he's the guy everyone asks about 'rationality' stuff, so when I mentioned I was reading up on decision theory he launched into his money pump spiel. This is roughly how he put it, I wrote it down afterwords:

'The money pump is the master argument against intransitive preferences. Suppose you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. Someone can now milk you: they offer to swap your C for their B, and you take it, because you prefer B to C; then they offer to swap your B for their A, and you take it, because you prefer A to B; then they offer to swap your A for their C, and you take it, because you prefer C to A. You're holding exactly what you started with, and you've paid a fee on every trade. Each single trade was one you preferred to the one before it, and yet the whole sequence leaves you worse off than doing nothing. So a rational person can't have cyclic preferences — rationality requires transitivity. It's the same kind of argument as a Dutch book against incoherent probabilities, and it's why the standard model says your preferences have to be a ranking you could in principle write down as a utility function. It's also the main thing that kills hyperbolic discounting, since if your discounting isn't exponential your preferences reverse over time and then you're pumpable too.'

I said surely you'd just see it coming and refuse the first trade. Dan said no, that doesn't help, because at each step you do prefer the thing you're getting, and refusing a trade you prefer is itself irrational, so a foresighted person with a cycle is just as pumpable as a naive one.

I don't know enough to tell weather he's right, and I'd rather not repeat any of it until I do.
