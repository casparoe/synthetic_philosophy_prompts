I'm in a reading group that's working through the ethics of memory and I'm supposed to open next session with a case, so I've been trying to test two positions against something real instead of the Germany/Holocaust examples everyone reaches for. The two positions, as I understand them: Margalit, who I read as arguing that duties of memory are duties of thick relations -- a family, a people, a community remembering its own dead -- and that it's a category error to turn that into a universal obligation owed to humanity at large, since the whole point is that you owe it to people you already stand in a special relation to; and Rieff, who runs the other way, arguing that the modern cult of collective memory mostly serves the present, that remembering doesn't reliably produce justice or stop repetition, and that forgetting is often the more humane and peace-making option. Rieff actually uses the Spanish transition and the pacto del olvido as one of his examples of forgetting that more or less worked, which is half the reason I want to use Spain.

So take Spain. After Franco died in '75 the transition ran on the 1977 amnesty law and an unwritten pact of forgetting -- no prosecutions for crimes on either side, Francoist monuments left standing, mass graves left unopened, the losers told to keep quiet for the sake of democracy. Then from about 2000 the families start doing it themselves, volunteers digging up civil-war and postwar graves, and one of the founders of the main association, Emilio Silva, dug up his own grandfather in the first of those exhumations. Then the state takes the thing over: the 2007 historical memory law, Franco's exhumation from the Valley of the Fallen in 2019, and the 2022 democratic memory law that declares the regime illegal, voids its convictions and makes the state responsible for finding and identifying the disappeared.

What I want is the mapping run in both directions. For each of those three moments, say what Margalit and Rieff would have prescribed and what they'd have said was actually going on -- and then, more interesting to me, where the case breaks each of them. Two things I keep circling and can't settle. First, Rieff's humane forgetting: the Spanish forgetting wasn't chosen by the people who had to do it, it was a bargain between elites that happened to leave the perpetrators' monuments up and the victims' graves shut -- is that even forgetting in his sense, or is he smuggling in a mutuality the case doesn't have? Second, Margalit's thick relation: in Spain the community that owes the memory is the same community that did the killing, the state exhuming bodies is the legal successor of the state that shot them, so who exactly owes what to whom -- does his framework have anything to say when the remembering community and the perpetrator community are one and the same, or does it assume a clean split that Spain doesn't give it? (Related, and maybe naive: is the duty owed to the dead or to the living? I keep feeling there's something almost magical in exhuming a grandfather's bones as if that answers for his death, but I can't tell if that's Margalit's point or just my own reaction.) End by telling me which of the two comes out of the case worse and what each would have to concede to survive it -- and dont just say the case "complicates" both, tell me specifically where each is silent or gets it wrong.
