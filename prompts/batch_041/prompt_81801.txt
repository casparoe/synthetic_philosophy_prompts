Three weeks ago my in-laws offered to lend my wife and me a considerable sum, enough to clear the deposit on a house we have been chasing for two years, and they insisted on no paperwork, no interest, no schedule, and no mention of it in front of the rest of the family; we would repay them when we were able, which everyone in the room understood to mean within a few years. I said yes before I had time to think, and I have been trying to work out ever since whether I was entitled to say it, because I know something about myself that they do not. I keep small promises with almost religious care, and I have twice broken large ones when the moment came, the cost fell entirely on me, and no one could have done anything about it. I have kept a private tally since graduate school — forty-seven promises kept, two broken — and both entries on the wrong side of that ledger share the features of this one: no witness with standing to enforce anything, a long gap between the promise and the reckoning, and a future self who will be deciding alone, with the money long since spent.

The reason this has become urgent is an encounter a fortnight before. My car died on a county road with no phone signal, and a retired farmer stopped, drove me and my dog ninety minutes out of her way to the next town, and waved away the cash I offered when we arrived. She said she would rather have it when I got home, and when I pressed her she said something I cannot get out of my head: that she had been burned before by people who were perfectly gracious at the roadside, and that what she was really doing was betting on the kind of person who would bother to send it. I sent it that week. But sitting in her truck I noticed that I could feel the gratitude and the intention to repay with complete vividness and sincerity, and that I can produce that feeling more or less on demand, and I am no longer confident that it is evidence of anything at all. My wife, who knows the history, has told her parents that I am good for it, which means her credibility is now standing behind mine. My father-in-law has also proposed what he calls a sensible arrangement: a smaller loan first, repaid within six months, to see how it goes. I think I know how that goes, and that it would tell him nothing, because a small sum under observation is not the same test.

What I want is not reassurance but a straight answer, and I would like the answer early rather than at the end. Should I go back to them and withdraw the promise, or accept it and set about becoming the sort of person who repays it — and if the latter, whether that setting-about can amount to anything, given that I cannot seem to install a disposition in myself merely by wanting one, and given that the entire value of my repaying, from their side, consists in my doing it when nothing is watching and nothing is at stake except the money. I also want to know what to make of my wife's guarantee, since if I am the one asking, her word is doing work that mine cannot, and whether the two bets — the farmer's and my in-laws' — are really the same bet, or whether my having come through for a stranger who was watching tells us anything about the case where no one is. And I want to know whether there is any honest form of words in which I can promise at all. I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of positions; I am asking what you would advise me to do. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables — the way you would write a letter to someone whose situation you were taking seriously.
