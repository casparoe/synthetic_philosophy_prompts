I teach a ninety-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences — eighteen students, movable desks, no computers needed — and I want them to feel two things in their hands: first, why the prisoner's dilemma stops being a dilemma once it is repeated, and second, why that escape is narrower and more fragile than the standard telling suggests. Design the session as a run of games rather than a lecture; nothing should be named until the debrief, and the students should arrive at the problems by playing into them.

Set the stage payoffs as T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, S = 0, written on the board and used throughout, with pairs scoring on sheets that accumulate points (absolute totals per pair, not a rank-order tournament, so that competition between pairs doesn't change the game). Use eight pairs and two rotating referees, and give me the materials list, the rules, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

Block 1: a genuinely one-shot, simultaneous, anonymous play, no communication, cards collected. Block 2: the repeated game with an uncertain end — after each round a die decides whether that pair's game continues — run twice, once with a high continuation probability (say five in six) and once with a low one (say one in three), with the pairs told the probabilities in advance. Block 3: a repeated game with a known, finite horizon of exactly eight rounds. Block 4: the high-continuation game again, but with implementation noise — each round the referee rolls, and on a six flips one randomly chosen player's move before scoring, so that a player can intend to cooperate and be seen to defect; neither player is told this has happened. Let the group try to repair the resulting feuds, and see whether what they invent is forgiveness or something more like contrition, where the player who caused the error apologizes for it rather than merely tolerating it in the other.

The debrief is the part I care most about. It should derive on the board the continuation threshold at which reciprocity stops paying against a defector — with these payoffs, (T − R)/(T − P) = 1/2 — and put it beside the two continuation probabilities the students actually played, so that their own data bracket the theoretical crossing point. It should set the finite-horizon block against the backward-induction prediction and be honest about the gap between the prediction (defect from the first round) and what people actually do (cooperate and then unravel at the end); if there is time for one more five-minute block, tell them the horizon is a hundred rounds and use the result the same way. It should say plainly what the noise block shows: that reciprocity needs error-correction as well as retaliation, that pure forgiveness is exploitable and pure retaliation is self-destroying, and that past some noise level unconditional defection outperforms reciprocity, so cooperation is an equilibrium condition rather than a moral achievement. Finally it should mark the line between explaining cooperation and justifying it, since this is a philosophy seminar and not a game theory course.

Introduce every idea in the debrief by explaining it, not by citing anything: no reading lists, no bibliographies, no names of papers or authors, and no quotations. Close by telling me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — including how to keep out-of-band deals out of Block 1, what to do about pairs whose games happen to run long or short, and how to stop a last-round defection in Block 3 from being taken personally — and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
