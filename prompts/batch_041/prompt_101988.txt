A few of us at work have a long-running argument about whether meaning makes anything happen—whether the semantic level of a system does real causal work or is just a convenient way of speaking, the way a program's source code is a convenient way of speaking about what the transistors are up to. I come at this from software rather than philosophy, and one friend keeps returning to a thought experiment from a popular book on consciousness, in which a brain is imagined as an enormous billiards table: countless tiny balls careening around and colliding, none of them intelligent. The claim is that certain stable, higher-level patterns in that sea of collisions—the author nicknames them simmballs, and calls the whole setup the careenium—are what symbols actually are, and that these patterns genuinely push the individual balls around. To make that respectable, my friend reaches for an analogy I have now heard from several people: a simmball is to the balls what a whirlpool is to the water molecules composing it, or what the Great Red Spot is to the gases of Jupiter—a higher-level entity that persists while its constituents are continually replaced, is perfectly real, has its own lawful behavior, and acts on the stuff it is made of. He uses the analogy to conclude that we should take the semantic level seriously: meanings are not decorative, they are causes, and the billiard-ball picture does not explain them away.

I would like you to audit the comparison rather than the conclusion. Start by laying out what corresponds to what—balls, collisions, the whirlpool, the water, the walls of the container, the laws governing each level—and then tell me where the correspondence fails, separating the failures that matter for the use my friend is putting it to from the ones that are merely incidental. What I most want to know is whether the comparison can carry the weight he places on it. A whirlpool is a pattern in a fluid, but it also does not stand for anything, does not mean anything, and its influence on any particular molecule may be fully accounted for by that molecule's local collisions; if that is right, does the analogy support the claim that symbols push the substrate around, or does it quietly substitute for semantics something the billiard picture was already content to grant? Tell me too what the analogy imports that the careenium lacks, what the careenium has that the analogy drops, and whether some better comparison exists for the claim he actually wants—that the pattern-level description is causally indispensable rather than merely convenient. If his conclusion survives the audit, say which part of it survives and on what grounds; if the argument only ever went through because the comparison smuggled in the very thing it was supposed to establish, say that plainly.
