I am a high school student who helps run our school's philosophy of religion club, and next month I have been asked to lead a forty-minute session on Maimonides' view of animal sacrifice. I have read some Maimonides in translation and I think I understand the basic idea—that the sacrificial commandments were a kind of pedagogical concession to the Israelites' historical situation, designed to redirect an already familiar practice rather than abolish it outright—but I am not confident I can explain it to peers who have never opened a medieval philosophical text. I do not want an explanation of the view; I want a plan for teaching it. Please act as an expert in medieval Jewish philosophy and in teaching difficult ideas to beginners, and begin by brainstorming the conceptual dependencies: what has to be in place before the concession reading even makes sense to my audience, what order the pieces should come in so that the view feels like a solution to a problem rather than an odd historical claim, and which parts of the standard picture of Maimonides I should bracket at this level. Do not give me readings, citations, or a lecture script.

The one example I want to carry the session is the generation in the wilderness, surrounded by idolatrous cultures and accustomed to expressing worship through sacrifice, but I am not sure whether that example does the work or whether it invites the wrong picture of God as a manipulator. Please tell me what the carrying example must have and lack, and whether I should replace or supplement it. I also need a misconception map: where my peers will likely think Maimonides is saying the sacrifices were a sham, that he is embarrassed by the Torah, that prayer simply replaces sacrifice, or that the concession was only temporary; exactly where in the sequence each of those should be headed off; and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level. Finally, give me one or two questions I can ask at the end to see whether they have understood the difference between saying the command was genuine and saying the practice was intrinsically ideal, along with a brief note on what a good answer would sound like. Think carefully about the order and the misconceptions, since the whole point is that they should leave with the interpretive puzzle rather than a slogan.
