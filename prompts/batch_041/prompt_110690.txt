So I'm in a virtue ethics seminar this term and we just finished the situationist critique — Harman, Doris, the whole person-situation thing, the seminary students in a hurry stepping over the guy slumped in the doorway, Milgram, all of it. And the more of it I read the more I keep tripping over something in my own school. The entire machinery we use to sort people runs on character. Professors write rec letters that say she's honest, he's genuinely kind, she's not the type to cut corners, and everyone involved expects those letters to predict what the person will do in a lab, a dorm, a law firm, some situation nobody has ever seen them in. Admissions does holistic review and treats a transcript and an essay as evidence about who somebody is. The conduct board says things like this is not who we are when they like the student and this shows who he really is when they don't. And then the same institution runs an honor code — pledges you sign, proctors, assigned seating during exams — which is basically a machine for changing the situation, which only makes sense if you think people get pushed around by the situation a lot. And it's the same people. The professor who assigned us Doris is writing the letter.

So what I want to know is what this practice actually presupposes. Not what a virtue ethicist would say about it, I mean what the thing itself commits to. What does the character-reference / holistic-evaluation system really need to be true about people — that traits are global and stable across situations, that they're local to situation types, that they're just social constructs or reputation facts, something else? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Does it need character to be causally efficacious, or only to be something other people reliably respond to? And is it even internally consistent, given that the honor code part looks like an admission that the situation does the work while the blame part looks like it isn't?

I also want you to be honest about where the practice is underdetermined. My suspicion is that a lot of it would still run fine on a much thinner theory of character than the one people probably think they're assuming, and that some of it doesn't need any traits at all, just predictiveness about how third parties will react. Separate that out for me — what's strictly presupposed vs. what merely fits well. And if you did replace the presupposed view with the most defensible situationist-friendly alternative, what concretely changes? What would a letter, an admissions file, or a conduct hearing look like? I don't need a bibliography or anything, just the argument. Also don't just tell me traits are real or unreal, that's not the question — the question is what the practice is betting on.

At the end, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields:

- "features": array of objects, one per feature of the practice you identify, each with "feature" (short description), "commitment" (the view it presupposes), "status" (one of "strictly presupposed", "merely fits well", "underdetermined"), "rivals_ruled_out" (array of short strings, empty if none), "confidence" ("high"/"medium"/"low")
- "internal_inconsistencies": array of objects with "features" (the two or more features in tension), "tension" (one sentence), "severity" (1-5), "cheapest_fix" (one sentence)

- "if_replaced": array of objects with "presupposition_dropped" and "what_changes"
- "bottom_line": 2-3 sentences

Everything else should be prose, and please be specific about which parts of the practice are doing the philosophical work.
