Bit of an odd ask, but here goes. I'm a 4th-year PhD student (finance, though I've been sneaking into the philosophy dept's seminars) and I'm trying to write a case-study chapter on the May 2010 flash crash — you know, May 6, when the Dow fell 998 points, about 9%, in a matter of minutes and then recovered most of it within the hour, with no real news to explain any of it. What I want to do is read that episode through the no-trade theorem and the whole "what does a price actually know" cluster of ideas, and I need someone to actually do the mapping instead of just gesturing at it.

Here's the version of the no-trade idea I'm working with, tell me if I've got it wrong: if all the traders share the same prior, and it's common knowledge that everyone is rational and everyone knows the others know that, and there's no other reason to trade (nobody's hedging, nobody needs liquidity, no risk-sharing motive), then nobody will trade on purely private information. The intuition being that any offer reveals something about the offerer's information, and once you iterate that up to common knowledge the informational edge evaporates. And a cousin result: two people with the same prior can't agree to disagree — if their divergent posteriors are common knowledge, the divergence is impossible.

So run the flash crash through that. Who are the players, what are they even trading on, does the common-prior assumption have any grip at all, and what happens to "common knowledge of rationality" when quotes just vanish and half the market is algos responding to other algos? I want to know which features of the episode the theorem actually explains and which ones it's silent about. My instinct is that it explains almost nothing and that that's the interesting result, but argue me out of it if you can.

Then flip it. What does the crash show about the no-trade framework? Is it a falsification, or does it reveal the theorem was only ever about a world with no liquidity needs and no noise, so it's near-vacuous for real markets — or, more interestingly, does it sort of vindicate it, in that the trades that happened weren't informational at all and the theorem was right that informational trade is impossible?

The epistemology part is where I'm stuck. A price is supposed to be the market's estimate of value, a way of aggregating dispersed information. But during those minutes, things that arbitrage is supposed to lock together — an ETF and the basket it holds, the futures and the index — came apart by absurd amounts, so you had several contradictory "prices" for the same underlying at the same time. Which one is the market's belief? Did the price aggregate information at all, or did it briefly become a mechanical artifact of order flow? And does the fact that it snapped back count for the aggregator picture (the dislocation got arbitraged away, so the mechanism worked) or against it (the mechanism failed first)? Related thing that nags at me: most of the biggest daily moves in stocks historically have no identifiable news attached, which makes me wonder whether the price is ever really doing the epistemic work we say it is, or just most of the time.

And please be honest about where both the theorem and the epistemiology framing run out — I'd much rather have "the framework is silent here" than a forced answer.
