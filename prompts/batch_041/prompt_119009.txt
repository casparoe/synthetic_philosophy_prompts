The question (short essay, 15 pts): Our unit covered three positions on whether the meaning of a text is fixed by what its author meant: actual intentionalism, hypothetical intentionalism, and anti-intentionalism (the intentional fallacy view). Consider this case. A novelist publishes a novel; twenty years later she gives an interview saying the book was a love letter to her hometown and that the ending is meant to be happy. But the ending, on any plain reading of the sentences and the conventions they rely on, is bleak and heavily ironic, and basically every critic reads it that way. (a) Explain how each of the three positions would handle the case. (b) Which position is under the most pressure from the case, and why?

Marcus's answer: All three positions have to decide what to do with the interview. For Wimsatt and Beardsley, who came up with the intentional fallacy, the whole point of their essay is that we shouldnt confuse the poem with the feelings it produces in us, so the author's private intentions, including what she says in interviews, arent what makes the text mean something. The text means what its words and conventions make it mean, and by that standard the ending is bleak and ironic, and the interview doesnt change that. I think the anti-intentionalist would say the interview isnt even evidence, since it's external to the text.

The actual intentionalist, like Hirsch, says the meaning is what the author meant, so the interview is decisive and the critics are just wrong about the book. That seems like the cleanest way to handle it, though it makes the author sound infallible about her own work.

The hypothetical intentionalist says the meaning is what an appropriately informed reader would hypothesize the author to have meant, using the text and whatever public context there is. So on this view both the critics' bleak reading and the author's happy reading are legitimate, since what matters is the reader's hypothesis and different readers will hypothesize different things.

(b) I think the case puts the most pressure on actual intentionalism, because it's the only one that has to say a whole crowd of professional readers is wrong, and it has to explain how the author can say something that her own text doesnt seem to support. The other two can just say the text's public meaning wins.
