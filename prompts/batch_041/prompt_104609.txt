I'm a statistician who designs elicitation mechanisms; the vocabulary I actually think in is the one of proper and improper scoring rules, calibration, the Brier and logarithmic scores, and the elementary result that a proper rule makes honest reporting a weakly dominant strategy. I have an amateur's grip on philosophy of mind, but I want the puzzle below restated in my own terms rather than explained to me in yours.

The puzzle philosophers call the wrong kind of reasons runs roughly as follows. Some considerations give you a reason to hold a propositional attitude — a belief, an intention, an emotion such as admiration or fear — because the attitude would be correct or fitting; other considerations give you a reason to hold the very same attitude because holding it would be good for you or would get you something, without bearing at all on whether the attitude is correct. A threat or a bribe can give you a reason to believe, to admire, or to intend something, yet the reason seems to be of the wrong kind: it does not make the belief true, the admiration warranted, or the intention appropriate. The puzzle is what marks the difference between the two kinds of consideration, and whether an account that explains value in terms of the fittingness of attitudes can survive the fact that both kinds of consideration are, in some sense, reasons to have the attitude.

I want a translation of that puzzle into the vocabulary I use, as faithful as you can make it. Treat an attitude as something like a report or an elicited quantity; treat its correctness or fittingness as something like the accuracy of a report under a scoring rule; treat the prudential, payoff-based consideration as an incentive layered on top. Then work the mapping out in detail. Where does a reason of the right kind correspond to a proper scoring rule, and where does the analogy strain? What does the contrast between proper and improper rules — and the fact that honesty is dominant only under a proper rule — correspond to on the philosophical side? Is the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem simply the problem of an improper rule, or is it something the scoring-rule framework cannot represent at all? And what plays the role of the demon's threat in a design where the only thing an incentive can do is change what an agent reports?

Then give me the residue report. For each element of the puzzle — the correctness of a belief, the fittingness of an emotion, the appropriateness of an intention, the very notion of a reason being of a kind, the possibility of reasons that are reasons for an attitude rather than for an action — say which parts translate cleanly into elicitation and scoring-rule talk, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. Where I would be tempted to conclude that a reason of the wrong kind is just an incentive that shifts behaviour without shifting evidence, tell me what the translation is quietly assuming — in particular, whether it presupposes that attitudes are voluntary or reportable, and whether that presupposition is doing illegitimate work. Finish with your view on whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary, something elicitation and scoring rules are constitutively blind to, or a sign that the philosophers' distinction was less clear than it looked.
