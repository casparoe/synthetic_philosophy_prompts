I have come to this topic from the outside — no training in philosophy, just a few years of reading and thinking on my own — and I have reached the point where I would rather judge the proposals than keep collecting them. The topic is panpsychism and the combination problem: the thought that experience goes all the way down, and the difficulty of explaining how a great many tiny experiences could add up to the single, unified experience of a person. What I want, before I read another attempt, is the specification. Tell me what any adequate account of combination would have to deliver.

Treat the following as a draft of the requirements that I would like you to correct, not as the answer itself.

1. The plain case. A brain whose parts each have some experience, and which itself has one experience. Whatever the account is, it has to say what happens here, and say it without simply announcing that the many become one.

2. The contrast case. There must be a principled difference between a brain and a mere collection — a crowd, a swarm, a galaxy, the whole cosmos — whose parts, if panpsychism is right, also have experiences, yet where there is nothing it is like to be the whole. If the proposed mechanism would fire for any collection of experiencers whatsoever, the account fails, however elegant it is.

3. Selectivity, and its mirror. If two streams of experience can fuse, why do mine and my neighbour's not? And running the process backwards: if you remove parts of a mind one at a time, does the unified experience wink out at a definite point, and what fixes that point — number, arrangement, integration, something else?

4. The fate of the parts. Are the micro-experiences extinguished, absorbed, merged into a new one, or preserved as ingredients? If they are extinguished, the account owes an explanation of why the destruction of subjects is unremarkable here when we treat it as momentous elsewhere. If they are preserved, the account owes an answer to how many subjects there are in my head and why I am aware of only one.

5. The character of the result. Granting a unified macro-subject, why is its experience the way it is — smooth, coloured, spread out in space — rather than a jumble or an average of the micro-feelings? A story about unification that leaves the resulting quality unexplained seems to me to have moved the problem rather than solved it.

6. Consistency with what we know about the parts. The account may not require causal powers that would show up in physics, or contradict what is known about the behaviour of the components.

7. Self-knowledge. Should the theory explain why I cannot introspect the micro-experiences, or should it predict that I could in principle? Either answer seems to commit the view to something, and I want to know which.

8. No hidden assumptions. If the answer is that parts combine when they form a certain organised whole, is it the organisation doing the work or the experiences? If the organisation, I do not see what panpsychism is adding; if the experiences, I want to know how organisation manages to select among them. I would rather the account be explicit than quietly borrow from the physicalist picture it is meant to improve on.

9. Degrees and thresholds. Is combining all-or-nothing, gradual, or subject to a threshold? Does it come in degrees of unification, and if so, what would half a unified mind be like?

Go through the list and tell me which of these are genuine requirements on any account and which are merely the preferences of one camp dressed up as demands. Then say whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect at least one is, and that this is the real result. Finally, name the single requirement that does the most to rule out the answers people actually give. I do not need a survey of who holds what; I need the standard, so that I can tell whether the combination problem is a puzzle to be solved or a refutation in itself.
