I have been arguing about strikes and union drives for years, always one case at a time, and I would rather have the standard before the verdict. So give me the specification rather than a ruling: what would an adequate theory of the right to organize and the right to strike have to deliver? The cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it cannot violate, with the consideration behind each requirement. Mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are merely the commitments of one side in the argument; I will do the scoring against actual theories myself.

Here are the cases I keep running into. A union wins a vote and thereby speaks for everyone in the unit, including those who voted against it and never joined, and it owes something to those people — whatever it owes them has to come from somewhere. A strike harms people who are not parties to the dispute at all: patients, passengers, parents. In many countries workers may lawfully do together what would be a serious offense if firms did it together, namely agree not to sell their labor below a price. The same act is a protected right in one jurisdiction and a dismissal offense in the next, and in some places a striker may be permanently replaced, so that the right remains legally intact and practically hollow. Sometimes a union strikes over its members' objections, and sometimes members strike over the union's. And where the employer is the state, the strike is aimed at the electorate, with the union sometimes helping to decide who sits on the other side of the table.

The distinctions I would not want collapsed: the right to organize, the right to bargain, and the right to strike; an individual's freedom of association and a group's standing as the recognized counterparty; a right and a legal permission; withdrawing one's own labor and preventing others from supplying theirs; the right to withhold one's labor and the right to keep one's job while doing it.

What I most want is the place where the list turns against itself. My suspicion is that the requirement to respect the dissenting worker — that no one be bound by an association they did not join, or made to fund one — sits badly with the requirement that organizing actually deliver power, which seems to need binding majorities, exclusive representation, and money. If those two cannot be jointly satisfied, say so, and describe the least bad ways of trading them off; if they can, tell me what I am missing. And tell me which single requirement does the most work, in the sense of eliminating the most otherwise attractive positions.
