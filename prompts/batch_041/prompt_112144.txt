So I'm in this ad hoc reading group that's been going through Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife, and last week we watched Children of Men as a sort of palate cleanser slash illustration, and now I can't tell wether the film confirms Scheffler or quietly refutes him. Scheffler's setup, as I understand it: you learn that humanity will be wiped out thirty days after your own death. You live out your natural life, nothing gets worse for you personally, there's just no posterity. His claim is that a huge amount of what makes our projects feel worth doing depends on the assumption that people will keep carrying them on, so the doomsday scenario would drain the point out of things even though it adds no suffering to your own life. Children of Men is basically that scenario at feature length: it's 2027, the youngest person on Earth just died at eighteen, the infertility has been going on for about two decades, and everyone currently alive will be dead before the species is. So nobody in the film is around for the actual extinction; they're all living inside the thirty-day window, just stretched out over a lifetime. And yet they keep doing stuff. Theo still goes to his bureaucrat job, Jasper still grows weed and fusses over his house, the government still deports refugees with full bureaucratic seriousness, and Theo's cousin the Minister of Arts is still curating the cultural heritage of a species that will have nobody to inherit it. Is that denial, or is it evidence that Scheffler overstates how much of our practical grip on life is hostage to the future? I keep feeling like the whole conjecture smuggles in an assumption that a project needs an audience to be worth having, and that this is weirdly close to the agent-relative vs agent-neutral value mess, but I can't make it precise.
