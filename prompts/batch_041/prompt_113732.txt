I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I work on distributed systems, so I tend to describe brains as processes sharing a bus, which I'm aware is a metaphor rather than an argument. I've read the popular accounts of split-brain surgery, and I have a set of gut verdicts about how many minds are present in a range of cases. I'd like to know what principle, if any, those verdicts add up to. I'm not asking for a reading list; I want the principle itself, stated as precisely as you can.

My verdicts:

1. The standard case: a patient whose hemispheres were surgically disconnected. Something is flashed to the side that cannot speak; the hand on that side picks out the matching object; the speaking side reports having seen nothing, then offers a confident explanation for why the hand moved. I say two streams of experience are running at once, but only one person. I don't think the explanation is a lie, and I don't hold it against him.

2. Same patient, different moment: an object sits in the middle of the table where both halves can see it, and both hands agree. I say one stream; there's nothing to double-count.

3. A partial case: the hemispheres still share a thin remaining bridge, so some information crosses, but slowly and only sometimes, so the patient can name what the far side saw on some trials and not others. I say one mind with a bad connection. I don't count a second mind here.

4. Alien hand after a stroke, hemispheres intact: one hand does things the patient disowns and restrains with the other hand. I say one mind. The hand is not a second agent; it's a broken part.

5. Blindsight: the patient denies seeing anything in the blind half of the field, yet guesses correctly about where things are. I say one mind. Correct guessing with nothing it's like to guess doesn't add up to a second stream.

6. The test where one hemisphere is put to sleep for a few minutes before epilepsy surgery, so for that stretch only one side is awake and answering. I say one mind throughout. Nobody arrived and nobody left.

7. Invented, but it's the one I keep going back and forth on. The patient in (1) works out a trick: the left hand writes on paper, his eyes read it, and the information reaches the speaking side a second or two late. Some days I say this is one mind that has learned to route around a severed cable. Other days I say it's two minds, one of which has learned to leave notes for the other.

8. Two people with intact brains typing to each other in a chat window, or one person leaving notes for himself. I say nothing has been merged or split; communication on its own doesn't change the count.

My tentative rule is that there is one mind per stream of experience, and that there are two streams exactly when there are experiences no single point of view can hold together. Cases (3) and (4) are where I lose my grip: in (3) I want to say one mind because information still crosses, and in (4) nothing crosses, but the hand's movements carry no experience at all, so there's nothing there to count. I can't state cleanly what separates information that is merely processed from information that is experienced, and my verdicts seem to turn on exactly that.

So: give me the principle, or the two or three competing principles, that come closest to these verdicts. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Tell me what the principle says about cases I haven't listed — invent at least one and run it through. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether I should give that one up. I'd also like to know whether two streams and one person is a stable position or whether one of those counts has to give. And finally: is the principle a claim about how brains are, or a rule we adopt for counting, and if it's a rule, what makes one counting rule better than another? Where does the process-and-bus picture mislead me, if it does?
