I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of social science, and I've been invited to contribute a short commentary to a general-readership forum on the World Happiness Report—the annual country rankings that are widely reported as a league table of national happiness. I have drafted the argument below in note form rather than as finished prose, and I'd like you to act as a demanding but constructive referee. Begin by restating my argument in your own words, so I can see whether it's coming across; then identify what you take to be its two or three weakest points; only after that give line-by-line comments. Please think carefully rather than agreeing by default—I'd rather be told the piece is confused now than in print. You have no internet access, so don't worry about supplying citations or a reading list; where you're unsure whether I've got some empirical detail right, just flag it as something to double-check rather than guessing.

My notes run roughly as follows. The headline ranking, as I understand it, rests on a single self-report item—the so-called Cantril ladder, which asks respondents to place their present life somewhere between the worst possible life and the best possible life they can imagine. The half-dozen explanatory variables the report discusses (income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption) are used to account for the *variation* between countries; they are not the thing being ranked. My first claim is that this makes the ranking a measure of evaluative *satisfaction*—a judgment about how one's life is going—rather than of happiness in any richer sense, and that such judgments are inherently comparative and aspiration-relative. This yields my central and, I think, most original point: because the question is answered against a respondent's own imagined best possible life, a country could in principle climb the table not by improving anyone's condition but by reconciling its citizens to worse prospects. I've been calling this the "lowered-aspiration effect," and I want to distinguish it from the older complaint that self-reports are unreliable, because my point is not that people misreport but that they truthfully report against a deflated benchmark. My second claim is the policy one: asked whether a country should try to top the rankings, I want to say no, but not for the usual slippery-slope reasons. My argument is that the ranking is a composite of what the report itself treats as correlational data, so treating it as a target invites both a Goodhart-style distortion (the measure stops tracking what it was meant to track once it becomes a goal) and what I've labeled the "leaderboard fallacy"—the inference from a position in an ordinal list to the existence of a single lever that moves you up it. I do want to concede that the rankings have done genuine good by pushing well-being onto policy agendas, and I'm unsure how much weight that concession can bear without undercutting my conclusion.

Please comment specifically on the following. First, is the lowered-aspiration effect a real argument or does it collapse into a version of the familiar claim that satisfaction measures are adaptive, and if the former, what would I need to add to keep it distinct? Second, does my move from "this measures satisfaction rather than happiness" to "a country shouldn't chase the ranking" actually follow, or am I quietly switching between an epistemic point about the measure and a normative point about policy? Third, have I described the report's methodology fairly, and if any of my characterizations look off, where should I be more careful? Finally, suggest how to tighten the piece: what should be cut, what's doing no work, and where a concrete illustrative case (say, two imaginary countries with equal material conditions but different aspirations) would earn its place.
