There's this idea I keep circling back to: that what you can imagine is a guide to what's possible. If I can picture something without contradiction, the thinking goes, then it isn't ruled out -- my imagination is a kind of probe for the shape of reality. And then there's the killjoy version, which is that this is a trap. Half the time when we think we're conceiving of something possible we're really just ignorant of the fact that it's impossible, and once we learn more the picture we thought we were having dissolves. People used to think they could imagine water that wasn't made of that stuff; after enough chemistry they couldn't really do it anymore. So the probe is only as good as your knowledge, which seems to eat the whole point -- unless you say the guide only works for a perfect mind and none of us have one, in which case its useless in practice. And the cases people fight hardest about (like the one where you imagine a duplicate of yourself with all the same physics but no inner life, or the reverse) are exactly the ones where it's least clear whether what's going on is genuine conceiving or just a failure to notice a hidden contradiction.

Write me a short story built on that, not a story that name-drops it. I want a character whose life makes the distinction actually matter -- an inventor, a patent examiner, a doctor signing off on what's impossible, a theologian, whatever you like -- and I want the twist to hang on one specific case where the two come apart. The thing I really want is a character who starts keeping track and notices that every time she learns something, something she used to be able to imagine closes off. Getting smarter makes her world smaller. Play that straight: no magic where imagining things makes them real, the constraints are real, her mind is the limited thing. No lecture scenes, no seminar where someone says ah, but is that really conceivable, and no narrator explaining the philosophy to me. The reader should be able to tell from what happens which way the story leans, and I'd like the ending to stay genuinely open about whether her last failure was a failure of possibility or a failure of conception. Under 1500 words.
