I have just spent an evening arguing with a close friend about whether an 'I' can be spread across more than one head, and I would like a poem that puts the case better than I managed to. My friend holds the ordinary view: a self is one organism's business, whatever persists of a person in other people is memory and influence, and to call that persistence a surviving 'I' is a sentimental figure. I hold the view I take from Hofstadter. On it, a self is not a substance but a pattern — a self-referential loop that a brain traces — and a loop of that kind can be traced again, more faintly, in another brain. Two people who know each other deeply therefore each run a low-resolution copy of the other; the copy is neither the original nor nothing; and when one of them dies, a loop that answers to her name keeps turning over, at reduced resolution, in the survivor. My friend takes the reduced resolution to be the refutation. I take it to be the interesting part.

Write me a sestina that shows this rather than asserts it, with the following constraints.

The six end-words should be the household's private vocabulary: six ordinary words — not abstractions like soul, memory, or love — that meant something particular only to the two of them. The poem should let a stranger infer each private sense from the way it is used, without a gloss.

The speaker is the survivor. The rule that every end-word must return in every stanza should carry the further sense that the shared lexicon is what keeps re-instantiating — that the pattern, and not any one word, is the person.

The envoi should be the poem at lower resolution: three lines that gather all six words and compress what the six stanzas said at length. The compression should be the point — the copy in the survivor's head stands to the dead woman as the envoi stands to the sestina.

Let the pronoun 'I', at least once in the survivor's mouth, plainly refer to the dead partner, and do not flag it as a slip or a mistake. A reader who knows the view should recognize this as the claim itself; a reader who does not should register only a small, unremarked shift of ownership.

Steel-man my friend in the poem; it should not be a hymn. The dead woman should not be idealized into a saint, and the copy should be allowed to be lossy in ways that are embarrassing as well as tender.

Do not name Hofstadter or the theory. A specialist in this view should find the poem right rather than merely name-checking it, and someone who has never heard of it should still find it a good poem.
