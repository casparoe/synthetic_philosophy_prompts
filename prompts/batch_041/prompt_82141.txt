Our school philosophy club meets on Thursdays, and each week one of us brings in a pair of sentences for the group to fight about. My sister, who is in the third year of a philosophy degree and takes the club far more seriously than the rest of us do, has appointed herself referee. She rules on one question only: do the two sentences express the same thought, in the sense that someone who accepts one has, simply by accepting it, accepted the other? She gives a verdict and a one-line reason, and she will not let a new verdict contradict an old one.

Her rulings so far:

1. "Hesperus is Hesperus" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus": different thoughts. An astronomer could discover the second; nobody could discover the first, and what can be discovered cannot be the same as what cannot.
2. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" and "Phosphorus is Hesperus": the same thought. Nobody could accept one and reject the other.
3. "The ancients believed that Hesperus was a god" and "The ancients believed that Phosphorus was a god": different thoughts, and the second report can come out false while the first is true, because the two names present one object to the ancients in two different ways.
4. "I am hungry," said by Alina, and "I am hungry," said by Bo: different thoughts, even though the words are identical.
5. "Sam has finished the assignment," said of one of the two Sams in our year, and the same sentence said of the other: different thoughts, even though the words and the name are identical.
6. "The liquid in this glass is water" and "the liquid in this glass is H2O," said to a student who has not yet taken chemistry: different thoughts. She could accept the first while doubting the second.

This week it is my turn, and my case is Juno. Juno's mother works in the school office, where everybody calls her Ms. Whitlock. Juno has known since she could talk that the woman in the office and her mother are one and the same person; the identity has never been a piece of information she had to pick up, and she has never been in a position to be surprised by it. My pair is "Ms. Whitlock will be at the concert" and "my mother will be at the concert," both as said by Juno.

I expect my sister to settle this in about four seconds, and the verdict interests me far less than what it reveals about her rule. So: what will she say? What rules fit everything she has ruled so far — I want at least two that fit her record exactly, and they had better disagree somewhere — what does each of them predict about Juno's pair, which one is she most likely running and why, and what should I bring the following week to force her to choose between them? If her record genuinely does not settle the Juno case, say so plainly, and tell me what she would be committing herself to with each possible verdict.
