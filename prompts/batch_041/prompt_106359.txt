ok so I've been going in circles with a friend about Benatar's asymmetry, and I've built what I think is an airtight arguement that lands somewhere I can't accept, and now I can't tell if the argument is broken or the conclusion is and I'm just being squeamish about it. I need you to find the seam.

The asymmetry, as I understand it (paraphrasing): the absence of pain is good, even when theres nobody around to enjoy that good; the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there's somebody who exists and is deprived by it. That's supposed to get you: coming into existence is always a harm, because a nonexistent person's state has the good (no pain) and none of the bad (no pleasure isn't bad), while an existing person's state has both. So never being born always beats being born, however good the life. Fine, that's the standard move.

Here's my reconstruction, then the blowup.

(1) A state of affairs with no suffering in it is, in itself, better than one with suffering, even if nobody exists in the suffering-free one. [the absence-of-pain horn]
(2) A state of affairs with no pleasure in it is, in itself, not worse than one with pleasure, so long as there's nobody who exists and is deprived. [the absence-of-pleasure horn]
(3) So a person who never exists has a state that is better (by 1) and not worse (by 2) than the state where they exist with any mix of pleasure and pain.
(4) So it's better for that person never to have existed, whatever their life would have been like.
(5) So bringing anyone into existence is wrong, or at least always worse. Antinatalism, done.

(6) Now run (1) and (2) on somebody who's alive and doing okay, and consider their dying. Once they're dead there's no suffering in the world on their account, which by (1) is good in itself; and there's no pleasure either, which by (2) is not bad, because after they're gone the only candidate for the deprived party is the very person who no longer exists.
(7) So by the same two horns, their dying is better in itself and not worse than their staying alive.
(8) So it's better for that person to die, whatever their remaining life would have been like -- and by the same step as (5), it's wrong or at least worse for them to keep living.

Which is nuts. It says every happy person should drop dead, and it's not some extra premise I smuggled in, (1) and (2) are doing all of the work. And nobody in this debate actually goes there -- the standard move is that the asymmetry is about coming into existence and says nothing about killing or dying -- but I can't see what in (1) and (2) is supposed to know that difference, since both cases are just "the person isn't around anymore." If I keep (1) and (2) I seem stuck with (8); if I cut either one I lose (5) and there's no asymmetry left to do the antinatalist work.

So which step do I cut, why does it look so innocent at the time, and what does cutting it cost me? I'd rather keep (5) than (8) if it comes to that, but I want to know the price. Also tell me if the real problem is earlier -- whether (3) or (4) already slides from "better in itself / better simpliciter" to "better for that person," and whether that same slide is what's bugging me in (6)-(8) or a seperate thing. And please don't just tell me the asymmetry is unmotivated -- I know that's a move, I want to know if it's the move here.
