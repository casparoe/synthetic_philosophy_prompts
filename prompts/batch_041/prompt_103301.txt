Two friends of mine from work got into an argument last week about Pascal, and I've been turning it over since without being able to settle it. Neither of them is a philosopher by training; one is a product manager, the other a backend engineer who reads a lot. I was a bystander, and I don't have a horse in this race, so I'd rather have the arguments themselves evaluated than get a general summary of Pascal.

Here's how it went.

(1) She opened by saying Pascal is a cynic dressed up as a moralist. Her evidence was the sequence she remembers from the Pensées: justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyranny, and therefore we must join the two — and since nobody could make what is just strong, we settled for making what is strong just. On her reading, everything after that is decoration. Justice, for Pascal, is whatever the established power enforces; his line about real justice is just the confession that he can't have it. He's Thrasymachus with better prose.

(2) He said that gets the direction of the argument backwards. Pascal plainly does think there's a genuine justice, he said — that's why he complains we can't see it, why he says we should follow what is more just, and why he keeps pointing at how custom and self-interest distort our judgment of it: the same thing lawful on one side of a river and criminal on the other, the child's claim that the sunbeam and the patch of ground are his, the whole catalogue of usurpation. His view, on my friend's account, is that real justice exists but is unreachable in this life, so force has to underwrite whatever justice we do manage to establish. That's not the same claim as power makes right.

(3) She came back with two things. First, Pascal tells us that justice is whatever is established, and that established laws are to be treated as just without being examined, precisely because they are established — if that isn't the reduction, what would be? Second, she said his three-tier picture of how people regard the great looks like a defense of going along with the status quo: the ordinary person reveres rank, the half-clever person sneers at it, and the wise person honors it rightly. If the wise person's reason for honoring it isn't its justice, she said, then the real justice he keeps invoking is doing no work anywhere in the system, and the sneering half-clever person is the only honest one in the room.

(4) He said that's a misreading of the tiers. Pascal's wise man honors established greatness while keeping the orders of greatness separate — he doesn't credit a duke with the greatness that belongs to a mind or to a saint. And Pascal is emphatic that rank and property rest on human institution, not on nature or merit; he even stages a thought experiment where a man is washed ashore on an island and mistaken for the lost king, and says the man must hold two thoughts at once: one by which he acts as king, and one by which he knows what he actually is. My friend's version: the wise man gives the established order exactly what it is owed, deference rather than esteem, and no more, and that presupposes a standard above the order rather than collapsing into it.

(5) She said that what the order is owed is exactly the contested question, and that a two-thought doctrine is still a doctrine of compliance. If the wise man's backstage thought never changes what he does, she doesn't see how it's more than a private consolation prize.

They left it there, both convinced the other had conceded the point.

What I'd like is a point-by-point adjudication of moves (1) through (5): for each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why. Then tell me what each of them is missing that the other sees, and who is closer to right overall. I'm especially interested in whether Pascal's position is actually coherent — can he hold both that there is a real justice and that the wise obey established law as such — or whether one of those has to give way, and if it gives way, which one Pascal himself would sacrifice. I don't need a bibliography; I need the arguments sorted out.
