ok so I help run a forecasting competition (basically a prediction tournament) and I've been trying to figure out what we're actually committed to philosophically, because something about it feels off and I can't put my finger on it.

Heres the setup. Every question is like "will X happen by date Y." Each forecaster gives a number from 0 to 1. At the end we grade everyone with a proper scoring rule -- the kind where you're rewarded for being both confident and right, and where the way to maximize your expected score is just to honestly report whatever you actually think. We pay out the top scores. We also, in the same breath, tell people that the best forecasters are the well-calibrated ones -- you know, when they say 70% it happens about 70% of the time -- and we run little workshops to improve people's calibration.

The thing is calibration and accuracy arent the same thing. I can be perfectly calibrated and useless (just say the base rate every time), and I can be very accurate on the questions that matter and still miscalibrated overall. So which one is our practice actually treating as the good? Are we assuming a credence can be "correct" to a degree, so that 0.7 is closer to the truth when the thing happens? Or are we really just training people to track long-run frequencies? The whole setup seems to quietly assume something about what a credence even is -- like it's a gradable, right-or-wrong-able mental state with a degree of accuracy -- and I want to know what that assumption rules out.

Also, our scoring is per-question and local: your score on question 5 doesn't depend on your scores on 1-4, or on whether your whole set of forecasts hangs together, or on whether you understood the domain at all. So I guess we're presupposing that the only thing that matters about a set of credences is how accurate each one is individually, and nothing holistic -- no coherence, no explanatory fit, no understanding. Is that something we're actually committed to, or just a convenient way to rank people on one leaderboard? Would a view where understanding or explanation is *also* an epistemic good even fit a single-number score?

I dont know the jargon here well. What I want is: what theory (or theories) does this practice presuppose, which rival theories does it quietly rule out, which of those commitments are actually load-bearing versus just design choices, and where the whole thing is underdetermined -- like several different views of what credences are could all license the same leaderboard. And if we swapped the implicit view for a pluralist one, what would actually have to change about the competition?
