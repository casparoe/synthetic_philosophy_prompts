I am a novelist who teaches a small evening seminar on perception and skepticism for continuing-education students, and I am currently grading the midterm essays. The question I set was this: Explain the argument from illusion and assess whether disjunctivism offers an adequate response to it. In your answer, reconstruct the argument's central premise or premises, distinguish metaphysical disjunctivism from epistemological disjunctivism, and say whether the disjunctivist denial of the common-kind thesis is enough to answer the argument or whether it merely changes the subject. One of my students, whose work is usually careful, submitted the following answer.

Student answer:

The argument from illusion begins from cases in which a perceiver is appeared to in a way that is subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception, but in which no ordinary object is present, or not present with the properties it appears to have. Since the perceiver's experience can be exactly the same in the illusory and veridical cases, the argument concludes that what the perceiver is directly aware of cannot be the ordinary object; it must be a mental item, such as a sense-datum. The argument therefore threatens direct realism, because it seems to show that we never directly perceive ordinary objects, only our own ideas or sense-data.

Disjunctivism responds by rejecting the common-kind thesis: the claim that veridical perception and illusion or hallucination are experiences of the same fundamental kind. For the disjunctivist, a veridical perception is not a case of the same kind as a hallucination, even if the two are subjectively indistinguishable. So the argument's inference from indistinguishability to sameness of kind is invalid. Metaphysical disjunctivism makes this claim about the nature of the experiences themselves; epistemological disjunctivism makes a parallel claim about the epistemic reasons available to the subject. According to epistemological disjunctivism, in the good case the subject's perceptual experience gives her a reason for belief that is not available in the bad case, so she can know the ordinary object is there even though her evidence is not subjectively distinguishable from the bad case. I think epistemological disjunctivism is the stronger view, because it directly addresses the skeptic. It also entails metaphysical disjunctivism, since if the good case gives a different reason, the experiences themselves must be different kinds.

The main problem for disjunctivism is that it seems to deny the obvious fact that hallucinations are experiences. If a hallucinating subject is not having an experience at all, then the view is implausible. But if hallucinations are experiences, then the common-kind thesis seems true again. So disjunctivism either denies something obvious or collapses into the view it opposes. A better response to the argument from illusion is to accept that experiences are representations and then argue that representations can be accurate or inaccurate without there being any special mental object. This preserves direct realism about the objects of perception while acknowledging the argument's insight about subjectivity.
