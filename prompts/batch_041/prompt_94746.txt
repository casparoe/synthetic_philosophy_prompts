I am writing a novel in which a professional gambler and a philosopher of science are the two characters I care most about, and I need a scene in which they argue their way through a wager neither can walk away from. The wager is Bertrand's chord problem in its plainest form: a chord is drawn at random in a circle, and the bet is on whether its length exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. The gambler has been offered the bet by a house that will not say how it draws its chords, and there are three familiar ways of drawing one — endpoints fixed independently on the circumference, a random radius with a random point on it, a midpoint taken uniformly over the disk — which give three different answers, a third, a half, and a quarter. The house's procedure sits in a sealed envelope. That is the whole of the scene's furniture.

What I need is the argument between them, and I need it to be a genuine argument rather than two monologues wearing hats. Let the gambler be the one who insists the problem has a determinate answer, and let him argue it from symmetry: the circle does not care which way is up, nothing in the statement of the bet privileges one direction or one scale over another, and once those symmetries are treated as constraints on the odds rather than as decoration, they narrow the three procedures to one. He should commit to which one, and show his work. Let the philosopher be the one who refuses him: she should hold that at-random is an unfinished instruction, that a probability is a fact about an arrangement — the hand, the table, the arm that throws — and that the symmetries the gambler invokes are either physical facts about the apparatus, in which case they are empirical and not a priori, or else they are a metaphysical preference dressed up as mathematics. She should also be able to say what a fair coin's fairness consists in without helping herself to a principle of indifference.

Then I want the argument to go one step past where I have seen it go. Neither of them knows what is in the envelope, so let one of them propose the obvious escalation: be indifferent among the three procedures themselves, average them, or weight them equally, and bet the resulting number. The other must say what is wrong with that move, or defend it, and this is where I want the scene to earn its keep. Does indifference at a higher level fix anything, or does it only postpone the trouble; and if it only postpones it, is there a level at which the regress stops, or does the principle have to be given up at the bottom? Let the philosopher turn the same weapon on the symmetries: a circle drawn on paper is never exactly round, a hand that throws a straw is never exactly unbiased, and an argument that depends on perfect symmetry appears to depend on facts that do not exist. The gambler should answer her rather than deflect. I suspect the answer has something to do with the difference between not knowing a bias and having no information at all, but I want him to find it, not to recite it.

Both of them should be sharp, and neither should be permitted a cheap victory. Each should grant the other a real point and then show what it costs: if the philosopher is right, the gambler wants to know how he is ever to price a bet whose apparatus he cannot inspect; if the gambler is right, the philosopher wants to know what privileges one parameterization of the chord space over another except the answer it happens to deliver. Let them interrupt, concede, and revise, and let each concession change what the other says next.

Write the scene as dialogue, roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, in a register that can be read aloud. Neither of them should cite anyone or appeal to authority; the argument has to stand on its own legs. Get the arithmetic right wherever they do any, and let them be precise about what at-random is being asked to mean. End with the disagreement narrowed to a single question they can both state — not dissolved, and not shrugged off — and make sure that by the last line the reader understands why the same physical fact can carry three prices.
