My roommate Dan and I got into a thing last week over this cheap gadget his dad gave us. It's one of those little boxes you screw into your mailbox that beeps in the house when the mail shows up. Turns out it isn't hooked to the door at all — it's just a light sensor. So it beeps when the mailman opens the lid, but it also beeps at night when a car's headlights sweep the box, and sometimes when the porch light flickers. We get false beeps constantly.

Dan says the box thinks there's mail when there isn't. It's just wrong a lot, he says, like a smoke alarm going off from burnt toast. His point was that being unreliable doesn't make it meaningless — the box's "mail's here" state is real, it's just badly calibrated, and that's no different from the rest of us being wrong about stuff all the time.

Priya says no, the box doesn't think anything, and it can't be wrong either. The only thing its little state is about is light — that's what makes it flip. Saying the headlight beeps are "false alarms about mail" is us reading our own idea into a piece of plastic. And she says if it really could be mistaken about the mail, there'd have to be some fact about what it's *supposed* to be tracking, and the only candidate for that is what the maker or the buyer meant by it — which is our meaning, borrowed, not the box's.

Dan came back with: fine, then my smoke detector doesn't detect smoke, and neither do I — my nose just reacts to particles, and "smoke" is our word for it. If you keep going that way, nothing means anything except by our say-so, which seems like a crazy place to end up. He also said the box is set up to text his phone the words "you've got mail," and asked whether that text is about the mail or not. If a string of words only means whatever a person reads into it, he says, then no sentence means anything either.

Priya said the difference is the box has nothing at stake. She can be wrong about smoke and it matters to her, she can try to get it right, she can be embarrassed about it. The box can't be wrong in that way, because there's nothing it's trying to get right. Meaning needs somebody on the hook.

Dan said "on the hook" is doing all the work there, and that a dog waiting at the door can be wrong about who's coming up the walk and clearly cares about it, and you wouldn't say the dog is just borrowing your meaning. He also brought up that our box isn't a plain wire anymore — the newer ones adjust themselves, and ours has supposedly learned to ignore the porch light flicker by now. So it's got its own habits, not just a tripwire.

Priya said a box that learns to stop jumping at the porch light is just a knob turning, like a thermostat that keeps the room steadier, and that Dan was quietly smuggling a whole inner life into the explanation of a beep.

I honestly couldn't referee this, and they're both still annoyed. Dan also kept saying it was basically some famous thought experiment he half-remembers from a philosophy class about a vending machine that can't tell a fake coin from a real one, which I don't know helps or hurts. Can you go through the back and forth and say who's right about which specific point — not just who wins overall — and what each of them is missing?
