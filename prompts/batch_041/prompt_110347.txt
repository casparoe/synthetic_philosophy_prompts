I have just moved onto a team working on scalable oversight, and I am struck by how completely our weekly argument has stalled. What we fight about is a recursive picture — my colleagues call it HCH — in which a person facing a question too hard for her breaks it into sub-questions, answers the easy ones herself, and hands the rest to copies of herself, iterating until every leaf is something one person can settle in a sitting, with the root answer read off the finished tree. One camp treats this as an idealization of deliberation: roughly what we would converge on given unlimited thinking time without losing the thread, so that the live questions are how closely a buildable system approximates it and where it fails. The other camp says the ideal is not even well-defined — that the requirement that each leaf be settleable by one person in a sitting presupposes the very notions of a well-formed question and of a unit of cognitive work that the picture was supposed to explain, and that the recursion halts only where the builder chooses to stop, so the fixed point is either nonexistent or is whatever the builder wanted it to be. Same papers, same examples, no movement in months, and both sides keep producing what look to me like real arguments.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that this is two disputes under one heading: an engineering question about what to build, and a conceptual question about what kind of object the ideal is, with the second being treated as though it could settle the first. Diagnose the disagreement from what each side actually says — substantive, verbal, or two camps answering different questions — and tell me what would count as progress here (a formal result, a distinction, a change of question) or why none should be expected. I want a committed verdict with reasons, not a survey of the options left for me to choose among.
