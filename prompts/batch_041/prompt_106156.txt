The question was worth 20 points, and the setup, more or less straight off the sheet, was this:

Consider a risk score S in [0,1] and a binary outcome Y (say, rearrest within two years), with a group label G taking values A and B. Call S calibrated within groups if for every group g and every value s we have P(Y = 1 | S = s, G = g) = s. Call S balanced for the negative class if the conditional distribution of S given Y = 0 is identical in groups A and B, and balanced for the positive class if the conditional distribution of S given Y = 1 is identical in the two groups. Assume the two groups have different base rates (P(Y = 1 | G = A) is not equal to P(Y = 1 | G = B)) and that the score is nontrivial (it is not constant and it is not a perfect predictor). A vendor tells the court that while a deterministic tool cannot meet all three requirements at once, their tool does, because it randomly flips a coin to break ties and thereby smooths out the score distributions. (a) Is the vendor right? Justify your answer. (b) What, if anything, does your answer imply for whether such a tool may legitimately be used as evidence at sentencing?

The student's answer: they restate the three conditions correctly and note that calibration pins the score to the true conditional risk while the two balance conditions constrain the score distributions within each outcome class, so with unequal base rates the requirements pull apart — a group with the higher base rate will tend to receive higher scores even among the Y = 0 cases, which is exactly what balance for the negative class forbids. They then argue that the deterministic impossibility is really an artifact of hard thresholds, and that a randomized classifier escapes it: by mixing two deterministic classifiers with different error profiles and a suitable coin weight, you can nudge the group-wise score distributions until the negative-class and positive-class distributions line up, while calibration is preserved because the coin flip doesn't change what the score means for any given individual. So their answer to (a) is yes, the vendor is right. For (b) they say that because the vendor's tool satisfies all three definitions of fairness at once, the earlier fairness objection is resolved, and the only thing left to argue about is predictive accuracy — and since one can always trade some accuracy for more fairness, the fact that the tool also holds up on accuracy means the vendor has come out ahead on both counts.
