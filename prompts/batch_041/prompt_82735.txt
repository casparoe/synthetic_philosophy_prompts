Okay, so this has been rattling around my head for about a week and I keep flip-flopping, so I'm outsourcing it.

Setup. I'm sympathetic to the idea that not every credence has to be a number. Show me an urn with 100 balls in it and tell me nothing at all about the mix. Then show me a second urn that's definitely 50 red and 50 black. I don't think the honest response to the first urn is to shrug and say 50/50 — the honest response is a range, a whole set of probability functions, every one of them compatible with what I actually know. I've absorbed this from enough rationalist blogs that I'd be sad to give it up.

Then my housemate (philosophy grad student, reads alot of decision theory) put the following on our whiteboard and said it kills the whole idea stone dead. I can't find the bad step and it's bothering me.

(1) A credence that isn't a single number is a set of probability functions. Call it a spread.
(2) When a spread has to choose an action, it picks the action whose worst case is best: for each action, work out how it does under every function in the spread, take the worst of those, then take the action with the highest worst case.
(3) So whatever the spread tells you to do, there is some member of the spread that would tell you to do the same thing.
(4) So you could swap the spread out for one of its members and your behaviour would be identical. The other functions in the set are doing no work.
(5) Or the spread tells you to do nothing at all — no action comes out ahead.
(6) But a mental state that doesn't tell you what to do isn't a credence. Credences are for deciding with. An attitude that leaves the decision open isn't a view about the odds, it's a confession that you haven't got one.
(7) So imprecise credences are either sharp credences in disguise, or they aren't credences. Either the framework is idle or its confused.

She also claims the argument doesn't depend on the rule in (2) — she says you get the same result if you use the rule where any action that's optimal for at least one member of the spread is allowed. I haven't checked that and I'm not sure I'd know how.

I'd like to keep (1), and I'd like to keep the negation of (7), but honestly I care more about not having a false step sitting in my own reasoning than about which side wins.

So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine to me in the first place, and what does giving it up actually cost? If you think every step survives, say that instead, and tell me what it means for (1). I'm not asking who said what or where to read more — I just want to see the machinery.
