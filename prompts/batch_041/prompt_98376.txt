I work in the statistics office of a small country, and every spring the international happiness rankings arrive and become a political event: ministers want to know how we can climb them, and journalists want to know whether the number means anything. The rankings are built largely from a single life-evaluation question, together with a regression that parcels the score out among income, social support, health, freedom, generosity and perceived corruption, and I need a reusable procedure we can run on any proposal that promises to raise our score — grounded in what the measurement literature and the philosophy of wellbeing actually establish, rather than in the rhetoric of the tables. Please set out the steps in order: what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than measurement. It has to be something two analysts can run in an afternoon from published data, with no new survey, and it has to survive being shown both to a minister who wants a yes and to an academic who thinks the whole exercise is meaningless. Say clearly which steps quietly encode a contestable view of what happiness is — that it is a single cardinal magnitude, comparable across people and cultures, that a government may legitimately try to maximise — so that we can see where the politics enters. Then run it on three cases: a tax incentive for charitable giving aimed at the generosity component; a push for faster GDP growth; and a programme to cut long commutes and mental-health waiting times, which our own affect data suggest would do more for how people actually feel. Include at least one case where the procedure tells the minister something he will not want to hear, and be honest about what the procedure cannot decide.
