I am midway through a long essay on populism and the idea of a popular will, and a friend in my seminar has handed me an argument that I have now been carrying around for a week, because I cannot decide whether it is a reductio of populism or a reductio of the entire vocabulary of democratic legitimacy, including the parts of it I am not prepared to give up. The seminar's topic that week was whether a multitude can be said to have a single will at all, and the argument runs as follows. (1) A decision is democratically legitimate only if it can be attributed to the will of the people, rather than to the will of whoever happens to hold office. (2) The will of the people is not the same thing as the verdict of any particular procedure: majorities are made by agenda-setting, referendum results by the wording of the question, deliberative outcomes by the framing of the brief, and there is no procedure-independent yardstick by which to say which of these verdicts the people really willed. (3) So whenever an institution declares that it has ascertained the popular will, it has at best produced a verdict that may or may not answer to the real thing. (4) The claim we are treating as populism's core is that legitimacy resides in the popular will itself, and not in the procedures and counter-majoritarian bodies that claim to mediate or constrain it. (5) But if the popular will is never the same as the output of any procedure, then no institution can be entitled to act in its name, and every claim to speak for the people is a usurpation -- including the claim made by elections. (6) Hence populism can consistently authorise nothing at all, not even the governments that call themselves populist; and any theory that locates legitimacy in the will of the people stands in the same position. (7) Therefore we should abandon the idea that legitimacy consists in the will of the people and look elsewhere for its foundation.

To force the point we invented a case for the seminar: a referendum whose question nobody disputes, held on a day when nobody is disenfranchised, intimidated or misled, returning fifty-two to forty-eight; a year later the same electorate, with no change in circumstances and no new information, is asked the same question with the clauses on the ballot reversed, and returns forty-eight to fifty-two. Both results are perfectly good facts about what that electorate preferred under those descriptions. The question is whether either of them is a fact about what the people willed, and whether it makes sense to say that the second corrects the first. My friend thinks the argument is airtight and that the conclusion is simply the price of honesty about mass democracy. My own suspicion falls on (2), but I cannot see how to deny it without also denying that a people can ever be misrepresented, bypassed or lied to by those who claim to speak for it, which seems obviously false; and if I am made to choose, I would rather keep (2) than (5).

What I would like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on populism. Which step should be rejected -- a premise, or the inference from the premises to (5) -- and what exactly is the error in it? Why did that step look so plausible in the first place? And what does rejecting it cost: does the price fall only on the populist, or would I have to pay it too, given that I want to go on saying that a government which has lost the confidence of the people has lost something real, and that constitutional rules themselves derive their authority from somewhere? I am less interested in being told that the conclusion is absurd than in being told precisely where the rot starts and who ends up paying for the repair.
