Construct a thought experiment in which a subject rationally believes a Moorean conjunction of the omissive form — a conjunction whose first conjunct is an ordinary proposition *p* and whose second conjunct is the subject's own lack of belief in *p*, on the schema "*p*, but I do not believe that *p*". The point of the exercise is to test the thesis that such conjunctions are not merely unassertable but strictly unbelievable, so the case must make the belief itself, and not just the utterance, come out rational.

Specifications:

(i) The setting must be realistic — a hospital, clinic, workplace, or domestic scene — not a science-fiction scenario; no demons, telepathy, perfect predictors, or brains in vats. A clinical or medical setting is welcome provided the phenomenon invoked is a real one.

(ii) The subject must have attended to *p* and must possess strong evidence for it, so the case cannot be bought cheaply through inattention, forgetting, or her never having considered the matter.

(iii) "Believe" must carry a single sense across both conjuncts. You may not slide between occurrent and dispositional belief, or between a credence threshold and all-out belief, nor may the second conjunct be read as a claim about what she would say or how she is disposed to behave, unless you argue that the distinction is doing genuine explanatory work rather than rescuing the case.

(iv) Her rationality about the second conjunct must not rest on her being systematically unreliable about her own mental states in a way that would, by parity, undermine her belief in the first.

(v) The scenario itself must fit in a single paragraph.

Then, in no more than three further paragraphs, supply the demonstration. (a) State exactly what she believes, what evidence she has for each conjunct, and why believing the whole conjunction is rational for her. (b) Determine whether both conjuncts are true in your case; if one is not, say which and explain why that outcome is forced by the fact that she believes the conjunction at all, rather than merely an artifact of how you wrote the case. (c) Explain why her asserting the same sentence would nevertheless be absurd, and locate the absurdity precisely — in the sincerity condition on assertion, in the presuppositions assertion carries, in the self-defeating character of the speech act, or wherever you judge it to belong. (d) Give the parallel treatment of the commissive form, "*p*, but I believe that not-*p*", and say whether your device can be adapted to make that form rationally believable too; if it cannot, identify exactly what blocks the adaptation. (e) Name the single feature of your case that is doing the load-bearing work, say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and describe how a defender of the opposing view would try to defuse your example — for instance by arguing that believing *p* carries with it a disposition to avow that one believes *p*, so that the second conjunct, once believed, undercuts the first.

This is for a graduate seminar on the paradox, where the case is meant to settle whether the impossibility is doxastic or pragmatic. A case that stipulates the subject's rationality instead of deriving it from her evidence and her situation will not serve the purpose.
