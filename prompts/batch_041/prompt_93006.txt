I have been working my way through Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I have stalled on the discussion of the location of meaning — the idea that a message's content is not sitting in the marks themselves but is conferred by a frame and a decoder, so that the same inscription can carry different contents depending on how it is read, and a text can bear an outer message, about how it is to be read, in addition to the inner message it states. Since I spend my working life around file formats, this reads to me like the gap between a payload and the header that declares what the payload is: the bytes do not announce their own interpretation, and a block of data with the wrong leading identifier is just noise.

I would like a poem that enacts this rather than explains it, and I would like the structure of the poem, not its commentary, to do the work. Please write a single poem of roughly twenty to twenty-four lines, in three movements, under the following constraints.

First, an opening frame of no more than four lines, spoken in the voice of the container or the format itself rather than in the voice of a person. It must serve as the outer message, telling the reader how what follows is to be read, but it must never state, in so many words, that it is a message or that this is how to read it.

Second, an inner movement of ten to fourteen lines presenting one concrete scene or sequence of events. The scene must be genuinely ambiguous between two readings: the same described facts have to admit two incompatible interpretations until the closing movement resolves them.

Third, a closing movement of no more than six lines that adds no new content of its own and instead re-reads the first two, settling on one of the two interpretations. The act of reading, and not any fresh material, must be what decides the meaning.

Two further requirements. A single line must appear verbatim twice, once inside the inner movement and once in either the frame or the closing movement, and the surrounding frame must force that identical line to mean two different things on its second appearance; it would be best if the two occurrences touched, the line closing the inner movement and opening the closing one, so that the two readings sit side by side. And some figure or device must appear within the poem whose act of decoding — reading, playing, executing, translating, listening — is what confers the meaning, so that the poem locates the meaning in that transaction rather than in the marks.

The result should satisfy two different readers at once: someone who knows the location-of-meaning discussion ought to recognize the poem as getting the distinction right, without the poem ever naming it, and someone who does not know it ought still to find the poem worth rereading, since the rereading is the whole point.
