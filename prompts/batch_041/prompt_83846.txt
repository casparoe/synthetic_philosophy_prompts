I'm a science journalist — behavioural econ, mostly, plus the philosophy-of-mind stuff that keeps leaking into it — and a friend sent me an arguement from a seminar on intertemporal choice that I've been chewing on for a week. It's not about anything exotic. It's the ordinary case of staying up till 1am eating the cake you swore off at breakfast. But the conclusion is so absurd that I assume something in the chain is doing something dirty, and I cannot see where. Numbered the way he sent it:

1. Weakness of will is acting against your own better judgment.
2. At any given moment your better judgment just is your strongest preference at that moment — the option you want most, all things considered.
3. When you cave, you take the cake. Nobody picks what they want less when they could have what they want more.
4. So when you cave, you're doing what you most want to do at that moment.
5. So you aren't acting against your own better judgment.
6. So there's no weakness of will, only a preference reversal. 8am-you ranks the diet above the cake, 11pm-you ranks the cake above the diet, and 11pm-you happens to be at the controls. Calling that a failure of will is like calling Tuesday's weather a failure of Monday's forecast.
7. The only way to get back a single "you" whose judgment could be betrayed is to aggregate the time-slices — to build one ranking out of what each of them wants.
8. But you can't. Aggregating several internally consistent rankings into one ranking that respects each of them and lets no single slice dictate to the rest is provably impossible; it's the same structure as the proof that no voting rule can satisfy a few obviously reasonable conditions without handing someone a dictatorship. So "what I really want" either just is what 11pm-you wants — and then nothing has gone wrong — or it's a slice we've arbitrarily enthroned, in which case we haven't discovered the real you, we've appointed one.
9. So akrasia isn't a real phenomenon. The word names an illusion we get by quietly switching which time-slice we're speaking for.
10. Which is nuts. Precommitment apps, gym contracts, "I wanted not to," the very specific flavour of regret where you were there and you watched yourself do it — none of that is an illusion, and a theory on which regret is a category error has proved way too much.

The two people I've asked both think it's step 3 (inferring the preference from the choice makes the whole thing unfalsifiable and quietly assumes what it's meant to prove), or else step 8 (the dilemma is fake, because a person isn't a parliament of time-slices, she's her plans and standing intentions). Neither of them has actually thought it through and I don't trust either answer. I'd rather keep 1 and 2 than give them up — I like the parsimony, the idea that your better judgment is just a preference with a longer time horizon, and the whole hyperbolic-discounting picture where a sophisticated chooser knows she'll cave and wants the fridge locked in advance — so if the fix costs me one of those, tell me what I'm paying.

Give me your answer as JSON and nothing outside the braces, with these fields:

- "diagnosis": one of "broken_step" or "every_step_survives_but_one_is_too_expensive_to_keep"
- "offending_step": the number, as an integer
- "why_it_looked_fine": two or three sentences
- "cost_of_dropping_it": two or three sentences
- "akrasia_after_the_repair": what weakness of will turns out to be once the fix is made, or whether it survives as a real phenomenon at all
- "alternatives": an array of at most three objects, each with "step" (integer), "why_people_blame_it", and "what_dropping_it_costs"
- "what_the_repair_leaves_untouched": the bit of the original worry this fix doesn't solve
