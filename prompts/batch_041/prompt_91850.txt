I've spent a long time in the rationalist blogosphere — Sequences-era LessWrong, the decision-theory wars, all of it — and I've recently landed in a graduate seminar on archaeological inference, where a slogan keeps getting deployed in ways that I suspect nobody has actually cashed out: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." I want a full disambiguation before the next meeting.

Here's the episode. A classmate argued that because the excavated sample from a particular settlement contains no trace of some practice — I'm keeping the details generic, since the same pattern recurs across cases — the practice probably didn't exist there. The professor replied with the slogan and moved on. Later in the same session, another student defended a different claim by asserting that absence of evidence is evidence of absence. Both statements got nods. I've internalized enough Bayesian machinery to be suspicious of that pair of nods, but I don't trust myself to say exactly where the sleight of hand is, because in some framings the professor is obviously right and in others the classmate is.

Act as a philosopher of science with genuine expertise in Bayesian confirmation theory and in the epistemology of archaeology — specifically the inference from taphonomy, preservation bias, sampling design, and formation processes to claims about past beliefs and institutions. Think carefully and work in stages: first brainstorm, listing every distinct reading of the slogan you can generate, including the bad ones; only then organize and evaluate. Don't stop at two or three readings.

What I want back:

1. A menu of precise claims. For each reading, state what it commits you to in terms of probabilities, quantifiers, and scope — whether it is a claim about likelihoods, about the possibility of proof, about the evidence-gathering procedure, or about what a community should believe. Mark each as trivially true, substantive and defensible, false, or ambiguous-but-repairable. Then say which reading the professor almost certainly meant, and whether that reading actually licenses dismissing the classmate.
2. The mirror slogan, read the same way, plus a verdict on which readings of the two are genuine contraries and which turn out to be compatible.
3. How 'you can't prove a negative' fits in: the same claim, a corollary, or something else entirely.
4. An assessment of a distinction I've been groping toward — record-absence versus world-absence, where the first is a fact about what the excavation and its detection profile could have turned up and the second is a fact about the past. Tell me whether that's the right cut, how you would sharpen it, and what the analogue of a detection profile is when the evidence is texts or standing architecture rather than bones and lithics.
5. A short list of diagnostic questions I can ask in the moment — the ones that would force whoever deploys the slogan to commit to a reading rather than retreating into vagueness.

No citations or reading lists, please; I'm after the argument, not the literature, and I would rather you reconstruct it from first principles than gesture at authorities. Write in American English, and flag anywhere you're genuinely unsure rather than papering over it.
