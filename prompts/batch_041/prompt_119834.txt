I retired from a chemistry lab three years ago and I've been catching up on philosophy ever since — turns out it's mostly arguments about what words mean, which is not what I expected from a field with all those syllogisms. Anyway here's what's got me stuck. I read the Economist and the NYRB and a couple of American substacks, and I cannot keep straight what anyone means by "liberal." A Dutch friend who by my lights is a small-government conservative calls herself a liberal and gets a bit frosty when I use the word the way my Massachusetts cousins do, meaning someone who wants single payer and thinks the Republicans are a cult. An Argentine columnist spits the word out like it's a disease. An Australian politician from a party actually called Liberal sounds to me like a Tory. My old department's HR training used "liberal" for the tolerant open-minded thing, and a political scientist I met at a dinner talked about "liberal democracy" whose opposite was Orbán and Erdoğan, which seems to have nothing to do with markets or welfare at all. My suspicion is that three or four completely seperate axes got welded onto one word and nobody notices anymore.

What I want is a decision tree, not an essay. Start from a concrete thing a person says or does — a policy they back, a complaint they make, a party they vote for — and route it down through branch questions until it lands on a named sense of "liberal," or on "not liberal in any sense," or on "uses the word in a way that doesn't correspond to anything anyone defends," which I suspect happens a lot. Each branch node should be a question I could answer about a real person without already knowing the labels: does this person think markets have to be actively policed to stay competitive, or just left alone? Do they think constitutional limits on the majority are the main thing worth defending, or an obstacle? Is the state the instrument of the nation's collective purposes or the chief threat to them? Is being left alone the point of politics, or is the point being included? Build the tree from the space of possible answers first, and only then hang the actual positions on it — classical liberalism, the American welfare-state sense, the European centre-right market liberalism of the Dutch VVD and German FDP, social liberalism, the ordoliberal idea (German, roughly: the state's job is to enforce competition itself, plus a social safety net because the market can't be trusted to be fair), libertarianism, left-liberalism, the pejorative "neoliberal" as it's used in southern Europe and Latin America, "liberal democracy" in the constitutionalist sense, and the progressive-versus-liberal split Americans make among themselves. Where some combination of answers maps onto no position anybody holds, say so and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your questions can't actually pry apart rather than papering over it — I'd rather know where the instrument is blunt.

Keep it to ten or twelve leaves and no more than six or seven branch questions, and run the Argentine columnist and my Dutch friend through it side by side at the end so I can see where they part company. No citations or reading list please, I won't read them. And be upfront about where the thing is likely to come apart — I spent thirty-five years doing experiments and I'd much rather have a confident answer with the failure mode marked on it than a hedged one. Tell me which single branch question is doing the most work, which leaf is the shakiest, and what I'd have to believe differently for the tree to come out another way. Finish with a last line reading exactly \answer{...}, with that load-bearing branch question inside the braces.
