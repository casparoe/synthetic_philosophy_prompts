I work on reinforcement learning — I train policies for manipulation tasks, and the vocabulary I actually think in is MDPs, state abstractions, policy classes, reward specification, exploration and exploitation, and meta-learning across task distributions. I have essentially no philosophy training and limited patience for writing that never cashes out into something I could build. A colleague who does have that training keeps needling me with a case study she calls sphexishness. A digger wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to her burrow and leaves it just outside while she inspects the burrow; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away during the inspection, she hauls it back to the threshold, and now — her routine apparently "reset" — she inspects the burrow again, and you can repeat this as many times as you like. My colleague says the interesting part is not that the wasp is a machine, but that "machine-like," "rigid," and "flexible" are description-relative verdicts: at one level of description the wasp runs a fixed routine, at another she solves a problem in a changing environment, and whether we call any agent — us included — flexible depends on how we carve the state space and where we place the loop. She also tells me that at least one critic of the example has argued that the wasp does eventually adapt after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may simply be the right thing to do, which she claims makes the case more interesting rather than less. I think the whole thing is a confusion about levels of description that my vocabulary dissolves in about ten minutes; she thinks I am missing the point, and I would like to know which of us is right.

So restate the debate as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — state abstractions, the difference between a policy, a policy-selection rule, and a learning rule; exploration; meta-learning over task distributions; reward specification; and whatever you need from the theory of computation — and then give me the residue report. Specifically: which of her claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in what I just listed? I am particularly interested in two pieces of residue. The first is the free-will worry. She insists that the real question is whether human flexibility differs in kind from the wasp's or only in the depth of the nesting, and that if it is only depth, then something in our self-image is false. I can state that in my vocabulary as a claim about a hierarchy of learning rules, and it looks to me like a plain statement about a regress that terminates wherever you stop describing — so tell me what, if anything, of the worry survives translation, and whether what is lost is something my vocabulary genuinely cannot express or evidence that the worry was never a substantive disagreement.

The second is the move I would make in reply. I will grant you a system that learns its own state abstractions and its own policy class from data and adapts both online, so that no fixed policy and no fixed abstraction appears anywhere in the system I describe. Is there any form of rigidity that remains invisible from inside that picture — a rigidity belonging to the learning rule itself, or to the way the system's own states are individuated for it — and if there is, does stating it require anything beyond my vocabulary, or is it just a theorem I have not bothered to write down? Be concrete about what would have to be true of a system for the residue to count as a genuine gap in my way of talking rather than an artifact of how the philosophers like to phrase things.
