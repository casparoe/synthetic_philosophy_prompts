I'm preparing a talk on what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and don't establish outside of mathematics, and a colleague has handed me an argument I can't defuse. He thinks it shows something sweeping about ethics, and I suspect it proves too much, but I want to know exactly where it breaks before I say so. Here is the argument, numbered:

(1) A correct ethical theory must be codifiable: there is a finite system of axioms and inference rules whose theorems are exactly the ethical truths.
(2) To be adequate to moral reasoning — aggregation over arbitrarily many individuals, arbitrarily nested cases, iterated hypotheticals — such a system must be at least as expressive as elementary arithmetic.
(3) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, any consistent formal system that is at least that expressive contains a sentence that is true but not derivable within it.
(4) Therefore a correct ethical theory would contain a truth it cannot derive.
(5) A theory that fails to derive a truth in its domain is not correct.
(6) Hence no consistent formalized ethical theory is correct. And by the second theorem, no such theory can even certify its own consistency, so we could never be in a position to know it is correct.
(7) So ethical truth is not capturable by any formal system; ethical judgment must be irreducibly non-formal.

He treats (7) as vindicating a kind of moral intuitionism. I cannot accept (7), but I also can't just wave my hands at the argument. If it matters, I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since I think the combinatorial structure of population ethics really does require that kind of expressive power, and I'd rather not rescue myself by denying it.

Tell me which step to reject, and then explain why that step looked so plausible in the first place and what rejecting it commits me to. If more than one step is available, price them against each other: which is cheapest to give up, which is most expensive, and what independent support each would need to survive. Please answer in American English.
