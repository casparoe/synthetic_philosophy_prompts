I'm a second-year undergraduate in a political philosophy course, and our lecturer closed the unit on distributive justice with a recap he explicitly told us to treat as the exam-ready statement of luck egalitarianism. What he gave us was this: luck egalitarianism is the view that the only justified inequalities are the ones people bring on themselves. It begins from the thought that it is unfair for someone's life to go worse than someone else's because of things they never chose — where they were born, which talents they drew in the genetic lottery, whether they get sick — and it turns that thought into a rule: neutralize the effects of brute luck and let people carry the consequences of their own choices, the results of choices being what the literature calls option luck. The just society, on this picture, is one in which everyone starts from the same position and the market and personal decisions sort people out from there; it also means the state must be willing to let the imprudent live with the wreckage of their gambles rather than bail them out, since bailing them out would only relocate an unlucky loss onto someone else. His worked example was a man who takes up smoking and gets cancer: on the luck egalitarian view, he told us, that man has no complaint, because the illness is the upshot of a choice — and the same goes for someone who gambles away their savings.

He then said the standard objection, which he finds decisive, is that all this makes the theory cold and punitive: it would have us abandon the imprudent, and it licenses prying into people's lives to determine whether their disadvantage was chosen. He closed with the thought that because luck egalitarians cannot agree whether the thing to be equalized is welfare, resources, or opportunity for welfare, and each of those choices generates absurd results, the theory never really gets off the ground. I have to revise this for an exam and would rather not memorize a formulation a grader will take apart; I am also inclined to think the harshness objection really is decisive, and I want to know whether that instinct is being propped up by the recap rather than by the view itself. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first should be the part of the recap I can safely reproduce, the next three should each take one place where the recap does luck egalitarianism an injustice — quoting the phrase at issue and saying what someone who actually holds the view would say instead — and the fifth should tell me whether the lecturer's closing verdict survives once the recap has been corrected.
