I'm in a small reading group on formal epistemology and we've spent the last few weeks on accuracy-first stuff — the approach where you score a credence function by how close it is to the truth and then try to derive norms of rationality from the goal of being accurate. One guy in the group, who's read alot more of this than the rest of us, keeps ending arguments with some version of "accuracy is all there is, epistemically" (he also says "the only thing epistemic rationality is answerable to is accuracy"). Last week he used it to do three things at once: to say that anyone who thinks understanding, or explanation, or coherence is epistemically valuable in its own right is just confused; to say that if two credence functions are equally accurate there's nothing epistemic to choose between them; and to say that this is why probabilistic incoherence is irrational, because incoherent credences can be accuracy-dominated. I'm fairly sympathetic but I suspect he's running a handful of different claims together, and I can't tell which of them the formalism actually delivers.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, from the nearly trivial to the very strong, and for each say whether it's a terminological/definitional choice, a substantive but defensible claim, or something a card-carrying accuracy-firster would reject? I'd especially like to know (1) whether his claim that accuracy is the only epistemic good is meant as a claim about *fundamental* value, with everything else derivative, or about all epistemic value full stop — and whether the view needs the strong version or can get by with the weak one; (2) whether "accuracy" is one thing or a family of things, given that scoring rules disagree (the Brier score and the log score rank credence functions differently), and if so whether "accuracy first" is underdetermined until a rule is fixed; (3) how much of the machinery survives without extra premises — I gather the dominance argument for probabilism is supposed to be robust across scoring rules, but that the accuracy-first case for conditionalization needs something extra like maximizing expected accuracy, and I don't know if that's right; and (4) whether the move from "accuracy is valuable" to "epistemic rationality is just instrumental rationality with respect to accuracy" is something the view is committed to or just one popular packaging of it.

What bugs me most is that "the only thing that matters is accuracy" sounds like a claim about what's *valuable*, but he wields it like a claim about what rationality *consists in*, and I can't tell whether those are supposed to be one claim or two with a derivation between them. Relatedly: is "accuracy" doing any work that "truth" wasn't already doing? If a single credence can be wildly inaccurate while the whole credence function is highly accurate, does the slogan even have a determinate reading? And if perfect accuracy would mean assigning 1 to every truth, which is impossible, doesn't the slogan have to be about expected accuracy or something else entirely? Please tell me which of the readings on your menu would actually license his conclusions about understanding and about ties.
