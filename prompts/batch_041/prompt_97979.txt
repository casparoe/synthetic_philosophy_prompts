I'm on the board of the homeowners association for the small condo building I live in (fourteen units; we've been arguing about a bike rack for two years now, that's a whole other thing), and after a year of it I've started to feel like the whole arrangement is one of those drawings where each hand is drawing the other one and you can never find the hand that's actually holding the pen. Here's the machine, roughly. The owners elect the board. The board writes and enforces the rules. The rules bind every owner, including the ones who voted against them and including people who bought in later and never agreed to any of it. If the owners want to change the rules we can — but only through an amendment procedure that is itself written into the same document, and some provisions the developer dropped in at the very start (parking, pets, the mandatory dues, the ban on short-term rentals) either need a two-thirds vote or are effectively frozen. Meanwhile the board has been quietly passing what it calls interpretive rules, and in practice those let it do a lot of what a real amendment would do without ever facing a vote. So the board answers to us, but it also authors the rules we'd use to discipline it, and it's bound by the rules it makes. I'm in a course on self-reference right now and I keep mapping this onto the material — Escher, Gödel, and Hofstadter's claim that below every tangled hierarchy there has to sit an inviolate level, one the system can't reach or revise from inside itself.

So treat my building as the practice and ask what it presupposes. Does an arrangement like this need an inviolate level somewhere — the developer's original declaration, or the state statute that lets HOAs exist and gives their covenants the force of law, or just the bare fact that the members own the land — a level that is not itself up for a vote, and if so where exactly is it and why does it have to be there? Which rival pictures of authority does the practice quietly rule out: that legitimate authority just is whatever the current owners happen to consent to, or that the whole thing is nothing but raw power with no ground of validity at all, or that there's no foundation and the regress simply runs forever (board above owners, owners above board, amendment clause above owners, statute above amendment clause, and then…)? Try to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined — I suspect a few quite diffrent theories of authority would all license the same bylaws. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the arrangement if we gave up on the inviolate level and treated every rule, including the amendment clause and the statute, as revisable by whoever currently holds power. I definately don't need a reading list or citations, just the analysis.
