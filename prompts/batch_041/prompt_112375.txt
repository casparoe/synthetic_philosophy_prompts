I'm a retired field ecologist — spent most of my career on seed dispersal in fragmented forests — and since retiring I've got into philosophy of biology. I'm definately not a philosopher, but I want you to argue for a position I've been calling the maintenance-first view of function. The idea is that neither the etiological (selected-effect) account nor the causal-role account is fundamental. What's fundamental is whether a trait's causal contribution is stable enough to be relied on in an explanation of how a system keeps going; selection history matters only because its one way of making that stability robust, not because it's what makes something a function. So the etiological account is a special case of the causal-role account under a particular stability regime, not a rival. Please make that argument, and use an example of a trait that seems to have a function even though it wasn't selected for it — like a developmental byproduct that gets co-opted for a new role — to show why the selected-effect view gets the order of explanation backwards. Don't worry about citing the literature; just give me the strongest version of the argument, and flag the main place where you think it's vulnerable.
