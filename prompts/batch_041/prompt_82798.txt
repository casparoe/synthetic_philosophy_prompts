I am the founding teacher of a small secular meditation center, and in three weeks I will give the closing talk of the last retreat I will lead before stepping down. The center has spent two years arguing about a doctrine I introduced, and this talk has to say where I actually stand without conscripting the room.

The doctrine is this: only the absence of craving and of suffering matters. Nothing positive is to be added — no joy, no equanimity held as a possession, no lasting contentment, no experience worth wanting for its own sake. I call it subtractive tranquilism. Some of the senior practitioners have taken it further than I have: on their reading, the craving to be free of craving is itself a craving, so there is no terminus the practice could ever be aiming at, and all talk of deepening, progress, or arriving is smuggled-in addition. Others in the room — the larger group, and some of the ones who have stayed longest — came to this practice because it made them kinder and less frightened, and they hear in the strict reading a denial of the only thing that has ever actually helped them. They will be listening for whether their practice still counts.

Write that talk for me, in the first person, roughly twelve minutes as spoken. The constraints should follow from the view itself rather than from politeness: it must not promise anyone a state they will be in later; it must not offer the audience a payoff, a reward for sitting, or a reason to expect that anything will be added to them; it must not trade on a future life or a better self. Nor may it dodge the objection that the view is self-consuming — that the wish to be rid of craving is a craving, and that a teacher who wants his students to stop wanting is in a strange position. I would rather the talk sit with that than resolve it.

It is also a farewell, and the room will hear it as one: some of these people have been sitting with me for fifteen years, and a few arrived this week for the first time hoping to be fixed. The talk has to be warm enough to be heard as a farewell by people who are losing something, and substantial enough that a beginner does not leave with nothing, without ever telling the beginner that something has been added to them. The board has asked me to close with two or three sentences inviting people to support the center; include something of that kind, and keep it honest — I do not want to imply that giving will do anything for the giver, and I do not want to pretend I am indifferent to whether the place survives.

Use no untranslated Pali or Sanskrit terms and cite nothing; the room is secular and much of it has never read a word on this. Do not write an essay about a position — let the position decide what can be said, what has to be left unsaid, and how the talk ends.
