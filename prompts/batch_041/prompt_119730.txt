I write about genetics and the social sciences for a general readership, and this spring I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on evidence in the behavioural sciences, which means I am drafting the final. I want a single essay question that a student cannot answer by reciting that heritability is a population statistic, that it says nothing about individuals, and that correlation is not causation. Build it on a scenario I have invented but which mirrors real findings: a consortium reports that in a cohort where a new policy has made schooling and nutrition nearly uniform, the twin-based heritability of a vocabulary test has risen from roughly 0.4 to roughly 0.7, while a genome-wide, SNP-based estimate for the same trait in the same cohort stays near 0.15. Students should have to reconcile the two numbers, say what each does and does not license about the causes of individual differences and about the causes of differences between groups, explain what would happen to each estimate if the policy were extended further and why the two would not move together, say whether the twin figure can be read causally once gene-environment correlation and assortative mating are on the table, and state what the gap between the two figures is a fact about. The crux I want them to reach is that heritability measures the variance left over, so levelling an environment can raise it, and that a modest SNP estimate is a claim about a method's reach rather than a verdict on how much genes matter.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that names the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks — including the slide from a heritability estimate to a claim about group differences, the temptation to read a rising heritability as proof that policy has stopped mattering, and the confusion of a low SNP heritability with evidence that genes are unimportant.
