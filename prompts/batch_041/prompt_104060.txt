I'm in a philosophy of language reading group that has been working through Grice, and I missed the session on meaning and conversation. A friend who was there sent me her write-up of it, and I've been leaning on it in a term paper, which I'm now starting to regret. This is the gist of it, in her words:

Grice's central discovery is that there is a systematic gap between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by it. He called the surplus "implicature": the extra content that a sentence carries beyond its literal meaning. To explain how hearers recover it, he proposed the Cooperative Principle — the rule that a speaker should make her contribution as informative, relevant, and clear as the exchange requires — together with the maxims that fall under it. Conversation runs on the assumption that this rule is being followed. So when a speaker breaks one of the maxims, the hearer casts about for an interpretation that would make the breach acceptable, and whatever she lands on is the implicature. Grice's favorite illustration is the professor asked to write a letter of recommendation who writes only that the candidate has excellent handwriting and never misses a lecture: the letter never says the candidate is no good, but the sentence implies it. Because implicatures arise from the maxims rather than from the words, they are always tied to the particular context and are cancellable — the speaker can add "but I don't mean to suggest anything of that kind" and take it back. What all this shows is that the whole of meaning, everything a speaker manages to convey, can be reconstructed from the presumption that we are all obeying the maxims.
