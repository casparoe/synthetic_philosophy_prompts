I'm putting together a handout for a grad reading group on the units-of-selection debate, and I want to replace the usual timeline (Darwin, then Wynne-Edwards, then Williams, then Dawkins) with a decision tree. Start from a commitment nobody in the group disputes — that some trait can spread through a population because it makes the group containing the bearer do better, even though it costs the bearer — and split it by the questions that actually seperate the camps, not by the names. I want the branches to turn on things like: whether the entity that gets selected has to be a replicator or just an interactor/vehicle; whether the counting of fitness happens within groups or of groups (the MLS1/MLS2 split); whether 'group selection' is allowed to produce adaptations at the group level or only changes in gene frequencies; whether the gene's-eye view is a claim about replicators only or also about what bears adaptations; and whether the whole question is a fact about nature or just a modeling choice. Leaves should include the gene's-eye view, the replicator/interactor view, MLS1 and MLS2 multilevel selection, the older group-selection line, the pluralist 'it depends on the case' answer, species selection/sorting, and a deflationary 'no fact of the matter' option. Phrase each node as a question someone can answer without already knowing which label it leads to, and use concrete cases as the branch points — a meiotic-drive gene that kills the organism carrying it, a chicken flock bred for egg numbers, a green-beard gene, a trait that only pays off when you're in a haystack with relatives, a species that squeaks through a mass extinction, a cancer lineage inside a body. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really tell apart, and mark the answer-combinations nobody seems to occupy, with a word on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to about a page.
