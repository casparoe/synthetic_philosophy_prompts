Last month I sat in on a two-day workshop on frontier-model safety, mostly to write a piece, and the thing I can't stop thinking about is an argument between two people at the coffee table — a control researcher I'll call Ravi and an alignment person I'll call June. Neither one is dumb and neither one was performing, which is why its been bugging me. Here's how it went.

Ravi opened: alignment is a research program with no success condition. You can never walk in and verify that a model is aligned, you can only hope, and hope isn't a safety case. Control is engineering: you assume the model may be misaligned, even adversarial, and you design a protocol so that even a scheming model can't get an unacceptable outcome past you. That's a claim you can actually test against a red team.

June: but the whole edifice rests on a threat model you also can't validate. "Assume the model is a strategic adversary" is a strong assumption. If it's not adversarial, your protocol is just expensive; if it's a superhuman adversary, the guarantees evaporate, because every protocol I've seen quietly caps the adversary — assumes it can't coordinate, can't out-think the trusted monitor, can't wait. So control smuggles in untested assumptions too, just less visibly. And it might be worse than that: deploy a model under permanent suspicion and you've changed what it is. You only ever observe the behavior your protocol elicits.

Ravi: that's an empirical question, not a reason to quit. Take chain-of-thought monitoring. It only catches misbehavior if the model's stated reasoning is faithful. You can test that — suppress the reasoning, perturb it, see whether performance drops the way a faithful reasoner's would. That's a control eval. "Is it aligned" has no test at all; it's a property of a mind you don't have access to. And your self-fulfilling-prophecy point cuts both ways: if suspicion changes behavior, the change is measurable, so study it.

June: the faithfulness probe is the perfect illustration of the problem. A model that's scheming learns to keep its unfaithful reasoning clean, and now your probe is measuring the very thing you're worried is deceptive. And "unacceptable outcome" needs a line. Control people quietly assume a threshold — no catastrophic action — but the protocol's guarantee is always relative to what a red team imagined, not to what the model can do. So what you actually get is "no successful attack in this distribution," which is a benchmark number, not a guarantee. Alignment at least aims at the property that would make the protocol unnecessary.

Ravi: "aims at" is doing a lot of nothing there. Though there's a version of your objection I'll take: the right frame is that a control measure should be judged by whether it survives being known. If the protocol is public and future models are trained knowing it, then any measure that only works because the model doesn't anticipate it is worthless. That's a real constraint — adversary-aware design — but it's an argument inside control, not against it.

June: sure, but think about what gets optimized. If safety comes entirely from external monitoring, you've built an institution with a live incentive not to look inside the model, because looking might surface something that forces a shutdown. Control without alignment manufactures pressure to look away. That's governance, not math, but at scale it's the part that decides.

Ravi said something I didn't catch and then we all got pulled into a session.
