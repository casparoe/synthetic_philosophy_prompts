I host a monthly philosophy discussion group that meets in the back room of a bar, usually about a dozen adults, none of us trained philosophers, ninety minutes including the drinking time. Last month we worked our way into Plato's last book, the Laws, and I came away with something I can't stop turning over. In the middle of an argument about how a city should educate its citizens, the main speaker proposes that the citizens be given wine on purpose, in organized drinking parties with a sober person presiding, because a drinking party is a cheap and harmless way to test and to train a person's self-control. The thought is that wine strips away the fear that normally keeps us decent, so you get to see what a person is really like once the fear is gone. But the test only works because the drinker knows he is being watched and dreads the humiliation of being seen to go too far, which means the thing being tested is not exactly the drinker's hidden self but his ability to keep an eye on himself; he even says that a man who suspects he can't hold his liquor should leave the party early, and that leaving early is itself an instance of the virtue. He calls the whole arrangement a kind of spectacle for the god. That struck me as a strange and interesting claim about virtue, that temperance might be a matter of fearing other people's eyes rather than a matter of inner strength, and I noticed that Plato's other writings pull the other way: the most famous drunk in his dialogues is the one who says the truest things at the party, and elsewhere he wants his best people kept away from wine altogether.

I'd like you to design a ninety-minute activity for my group that lets them feel the force of this rather than hear me explain it. I want them to run into the idea in what happens to them: that a vice can be used as an instrument of education, that the instrument works through visibility and shame rather than through truth-telling, and that the very same scene can read as either a diagnostic or a corruption depending on who is presiding, who is watching, and whether anyone is free to leave. The setting is a bar's back room, so real drinks are around, but I don't want the activity to depend on anyone actually drinking and I certainly don't want anyone pressured into it. Build the loosening of inhibition out of rules, roles, and small stakes — tokens, forfeits, privileges, the right to speak or to refuse — so that it works for a table of people sipping soda. Give me the roles, including the sober presiding role and any observers you want, the round-by-round rules, what each round is meant to do to the participants, the materials, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the parts of Plato's idea. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time and how I should handle it, and tell me plainly which part of Plato's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words at the end.

Two last things. Please don't give me a reading list, citations, or anything I'd have to look up — I'll go back to the texts myself — and just say in your own words which reading of Plato your design assumes, since I may well have gotten him wrong. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, the way you'd write it out in an email to a friend who's about to run the thing. And tell me where your design is most likely to fail: what would make you think the activity isn't actually producing the experience you're aiming for, what evidence from a first run would change your mind, and whether some other way of staging it might do better. Flag honestly the point in your reading of Plato where you're least confident.
