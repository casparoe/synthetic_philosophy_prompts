I'm in a graduate seminar on collective action, and I keep running into the phrase "the tragedy of the commons." Most recently it came up at a lab meeting about our shared compute cluster, where two people monopolize the queue and nobody maintains the scheduling scripts. A colleague cited the phrase to argue that the only serious fix is metering — give everyone a monthly quota of hours and let them trade it — and when I asked what exactly the phrase claims, he said it claims that shared resources inevitably get depleted, and that this is precisely why the arrangement has to be converted into individual holdings or prices. I doubt that is what the phrase means, or at least that it means only that, but I can't disentangle the possibilities well enough to push back. A second thing muddies it for me: the same seminar has discussed shared resources that aren't used up by being used — a communal annotated bibliography, a shared codebase — and I genuinely can't tell whether the slogan is supposed to apply to those at all, or whether "commons" is quietly being used in two senses, one about rivalry and one about mere joint access.

So I'd like you to separate out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making, ranging from the near-tautological through the empirical to the plainly normative, and to say of each whether it is plausible, whether it is true, and — the part that matters most to me — whether it would actually license my colleague's slide from "shared" to "metered." Please be explicit about where a reading depends on assumptions concerning the users' motives, concerning whether the resource is subtractable, concerning whether institutions are absent by definition or merely by stipulation in the example, and concerning whether the claim is one of inevitability or of a tendency that better rules could defeat; and say something about what game form each reading presupposes, since I suspect half the disagreement in the room is about whether we are even looking at a prisoner's dilemma. I want a committed answer rather than a menu I have to adjudicate myself: tell me which single reading is the most defensible precisification of the phrase, and which reading my colleague is most likely relying on, and if the strongest reading does not support his conclusion, say so plainly rather than softening it. Give me the whole thing as a numbered list of exactly five items, one candidate reading per item, each item a sentence or two, and no introduction or conclusion — the fifth item should be the one I ought to press him on, and the item you take to be the best precisification should be flagged as such within the item itself.
