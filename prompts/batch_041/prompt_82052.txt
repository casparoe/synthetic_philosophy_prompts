I have kept a sourdough starter alive for the better part of six years — fed it a few hundred times, split it, given portions away — and the stuff in the jar now shares almost none of its matter with what I started with, which prompted a friend who teaches metaphysics to introduce me to what she called the special composition question, i.e. the question of when some things add up to a further thing, and what that further thing is. Since then I have been collecting cases of my own and have grown suspicious of how the arguments are usually run: someone produces a single example — a bowl repaired with gold-joined seams, a slime mould that assembles itself out of cells that had been living independently, a bucket brigade passing sandbags down a flooded street with nobody touching anybody else, a lichen in which neither partner survives apart from the other — and announces that this or that family of answers is finished. Before I read any more of that, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that any adequate answer to the special composition question has to satisfy: what it must deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse (between composition and identity, between being one thing and being a heap, between being joined and merely being in contact), and which findings from the sciences it cannot contradict.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it, and mark clearly which ones are genuine constraints and which are merely the prejudices of a particular camp. Among the demands I have heard people make are: that ordinary objects exist and that our ordinary counting and predication come out largely correct; that composition be principled rather than brute or arbitrary; that it not be vague if parthood is precise; that organisms be privileged over heaps; that no privileged status be assigned to life, since biology does not treat living things as physically special; that composites not be causally idle given the completeness of physics; that the answer supervene on intrinsic features of the parts; and that the theory not be gerrymandered case by case for the sake of intuition. Sort these, add any I have missed, and flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect there is at least one such tension and it is the part I most want to see, since if the list is inconsistent then that is itself the result. Tell me also which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Then, briefly — I know this is the part I am most likely to get wrong — score the main families of answers against the list: nihilism, universalism, the view that only organisms compose, and the views that make composition conventional or context-dependent. Keep that scorecard short and skip the bibliography; a table for the desiderata would be welcome. The purpose I care about is narrow, in case it helps you calibrate: I want to know whether there is a fact of the matter about which composite entities — colonies, forests, rivers, the jar on my counter — are the sort of thing that could have interests at all.
