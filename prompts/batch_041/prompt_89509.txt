I'm putting together materials for a grad seminar on formal epistemology and philosophy of science, and I keep getting stuck on a cluster of issues around Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor. A colleague of mine has a view I've started calling machine relativism. She says the standard invariance result only tells you that any two universal prefix-free machines yield complexity measures that differ by at most an additive constant, and for the finite hypotheses we actually care about -- theories with a handful of parameters, short programs, that kind of thing -- that constant can easily swamp the difference in length, so simpler is not a mind-independent property. On her view the universal prior is just one prior among many, and choosing it is a pragmatic convention rather than a rational requirement. I think she's half right, but I suspect the practical robustness is better than she lets on, especially if we restrict to hypotheses computable within realistic resource bounds and fix a canonical-ish encoding of the data. My credence that she's right -- that is, that for finite hypotheses of the kind working scientists compare, the Kolmogorov-style ordering is not robust across reasonable universal machines, so Occam's razor gets no machine-independent justification from algorithmic information theory -- is about 0.6. Hers is about 0.25.

I'd like your own number for that claim, but more importantly I want the decomposition. Break it into the sub-questions it actually depends on, give each a rough probabilty with one line of justification, and show how you combine them into the final figure. Some candidate terms, though don't feel bound by them: how bad the additive constant really is for short strings; whether there is a canonical or privileged reference machine, or whether that idea is even coherent; whether resource-bounded complexity changes the picture in a way that helps or hurts the razor; whether the real justification for the razor is predictive convergence rather than truth-tracking, and if so whether that makes the machine-relativity objection less damaging; whether human simplicity judgments track description length at all; and whether reasonable machine can be defined without circularity.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what kind of arguement or toy example would move it most -- ideally something I could actually run in a 90-minute seminar without assuming the students have seen the standard results. If you think the claim is too ambiguous to have a truth value as stated, precisify it yourself, say what would count as resolving it, and then give the number for your precisified version. Also, if you can, tell me where the real disagreement between me and my colleague most likely lives: is it a disagreement about the additive constant, about what justification is supposed to mean here, or about whether the razor even needs a machine-independent foundation in the first place?
