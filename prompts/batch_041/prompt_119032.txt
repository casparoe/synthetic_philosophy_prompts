I'm producing a short-run podcast on constitutional theory, and the guest for an upcoming episode is a philosopher who has spent the past several years developing and defending what she calls a trusteeship model of constitutional precommitment. On her view, a founding assembly holds its authority in trust for the future citizens who will live under the constitution it writes, and entrenchment is legitimate only insofar as it preserves the capacity of later majorities to govern themselves — never when it merely locks in a substantive policy outcome the founders happened to favor. She pairs this with a distinction she draws between precommitment against passion (binding yourself because you expect to be temporarily irrational) and precommitment against judgment (binding yourself because you expect to revise your ends for reasons you now think poor), and she claims that only the former can justify entrenchment. Her habitual move when a hard case is raised is to redescribe it as an abuse of the institution rather than a defect in the theory; part of my job is to make that move progressively harder to reach for.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. The sequence should include, at minimum: one question on the accountability gap in the trusteeship metaphor (a trust normally presupposes a beneficiary who can call the trustee to account, and future citizens cannot); one on the passion/judgment distinction, pressing on who adjudicates at the moment of revision which kind of change is underway, and whether that adjudication is itself reliable; one on the succession problem, where the generation that bound itself is dead and the bound generation never agreed, and asking whether the precommitment analogy survives when binder and bound are not plausibly the same agent; one on whether her preservation-of-capacity condition can itself be entrenched without collapsing into the substantive entrenchment she rejects; and one that puts to her a device I have constructed for the episode, which I call the sunset proviso — a rule under which every entrenched clause lapses at fixed intervals unless re-ratified by the generation then living — asking whether her model can accept the proviso and whether accepting it leaves entrenchment with any work to do. Close with a question about what evidence, case, or argument would count against the model rather than merely complicate it.

For each question, anticipate the answer she is most likely to give and supply a follow-up keyed to that answer, so that the follow-up only makes sense given the reply it follows. Do not include warm-up or background questions, and do not ask her to name the thinkers she is drawing on.

Return the result as a JSON array of objects, one per question, using exactly these fields: "id" (a two-digit string, in order), "question" (the question to put to her, phrased as I would say it aloud), "target" (the specific commitment or distinction in her position the question aims to pressure, in one sentence), "likely_reply" (the answer she is most likely to give, in one or two sentences), and "follow_up" (the question to ask next, given that reply).
