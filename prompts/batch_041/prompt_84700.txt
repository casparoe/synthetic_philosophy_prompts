I have been reading about Daniel Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia," and a summary I came across in a popular philosophy book runs roughly as follows:

"Dennett's 'Quining Qualia' is the definitive refutation of qualia. He begins with the case of Chase and Sanborn, two coffee tasters who both stop enjoying a brand they used to like. Chase says the coffee has changed; Sanborn says his palate has changed. Since their behavior is identical — they both push the cup away — Dennett points out that there is no way to tell which of them is right, and therefore no fact of the matter about what their experience is actually like. This proves that introspection is unreliable. He then argues that the very idea of qualia is self-contradictory: qualia are supposed to be intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly knowable, but no property can be all four at once. Since qualia cannot be observed or measured, and since two people can behave identically while reporting opposite experiences, qualia are a useless fiction. Dennett concludes that consciousness is an illusion and that we should replace talk of subjective experience with talk of brain states and behavior. The essay is a behaviorist manifesto: if it can't be measured, it isn't real."

Something about this seems off, but I don't know enough to say where. I am inclined to think Dennett is being unfairly caricatured, but I also suspect that some of the summary is right and that the Chase and Sanborn case really does put pressure on the idea that there is a determinate fact about what someone's experience is like. I would like an accuracy audit. Please go claim by claim and tell me:

1. Which parts a careful reader of Dennett would accept as accurate.
2. Which parts are common simplifications or pedagogical shortcuts that are acceptable in a popular summary but would need qualification.
3. Which parts are misrepresentations — and specifically, what Dennett's actual target is when he attacks qualia, and how that differs from the broad notion of phenomenal character or "what it is like."
4. Whether the summary's conclusion — that Dennett thinks consciousness is an illusion and that we should be behaviorists — follows from anything Dennett actually argues, or whether it is an overreach.
5. Whether the Chase and Sanborn case is best understood as showing that introspection is unreliable, that there is no fact of the matter about qualia, or something else. What work is the case actually doing in Dennett's argument?
6. A separate charge I have heard: that Dennett's argument is at bottom a verificationist argument — that differences which make no observable difference are no differences at all — and that verificationism is self-refuting, so the argument fails. Is that a fair characterization of Dennett's strategy? Does his case against qualia depend on verificationism, or can it be reconstructed without it?
7. Finally, what is the strongest version of Dennett's argument against qualia, and what is the strongest objection to it? I am not asking for a verdict on who is right, but for a clear statement of the dialectic so I can see where the summary went wrong.
