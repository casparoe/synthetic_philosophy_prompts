I'm about a third of the way into a novel — the narrator is a junior analyst at a small peace-research outfit — and one chapter is just the institute's annual report, printed straight, no commentary from me. I want it deadpan and funny, and the funny has to come out of the methodology, not out of jokes about bureaucrats in lanyards.

Setup I've already written into the book: the institute stakes its whole public existence on one headline number, "violent deaths worldwide," and this year it's the lowest ever recorded. I need the report to give the game away in its own footnotes without ever noticing its doing that. The count covers only intentional killing by an identifiable actor. The switch from absolute numbers to per-capita happened in the year the absolute numbers went the wrong way. The population denominator comes from a body with an interest in it being big. And this year they retired a whole category — deaths from deprivation caused by blockade and sanctions — and folded it into a line item called "excess mortality, unattributed." Throw in a sidebar celebrating a dramatic drop in one subcategory that only looks dramatic because they changed what's in it.

Then at the back, one dissenting internal memo from a staffer who isn't arguing about which number is right but about whether there's a fact of the matter at all. Her move is something like: the question was never whether violence fell, it's what we'd have to see in order to say it didn't.

I want a reader to finish it unable to tell whether the decline is real or whether the question even has an answer, and I don't want the narrator or the report ever to ask that out loud. Hard cap 800 words, and it should look like a real annual report — headings, a methodology note, the memo as an appendix. Someone who actually works with conflict data should wince in recognition, not wince at me.
