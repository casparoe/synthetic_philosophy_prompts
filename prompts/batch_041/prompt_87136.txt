I'm drafting a short essay on the lottery and preface paradoxes and would welcome your comments. My thesis is that they are one paradox rather than two, and that the standard responses—abandon closure, abandon the rule that high probability suffices for acceptance, or restrict acceptance to the certain—misidentify the culprit. The culprit, I argue, is the assumption that acceptance is a single propositional attitude governed by one threshold. I propose instead partitioned acceptance: a proposition is accepted only relative to a partition (a question under discussion), and closure holds within a partition but not across partitions. The lottery paradox then arises from treating "ticket i loses" as acceptable relative to the same partition for every i, when the operative partition is "which ticket wins"; the preface paradox arises from conjoining claims that answer different questions (the chapters). Partition-relative acceptance lets us retain both deductive closure and high-probability acceptance, because the conjunction of claims drawn from different partitions is not accepted relative to any single partition.

Please comment on (1) whether this is a fair diagnosis of what the two paradoxes actually turn on, (2) whether partitioned acceptance is a genuinely distinct move or a notational variant of an existing one, and (3) what the strongest objection to it is. Please also separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on acceptance and these paradoxes, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
