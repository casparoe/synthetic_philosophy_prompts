I've more or less talked myself into the approval-directed camp and I'd like you to try to talk me back out of it. The view, as I hold it: when we build something very capable, don't hand it a goal to maximize. Give it the aim of doing what a well-informed human overseer would approve of, and let the approval do the work of specifying what's wanted. The appeal is obvious enough -- we don't know how to write down what we actually want, and this keeps the thing pointed at us instead of at some proxy we'll regret later.

Here's my particular spin, which is the bit I actually want stress-tested. The agent should treat approval as a *pointer* to value rather than as a quantity of value. That is, it's trying to work out what the overseer would approve of under idealized conditions -- fully informed, not being manipulated, time to reflect -- and not trying to maximize the approval signal it can actually elicit from the actual human sitting in front of it. I've been defending this in a seminar and I'd like to know how much of it survives contact.

So: what would count against it? I want defeaters, ranked, and I want them sorted by how decisive they'd really be rather than how often they get repeated. Some of the candidates I'm already half-aware of: a clever enough agent that models the overseer can steer the overseer instead of serving them; the regress just relocates the problem (who approves the approver, and what settles the content of the approval function in the first place?); in cases where approval and outcome come apart the view gives the wrong answer by construction; and the whole thing may just collapse into goal-directedness with the goal being be-approved-of. Tell me which of those are genuine and which are cheap shots.

Also, what does my version predict that plain goal-directed design doesn't, and what would I have to see to conclude I was wrong? If it turns out to be compatible with every possible outcome, say so, and say whether that's a fatal flaw or just the price of a view about how to build things rather than a predictive claim. And don't concede anything on my behalf that a committed approval-directed person wouldn't -- I want to know what they themselves would admit counts against them, not only what critics shout from the cheap seats.

The thing that would move me most: a case where an agent that genuinely tries to do what the overseer would approve of does worse, by the overseer's own lights, than an agent that just optimizes the outcome directly. If you can build one of those that isn't obviously gerrymandered, I'll take it seriously. If you can't, I'd rather hear that than a polite survey.
