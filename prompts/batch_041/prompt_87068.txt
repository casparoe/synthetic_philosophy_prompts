I got into a stupidly long argument with my friend Dev last night about Jeffrey conditionalization and I'm still slightly annoyed about it. His line was that the whole thing is basically a non-rule: updating on uncertain evidence, he says, is just ordinary conditionalization on a proposition about your own subjective state, and so all the famous embarrassments -- that your posterior depends on which partition of "the evidence" you happened to use, that two successive rounds of uncertain-evidence updating can fail to commute, that you seem to need some extra rigidity assumption before the formula gives you any answer at all -- are artifacts of asking a bookkeeping identity to do the work of an update rule. I think he's cheating somewhere, but I couldn't say where after three beers, and annoyingly the guest I've got lined up for my podcast this month has spent years defending pretty much his position. She calls it the deflationary reading: the formula doesn't tell you how to change your mind, it just records a consistency condition that holds whenever your new credences could have been got by first conditioning on a proposition about your own experience and then marginalizing; "uncertain evidence" is only well-defined relative to a partition the agent has already committed to; and once you see that, partition-dependence and non-commutativity stop looking like defects. She's also fond of saying the interesting question was never which rule but when the input to a rule is well-defined, which I suspect is the move I need to be ready for.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions that get past her restating the view. I want at least one on what the deflationary reading actually forbids -- if it's a consistency condition rather than a rule, I want to know whether it has any normative teeth at all, and what a rational agent is supposed to do at the moment of uncertain experience if the formula is silent; at least one on whether her view quietly requires certainty about your own posterior credences (this is where I'd like to press the anti-luminosity point, that no non-trivial mental fact is such that you're always in a position to know it -- if she needs introspection to be luminous for the input to be well-defined, say so), and what happens to an agent who is simply unsure what her own credences are, or whose credences are imprecise; at least one on the non-commutativity of successive uncertain-evidence updates, where I expect her to say you should hold the partition fixed across the sequence, so the follow-up has to be why a rational agent's partition should be stable over time when the whole point of learning is that new distinctions become salient; and at least one on whether rigidity (your likelihoods within each cell don't shift) is a substantive assumption she's smuggling in or something her reading makes true by construction, in which case I want a concrete description of a case where it fails. Add whatever else you think earns its place, but keep it in the weeds -- no "how did you come to work on this", no "what do you say to critics who think Bayesianism is unrealistic", and please don't pad it with stuff I'd have to go read five papers to follow; I want the questions themselves to be followable by someone who's only got the setup above.
