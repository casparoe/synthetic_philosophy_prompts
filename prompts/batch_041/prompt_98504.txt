So I just got off a two-hour call with my friend Devi and we are no closer than when we started, which is honestly how every conversation about this goes. The topic is whether species are real. I need you to help me figure out what we're even disagreeing about, because I've stopped being able to tell.

Here's the setup, so you know we're not just talking past each other on the facts. We agree on basically all of the biology. We agree reproductive isolation comes in degrees; that ring species exist where the two ends of the ring can't interbreed but every neighbouring pair along the way can; that bacteria and other asexuals don't interbreed at all so the whole interbreeding criterion just doesn't apply to them; that there are cryptic species that look identical to the eye; and that lateral gene transfer makes the tree of life look a lot less like a tree once you get down to the microbes. None of that is in dispute.

She thinks species are real, just that there's no single privileged way to draw them — she calls herself a pluralist, says there are many legitimate species concepts, and that this doesn't threaten realism at all. I think "species" is a filing system that's handy for certain jobs (conservation law, ecology, museum drawers) and that when we ask whether something is "really" a species we're asking about our rules, not about nature. We're stuck. Every time I push her she says I'm confusing "no single true concept" with "no fact of the matter"; every time she pushes me I say she's smuggling realism back in by redefining real to mean "whatever our best concept happens to pick out." We each think the other has made a category error and neither of us can say exactly what the error is.

But here's the thing that actually started the fight this time, and I want to know if it does the work she thinks it does. She proposed a test case. Suppose a lab induces a single mutation in a population of flies so that the mutants and the originals can no longer interbreed at all — one generation, no gradual divergence, no geography, no long history. She says: fine, that's a new species the instant it happens, species are just lineages that have become reproductively separate, end of story. I say: so a species can pop into existence by fiat in an afternoon? That doesn't sound like a fact about nature, that sounds like we decided to relabel. She says the relabel tracks a real event, the isolation. I say the event is real but calling it a new species is the decision, and you could just as well call it the same species, now split.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? I want a real diagnosis, not a tour of the species concepts. Is it (a) verbal — a fight about the word "species" that would dissolve if we just stopped using it, (b) substantive — a genuine disagreement about what the biology commits us to, (c) a disagreement about standards rather than facts, i.e. about what it even takes for something to count as a natural kind, or (d) two people answering different questions under one word — me "how should we file things," her "what lineages are out there"? And does her instant-speciation case actually tell those apart, or is it just a flashpoint that feels decisive to each of us for different reasons? If you think the debate is stalled for some structural reason, say what it is and what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a new kind of case, giving up the word, or nothing at all. And if one of us is just plain wrong, please say so and why, because I'd definately rather be told than keep having this call every few months.
