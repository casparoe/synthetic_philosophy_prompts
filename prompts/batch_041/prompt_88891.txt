So I'm the oldest person in a grad seminar on early modern women philosophers — I'm a longtime LessWrong/ACX lurker who went back to school late, so half the time I'm just trying to keep up with the 24-year-olds — and after the session on Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz, two of my classmates got into it in the hallway and somehow I ended up as the referee, which I was definately not qualified for. I'll reproduce the exchange as fairly as I can, since I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Call them D. and M.; both read Spanish, both are smarter than me, and neither was strawmanning the other as far as I could tell.

D.'s line was that the whole defense of women's education in the letter is instrumental, top to bottom. Every reason she gives for why a woman should be allowed to study is a reason about what the studying is for — so she can instruct other women, so she can raise children in the faith, so she doesn't fall into error when she talks about God, so she can serve God better. D. says she never claims that understanding something is good for a woman in itself; she explicitly disclaims any ambition to teach publicly, and she never asks for anything like a chair or a pulpit. So she concedes the frame: a woman's worth lives in her roles, and study is just a better tool for filling them. M. shot back that this only works if you skip the opening, which is a whole autobiography of an irresistible, God-implanted drive to know — she describes study as something she couldn't stop doing, and treats that drive as a talent it would be wrong to bury, and a buried-talent argument isn't about roles, it's about the mind's activity being a good. M. added that her handling of the Pauline passages about women keeping silent is a limiting interpretation: the ban is on public teaching, not on study or private understanding, which carves out a sphere where a woman's intellect answers to truth and to God and not to a role assignment. D. came back with two things: in the parable the talent is given to be used for the master's purposes, so that's still instrumental; and look at what she says she's actually trying to avoid — ignorance of what she's obligated to know, and error. Correct belief and duty, not understanding as such. And D. said the whole "the hedges are just strategic" move is unfalsifiable: if every concession is rhetoric and every disclaimer is coerced, then no feature of the text could ever count against the reading, and at that point you're reading your hopes into her. M. replied that the unfalsifiability complaint cuts both ways, since she's writing to a bishop who had just told her to quit secular study, and a woman under that kind of pressure obviously had reasons to hedge; and M. said the duty-to-know point doesn't fold into the roles point at all, because knowing what you're obligated to know is a duty that falls on her as a soul before God, not on her as a daughter or a nun — it would bind her even if she had no social role whatsoever, so D. has been running two different kinds of instrumentality together this whole time. D. conceded there might be a difference but said neither one is intrinsic value, which is what M. needs.

I don't have a dog in this fight and I don't want a vibe, I want the actual scorecard.
