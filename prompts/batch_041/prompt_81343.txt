So I just spent three hours arguing with my friend Dev about Hofstadter's MU puzzle and I want a second opinion, because I think I talked myself into a position and I can't tell anymore if it's real or just word games. Quick recap in case it's not on your radar: one starting string, MI, and four rules -- if a string ends in I you can tack a U on the end, you can double everything after the M, any III can become a single U, and any UU can be deleted. Can you ever turn MI into MU? No. And the reason is that you count the I's: rule two doubles that count, rule three knocks it down by 3 at a time, the other two rules don't touch it, so starting from 1 the I-count is never divisible by 3, while MU has zero I's, and 0 is divisible by 3. That part isn't the fight.

Here's what I ended up defending. (A) The move that settles this is a different kind of thing from any move inside the system. Every inside move writes down a new string, one at a time, and you can run them forever; the counting move writes down no string at all -- you could add it to the rulebook and it would never derive a single new string, it only ever tells you what can't be done. What it gives you is a fact about every derivation that will ever exist, which no finite run of derivations can give you. (B) So understanding a system is not just more of the same as operating inside it, and building a bigger system that has the counting rule baked in doesn't get you out of this, it just moves the problem up a floor, because now the bigger system is the one nobody understands from inside. (I keep seeing "jumping out of the system" as the phrase for this.)

Dev hit me with three things and I don't know which one is the killer. First: the counting criterion isn't only necessary, it's sufficient -- a string is reachable from MI exactly when it starts with one M, contains only M, I and U, and has an I-count not divisible by 3. So the grand outside insight cashes out as a decision procedure, an actual algorithm you could code up, which is awkward for (A) to say the least. Second: the invariant is just a homomorphism onto the numbers mod 3, and if that's what the jump is, then the jump is available to anything that can guess and check, which makes it sound alot cheaper than I was making it sound. Third: if the jump is always available, since you can always go up a floor, then it can't be what separates understanding from symbol-pushing, because a machine can also be redescribed one floor up -- so either (B) is trivial or I've defined the jump as the thing machines can't do and then announced that machines can't do it.

What I want from you: what would actually count against (A) and (B), sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which of Dev's three is which. What would a defender of my view concede as counting against them, as opposed to what a critic would just assert? Does my view predict anything that its rivals don't, or have I dressed up an intuition as a thesis? And tell me straight whether (B) has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that's a real problem or just the price of a view like this. The thing that would move me most is a case where the outside move does something a bigger inside system provably couldn't -- if diagonalization is the only place that happens and the MU case is the exact opposite, then I want to know that my whole framing is just the arithmetic hierarchy wearing a costume.
