I am a novelist preparing to interview, at a small literary festival, a scholar who has advanced a heterodox reading of John Maynard Keynes's ethics. Her central claim is that Keynes's conception of the good life is not a version of utilitarianism with a long time horizon but a form of aesthetic perfectionism: the point of economic abundance is to release people into what she calls the art of life. I want the conversation to be substantive enough for listeners who know only the caricature of Keynes as a technocrat, but I do not want the guest simply to rehearse the well-known facts of his Bloomsbury attachments or his prediction that the economic problem might one day be solved. Please draft eight to ten interview questions.

I have been working with a distinction I would like the questions to test. Call the first category scaffolding virtues: the bourgeois habits of thrift, calculation, deferred gratification, and purposeful work that Keynes thought were necessary to build abundance. Call the second category terminal enjoyments: the activities—friendship, love, aesthetic appreciation, conversation, the pleasures of the mind—that abundance is supposed to make available. The guest's reading treats the scaffolding virtues as merely instrumental and the terminal enjoyments as constitutive of the good life. I am not sure the distinction is stable, and I want the questions to find out whether it is.

At least one question should press on the relationship between the early Cambridge ethics of G.E. Moore and the later economic writings about leisure and abundance; at least one should press on the objection that Keynes's good life is parasitic on inequality, inherited wealth, and a servant class; and at least one should press on what a post-scarcity existence would actually look like at the level of a single life, rather than at the level of aggregate economic possibility. For each of these three, include a follow-up question keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. I would also like one question that turns the novelist's craft back on the guest: since novels are in the business of showing how abstract goods are instantiated in particular lives, ask whether Keynes's list of terminal enjoyments is too thin to guide a novelist—or whether its thinness is a virtue.

Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading recommendations. The questions should be answerable from the guest's own position, and they should not require either of us to look anything up during the interview. Avoid generic opening questions about how she became interested in Keynes.

End with a single sentence, on its own line, beginning with the word Verdict followed by a colon, that summarizes the through-line the questions are designed to expose.
