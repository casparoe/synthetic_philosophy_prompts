For our school philosophy society's annual creative writing prize I have to submit a short story of roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words, and I have decided to build mine around the continuum hypothesis. What I care about is not the technical result, which I know is that the hypothesis can neither be proved nor refuted from the standard axioms of set theory, but what people take that result to mean, and I would like the disagreement itself to carry the story rather than any explanation of it. My brother, who is reading mathematics at university, has been arguing with me about this for weeks, and he has convinced me that there are two serious positions. According to one of them, the independence result shows that there is no single universe of sets for the hypothesis to be right or wrong about: there are many equally legitimate universes, some in which it holds and some in which it fails, and asking which of them is the real one is simply a mistake. According to the other, the hypothesis is true or false and always has been; our axioms are merely incomplete, and the proper response to independence is to hunt for further axioms, principles we have independent reason to accept, that would decide the matter, though he says the awkward part is that different candidate axioms point in opposite directions, some making the hypothesis true and some making it false. I would like a reader to feel the force of both positions, and to see that someone could reasonably give a decade of their life to the second while knowing the first might be correct.

I have a setting in mind, but please treat it as a suggestion rather than a brief. I imagine a small mathematics institute that occupies a former seaside hotel on a cold coast in the off-season, with two long-term residents and one person who keeps the place running, either a caretaker or a first-year student who has come to catalogue the library. The two residents have spent years on the problem from opposite directions: one of them believes that there is a fact of the matter and that the right axioms will eventually be found, the other believes that the search is a category mistake. I would like the mathematics to reach the reader obliquely, through the way these people talk about their work, what they notice in each other, and what they have given up for it, and I would rather the story never used the phrase continuum hypothesis more than once or twice, so that the idea is doing work in the drama instead of being explained in it.

Please do not let anyone deliver a lecture on set theory, and please do not end by awarding victory to either position or by having a late revelation settle the question. I am not looking for the story of a genius who goes mad, and I do not want any quotation, real or invented, attributed to an actual mathematician or philosopher. What I would most like is an ending that lands on something concrete and ordinary, an object or a routine or a small decision, that leaves both positions standing and makes the reader uneasy about which one they believe.
