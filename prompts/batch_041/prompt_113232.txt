I've been trying to figure out when it actually makes sense to compare how well off two people are, and my gut keeps handing me verdicts I can't line up. Someone with a broken leg is in more pain than someone with a mild headache -- that comparison is clearly meaningful, there's a fact of the matter. But that her pain is about three times his -- I want to say there's no fact of the matter there at all. Someone who's been clinically depressed for years is worse off than someone who's just bored: meaningful; worse off by a factor of two: nothing there. Comparing how much two people both enjoy the taste of coffee feels meaningful-ish to me; comparing how much I enjoy coffee to how much you enjoy listening to music feels like comparing nothing to nothing. And here's the one that trips me up: I think "she's better off than he is, all things considered" can be plainly true even when they're better off in totally different ways, yet I also dont think you could assign each of them a number and subtract.

My half-baked principle is that a comparison is meaningful exactly when it stays on a single dimension both people share and lines them up by something like intensity, and meaningless when it has to trade one kind of good off against another. Does that principle actually get all my cases, or does it force me to drop one? And if the overall "better off" verdict is the one that has to go, just say so. If the whole exercise is muddled -- say if "meaningful" is quietly doing two different jobs here -- tell me straight instead of humoring it.
