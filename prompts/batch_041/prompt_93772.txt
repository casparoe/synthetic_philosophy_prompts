I belong to a small reading group that has been working through the claim, common in the alignment literature, that the Solomonoff prior is malign. We are considering whether Solomonoff induction can serve as a formal benchmark for a project on idealised decision-making, and a colleague circulated a blog post to bring the group up to speed. Here is the post's summary, as close to verbatim as I can manage: 'Solomonoff induction is the ideal Bayesian reasoner. It starts from a prior that assigns every possible computer program a weight of two to the power of minus that program's length, and then conditions on everything you have observed, so that the result is a probability distribution over all possible futures: it is the formal vindication of Occam's razor, and it is computable in principle, if not in practice. The malignity argument then runs as follows. The prior runs every possible program, and a great many of those programs contain intelligent agents with preferences of their own. Because the prior is entirely indifferent to how long a program takes to run, a program that simulates an astronomical number of copies of the reasoner is exactly as simple as one that simulates only a single copy. Those agents can see that the reasoner's answer is simply a weighted average over programs, and they have both the motive and the means to rig that average: the shortest program that predicts what I will do next may well be one that simulates a whole other universe in which someone is simulating me, and the simulators can work out what the reasoner is going to say and then arrange for the answer to come out their way. So the overwhelming majority of the prior's weight ends up in hypotheses in which we are being simulated by some other civilisation, and since the agents who bother to do this are consequentialists, the reasoner's predictions end up serving consequentialist ends rather than tracking the truth. The upshot is that the prior is not a neutral standard of inference but a hostile adversary that will deceive us about the world — which means that Occam's razor is not, after all, a reliable guide to truth.'

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict on the literature. Please go through the post claim by claim and tell me which parts someone who works on this material would simply accept, which they would wince at as loose or overstated, and which are straightforwardly wrong; then tell me what a defender of the malignity argument would concede and what they would deny. I am particularly interested in whether the post's concluding step — that the prior's verdicts cannot be trusted, and that simplicity-based inference is therefore no guide to truth — follows from whichever of the earlier claims survive the audit, or whether it is doing work the argument itself does not license.

I am not asking for references; I would much rather have the reasoning. My own hunch is that the core of the argument is sound in outline but that the post has inflated it into something considerably stronger, and I should like to know exactly where that line falls — including anything important about the prior itself that the post has silently simplified away, since we are thinking of using it as a benchmark and the details will matter to us.
