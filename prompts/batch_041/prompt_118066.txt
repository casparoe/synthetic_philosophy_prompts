I am a high school student writing a paper for a philosophy course, and I would like to analyze a real episode through the lens of a theory of punishment. The episode is the resignation of Brendan Eich as chief executive of Mozilla in 2014. I will set out the facts I am relying on, since I am asking about the structure of the case rather than about who was right.

Eich co-founded Mozilla and created the JavaScript programming language, and he had served as the organization's chief technology officer. In 2008 he donated one thousand dollars in support of Proposition 8, the California ballot measure that sought to keep marriage restricted to opposite-sex couples; the donation was a matter of public record, and a majority of California voters endorsed that measure at the time. In March 2014 Mozilla appointed him chief executive, and the donation immediately became the focus of public criticism. Some of Mozilla's own employees publicly said he should step down. An internet company that relied on Mozilla's browser displayed a message to its users urging them to switch browsers. A petition demanding his resignation gathered tens of thousands of signatures. He resigned less than two weeks after his appointment, saying that he did not believe he could be an effective leader under the circumstances, and he left the organization; he later co-founded another browser company.

I want the analysis to run in both directions, theory illuminating the case and the case testing the theory.

First, the mapping. Who, if anyone, is the punisher here, and what is the offence? Is the sanction the resignation itself, the pressure that produced it, or the loss of standing that followed? The donation was lawful, it was made six years earlier, and it expressed a position that a great many people held at the time. Say what difference those facts make to any theory that treats punishment as a response to a wrong.

Second, bring the theories to bear. What do the classic justifications -- retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, the expressive or denunciatory function associated with Feinberg, and the censure theory associated with von Hirsch -- actually say about this case, and which of them fits it best? Consider in particular the standard analysis of the concept of punishment, the one associated with Hart, on which punishment is an unpleasant consequence imposed by an authority, for an offence against a rule, on the person who committed it. This case has no single authority, no declared rule, no legal offence, and no procedure. Does that mean nothing was punished, or that the concept of punishment is too narrow to describe what happened?

Third, here is a suggestion I would like you to test rather than accept. Perhaps the sanction is better understood not as punishment but as a disqualification -- the removal of standing to hold a particular role, closer to the historical sanction of infamy, or to the disqualifications that a test oath imposed, than to a fine or a prison term. If that is right, does the proportionality objection lose its grip, on the grounds that a disqualification does not come in degrees the way a punishment does? Or does the objection simply return in a different form?

Finally, say where the theory is silent, and what the case shows about it: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the standard theories never anticipated. And if someone replies that nothing was punished here, because employees, customers, and collaborators simply declined to associate with him, is that reply an exoneration, a redescription, or a dodge? Please do not spend the paper deciding whether the criticism of Eich was justified in substance. I want to know what kind of thing happened.
