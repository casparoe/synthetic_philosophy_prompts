I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who runs a graduate reading group on probability and decision theory, and I have reluctantly agreed to design the session she insists every syllabus gets wrong. Her complaint is that students can recite the Dutch book theorem and still not feel its force: they see a table of bets that loses whatever happens, nod at the arithmetic, and walk away thinking the result is a trick about bookies rather than a constraint on their own credences. What she wants is an activity in which the participants themselves, not the facilitator, assemble the trap, and in which the trap closes through a sequence of individually reasonable choices rather than a single omnibus bet. I teach a mixed upper-level undergraduate and graduate seminar, sixteen students, one ninety-minute block, in a room with a whiteboard, a deck of cards, and a bag of poker chips; I can print handouts and give out small tokens or points, but I do not want anyone to risk real money. The students have had one week on probability and expected utility, so they know the vocabulary of credence and odds, but they have not seen any formal treatment of Dutch books or conditionalization. I want the activity to make them feel the difference between a synchronic incoherence in their credences and a diachronic vulnerability that arises even when each momentary credence looks fine, and to leave them with a vivid sense of why a bookie who only offers bets that seem acceptable at the time, and who has no private information about the outcome, can still guarantee a loss. The idea should show up in what happens: the rules, roles, and payoff scheme should arrange for them to run into the problem before anyone names it, and the facilitator should not announce the target concept until the debrief.

Please design the activity in full. Give me the roles and rules, the materials and setup, a payoff scheme that uses tokens or points rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the Dutch book argument, including the synchronic and diachronic versions and the distinction between a sure loss and an expected loss. I would like at least one role for a bookie and one for a bettor, with the bookie played by another student rather than by me, and a mechanism that prevents the bookie from simply offering a bet the bettor would obviously reject; ideally every bet should look fair or favorable from the bettor's stated odds at the moment it is offered, and the bookie's advantage should come only from the structure of the bettor's commitments, not from superior information or force. The initial elicitation of the bettor's odds should feel like an ordinary warm-up exercise, so that the participants do not realize they are constructing the premises of their own exploitation. If you can build in a phase where the bettor must update after evidence and the bookie can act both before and after, so much the better. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle those failures without breaking the illusion, and be frank about which feature of the Dutch book argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief, for instance the idealizations about logical omniscience, infinite divisibility, or the assumption that agents can be forced to accept a package of bets. I do not need citations or reading suggestions; I need something I can run.
