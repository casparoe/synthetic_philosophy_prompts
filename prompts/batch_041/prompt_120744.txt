I've been out of the lab about six years now — thirty-five years of experimental geophysics before that — and I've been spending my retirement reading history, which has been more humbling than I expected. A historian friend and I have been having the same arguement for months about "path dependence," and it's gotten to the point where I want to write something serious about it rather than keep losing at dinner. My hunch, and I'd like the proposal built so that it tests the hunch rather than assumes it, is that the phrase is doing at least three different jobs that get run together. There's the economic-history job: an early choice gets amplified by increasing returns and you can't get off the track. There's the job I know from my own field: hysteresis, where the state you end up in depends on the route rather than only on the forces acting now — a claim about irreversibility. And then there's the evolutionary job, the old thought experiment about rewinding the tape of life and letting it play again — a claim about inevitability, about whether the same forms would come back. My friend says path dependence is just a fancy way of saying things happened the way they happened, and that counterfactuals about the past are unanswerable; I think he's about half right, and that the way to be more than half right is to separate "could it have been otherwise" from "could it be otherwise now" as independent axes. A system can be locked in in one sense and wide open in the other, and I suspect the historical literature mostly produces evidence for the second and then concludes the first.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal for a one-year residential fellowship at a small institute for the history of science. I need the question stated so that it actually has a determinate answer, and an account of why the existing ways of talking about lock-in and contingency leave it open — positions and arguments are fine, but no citations, no reading list, and please don't invent references, I'll build the bibliography myself and I'd rather have nothing than something I have to check. For the method: I have no lab and no budget for archive travel, so I'm thinking a small number of paired cases where the outcome was claimed to be locked in, plus a formal piece where I try to say what "would have arrived anyway" could mean in terms of the size of the set of histories that reach the same end, or basins of attraction, or some notion of robustness across plausible replays. Be honest with me about whether that formalization is doing real work or whether it's just restating the puzzle in physics costume. Tell me what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing (I'd genuinely rather have a proposal that names a real failure mode than one that pretends there isn't one), and give me a quarter-by-quarter shape for the year that isn't fantasy.

One thing I care about more than I probably should: when you set out what's at stake, keep seperate what is actually settled among people who work on path dependence and contingency and what is genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus. If a distinction I've drawn is one that specialists would call confused, or one that's already been dissolved somewhere, say so in the proposal instead of politely building on it.
