For years I've watched two camps talk past each other about Buridan's ass, and I'd like the document they should have written together instead of another decade of comment threads.

Camp one holds that tie-breaking just is agency: an agent need not have a strict preference in order to act. Faced with two bales it values equally, or that are on a par, or that it simply cannot rank, the donkey acts by picking, and picking is not a defective substitute for choosing but the most basic exercise of it — there is a reason to act on some option and no reason to prefer either, so any option will do. On this view the ass starves only under a bad model of action, one that makes a strict preference a necessary condition of movement. Camp two holds that there is no action without a reason. Everything that looks like a reasonless pick is either covertly motivated — the donkey prefers the left bale slightly, or has a standing policy of going left, or prefers not to deliberate further, in which case there was no genuine tie to begin with — or it is not an action at all but an event that happens to the agent, like a sneeze. On this view a genuine tie leaves a genuine normative gap, and declaring "I'll simply pick" does not close it: a rule that says "when tied, take the left option" is itself a substantive preference, for leftness, and merely relocates the tie to the meta-level.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I do not want a verdict on who is right; I want a constructive text both could put their names to, with whatever disagreement survives made exact. Include:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what the starvation case is a case of; about what an agent must possess in order to act at all; about how the puzzle differs across four things that get run together, namely (i) equality of value, (ii) options being on a par, meaning neither is better than the other yet they are not exactly equal either, a positive relation rather than a mere absence of comparability, (iii) incomparability, where no positive relation holds at all, and (iv) the agent merely having no preference even where the options are perfectly comparable; and about which of these the case actually turns on.

2. The remaining points of disagreement, each phrased so that the side that holds it would accept the wording as its own, rather than a version the other side has written for it.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect one of them to be whether picking is itself an exercise of practical reason or a non-rational event, and another to be whether doing nothing — standing still and starving — is a default that needs no justification or an omission that needs as much justification as either bale. Identify the rest yourself, and say which are load-bearing and which are downstream.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or contrast that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out recorded in advance. Where a crux could in principle be settled by something empirical, say what that would be; where it is purely conceptual, say why no fact could settle it.

5. A closing section stating which parts of the dispute you take to be merely verbal — two descriptions of one underlying structure — and which parts are substantive, with your reasoning.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with their tests, then the verbal/substantive verdict. Keep the agreed claims non-vacuous: if a claim would be accepted by anyone regardless of which camp they are in, cut it.
