I am a retired physical chemist — forty years at the bench, and for the past three an autodidact in philosophy, working through decision theory and the theory of practical rationality with a reading group at the public library. I can usually follow an argument when the mathematics is kept honest, which is perhaps why the following one has me stuck: every step looks like something I would have signed off on in a referee's report, and yet the conclusion is one I cannot bring myself to believe. I have written it out in my own notation, and I would be grateful if you would tell me which step to give up, why that step looked as harmless as it did, and what I would have to pay to keep the rest. I would most like to keep the first step, though I am not at all confident it is the one that deserves to survive.

(1) A ranking of options is rationally permissible only if an agent who holds it cannot be made worse off — worse off as she herself would judge, surveying the whole episode — by a finite sequence of exchanges each of which she would freely accept at the moment it is offered. (2) Suppose an agent ranks three goods cyclically: she prefers B to A, C to B, and A to C, with money held constant. Let her start out holding A, and let her prefer more money to less and treat the goods as independent of the cash. Offer her, in turn, B in exchange for A plus a penny; then C in exchange for B plus a penny; then A in exchange for C plus a penny. At each offer she prefers what she receives to what she surrenders, so she accepts, and she ends up holding exactly her original bundle, three pennies the poorer. By (1) her ranking is rationally impermissible. (3) Now suppose instead that her ranking is perfectly transitive at every moment but not constant across moments: she ranks B above A today and A above B a fortnight hence, other things equal. Let her start out holding A; today offer her B in exchange for A plus a penny, and a fortnight later offer her A in exchange for B plus a penny. She accepts both, and is once more back at her starting bundle, two pennies the poorer. By (1) this ranking is rationally impermissible as well. (4) But no adult's ranking is constant across moments — tastes are formed and revised by experience, and we count much of that revision as growth rather than corruption. So (1) rules out every adult's preferences, the most careful reasoner I have ever met included. (5) That cannot be right, and yet (1) appears to say no more than that a rational agent should not be reliably defeated by her own preferences when she acts on them, which seems to me the very least one could ask of her.

I have stared at this for a month and keep losing my place somewhere between (2) and (4). Where does the reasoning break — and if the break is not where I fear it is, what must I surrender in exchange for keeping the pieces I would rather not lose?
