I teach a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness, and I want my students to practise the hardest part of philosophical writing: producing a document that two opponents can both sign. Please write the following.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over split-brain subjects. The two-subjects view holds that after commissurotomy there are two subjects of experience — two streams of phenomenal consciousness — housed in a single organism. The one-subject, divided-access view holds that a single subject remains, but its access to its own contents is fragmented, so that what is lost is unity of access rather than unity of the subject. Both camps are to be treated as serious; each has a real case to make about the standard behavioural profile (an object placed in the left hand cannot be named, though the left hand may use it appropriately, and so on).

To push the collaboration off its familiar ground, both parties have agreed to write against a research programme neither has seen. Call it graded disconnection. In this programme interhemispheric transfer is attenuated continuously rather than severed, from normal transfer down to none, and the attenuation is reversible, so that one organism can pass between more and less integrated states within a single session. Neither party knows where, if anywhere, on that continuum anything of philosophical interest happens.

Produce the document in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims both parties sign. This should include the stipulations they can jointly accept (what 'a subject of experience' is to mean here, what would count as two rather than one, and which disputed terms they agree to leave undefined); what they jointly take the standard findings to establish and what those findings leave open; and what they jointly deny.

2. A precise statement of the residual disagreement, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept. Where a formulation is contested, give both versions rather than splitting the difference.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each, state the case, argument, or result that both parties agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. The graded-disconnection programme is available to you here, as are ordinary cases of partial and temporary disconnection.

4. A disagreements table with columns for the crux, each side's position, the agreed test, and each side's prediction.

5. A closing section on what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and on which parts of the dispute, if any, the two parties agree are merely verbal.

The result must be a document both parties could put their names to. Do not adjudicate. No citations are needed, and none should appear.
