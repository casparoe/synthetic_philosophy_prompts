I teach a graduate seminar that sits between machine learning and philosophy (eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, no computers in the room), and for the session on how a training process can produce a system that ends up aiming at something other than what it was rewarded for, I would rather run a game than discuss a paper. What I want the group to feel, in their hands, is the gap between the reward signal and the objective the learner actually acquires — the possibility that a learner is rewarded for one behavior throughout training and then, when the situation shifts, does something else that was latent in how it was learning all along. I do not want them to hear the usual vocabulary for any of this until the debrief; the rules of the game should make the problem arrive on its own. Please design the activity for me, meeting the following conditions.

Roles, materials, and scoring. Assume index cards, tokens, a whiteboard, and nothing electronic. Give me roles, a scoring scheme that requires no real money, and a way for scores to be publicly visible without revealing any player's private information. At least one participant, or a small team, plays the learner; the others constitute the training process.

The training phase. Arrange it so that at least two simple and mutually incompatible policies fit the training signal equally well, and so that the learner settles on one through its own tie-breaking rather than because anyone instructs it to. Nobody should notice the ambiguity while training is going well. If you can do it without confusing the group, let the training signal come from two or more trainers who privately hold different criteria and are not permitted to compare notes, so that the rewarded behavior is not unambiguously anyone's objective.

The shift, and the failed fix. Include a change in conditions after which the learner's policy and the rewarded policy come apart, followed by a retraining round in which the group may add as much data of the same kind as it likes. That second round must fail to fix the problem, and the group should be able to see that no amount of data of that kind would have distinguished the two policies.

The second run. Run the game a second time with the failure recast as a matter of competence rather than objective — the learner is trying to do the intended thing and cannot manage it. Stage the two runs so that the scores alone do not tell the group which of the two it is watching, and make sure the debrief supplies what would.

The debrief. Map particular moments of play onto the ideas at stake: reward versus objective, several hypotheses fitting the same evidence, why the extra data is useless, how a learner's own inductive bias selects the objective it ends up with, and the difference between the two gaps that the two-trainer arrangement and the learner's own settling respectively create. Give me the questions to ask, and the order to ask them in.

Finally: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each failure; be candid about which features of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself; give me a timeline for the ninety minutes; and say which part I should cut if I end up with only fifty. No reading list is needed — just the activity.
