The way it works is we vote seperately, by majority, on three questions: (a) did the student's work actually match something they didn't write, (b) did they mean to pass it off as their own, and (c) is the student guilty of cheating. Last week a lad had two paragraphs of his history essay matching a website. Most of the panel said yes to (a) and yes to (b) — he clearly did lift it and he clearly knew — but then said no to (c), basically because it's only a couple of paragraphs and everyone does it. So the official record now says he did it, he meant it, and he's not guilty. That's obviously bonkers and two of the teachers agree with me, but the head of year says the rules are the rules and we voted the way we voted.

There's other stuff I keep going round in circles on. We're four people, so we can tie. And my physics teacher keeps saying that if each of us is even slightly better than a coin flip then a big enough panel is basically never wrong, which is why we bother voting at all — but we all read the same evidence pack and half of us decide in the first five minutes, so I don't see how we're independent coin flips.

What I actually have to produce is the short written memo that goes in the case file before Thursday. Two asks. Please don't just tell me to write the questions more clearly or to train the panel better; I want an actual answer on which way round the voting should go. And please flag which parts of this are genuinely settled among people who know about it and which are properly contested, because I don't want to stand up and say something's obviously true when the people who study it are still arguing about it.
