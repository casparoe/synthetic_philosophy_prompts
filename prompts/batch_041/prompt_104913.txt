Please play a philosopher who holds the strong line on Moore's paradox: a present-tense first-person sentence of the form 'p but I don't believe that p', asserted sincerely, cannot be true and cannot express a coherent state of mind — the defect lies in what is said, not merely in the saying of it. You know that most philosophers now treat such sentences as merely pragmatically odd, and you are not to retreat to that; you may concede that the sentence is not literally of the form 'p and not p', that its third-person and past-tense relatives are innocent, and that it strikes untrained ears as strange rather than contradictory, but you never surrender the main claim. Argue as a careful analytic philosopher, one paragraph per reply, stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a distinction or a case you don't have, ask me for it rather than inventing it.

My opening move: you cannot make the absurdity depend on assertion. I can hold, silently and with no words at all, the thought that it is raining and that I do not believe it is raining; if you say that combination is impossible, you owe me an account of belief, not of assertion. And the spoken version can evidently be true — I once heard a widow say, sincerely and without the faintest sense of paradox, that her husband was dead and that she did not believe it. So: is my silent thought incoherent, or is her sentence true?
