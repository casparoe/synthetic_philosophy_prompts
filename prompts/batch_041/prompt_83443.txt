I sit on a county fatality review panel that examines the deaths of adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities living in licensed group homes. We take up three or four deaths a month. I have no training in philosophy — I spent twenty years in freight logistics before this — but the panel's central question is one I have been chewing on by myself for a long time: when a resident dies and the people responsible for them did not do something, did their not doing it cause the death? Our reports go to the state licensing agency and get quoted in the local paper, so the wording matters to real people's jobs and to the facility's license.

Our current practice is to write findings the way a lawyer would, asking only whether the death would have been avoided had the thing been done, then letting the panel argue over whether to attach the word cause. It goes badly. Two members reviewing the same file come back with opposite findings, and the strength of the language in our public report depends on who happens to be in the room. I want a written procedure the panel can run, case by case, to decide whether a given omission is a cause of a death. Ground it in whatever philosophy has worked out about causation by omission, because I think our problem is philosophical rather than merely editorial.

Here are the pieces I have managed to get straight on my own, and that the procedure has to handle. An omission is not an event: there is no place and no moment at which it happened, so the ordinary test — had she done it, the death would not have occurred — is carrying a great deal of weight, and I suspect it is carrying too much. For any death there are countless things nobody did, and something has to pick out the one or two omissions we are willing to call causes; I do not believe the counterfactual test alone can do that picking. My own hunch is that what does the picking is the presence of a duty or an expectation — that a norm was in play — which would mean our findings are partly judgments about what people owed one another, dressed up as findings about what happened. And the same omission can belong to a person, to a shift, or to an institution, and I do not know how to say which of those caused the death when the honest answer is a bit of each.

The procedure should be an ordered list of questions, with a rule for when to stop, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and an explicit mark at the points where the step is not a factual test at all but a decision about what standard to hold people to. Those are the steps I need to be able to point to, because the board will ask. It must be runnable by me in under an hour with the file open, with no philosopher in the room, and it must produce findings that survive a lawyer and a nurse on the panel taking them apart.

Then run it on three cases: a resident in status epilepticus whose aide did not call for an ambulance at the threshold written into the protocol; a resident who choked during a meal on a shift when the home was one aide short of its licensed staffing ratio, where the missing aide would have been in a different room and probably could not have helped; and a resident who died of an untreated infection after a family member refused the treatment and the physician did not press the point.

One of those three should come out in a way the panel will not like, and I want you to say so plainly, since I will be the one presenting it. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will hand me a confident-looking answer that a better philosopher would call arbitrary.
