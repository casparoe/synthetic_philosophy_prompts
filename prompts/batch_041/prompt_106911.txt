Every time the simulation arguement comes up among my friends, nobody budges. The version we fight about is the trilemma: civilizations either tend to go extinct before they can run whole-brain ancestor simulations, or they get there and don't run them, or we're almost certainly simulated. The fight is never about the arithmetic. One friend says the argument is basically sound and the only real question is which branch we're on, and that resisters just won't take self-locating credence seriously. Another says the probabilities are meaningless because there's no privileged reference class for "the fraction of observers who are simulated" — pick a different class and you can get any number you like. A third says it's a pseudo-question: if the simulation is perfect, being simulated isn't a further fact about us, just a redescription, so there's nothing left to be probable about. A fourth thinks it's an ordinary empirical hypothesis and we'd confirm it by finding rendering artifacts, discretization, cosmic-ray glitches, that sort of thing. And a fifth says the whole thing is a proxy war for whether self-locating uncertainty is genuine uncertainty at all — the same issue as Sleeping Beauty and the doomsday argument — so it won't be settled here if it can't be settled there.

My hunch is that these aren't five answers to one question: at least two of them are answering different questions under one name, one about what an agent with no special information should believe about her own substrate, the other about whether being simulated is even a physical hypothesis. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath — about priors, about the reference class, about what makes a hypothesis testable? Diagnose it for me: which parts are verbal, which are substantive, and what would actually count as progress — a formal result about self-locating belief, a detectable signature, or a distinction that shows the question was ill-posed. And if you think it can't progress, say why.
