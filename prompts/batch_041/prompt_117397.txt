For the last several years my main window on philosophy has been the rationalist blogosphere, and I've absorbed enough of it that I now flinch whenever someone says 'Occam's razor.' The flinch got worse last week. A friend and I were arguing about a case in which several people arrived at the same conclusion independently and around the same time — call it five coincidences — and he proposed a single hidden coordinating cause, on the grounds that 'Occam's razor: one cause is simpler than five coincidences, and simpler explanations are more likely to be true.' I wanted to object that the one-cause story can turn out to be the longer story once you spell out the mechanism and the way it produced each apparent coincidence, and that the five-coincidence story might be the shorter one if you're allowed to describe it as 'independent discovery, a common phenomenon.' He called that a cheat, I couldn't say which rule it broke, and we adjourned. I have also been carrying around two half-digested claims from the same blogs: that Solomonoff induction is supposed to be the formal vindication of Occam's razor, and that the 'no free lunch' results are supposed to show that no simplicity bias can help you in general. I suspect those two aren't actually in tension, but I can't reconstruct why.

What I want from you is not a defense of simplicity or a verdict on my friend's argument, but a disambiguation of the slogan he used. 'Simpler explanations are more likely to be true' — lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, arranged from the ones that are near-tautologies to the ones that are genuinely radical, and give each a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. I'd expect the menu to include at least a pragmatic reading (use simple hypotheses as a search heuristic, because they're easier to state, test, or falsify), an ontological reading (posit fewer entities, or fewer kinds of entity), an information-theoretic reading (prefer the hypothesis with the shorter description, in the sense of Kolmogorov complexity or of some specified coding language), and a probabilistic reading (let simplicity enter only through the prior, so that it's Bayes doing the work rather than a separate rule). For each reading I want to know what it commits you to, and in particular what it says about the case that stumped us: does it compare the one-cause story to the five-coincidence story on entity count, on description length, or on prior probability, and is there any sense in which one of these is the real one the others are groping toward?

Then sort the readings by what they can actually do. Which reading is someone usually invoking when they drop 'Occam's razor' to end an argument — I assume the probabilistic one, but I want to know whether that's just because it sounds the most rigorous. Which readings would genuinely license my friend's move from 'one cause' to 'more likely to be true,' and which would leave him needing an extra premise about how the world is set up — a privileged description language, a prior over hypotheses, or a fact about which entities are the natural ones? Where do the formal results bite: does the Solomonoff-style convergence result give you a theorem, and if so a theorem about what — prediction, truth, or something weaker — and under what assumptions about the environment? Why does the arbitrariness in the choice of universal machine not wreck the whole thing, and why does that same fact look like it wrecks the plain-English version of the slogan? And how do the no-free-lunch results fit: are they telling us that a simplicity bias is unjustifiable, or only that it must be a bias toward the actual world rather than a free lunch? Give me the menu with verdicts and reasons, in the order you'd want a careful reader to see it, and tell me which single reading I should have appealed to when I wanted to say my friend was cheating — or whether I was the one who was confused.
