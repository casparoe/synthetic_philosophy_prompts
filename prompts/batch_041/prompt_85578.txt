Having spent a career in atmospheric chemistry and my retirement reading political philosophy, I keep returning to an inconsistency in the political arrangements I have lived under. Our legislature fixes a numerical inflation target in law and then delegates the instruments for meeting it to an unelected committee, appointed to staggered terms longer than an electoral cycle and deliberately insulated from voters; this is defended as elementary good sense, because monetary policy is technical and because incumbents cannot be trusted with the money supply in the run-up to an election. The same legislature fixes emission targets in law just as firmly, yet the instruments for meeting them — carbon taxes, bans, building standards — are contested in every election and occasionally put to a referendum, and no one thinks it scandalous that the electorate rather than a panel of specialists decides. And criminal guilt, which in practice often turns on contested technical questions of forensic statistics and medical causation, is settled by twelve randomly selected laypeople. What conception of political authority does this pattern presuppose? Does it assume that expert knowledge licenses insulation in some domains but not others, that legitimacy requires accountability wherever the burdens fall, or something less principled? Tell me which rival conceptions the pattern rules out, which it merely happens to fit, and where it is underdetermined; and if the implicit conception were replaced by a consistently democratic one, what in these arrangements would have to change?
