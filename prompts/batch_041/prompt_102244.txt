I'm in an ethics course this term and we spent a week on the hedonic calculus, and the utility monster thought experiment came up — the one where a being gets astronomically more wellbeing out of a resource than anyone else does, so the greatest-happiness arithmetic keeps handing it everything. In class everyone agreed the monster is absurd, but when I started writing out my own cases and going with my gut I couldn't get my verdicts to line up at all, and I want to know what principle, if any, they actually add up to.

Here's what I've got so far. (1) A being that turns a slice of cake into 500 units of wellbeing, versus a thousand ordinary people who'd each get 1 unit from a slice: I give the cake to the being, no hesitation. (2) But a being that's merely three times more efficient at converting resources into wellbeing shouldn't get three times the resources — I'd split it roughly evenly, maybe tilt a little. (3) A being that feels pain ten times as intensely as the rest of us: relieving its pain outranks relieving a handful of ordinary people's pain, but somewhere around 'a dozen' I stop and think the ordinary people should come first. (4) A being whose 500 units of bliss come from something I find totally worthless — say, watching dust settle — feels 'cheaper' to me than a person's single unit from finishing something they cared about, and I wouldn't hand it the cake even though the arithmetic says I should. (5) A being whose ecstatic state requires several ordinary people to be miserable as a byproduct: no. (6) A being wired to feel enormous passive pleasure but that has no preferences and never wanted anything, it just glows: I discount it heavily, maybe all the way to nothing.

My tentative guess is that I'm weighting each being's wellbeing by its capacity to have it, but with a ceiling, and with some kind of quality filter on top, and maybe treating pain and pleasure asymmetrically since (3) and (4) seem to pull in opposite directions even though both are just 'more units per being.' Is there a single principle that gets all six, or am I going to have to drop one? If it helps, I'd also like to know what it predicts for two cases I keep dodging: a huge number of barely-sentient beings (like a trillion insects) versus one ordinary person, and a single ordinary person versus a modest number of copies of themselves — does the count of beings matter separately from their capacity? And if my verdicts can't all survive, tell me which one has to go and why, not just that they're inconsistent — I want the principle stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each verdict is doing the work for.
