I'm assembling a problem set for a graduate seminar on the concepts of health and disease, and I need one teaching case that does what the stock examples in the literature don't. Nearly every textbook illustration of the naturalism/normativism divide runs in the same direction: the normativist classifies some contested or socially disfavored condition as a disease, while the naturalist balks because nothing is malfunctioning. I want the reverse direction, and I want it built rather than asserted. Specifically, I'd like a thought experiment in which a naturalist who defines disease in terms of disrupted species-typical functioning (fixed by a design or natural-selection standard, not by mere statistical frequency in the local population) returns a verdict of disease, while a normativist who defines disease in terms of a state that the relevant community disvalues or counts as harmful returns a verdict of not-a-disease — and where each verdict is generated by that theory's own criterion rather than smuggled in by stipulation. Constraints: the case must be realistic and contemporary, something a general practitioner or a public-health officer could actually confront, with no science-fictional organs, no imaginary species, and no appeal to conditions whose classification is already a live political controversy; the scenario itself should be no more than a paragraph; and ideally the case should isolate a single variable, so that you can show the verdicts flipping when that one feature is changed. If it helps to sharpen the construction, you may draw on the reference-class problem (whose functioning counts as the baseline), the distinction between a condition's being a malfunction and its being an illness or a lived harm, or a hybrid view that treats disease as harmful dysfunction — but the case should stand on its own for readers who don't already know those labels.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning on each side: state the condition, what each theory says about it, and exactly why, being explicit about which premises are doing the work. Then identify the one structural feature that makes the two theories peel apart, and tell me what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed or reversed. Finish with the strongest line of resistance available to whichever theory comes out badly here — how a defender would try to argue that the case doesn't actually meet its own criteria, or that the counterintuitive verdict is a feature rather than a bug. I'm not looking for a reading list or citations; I care about the machinery of the case and the honesty of the walkthrough, so that students can see that the verdicts fall out of the theories rather than being assigned to them in advance.
