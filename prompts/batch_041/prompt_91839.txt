George Berkeley's The Querist, a pamphlet on Irish poverty written entirely as a series of questions put to the public, contains an account of money that its author seems to have thought self-evident and that stands at an angle to the monetary thinking of his day. Money, on this account, is not a commodity but an instrument: the "true idea of money, as such" is that of a "ticket or counter"; gold, silver, and paper alike are "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring" the value of things; "the four elements, and man's labour therein" are "the true source of wealth"; money is useful only insofar as it "stirreth up industry"; wealth is proportioned to industry, and industry to "the circulation of credit", "by what marks or tokens soever"; and since "less money swiftly circulating" may be "equivalent to more money slowly circulating", a nation can be rich while its coin is scarce. Berkeley also holds that the "value or price of things" is "a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty" -- a formula that appears to make the quantity of money matter after all.

Compare this ticket theory of money with the commodity theory it was arguing against: the position, prominent in the English recoinage dispute of the 1690s and still alive in the sound-money tradition, that a coin's value is the value of the metal in it, that a decree raising the nominal value of the coinage changes nothing real, and that debasement and clipping are consequently forms of theft. Treat both as theories of what money is and does rather than as episodes in the history of economic thought; I want an argument, not a survey of who influenced whom.

Work through the following, in order.

1. Three cases, one at a time: (a) inconvertible paper money circulating for decades at a stable value; (b) a ruler who doubles the face value of every coin without altering its metal; (c) a bank that issues notes far beyond its reserves. For each, say what the ticket theory licenses or predicts and what the commodity theory licenses or predicts, and mark the points at which each theory is silent or has to borrow from the other.

2. Whether the ticket theory can say anything about the price level, and whether Berkeley's own demand-and-plenty formula can be squared with his claim that the tokens are indifferent. If it cannot, say which half of his account he should give up.

3. Why money is accepted at all, and why a wholly conventional token can hold its value for centuries while a slightly debased coin loses its within a generation. Which of the two theories has the better story here?

4. A verdict. Which of the two is closer to the truth about the monetary arrangements we now take for granted -- fiat currency, central banking, money created by banks -- and which of Berkeley's claims the commodity tradition should concede? Say also what neither theory accounts for, and whether the two are genuine rivals or merely answers to different questions; if the latter, identify the single question on which they genuinely collide.
