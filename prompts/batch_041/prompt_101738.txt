You are a philosopher who works on personal identity, autonomy and the ethics of advance care planning, and your speciality is auditing other people's positions for hidden inconsistencies rather than defending a line of your own. I am drafting a position paper on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and before I commit to it I want a consistency audit. Please begin by setting out, in your own words, what each of my commitments amounts to and what further claims each one commits me to; then work through the commitments pairwise; and only then deliver a verdict. Think carefully about which apparent conflicts are real and which dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely, and do not treat every tension as equally serious.

Here is where I have landed.

1. An advance directive has authority because it is an exercise of the author's autonomy: a competent person is entitled to settle in advance what may be done to her once she is no longer competent.

2. That authority does not depend on the later self being the same person as the author. Even if the patient with advanced dementia has lost any narrative continuity with the person who signed, or counts as a different person on some metaphysical criterion, the directive still governs.

3. What does the work, then, is precedent autonomy. The question of what she wants now is not the decisive question, and where the earlier directive and the present wishes conflict, the directive should generally prevail.

4. All the same, I would let a later self revoke it — provided the reversal is settled rather than isolated. If the patient, in a lucid interval, articulates a reflectively endorsed change of values, I think her revocation should override the directive. But in the repeat-signature case, where a woman re-signs an identical directive every year for a decade and then, in a single lucid interval, signs a revocation before lapsing again, I would still honour the decade-long directive.

5. I am not a welfarist about any of this. What gives a directive its force is the author's standing to direct her own care, not the fact that honouring it leaves anyone better off.

6. I also hold that a liberal state can enforce advance directives without violating its neutrality, since what it enforces is the individual's own choice rather than any contested conception of the good.

Please then do the following, in this order.

(a) Identify which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only appear to. Be explicit about the ones that dissolve, and say what does the dissolving.
(b) Rank the genuine tensions by severity, from most to least serious, rather than presenting them as a flat list.
(c) For each genuine tension, state precisely what would have to give, which commitment is cheapest to revise, and what that revision costs me elsewhere.
(d) Tell me which single revision would clean up the largest number of tensions at once, and whether adopting it would force further changes I have not yet noticed.

Write the whole answer in British English, and keep it to the audit itself — I do not need a survey of the literature or a list of further reading.
