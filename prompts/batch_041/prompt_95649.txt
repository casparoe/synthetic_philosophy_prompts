I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I am trying to pin down what it would actually mean to say that the sciences are dividing the world at its real seams rather than imposing convenient labels. Before I read any particular account of natural kinds — essentialist, cluster-based, pragmatist, pluralist, or something else again — I want the specification written down first: what any adequate theory in this area has to deliver.

Write that specification. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a commitment of one camp rather than another. I want the theory to be answerable at least to the following:

- The chemistry cases. Carbon is a single element, yet graphite, diamond, and graphene are about as different as substances get. For a handful of elements, thallium among them, the standard atomic weight is published as an interval rather than a single number, because different terrestrial sources have different isotope ratios. So is the real kind the element, the substance, or the sample?
- Biological species. Speciation is gradual, there is no shared essence, and in microbes the genealogy looks more like a web than a tree, yet biologists treat species as more than filing cabinets.
- Role kinds. A vitamin is unified by what it does in a body rather than by shared chemistry; its members are chemically unrelated. The same seems true of predator, gene, and antibiotic resistance.
- Kinds that may depend on us. Race, and diagnostic categories such as the autism spectrum, which may change the people sorted into them.
- Kinds settled by decision rather than discovery. The definition of planet the IAU adopted in August 2006, which demoted Pluto; the age of majority.
- Machine-generated categories. Clusters produced by unsupervised learning on large biological datasets that predict outcomes well, where nobody knows what, if anything, holds the cluster together.
- Residual and bookkeeping categories. The original junk DNA, later found to be heterogeneous; weeds; the contents of my kitchen drawer.

Beyond handling those cases, what must the theory explain — for instance, why generalization and prediction along kind lines work at all? What distinctions must it respect, such as kind membership versus degree of membership, one true taxonomy versus many legitimate ones, naturalness as all-or-nothing versus a matter of degree, mind-independence versus classification-dependence? And what established results must it stay consistent with?

Then the part I care about most: flag any subset of these requirements you suspect cannot be satisfied jointly, and say which single requirement does the most to rule out candidate theories. Keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them; I will work through the named positions myself.
