'David Lewis's triviality result is the reason contemporary philosophers are so wary of saying that indicative conditionals have truth conditions. The result concerns the attractive idea that the probability of "If A, then B" is simply the conditional probability of B given A. Lewis showed that if you accept this equation for every indicative conditional, you are forced to conclude that every conditional is either certainly true or certainly false. There is no room for uncertain conditionals. The only way to keep the equation is to restrict it to simple, unembedded conditionals and deny that conditionals can appear inside larger compounds; if you allow them to embed, the equation collapses. Most philosophers have responded by giving up the truth-conditional picture altogether: on the dominant view, an indicative conditional does not state a proposition at all; it expresses a high conditional probability, and its "probability" is just the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. Lewis's theorem is thus the central argument for the expressivist view of conditionals.'
