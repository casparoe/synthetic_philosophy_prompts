I am a retired experimental scientist — a chemist — who has spent the last few years, for the first time in my life, reading philosophy, and I have lately been working through the question of how we can know anything at all about the distant past. In that reading I keep meeting a pattern in historical reasoning that I cannot put a name to, and I suspect it is not one thing but several that have been run together.

The pattern is this. To decide whether some ancient event occurred, we count the reports of it. Three surviving authors, from three different places and times, look like three witnesses, and their number is felt as strength. But I have learned to ask what stands behind each report. Very often two of the three descend, directly or through lost intermediaries, from a single earlier account, and the third may be doing so as well. What looked like three witnesses is one witness in three costumes. The number of reports has not changed; the number of independent reports has, and it is the second number, not the first, that ought to govern our confidence — except that the second number is generally unknowable, and the only evidence we have for it is the very agreement and disagreement among the reports whose worth we were trying to judge. The agreement can mean the reports are independent and the event is real, or that they share a common ancestor; the disagreement can mean they are independent, or that later hands edited a common original. Each reading is available, and the one adopted tends to be the one that suits the conclusion already in view.

Two lesser versions of the same trouble keep surfacing. When two reports share an unusual error, we treat the shared error as proof of a common ancestor — rightly — yet we are reluctant to apply the identical reasoning to a shared true detail, which would equally indicate a common ancestor rather than corroboration. And the further back one goes, the more the reports seem to funnel through a single lost source, so that an apparent abundance of testimony is really its scarcity: the many streams, traced upstream, are one spring.

What I want is the name of the underlying situation, if it has one — the case in which the evidential weight of testimony is fixed by the independence of the sources, while the independence of the sources can only be estimated from the testimony itself, so that the whole appraisal threatens to run in a circle. If a concept already covers this, I would like to know how it is defined and how it differs from the nearer things it resembles: the plain caution that sources may be derivative, the common-cause point that agreement need not be corroboration, and a worry from my own training about treating readings as independent when they share an instrument or a sample. If no concept covers it exactly, then name it, and tell me what it does and does not cover — whether it is one phenomenon or two, and where the boundary lies between a deliberately forged common source and an honestly shared memory.
