I have recently been asked to set the agenda for a small team working on cooperation between agents that cannot causally influence one another: acausal trade, superrationality, program equilibrium, and the decision theory surrounding them. My impression is that the field has organized itself around whether such trade is possible, and I no longer think that is the binding question. What I want to know is what must be true of a trade for it to be load-bearing, in the sense that an agent could rationally build it into its own design, or an institution into its policy.

Please give me a ranked agenda of the open questions that matter most, scoring mattering by three things: how many other questions depend on the answer; how much the answer would change what an agent or institution should do; and how tractable the question is for someone reasoning at a desk who cannot run experiments on other agents. I keep circling three candidate cruxes and would like each of them placed explicitly on the list: first, whether acausal trade presupposes that the traders' decision procedures are mutually knowable, and if so whether logical uncertainty makes that presupposition vacuous; second, whether the alleged gains from trade are genuine or an artifact of double-counting the same possible worlds under different descriptions; third, whether the whole framework collapses into ordinary expected-value reasoning once one fixes a prior over other agents' policies. Tell me which of these is upstream of the others, which is overrated, and what argument or evidence over the next year or two would move any of them up or down my list.
