I'm writing a long piece for the ideas section on the ethics of technically true speech — the press statement that says there are no plans at this time, the apology that concedes only that mistakes were made, the interview in which nothing false is said and nothing true is volunteered. Underneath it sits a question the philosophers have argued over for decades: whether misleading someone is really a lesser wrong than lying to them, or whether the difference is one we care about for reasons that have little to do with the harm done.

The literature on that question is tidy in a way the life it describes is not, so I want to hang the essay on a novel: Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. Stevens, its narrator, is the most thoroughly misleading voice I know in fiction, and I have come to think he never actually lies — that the whole book is conducted in statements that are true and arrangements that are not. He misleads by what he declines to say about Miss Kenton, by the way he narrates the dismissal of the two Jewish maids at Darlington Hall, by his account of the evening his father died upstairs while he served dinner below, and above all by the person he presents himself as being: a butler of dignity, a professional, a man with no opinions about his employer's politics. He does it to us as well as to the people around him — the novel withholds what we need in order to judge him, and then lets us see what we have been believing.

Here is what I want from you. Read the novel through the lying/misleading distinction, and tell me where the distinction illuminates the book and where the book puts pressure on the distinction. I am interested in four things in particular.

First, the standard line: a lie is the assertion of something false with the intent to deceive, while misleading is bringing someone to a false belief without asserting anything false. If that is the distinction, does Stevens ever cross the line, and does it matter whether he does? Second, the moral asymmetry most of us half-believe — that misleading is the milder sin. I suspect the novel inverts it. A lie leaves a fact on the table, one that can be denied, corrected, or thrown back at the liar; Stevens's manner leaves nothing to deny, because he has said nothing false, and so it cannot even be objected to. I would like you to test that thought rather than simply accept it. Third, the awkward case of self-deception: if deception requires the intention to produce a false belief in someone else, then by definition Stevens cannot be deceiving the person he deceives most thoroughly, which is himself. Does the novel show that the definition is wrong, or that there is a separate phenomenon here we should stop calling deception? Fourth, a distinction I have been trying out and cannot find in what I have read. I can see three ways of misleading: by assertion, where a true sentence is arranged to suggest a false one; by omission, through silence or the changed subject; and by demeanor, where one deceives by the persona one presents rather than by anything one says. Is the third genuinely distinct from the other two, or does it reduce to them? And is Stevens chiefly a case of the third?

I should say what I do not want. I do not need a bibliography, and I cannot use citations I cannot check; I need the argument and the scenes. Nor do I want the novel reduced to an illustration of a taxonomy — if the reading flattens Stevens into a case study, it has failed, and I would rather you tell me where the lens distorts the book than force it. There is a real question whether the book is about deception at all, or about dignity, self-justification, and a certain English reticence that is not quite either. And I would like to know what fiction can do here that an argument cannot: whether making the reader the victim of a slow misleading teaches something about the wrong of it that a definition cannot, or whether that is a sentimental claim about literature that I should not put in print.

Give me something in the neighbourhood of 1200 to 1500 words: a reading with a thesis I can put at the centre of the piece, the scenes it turns on, an honest reckoning with the parts of the novel that resist it, and a closing paragraph on what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about lying and misleading that the debate does not have on its own.
