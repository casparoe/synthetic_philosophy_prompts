Since retiring from the lab I have been sitting in on a philosophy of probability reading group, and I am trying to get straight about the Principal Principle. A member introduced it to me this way: objective chance is best pictured as a perfectly calibrated expert on how things will turn out, a forecaster who is never misleading, and the Principal Principle just tells you to take the expert's credence as your own whenever you have nothing inadmissible to add to it. He then leaned on the picture to conclude that the principle is nothing but a coherence requirement on our credences, and that we need not regard chances as features of the world in their own right, since deference to an expert is a purely epistemic matter.

Please audit the analogy rather than the metaphysics. Give me the correspondence table — the expert, the expert's opinion, my own evidence, the admissibility clause, and my ability to identify the expert independently of the advice — and then say where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in three cases: an expert whose opinion is itself among the facts he is opining about, so that learning he holds it could undermine the opinion (as when a chance event carries information that undercuts the chance it came from); an expert whose holding of an opinion is itself an event in the world and so has a chance of occurring; and the fact that I can certify someone as an expert before hearing a word of advice, whereas chance is arguably identified in part by the deference norm itself. Separate the disanalogies that bear on my colleague's use from the merely picturesque ones, tell me whether his deflationary conclusion is supported by the analogy or merely carried by it, and, if the analogy cannot be repaired, say what would serve better.
