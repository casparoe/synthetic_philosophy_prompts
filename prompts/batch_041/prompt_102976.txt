I want your credence in one precisely stated claim about pain, together with the decomposition that generates it — not a survey of the literature, and not a hedge that the question is really several questions. Pick a number.

The claim, call it S: it is possible for a subject to be in a state that is genuinely a pain — same bodily location, same sensory quality, same intensity, produced by the same nociceptive machinery as an ordinary pain — and for that state not to be intrinsically bad for the subject, where intrinsically bad means bad for the subject independently of consequences and independently of the subject's own beliefs or desires about it. Equivalently: the awfulness of pain is not a necessary feature of the sensory state itself.

Some context to pin the target down, since the case is where the interest lies. There is a rare syndrome, pain asymbolia, usually following damage to the insula, the anterior cingulate, or the white matter connecting them, and sometimes following cingulotomy or morphine analgesia. Such patients identify a pinprick, localize it correctly, and describe it as sharp or hot, yet show no withdrawal, no guarding, and no distress — some of them smile or laugh. They report that they feel the pain but are not bothered by it. Grahek's gloss on this is that they feel pain but are not in pain. Two complications you should not quietly drop: the lesions are anatomically extensive, so the syndrome may not be a clean single-variable subtraction of one component from an otherwise intact state; and a recent line of argument holds that what these patients retain is not a pain at all but a residual non-painful sensory capacity, so the syndrome would not exhibit pain-minus-awfulness at all.

Now give me the following.

1. A single number for S.

2. The sub-questions the number depends on, each with its own rough probability and one line of justification. I suggest at least the following, but replace or add terms as you think correct:
   (a) the clinical reading — do asymbolia patients retain a genuine pain sensation, rather than a non-painful sensory remnant?
   (b) conditional on (a), is the retained sensation wholly non-bad, or still mildly bad but merely unmotivating?
   (c) the conceptual question — is pain's badness best located in the sensory state itself or in the subject's affective and conative response to it, i.e. does S come out true almost analytically once the metaphysics of valence is fixed?
   (d) whether S should be read as metaphysical possibility (a creature with nociception and no aversion) or as a claim about the human case specifically.
   (e) how much the extensive-lesion and classification objections should discount any inference from asymbolia to S.

3. How those sub-probabilities combine into your final number, and which term the number is most sensitive to.

4. What single piece of evidence or argument, obtainable without running a study, would move your number most, and in which direction.

For what it is worth, I am at about 0.75 and a colleague I have been arguing with is at about 0.3. After you have given your own number and decomposition, tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in — not the headline number, but the term underneath it. And state explicitly what would count as settling S, since both possible and intrinsically bad are doing a great deal of work in the formulation.
