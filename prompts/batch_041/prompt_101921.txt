I am assembling a one-page handout for a graduate seminar in philosophy of science on idealization and unrealistic assumptions — the family of views running from the older positivist idea that a theory's assumptions need not be realistic provided its predictions are good (the position Friedman defended in his essay on positive economics, which Samuelson later christened the F-twist) through to the contemporary literature that treats models as deliberately simplified, distorted, or isolating representations. What I want is not prose but a decision tree that sorts a reader into a named position on one question: when a model's central assumptions are known to be false, what is the epistemic status of the model and of the claims it licenses? Please build the tree from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of names, so that each internal node is a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf is a position (or, where the answer-combination has no defender, a leaf marked unoccupied). Phrase every node as a concrete case or a forced choice rather than as a request to endorse a label, and make sure the cases genuinely pull the positions apart.

The nodes I would like you to work with, though you may add one or two if the tree needs them: first, whether the falsity of an assumption is something the model is answerable for at all — with the options that a false assumption can be a positive strength (the more unrealistic the assumptions, the more significant the theory), that it is a debt the model must eventually pay down, that it is irrelevant because the assumption was never meant to describe the world but only to hold something fixed, and that it disqualifies the model unless the assumption is at least approximately true. Second, the frictionless plane: when later work adds friction and the model is de-idealized, does the successor come closer to the truth about real surfaces, or is it merely a different model with the same standing as its predecessor? Third, an economic model whose agents are assumed to maximize expected utility infallibly, and which predicts aggregate market behavior well: does that success give reason to believe something about the unobservable decision processes of real agents, only that the model is empirically adequate about observables, or nothing at all about agents, the model being a calculating device whose success shows only its usefulness? Fourth, the contrast between treating a massive body as a point mass (a distortion) and assuming away a factor known to be operating (an isolation): do these two kinds of false assumption have different epistemic standing, or are they points on one continuum of idealization? Fifth, whether the model is a description of the world or a tool for exploring what follows from a set of commitments — something like an experiment performed on a system that does not exist. And sixth, whether the regularities a successful model encodes are true of the world (at least approximately) or only locally true of the model system, the world never quite matching them.

Let the leaves be, at minimum, Friedman-style instrumentalism about assumptions, model fictionalism, constructive empiricism, Galilean idealization paired with convergent realism, minimalist idealization or isolationism, a Cartwright-style view on which the fundamental laws lie about the world and only phenomenological regularities are locally true, and a model-as-experiment or exploratory-modeling view; include assumption-realism as the leaf for the fourth option of the first node, and invent a leaf name where a combination is occupied but I have not named it. For each leaf, add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Because the whole point is diagnostic rather than encyclopedic, please flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — I expect instrumentalism, fictionalism, and constructive empiricism to collapse into one another unless the third node is doing real work, and I expect the two realist-flavored idealization views to come apart only if you add a question about whether de-idealization is guaranteed to terminate — and note which answer-combinations are unoccupied, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Aim for six to eight internal nodes and seven or eight leaves, and keep the whole thing to a single page.
