Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over public reason and religious arguments in politics. Call them the restraint camp and the inclusivist camp. The restraint camp holds that when citizens or officials exercise coercive political power, they owe those subject to it a justification framed in terms that do not depend on any one controversial comprehensive doctrine, and that religious premises therefore cannot serve as the sole operative ground of such coercion. The inclusivist camp holds that this demand is either too strong, because it burdens religious citizens asymmetrically and misdescribes democratic persuasion, or too vague, because a suitably broad ideal of public justification can admit religiously grounded arguments that are offered in good faith, addressed to others as free and equal, and open to contestation. Neither camp should be represented as denying the obvious: the restraint camp may grant that religious motivations are politically legitimate; the inclusivist camp may grant that coercion needs some public justification.

A mediator has proposed the following compromise, the Two-Track Rule: first, every coercive law or policy must be defensible by at least one rationale that does not depend on any controversial comprehensive doctrine; second, citizens and officials may also advance religious or other comprehensive rationales, provided they do not treat those rationales as the sole ground for coercing those who do not share them; and third, the first requirement is waived for decisions that are non-coercive, symbolic, or internal to a voluntary association. The two camps have agreed to treat the following cases as relevant evidence for the dispute: the Municipal Hospitality Ordinance, in which a council member votes for a shelter-access bylaw because of a religious command to welcome the stranger but offers only a cost-and-feasibility rationale in public; the Conscientious Exemption Campaign, in which a religious community opposes a vaccine mandate on grounds it says cannot be translated into neutral terms and asks only for an exemption rather than a repeal; the Untranslatable Premise Case, in which a policy is supported by a religious tradition that insists its reasons lose their force when restated in secular language; and the Two-Track Reversal Case, in which a religious majority enacts a law with a technically available secular rationale that no one would have proposed absent the majority's doctrine, and the minority calls the rationale a fig leaf while the majority insists the rule asks only for availability, not causal efficacy.

Draft the document both camps could sign. It should contain a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about the Two-Track Rule, the cases, and what would count as defeat for each camp. It should then contain a disagreements table with columns for the precise point of disagreement; the restraint camp's formulation, in wording its own side would accept; the inclusivist camp's formulation, in wording its own side would accept; the crux; the case or argument both camps agree bears on that crux; the restraint camp's advance prediction; and the inclusivist camp's advance prediction. End with a short section distinguishing any disagreements that are merely verbal from those that are substantive, and identifying what, if anything, both camps agree would actually move the debate.

Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or attributions to particular authors or texts. Do not rely on any particular historical episode that a reader could not reconstruct from the prompt. Keep the prose formal and precise, and make each side's formulations strong enough that its proponents would recognize them as fair.
