After thirty years as a field ecologist I retired, and at the urging of a friend I have begun attending a weekly philosophy discussion group. I am new enough to the subject that the argument I am about to describe outran me, though I would like to know how it should come out. It concerned guilt and shame, and it took place between two other members, whom I will call she and he. I have no stake in either side and changed my mind about who was winning several times.

(1) She opened by claiming shame is the better moral emotion, or at any rate the more serious one. Guilt, she said, is act-directed: it tells you that you did a bad thing, and it is discharged by repair. But repair is often cheap, and once it is made you are unchanged. Shame is self-directed: it tells you that you are the sort of person who does such things, and that verdict cannot be discharged by an apology. If you want people to change rather than merely to compensate, you want shame.

(2) He replied that this is exactly the problem. Shame convicts the whole self on the strength of one act, which is a false verdict — nobody is wholly bad because of a single failure — and a false verdict is a poor foundation for morality. Guilt is proportioned to the act, and its natural response is repair rather than concealment. He added that shame is inherently social: it presupposes an audience whose regard one has lost, which makes morality hostage to other people's opinions.

(3) She answered that the audience need not be real. People feel shame alone in an empty room, measured against a standard they themselves endorse. The witness can be internalized, and when it is, the standard is one's own, so the charge of heteronomy fails.

(4) He said that if the witness is fully internalized then what remains is guilt under another name: a standard, a violation, and a bad feeling. The distinctively shameful element is the standing-down, the loss of face. Strip that out and you have guilt; leave it in and you have an emotion that tracks rank rather than rightness — which is why shame attaches to things that are not wrong at all, such as a father's bankruptcy or a sister's divorce.

(5) She denied that shame is essentially about rank. She distinguished two things people call by that name: being lowered in the eyes of a community, and falling short of an ideal one holds for oneself. Only the first is about status; the second is about aspiration, and it is the one that does moral work, because it makes a person dissatisfied with being merely decent. Guilt, by contrast, is a bookkeeping emotion: it is satisfied when the debt is paid and says nothing about what one should become.

(6) He objected that her second kind of shame is just self-directed guilt about an ideal, so that the moment the ideal is specified we are back to a standard and a violation. And he pressed the social point: in practice it is shame, not guilt, that enforces a community's norms — and shame enforces whatever the local norms happen to be, being indifferent to their content. That, he said, is not a defect one can patch by pointing to a private ideal; it is the emotion's nature.

(7) She replied that guilt is equally indifferent to content — people feel guilty about breaking pointless rules, about enjoying themselves, about acts that harmed no one — so if indifference disqualifies an emotion it disqualifies both. And on the practical point she said the order of explanation runs the other way: guilt presupposes that one already cares about the person wronged or the standard violated, whereas shame is what creates that caring in the first place. Guilt, on her account, cannot be the more fundamental of the two.

(8) The group then turned to whether a society can deliberately choose which of these emotions predominates. She said it can: dominant moral emotions have shifted before, and one can cultivate the emotion one wants by changing what is publicly praised and blamed. He said it cannot: you do not decide to feel an emotion the way you decide to adopt a policy; what you can change are the scripts and the sanctions, and the emotions follow or they do not. He added that a society that sets out to manufacture guilt tends to get shame instead, people feeling bad about feeling bad, which is shame before a new audience, so the project is self-defeating. She answered that this shows only that the means are unreliable, not that the end is impossible, and that if he were right that we cannot choose, his own complaints about shame would be idle, since no one could have done otherwise.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify the rating. Then say which of the two is closer to the truth overall, and what both of them are missing. If there is a case that would separate their two views, I would like to see it, since in my old field we settled disputes by finding the observation that discriminated between the rivals, and I could not construct one here. Open with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of what I have described. Please write in American English.
