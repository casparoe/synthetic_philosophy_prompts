Consider a variant of the raven paradox. There are two finite populations: R, a small population of ravens, and S, a much larger population of non-ravens. The hypothesis H is that every raven in R is black. An investigator samples one object uniformly at random from the union of R and S. The object is a white swan, hence a non-black non-raven. The investigator is considering two evidential claims: (A) the observation confirms H; (B) the observation is evidentially irrelevant to H. I want your credence that (A) is true under each of two information conditions: first, the investigator knows that the sampled object came from S; second, the investigator knows only that the object is a non-black non-raven and does not know whether it came from R or S. Assume that the marginal probability that a randomly selected raven is black is 0.9, and the marginal probability that a randomly selected non-raven is black is 0.1, with populations large enough that sampling without replacement can be treated as sampling with replacement. Give a number between 0 and 1 for each condition. Then decompose each number into at least the following terms: the probability that the Bayesian likelihood-ratio account is the correct account of confirmation; the probability that the likelihood ratio is greater than one under the specified information; the probability that the confirmation, if any, is non-negligible rather than merely positive; the probability that the result is an artifact of the choice of predicates rather than a feature of the evidence; and any further term you think belongs. For each term, give a number and one line of justification. Then combine the terms into your overall credence for each condition, state which term you are least confident in, and say what evidence or argument would move that term most.
