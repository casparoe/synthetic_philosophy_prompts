The fault line I care about is whether beauty and friendship are good because of what they're like as experiences or because of what they're of, and every question in this thing should trace back to that. I run a reading group that just finished the last chapter of Principia Ethica (Moore, 1903), and I want a "which Moorean are you?" sorting quiz so people stop just announcing they're pluralists about intrinsic value and going home. What I need:

Eight questions, each a concrete case rather than a doctrine question. No "do you think beauty is intrinsically good?" items — people pick the flattering answer and you learn nothing. Two or three answer options per question, and every option has to be something a reasonable person might actually say, not a strawman sitting there to be avoided.

The cases should force commitments on things like: whether a stunning, uninhabited landscape makes the world a better place than a swamp does, how much better, and whether your answer changes if no one could ever perceive it; whether someone whose friends are secretly automata, or who's living in a very good simulation of having friends, has got the good Moore is pointing at; whether it matters that your friend loves the same beauty you do or whether that's just two separate goods sitting side by side; whether loving someone who doesn't love you back is still partly good; whether a friendship plus the beautiful thing it makes possible can be worth more than the two values added up. On that last one — Moore's principle is that the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts, so make at least two questions pull "organic unity" verdicts apart from straight "add up the parts" verdicts.

Leaves: I want the usual suspects plus at least one leaf that's a view Moore himself didn't hold and might not have considered, and at least one leaf for someone who thinks impersonal world-value talk is a mistake altogether and only someone's life can be better or worse. Each leaf gets a name (or "unnamed" if nobody's defended it), and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Include Moore's own position as a leaf and tell me whether the quiz can actually seperate it from the nearby views, or whether it collapses into one of them once the reader answers the cases.

Then the scoring key: which answer patterns land you where, including patterns that land you nowhere, and what you think that means.

Two things I want flagged rather than papered over. First, any pair of leaves the quiz can't pull apart from case answers alone — say which ones and why. Second, combinations in the space that nobody occupies, with a short diagnosis: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. I'd rather have an honest gap than a fake leaf invented to fill the grid.

Audience is the reading group, mostly non-philosophers, so keep the cases ordinary — no science fiction unless a case really needs it, and no relying on readers knowing the technical vocabulary going in.

Start right in on the quiz itself, no preamble and no restating what I asked for.
