I write a fortnightly column on philosophy for a general-interest ideas magazine, and I am working up a piece on the dispute over internal and external reasons — the distinction usually credited to Bernard Williams, between the claim that a reason statement is true only where sound deliberation from the agent's existing motivational set would move him, and the claim that the facts can give an agent a reason whether or not anything in his psychology answers to it. I have spent a fortnight reading around the debate and cannot find a single illustration I can put in front of a general readership. The cheerful amoralist is too easy for the internalist, who simply pronounces the man defective; the stranger on the far side of the world is too easy for the externalist, who inflates what may be no more than a claim about what we ought to feel into a claim about what the stranger is owed. I would rather commission a case to specification than improvise one, because the argument of the column will stand or fall with whether the case does honest work. What the case is for: to test whether the disagreement between the two views is substantive — whether there is any situation in which they must return different verdicts about one agent and one act — rather than a difference in what the word reason is being asked to do.

Here are my specifications. (1) Mundane and unexotic: a situation an ordinary reader could find themselves in, with no science fiction, no brain implants, no demons or perfect predictors, no fictional moral patients, and no stipulation of the verdicts inside the scenario itself. (2) The agent is fully informed about every non-normative fact bearing on the act and instrumentally impeccable, so that there is no false belief and no inferential error for the internalist to repair. (3) The case must not be one the internalist can dispose of by diagnosing a specific defect — ignorance, irrationality, depression, addiction, fanaticism or plain wickedness. Whatever silence in the agent's motivational set the case turns on has to be silence that the internalist cannot independently identify as a failure of practical reason without begging the question, and you must argue this rather than assert it. (4) No sound deliberative route from that motivational set may reach the act: not correcting a belief, not vividly imagining the situation, not noticing that one of his existing desires is broader than he had taken it to be, and not acquiring a new desire by way of one he already has. If such a route exists, the case has failed, and I would rather be told so than have it papered over. (5) The externalist's verdict must nonetheless have real grip on a reader who is not a philosopher; the case must not be one in which 'he has a reason anyway' is a merely technical pronouncement. That tension is the point of the piece. (6) One paragraph for the scenario. (7) If you can make the reason at issue a non-moral one — prudential, epistemic, aesthetic — do, since the moral versions have been worn smooth; if it can only be made to work as a moral case, say so and tell me why.

Deliver the scenario, then the demonstration: the verdict the internalist must return and the verdict the externalist must return, each derived from the view rather than asserted; the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and what becomes of the case when I remove it — in particular, whether it collapses into an ordinary case of ignorance or irrationality, where internalism wins by default; the strongest rejoinder available to the internalist and what accepting it costs him; and whether the case, if it works, shows that there are external reasons, or only that reason is being used in two senses. If the specifications turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, tell me which one is doing the impossible work. Finally, try to build the mirror image — a case in which the internalist must say the agent has a reason and the externalist must deny it — and if you cannot build one, tell me exactly what blocks it.
