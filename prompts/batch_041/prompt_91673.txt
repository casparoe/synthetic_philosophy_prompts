I help run the pre-departure orientation for my university's study-abroad office, and every year the same argument breaks out among the student leaders, usually over dinner and never to any conclusion. One camp holds that a guest follows the local conventions without comment or exception — you tip what locals tip, you queue the way the queue works there, you do not walk into traffic where crossing is frowned upon — because the conventions are part of the terms on which you are being hosted, and to flout them is to treat your hosts' expectations as optional. The other camp holds that most of these are just habits, that a visitor's moral obligations are exhausted by not harming anyone and by ordinary respect, and that the guest who over-tips in a country with no tipping custom, or who stands obediently in a line that exists only by custom, is not being virtuous but is being a nuisance or a free rider on other people's manners. Last year a returning student described a host family that had been quietly offended for weeks over something nobody would name aloud, and a second student described feeling complicit, every time she left a tip, in a labor market she thought was unjust; both stories landed as anecdotes rather than arguments, and we realized we could not actually say what was at stake. I want to hand out something better this year, and I want it to work in both directions, since the incoming exchange students ask us the mirror-image question — whether they are obliged to adopt the customs here.

Please write a taxonomy — a decision tree that sorts the positions rather than a list of names — that I can print on two sides of a single sheet for that session. Build it out of the questions that genuinely divide the positions, so that a reader answers each node from a concrete case rather than from a label: whether the convention has a point an outsider can recognize (coordination, fairness, courtesy, or nothing of the kind); whether the obligation, where there is one, is owed to the people whose expectations constitute the convention, to the practice's beneficiaries, or to no one in particular; whether these obligations are moral at all, or belong to a separate register of manners whose breach is a fault but not a wrong; whether the visitor's non-membership and non-consent make a difference, and if so whether that excuses them or merely changes whom they owe; whether the content of the convention matters morally, so that a tip that quietly shifts a wage onto the customer differs from a tip that is merely expressive, and whether a convention one thinks bad can still generate a local duty to comply while being opposed in public; whether the relevant unit of assessment is the individual act or the practice taken as a whole; whether it matters that the locals themselves disagree about the convention, or that it is legally enforced in some places and only customarily enforced in others; and whether jaywalking at two in the morning on an empty street belongs in the same family of cases as removing your shoes at the door or bringing the wrong gift to a wedding. I would like twelve to fourteen leaves, each labeled with the name of a position where a name fits and marked unnamed where none does, and each carrying one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders have to swallow to stay where they are. Please also flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers the tree permits that nobody seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Since the readers will be nineteen years old and reading this on a bus: keep the nodes to a line or two each, and make sure every node is phrased as a question someone could answer without having already decided which leaf they are supposed to land on.
