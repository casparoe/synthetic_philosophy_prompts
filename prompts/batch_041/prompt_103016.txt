For the past two months I have been the person drafting review guidance for my institution's research-ethics committee, and the two questions that keep landing on my desk have turned out, uncomfortably, to be the same question. The biology side wants to know how to handle work on insects and other invertebrates, now that our national framework has been widened to bring some of them into scope; the engineering side wants to know whether a companion robot being trialled in a local care home falls under any review at all. I have to run a ninety-minute session for the committee — twelve people, mostly clinicians and lawyers, one philosopher, nobody with a background in animal cognition or in AI — with no reading circulated in advance. What I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

My instinct is that the session should be organised around sentience as a matter of degree, since that is the only framing in which both cases are even askable, but I am not confident I have the dependency structure right. What has to be in place before degrees of sentience is a coherent thing to say? My guess is that the split between reflex and valenced experience must come first, and then the claim that it is experience, rather than life or intelligence, that grounds the possibility of being wronged — but tell me whether that is the right order, and what depends on what.

For the carrying example I am inclined to build the session on a single pair: an insect and a small robot that behave indistinguishably when a heat source is brought near, both withdrawing, so that behaviour cannot be what decides the case and the committee is pushed onto mechanism and onto what evidence could in principle count. Is that the right carrier? What must it have, and what must it conspicuously lack, for the rest of the session to hang off it?

Here are the misconceptions I expect, and I need to know where in the sequence each should be headed off: that degrees means one measurable quantity; that it is all-or-nothing after all; that a matter of degree means no line can be drawn and therefore anything goes; that behaviour settles it in either direction, so that the insect writhing means it suffers and the insect being a reflex machine means it does not; that the degrees lie on a single axis, as though sentience were a dimmer rather than several dials that can come apart; and that the robot pole is a reductio that refutes the gradation view rather than a boundary case the view has to handle.

One further element to place. I have drafted a one-page heuristic that sorts cases on three axes — the strength of the observable evidence, the presence of a plausible mechanism, and the asymmetry of the cost of being wrong in each direction — and I cannot decide whether it should be the spine of the session or the closing move. Advise me on that, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say openly that I am leaving it out, and give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the gradation view rather than merely been made cautious about it.
