I am assembling a small anthology for a reading group that pairs short fiction with problems in social choice, and I would like you to write the anchor story. The premise is this: a deliberative body of seven members — choose whatever setting keeps the cast small and the stakes legible, say the council of a generation ship or a village land tribunal — must decide a single question that everyone agrees is a logical function of three sub-questions. Make the compound question something like whether to approve a proposal that is admissible only if it is safe, affordable, and freely consented to, so that each member votes separately on the three conditions and then on the proposal itself, and every member's three votes together with their verdict are perfectly consistent with one another. What I want the story to turn on is a discovery the members make only once the count is done: tallying the three sub-questions one by one yields approval, while tallying the proposal directly yields rejection (or the reverse), so the group's aggregate judgment is incoherent even though no individual's is. Let the discovery arrive as a mundane arithmetic shock — one person has the figures, another rechecks them — and let the rest of the story be the argument that follows: whose tally is the body's real decision, whether the members should have anticipated this and voted differently, and what, if anything, the outcome now authorizes them to do.

I also want one character, ideally the least formally trained of the group, to press the harder point that the fix cannot be a cleverer voting rule — that any method which honors a few plainly reasonable demands will end up violating one of the others — and I want that point to land through the drama rather than as a lecture, with the other characters resisting it on practical grounds. Please keep it to roughly 2,000 to 3,000 words, third person, with no narrator's gloss at the end; the conceptual content should be carried entirely by what the characters notice, say, and do. If you can, let the story leave open which tally the group should have adopted, while making it unmistakable why the two cannot both be right.
