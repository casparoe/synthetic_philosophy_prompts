Next month I have to present to our aquaculture standards committee and I want to be properly ready for the Q&A, because last time they ate me alive. My pitch is that we should extend the slaughter-welfare rules we already apply to farmed finfish to decapod crustaceans — crabs, lobsters, the big prawns — and that the trigger for protection should be evidence of nociception together with the kind of motivational trade-off behaviour we'd normally read as pain, rather than the presence of a vertebrate-style brain. My main moves are going to be: (1) the precautionary argument from the asymmetry of moral risk, i.e. if we're wrong that they don't suffer the cost is enormous, and if we're wrong that they do the cost is a bit of extra kit and gentler handling; (2) the evolutionary point that decapods show wound-directed behaviour and long-lasting motivational trade-offs after noxious stimulation, which is roughly the same sort of evidence we already accept in fish; (3) the consistency point that we regulate fish slaughter on exactly that kind of evidence, so exempting the crustaceans is arbitrary; and (4) a concrete pilot — mandatory electrical stunning before boiling for decapods above a certain size, with a stocking-density cap during holding.

The room is going to be: two fish-health vets who've spent their whole careers on finfish and are frankly a bit miffed at anything that takes attention off sea lice; a crustacean physiologist who is a hard sceptic about invertebrate pain and will keep saying that nociception isn't pain; a welfare scientist who thinks the whole fish-pain literature is confounded and over-read; an economist who only cares about compliance cost and the level playing field; and one lay member who will ask the human question in plain language. There's a chair who likes to wrap up early and hates a row.

Can you write me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the proposal; for each one a two-minute reply and a one-line note on where the reply is soft; what I should concede up front so I don't come across as a zealot; the objection that would sink the whole thing if it lands; and the one question I'm dreading and hoping nobody asks. I don't need a reading list or anything, just the prep. And please write it in British English — I keep getting told off for Americanisms in these documents.
