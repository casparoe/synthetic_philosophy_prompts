I am a third-year undergraduate in political theory, and I volunteer at our city's Office of New Americans, which is how I have ended up invited to give the welcome address at next month's naturalization ceremony. I have about five minutes at the lectern and a room of roughly sixty people: forty candidates taking the oath, their families and friends, the presiding judge, two local officials, a reporter from the community paper, and, because they saw the announcement, my instructor and two classmates from a seminar on democratic theory. The families want to be moved. My instructor will hear every sentence as a claim.

Please act as a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and who also writes speeches for civic occasions. Treat the problem as a problem before you treat it as material, and work step by step: begin with a brainstorming page in which you list eight or ten candidate ways of opening the address and say, for each, why it is or is not a dead end. Do not begin drafting the address until that list is finished.

Here is the background. In seminar we have been working on what I can only call the boundary problem: any democratic decision presupposes a bounded set of decision-makers, so the demos cannot democratically decide who belongs to the demos without already being one. Three further thoughts should be doing work in the speech, though none of them may be named as a theory or attributed to anyone: first, that a person can be subject to a state's coercive rules without being a member of its demos, and can be affected by its decisions without ever being subject to them, and that these two facts draw the boundary in different places; second, that nearly everyone in any demos entered it by inheritance rather than by choice, so a naturalization ceremony is the rare case in which membership is explicitly conferred rather than silently transmitted; third, that the words "you are now one of us" are supposed to be performative — they are supposed to bring about the very "we" they invoke. I would like the address to sit honestly with the fact that it cannot quite do that.

Constraints for the address itself:

- It must be warm, plain-spoken, and genuinely welcoming. This is a celebration that people have waited years for; nothing about the philosophy may be allowed to sour it, and there should be no jargon a guest in the third row would have to decode.
- The philosophy must do structural work, determining which formulations I can and cannot use, rather than appearing as an aside. In particular, I may not simply assert that we have chosen them, since the "we" that does the choosing is exactly what is in question, and I may not resolve the difficulty by appealing to values we all supposedly share, since a shared moral inheritance is one of the things the boundary problem throws into doubt.
- Ground it in this particular room rather than in citizenship in general: at least one concrete, specific detail about this ceremony, not an abstraction.
- No citations, no names of theorists, no titles of works, no quotations, and no appeals to documents. I have no access to sources, and for the purposes of this task neither do you. If you find yourself reaching for a famous line, paraphrase the thought or drop it.
- Banned, because I would be embarrassed to hear myself say them: journey, melting pot, nation of immigrants, dream, tapestry, and any sentence beginning "this country was built by".

Deliverables: (1) the brainstorming page described above; (2) the address, around 700 words, written to be spoken aloud, in short sentences, without headings; (3) a closing note of no more than 150 words naming the single most important sentence the address was unable to say, and what it would have cost the address to say it.
