I am a retired natural scientist — thirty years in atmospheric measurement, which is to say thirty years of turning a vague quantity into a number that other people would then argue about — and since retiring I have been working through philosophy on my own, mostly philosophy of science and, more recently, ethics. A small research institute that publishes an annual report on violence has invited me onto its methods committee, and I have found myself out of my depth in a specific way. In my old field, once we had fixed an operational definition of, say, aerosol optical depth, the disputes were about instruments and error bars, not about whether the quantity existed. Here the disputes seem to begin one level up, and I cannot tell which of them are real.

So please act as a philosopher of the social sciences and of measurement, with a working knowledge of how violence has been defined and contested, and write me a decision procedure that the institute's four analysts can actually run — first incident by incident, then in aggregate, when they compile the report. The stakes are not trivial: the headline figure, the change in violence over a fixed multi-decade window, drives press coverage, next year's funding, and an annual diplomatic complaint. The analysts are careful but not philosophers; each can give an incident about fifteen minutes, the whole coding round is one week, and a senior editor signs off on the final number. What I need is something reusable, not advice about this year's report.

The procedure should specify, in order, what question to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, the points at which it returns a definite count-it or do-not-count-it, and the points at which it must hand the decision up to the editor because the question is one of value or of stipulation rather than of fact. It must also handle aggregation: whether to publish a rate, a count, or both; what population to divide by and why; and what to do in a year when the rate and the count move in opposite directions.

Please treat the following four distinctions as raw material to be disciplined, used, or rejected — not as gospel. First, the difference between violence as an act, violence as an outcome or condition, and violence as a relation (who is doing it to whom, and with what standing). Second, the difference between a harm that is itself a violence and a harm that is merely a downstream consequence of one. Third, the difference among intended, foreseen, and unforeseen harms, and whether the first two belong in the same column. Fourth, the difference between a figure that measures something in the world and a figure that is a stipulation we have agreed to publish; I suspect the report should be explicit about which of its numbers are which, and I would like the procedure to make that distinction do real work rather than decorate the methodology section.

Then run the procedure on three cases, at least one of which must come out in a way the institute will not like:

(a) deaths in a famine brought about by a blockade and by the deliberate diversion of relief;
(b) a decade in which absolute homicides rise while the homicide rate falls, because the population grew faster;
(c) a case of your choosing in which a killing by the state — an execution, or a death in custody — is treated differently from an otherwise similar killing by a private person, and the procedure must say whether that asymmetry is a finding about the world or a stipulation we have adopted.

Finally, the report has been attacked by critics who say the whole question of a trend in violence is not a factual question at all, since violence is a value-laden category and any trend line merely encodes the definer's values. Tell me what the procedure can and cannot say in reply: whether that objection is fatal, merely a caution, or itself confused, and whether the explicit marking of stipulations is the right response to it.

Begin with a brief brainstorm of the candidate definitions of violence and what each buys and costs, before committing to the procedure. Think carefully about where the procedure will misfire, and do not hide the judgment calls inside steps that merely look procedural. End with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence the single most consequential design decision in the procedure.
