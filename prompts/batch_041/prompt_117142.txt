Construct a thought experiment that pulls apart two distinctions usually treated as interchangeable: the line between doing and allowing, and the line between killing and letting die. Fix the two lines as follows. Doing versus allowing is settled by bodily movement: an agent does something when her body moves as part of a course of conduct she has adopted, and merely allows when it does not. Killing versus letting die is settled by causal role: an agent kills when her conduct belongs among the causes we would cite in explaining the death, and merely lets die when the death is fully explained without her, so that her contribution is at most a background condition.

I want a single realistic scenario, one continuous setting that a normal person could occupy, in which these two tests come apart in both directions at once: one agent who does something by the movement test yet only lets die by the causal test, and one agent who merely allows by the movement test yet kills by the causal test. Keep the two deaths as alike as possible in everything except the variable under test: comparable victims, comparable motives, comparable foreseeability, comparable outcomes. The point of the case is to test whether these are really one distinction or two.

Constraints: one paragraph for the scenario itself; no trolleys, no science fiction, nothing that requires the reader to accept a strange metaphysics. Above all, none of the stock illustrations — no life-support machines, no euthanasia, no starving infants, no runaway trains. Those are excluded precisely because the classification would then be driven by familiarity with the examples rather than by the structure of the case.

After the scenario: (i) classify each agent's conduct under each of the two tests, and name the exact feature of the case that makes the tests diverge; (ii) say which of the two classifications the moral verdict ought to track, and what a defender of the view that doing/allowing and killing/letting die are one and the same distinction would say to resist your case; (iii) describe the smallest change to the scenario that would collapse it back into the ordinary pattern in which the two tests agree.
