I'm a second-year doctoral student in a science-and-technology policy program, and I have drifted into a reading group that is nominally about AI governance but keeps turning into an argument about the economics of safety. The recurring dispute is whether caution is something an organization can simply decide to have, or whether it is rationed by competition, and I have decided to write my seminar paper as a case analysis rather than as another piece of theory. The two ideas I need to work with are these. The competitiveness constraint is the claim that under racing conditions unilateral caution is taxed: an actor can buy only as much safety as its rivals will tolerate, because whoever slows down to be careful loses to whoever does not, so the binding limit on safety is not what the actor knows how to do but what the competition permits. The alignment tax is the price, in capability or in speed, of building something safe rather than merely effective, the gap between what one could ship and what one would be willing to ship. Both are usually asserted at a level of abstraction that makes them nearly unfalsifiable, and I would like to see them run through a case whose decision structure is visible in detail.

The case I want to use is the 737 MAX. Here is the outline I will fix so that we are working from the same facts: Airbus launched a re-engined version of its narrowbody at the end of 2010 and rapidly accumulated an enormous order book; Boeing, which had been studying a clean-sheet replacement for the 737, abandoned that plan and in 2011 launched a re-engined 737 instead; the selling proposition to airlines was that the aircraft would remain a 737 as far as pilots and their training budgets were concerned, which meant that handling differences caused by the larger and differently mounted engines had to be masked by a flight-control feature rather than disclosed to crews; that feature drew its input from a single angle-of-attack sensor and could trim the nose down repeatedly; two aircraft crashed within five months, killing 346 people, and the fleet was grounded worldwide from March 2019 until late 2020; and certification had been substantially delegated to Boeing's own designated employees, with references to the new system removed from the flight manual at Boeing's request. What I want from you is the disciplined mapping, run in both directions. Who or what is the competitor, and what exactly is racing: a rival's product, a delivery date, a promise already made to customers, a share price, a regulator's standing? What is the tax, precisely, and who was going to pay it? If the tax was the simulator training that a different handling solution would have required, the parties who would have paid it were the airlines, and the parties who paid the actual price were somewhere else entirely, which seems to me the crux of the case. Tell me which features of the episode the two concepts genuinely explain, such as why the workaround looked rational at the time, why the safety analysis was produced by the party with an interest in its conclusion, and why no one positioned to slow the program down had an incentive to; and then tell me which features they misdescribe or simply miss, bearing in mind that the competitor was not racing toward a decisive capability, that the constraint was partly endogenous because Boeing's own marketing commitments and its need to protect a share price set the deadline as much as Airbus did, and that the safety case was not a cost to be traded against capability but a judgment made by an interested party. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

Then the other direction, which is the part I care about most: what does this episode show about the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax as ideas? If comparable pressure can be generated by a firm's own prior promises, by a regulator's thin resources, and by a market that will punish a two-year delay, is the competitiveness constraint a fact about the world or a story actors tell in order to make their choices look forced, and does it matter for the theory which of those it is? Does the alignment tax survive translation from a technology cost into a distributional one, that is, is the interesting question not how much safety costs but who pays for it, and why the eventual payers had no seat at the table? Note, because it bears on the verdict, that the regulators' own later flight testing suggested the aircraft might have met certification standards without the feature at all. Finally, tell me where the analogy to frontier AI holds and where it breaks, in particular whether the existence of a settled safety standard and an accountable regulator in aviation makes this a stronger test of the theory or a weaker one. I do not want the standard Manhattan Project comparison, a cultural history of Boeing, or a lecture on the ethics of automation. I want an analysis that could be read by someone who already knows the facts and cares only about whether the two concepts earn their keep, ending with a verdict on whether they explain the episode or merely redescribe commercial pressure in a more technical vocabulary, and with an account of what a defender of the competitiveness constraint would have to claim about this case in order to keep the idea intact.
