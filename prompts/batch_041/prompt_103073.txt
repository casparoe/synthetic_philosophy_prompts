I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and the broader rationalist blogosphere — I read the Sequences years ago and never quite kicked the habit — and Sen's liberal paradox has become one of those results I trot out at dinner parties the way other people trot out Arrow's theorem. The version I've internalized goes something like this: if you want a collective decision rule that respects unanimous preferences (the Pareto principle) and also grants even a minimal set of personal rights, you're asking for a contradiction; no rule does both. The moral I've always drawn is that liberal societies are stuck with a permanent inefficiency — that honoring rights means sometimes overriding what literally everyone prefers, and that the mature response is to treat rights as a kind of friction you accept for other reasons. Lately, though, I've noticed that the standard illustration everyone reaches for involves one person's preferences about another person's private life — the prude who wants the book banned, the abstainer who wants the drinking outlawed — and it occurs to me that the "unanimity" being steamrolled is unanimity about precisely the sort of busybody preference a liberal already has independent reason to keep out of the social calculus. So my question is whether I should now conclude that the rights-versus-efficiency trade-off is real but confined to a narrow class of meddlesome cases, or whether there's a more general reading on which even purely self-regarding unanimity can collide with minimal rights.

And if it's the more general reading, I'd like to know what the impossibility actually is, in its own terms. Is it a fact about aggregation rules — no rule of a certain kind can exist — or about the informational base such a rule is permitted to consult, or about something in the very notion of an individual being "decisive" over a pair of alternatives? I've seen the result cited alongside Arrow's as though the two were the same species of theorem, and I suspect that's sloppy but I can't articulate why. What I want is to stop deploying the paradox as a slogan and understand what it does and doesn't establish: whether it's a claim about the formal constraints on a social preference relation under unrestricted domain, or a claim about actual liberal institutions being doomed to inefficiency, or both, or neither. If the meddlesome-preference move is the standard escape, I'd also like to know whether it's considered a genuine resolution or just a restriction of the domain that quietly assumes the conclusion.
