Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether a community that cuts its high-achievers down to size is defending something real or merely indulging envy, and we got nowhere — which left me convinced that the dispute is better staged than stated. I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar in social and political theory: sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, and no assigned reading beyond a general familiarity with arguments about merit and desert. Please design a runnable classroom activity for that slot.

What I want the session to do is make the students feel the pull of both norms from the inside before either is named. On one side, the meritocratic promise: effort is invested, reward follows, and the person who comes out ahead experiences their advantage as earned. On the other, the egalitarian reflex: visible superiority is a provocation, and a community may be entitled — perhaps obliged — to humble anyone who rises too far above the rest. I want the mechanics, not my commentary, to generate both. So arrange a production phase in which students accumulate a score through a mix of costly effort and a publicly drawn luck component, then an assembly phase in which they operate under a short, plainspoken village charter that encodes an unnamed Jante-style ethic (clauses forbidding anyone to think themselves special, or as good as anyone else, and licensing the group to censure those who stand out). Give each participant a finite budget of esteem and scorn tokens, and make censuring a top earner individually rewarding for the censurer while transferring nothing to anyone — so that cutting the successful down leaves no one better off, and yet, under the charter, feels like justice. I want the students to arrive at that structure themselves and only recognize it in the debrief. Do not let the activity resolve which side is right; I want both grips intact at the end.

Deliver it as something I can actually run: the full rules, the materials (assume only index cards, beans or tokens, a scoreboard, and a printed one-page charter), a payoff scheme that needs no real money, the roles if you use them, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the meritocratic promise and its dependence on luck, onto the enforced egalitarian norm, onto the leveling-down structure in which the better-off are made worse off and no one is made better off, and onto the difference between envy and a genuine demand for justice. Include the check that would tell me the students felt the thing rather than merely heard about it.

Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — I expect the group to be too polite to censure anyone and collapse the tension, and I expect the luck draw to be read as fair because the winners will insist they made their own luck. And be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in speech, rather than pretending the game covers it.
