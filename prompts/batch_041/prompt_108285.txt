I've been going down a rabbit hole on the race to the bottom thing - the Moloch idea, where everyone would be better off if everyone just stopped, but whoever stops first gets eaten, so nobody stops. Arms races, recommendation algorithms, fishing fleets, antibiotics, all of it. I get why people find it so compelling, and honestly the more examples I collect the more it feels less like a pattern you notice and more like a law of nature: put a bunch of agents in competition with no way to make a binding deal, and the outcome grinds downward. I'm not an economist or a philosopher, I just read a lot of blog posts and annoy my friends about it, so maybe I'm missing something obvious.

But then I read about the opposite happening with environmental rules, and now I can't fit the two pictures together. Supposedly California passes some strict emissions rule, and because California is such a huge slice of the car market, it's cheaper for the manufacturers to just build every car to the California standard than to build two seperate versions - so the strict rule quietly spreads to states that never voted for it, and from there sometimes goes global. I think the person writing about it called it the California effect, and they contrasted it with the corporate registration thing, where states seem to compete to have the loosest rules so companies will set up there (I want to say thats the Delaware effect but don't quote me). Same setup both times as far as I can tell - lots of competing jurisdictions, each scared of losing business to the others - and yet one case ratchets standards up and the other ratchets them down. I genuinely don't see what the difference is supposed to be.

So what I'd like from you is a real comparison of these two dynamics rather than a reassurance that competition is sometimes good and sometimes bad. Walk me through what maps onto what: who's the agent, who's the thing being competed for, where the pressure comes from, who pays when it goes wrong. Then tell me which knob actually decides the direction - my guess is it's something about whether the harmed party gets to be a paying customer, and whether the thing being competed over is something a buyer can see and pay extra for, but I'm probably half wrong. I especially want to know where the Moloch picture breaks down here, because if a big enough market at the top can flip the whole thing without anyone signing an agreement, then the race to the bottom starts to look like one special case instead of the default. And push back on me if the ratchet-up case isn't as clean as I'm making it - maybe it's still a race to the bottom, just with the bottom landing on small producers in poor countries who can't afford to retool, in which case the two things aren't opposites at all and I've just been looking at who's standing in the pit. If it is a real difference, could you actually engineer the ratchet-up conditions for something that's currently a race to the bottom, like fishing or AI safety, or does the trick only work when there's a rich demanding market up top doing the pulling?
