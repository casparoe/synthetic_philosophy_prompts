I have been reading Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding on my own, and I keep returning to a stretch in the section he titles "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion." He writes: "There are no ideas, which occur in metaphysics, more obscure and uncertain, than those of power, force, energy, or necessary connexion, of which it is every moment necessary for us to treat in all our disquisitions." And then, a little further on: "When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

What I cannot work out is what claim he is actually making here, and how far it reaches. On one reading he is only reporting a fact about observation — that no single experience ever discloses a tie between cause and effect — which is a point about what we perceive, not yet a point about what there is. On another reading he has already drawn the conclusion that causation simply is constant conjunction, so that any talk of a real power or necessity in nature is empty. These seem to come apart, and the second looks like it needs more than the first. Could you help me see which reading the passage better supports, or whether Hume is deliberately letting the two run together? And then, if the stronger reading is the right one, how much of the present-day dispute over laws of nature — whether laws are merely regularities or involve genuine necessitation — is really just his argument restated, and how much of it is doing something he did not? In particular, I would like to know whether someone who accepts the observational point but resists the further conclusion has a coherent position, or whether the first point already forces the second.
