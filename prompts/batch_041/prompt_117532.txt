I am a novelist, and I have been reading scenes from my work in progress to a friend of mine — a philosopher whose graduate seminar is on weakness of will and intertemporal choice — and asking her to mark, scene by scene, whether my protagonist's lapse counts as weakness of will in the strict sense. (The protagonist, Ines, is a translator in her forties, and the question matters to me because I want her own descriptions of herself to be exact.) My friend's verdicts so far: (i) Ines wakes at 5:40 with a swim planned, thinks in so many words that she should get up and that the swim matters more to her than twenty minutes' sleep, and stays in bed — weakness of will. (ii) At a colleague's party, with no promise made to anyone, no resolution taken beforehand, and no struggle in the moment, she thinks she ought to be going home, goes on talking, and is quietly disgusted with herself the whole time — weakness of will. (iii) She hands her laptop to her brother for a fortnight so that she will be forced to finish her translation, then on the fourth day wheedles it back out of him, arguing to him and to herself the entire time that this is not what she wants to be doing — weakness of will, and my friend calls this the paradigm case. (iv) She decides on Sunday to skip lunch on weekdays; on Tuesday, at the table, she thinks the matter through again, concludes without any regret that the plan was foolish, and eats — not weakness of will; she changed her mind. (v) She books a 5 a.m. flight to save money, having judged the saving worth the hour; at 4 a.m. she pays more than the saving to move to a later flight, and reports no conflict whatever, never once tells herself she is doing the wrong thing, would choose the same again, and says only that she is not a morning person — not weakness of will; a reversal of preference, not a defeat of judgment.

The scene I am actually stuck on is this. Ines's nine-year-old daughter knocks a glass off the table, and Ines shouts at her — hard, and for longer than the accident warrants. Afterwards she says that it was over before she had formed any thought at all, that it never crossed her mind at the moment that she ought not to be doing it, that there was nothing she decided against and no deliberation to lose. And yet she is appalled by it, disowns the anger entirely, says it came from somewhere she does not recognise as hers, and says she has spent years deciding not to be that kind of mother — a judgment she holds as firmly as ever and has never retracted.

I would like you, as someone with a specialist's command of the akrasia literature and of intertemporal choice, to predict what my friend will say about this scene, and then — this is what I am really after — to reconstruct the rule she is running. I suspect that more than one rule fits all five verdicts: one that treats weakness of will as a present judgment defeated, another that asks only whether the act flowed from a motive the agent disowns. Begin by listing the candidate rules and checking each against all five verdicts; then say what each predicts about the shouting scene, which you would bet on and why; and finally describe the single further scene that would force her to choose between them, ideally one that separates them in the direction opposite to this one. I am told the flight case has the standard hyperbolic-discounting shape, if that helps. Open with your predicted verdict in one sentence. No more than 500 words, and no references — just the argument.
