Okay, I've been carrying this argument around for a week and it's starting to make me a little crazy. A friend of mine built it, and neither of us can figure out what to do with it. Here it is.

Setup: a lottery, 1,000 tickets, exactly one winner, drawn at random, everybody knows all this, no funny business.

(1) For each ticket, the chance it loses is 0.999, and no ticket is special.
(2) If the chance of p is 0.999 and you have no other information bearing on p, it's rational for you to believe p. Call this the high-probability rule. It's just the claim that we're allowed to believe things we aren't 100% sure of, which is basically everything any of us believes.
(3) So for each of the 1,000 tickets, it's rational for you to believe it will lose.
(4) Rational belief is closed under conjunction: if it's rational for you to believe A, and rational for you to believe B, it's rational for you to believe A-and-B. I can't give this up without giving up multi-step reasoning, since chaining two beliefs together seems to require exactly this.
(5) So it's rational for you to believe that all 1,000 tickets lose.
(6) You also know with certainty that at least one ticket wins.
(7) So it's rational for you to believe a contradiction.
(8) Nothing contradictory is rational to believe. That isn't a constraint you can pay off later, it's kind of what rationality is for.
(9) So one of (1), (2), (4), or (6) has to be false.

Fine, whatever, maybe we just don't get to believe lottery tickets lose. But now the same structure with no lottery in it. I finish a book with 1,000 substantive claims in it. Honest me thinks each one is 99.9% likely — I was careful, I checked. By (2) I believe each. By (4) I believe the conjunction. But I also know, from long experience with my own work, that at least one of the 1,000 is probably wrong, which is exactly why I write a preface admitting it. So I rationally believe my own book is false, and so does every careful author who ever lived, and the only non-irrational authors are the ones who think they're infallible. That can't be right. (Also note that believing all 1,000 tickets lose means I'm guaranteed to be wrong about exactly one of them. A rule that guarantees I hold a false belief is bad, but a rule that guarantees I withhold belief from something I'm 0.999 sure of seems just as bad.)

My friend's take is that this shows the whole idea of fallible rational belief is incoherent and the honest position is some kind of skepticism where you only believe what you're certain of. Which would be a hell of a thing, since then I believe almost nothing. I really don't want to give up (2). But I also can't stomach (8) — I'd rather my beliefs be a mess than say contradictions are fine. So it has to be (4)? Except I don't see how to drop (4) and still be someone who reasons at all: I believe A, I believe A implies B, I conclude B — where is that permitted if I can't conjoin A with A implies B?

Someone else told me the fix is that rational belief isn't closed under conjunction, only acceptance is, and that I should stop running the two together. That just sounds like renaming the problem to me, but maybe it isn't.

So: which step do I reject? And please don't just hand me the label of the standard move, I want the actual diagnosis — what exactly is wrong with the step, why it looked so good, and what it costs me to give it up. In particular: if I fix the lottery by giving up X, what happens to the book case, and if I fix the book case by giving up Y, what happens to the lottery? And tell me whether some version of this argument survives whatever I give up, because my suspicion is that it does and that's the real problem.
