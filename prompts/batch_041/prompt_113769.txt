I'm in a seminar on moral worth this term — we spent two weeks on the Kantian idea that an action only has moral worth if it's done from duty rather than merely in accordance with it — and I thought I had a decent grip on it untill my study group met last night. One of my friends built out the argument below, and now half the group thinks it's airtight and the other half thinks there's a trick buried somewhere in it. I'd like to keep (1), I'm not giving up the denial of (8), and honestly (3) seems to me like something everybody already accepts, so I'm suspicious that the damage has to be somewhere else. But every step looks fine to me on its own and I can't say which one is doing the work.

(1) An action has moral worth only if the agent does it for the right reasons — which means the considerations that actually make the action right have to be the ones that moved her.
(2) The considerations that make an action right are facts: that this would relieve someone's pain, that this would be a betrayal, that this would break a promise.
(3) Facts by themselves can't move anybody; only psychological states can, like a desire or a belief or a feeling. So what moves an agent is never the fact, only her state.
(4) So her motive is at most her belief about the right-making fact, never the fact itself.
(5) She's ready for the obvious comeback, that the content of her belief just is the fact and so the fact is what's moving her. She says that's a dodge, because mental content can be false. Take a world where her belief is wrong but everything inside her head is exactly the same — she does exactly the same thing. So the fact itself made no difference to what she did; only the content did, and the content is a state of her, not a fact about the world. She calls this the swap test.
(6) So even the most conscientious agent acts for a reason that's at best her belief about what's right, never for the considerations that make it right.
(7) Therefore nobody ever acts for the right reasons.
(8) Therefore no action has moral worth.

So: which step does the argument actually need, and where does it break? I want to know why the bad step looked fine to all of us, and what I'd be paying if I gave it up — in particular, if the answer is that I have to abandon (3) or (4), tell me what that costs me, since the Humean thing in (3) feels like the least negotiable part of the whole thing to me. And if this line of reasoning takes down more than moral worth — if it turns out to show that nobody ever acts for any reason at all, or believes anything for a reason — I'd want to know that, because then I'd at least know it's doing something illegitimate and could try to say what.
