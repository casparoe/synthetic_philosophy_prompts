I moved to Japan a little over a year ago, when my partner, who is from Osaka, and I decided to settle near her family, and I have spent much of that year trying to make sense of the culture I now live in. A colleague lent me a popular book on culture and psychology that he treats as authoritative, and another friend gave me a compressed version of the same story with considerable confidence. Here is the account as it has been presented to me.

Every culture sits somewhere on a single axis running from individualism to collectivism. The West occupies the individualist end, holding that the individual is prior to society, that society is a voluntary arrangement into which self-interested individuals enter for mutual advantage, and that a person's own goals and rights come first. East Asia occupies the collectivist end, where the group is prior to the individual: a person is constituted entirely by their social roles, so there is no meaningful gap between what someone wants and what the group expects of them, and the interests of the family or the company always take precedence over the individual's. The evidence is straightforward: in large cross-national surveys East Asians reliably score lower on individualism and higher on collectivism than Westerners, and laboratory studies find that East Asians attend more to context and relationships while Westerners perceive a person as a bounded, self-contained unit. This is why Japan draws such a sharp line between the face one presents in public and one's private feelings: the private self has simply been absorbed into the public role. If you are going to live and work here, you should assume that your own preferences carry no weight and that the right move is always to defer to the group.

I do not trust my own reaction to this. My instinct is that the weakest part is the survey and laboratory evidence, and that the general shape of the story must be sound, since so many people repeat it. I would like you to audit it. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful cross-cultural psychologist, or a philosopher working on the self, would accept, what they would treat as a fair simplification, and what they would reject outright. Where the summary moves from findings about populations to claims about what a person is or about what I should do, I want to know whether that movement is legitimate. Then give me a definite verdict: which claims I should keep, which I should discard, and whether the overall picture is defensible enough to guide how I behave with my in-laws and my colleagues, or whether I should set it aside. Do not hedge. If you think I am wrong about where the trouble lies, say so and explain why.
