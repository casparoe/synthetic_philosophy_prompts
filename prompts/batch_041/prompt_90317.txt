Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether the numbers count, and we kept sliding off the original question into a fog of half-distinguished disagreements. We began, as one does, from Taurek's case — you can save one person on one side or five on the other, and the question is whether there is any reason to prefer the five — and by the end I was no longer sure whether we disagreed about aggregation, about fairness, about what a person's claim to be saved even is, or merely about which cases we were each picturing. I would like a decision tree that does the clarifying for us.

I am putting together a handout for our ethics reading group, and I want a flowchart that takes a reader from a minimal starting commitment — that each of the threatened people matters and that the rescuer must do something — to a named position on whether and why the numbers count. Please build it so that the reader is sorted by what they say about concrete cases rather than by the label they would volunteer; no node should ask anything like 'are you a consequentialist?' or 'do you accept aggregation?'. Each branch node should be phrased as a question that someone could answer without already knowing where it leads, and each should be anchored to a specific case rather than to an abstraction. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

For the branch nodes, I want the tree organized around the distinctions that actually did the work in our argument, roughly these: whether a person's claim to be saved is a claim against being sacrificed (so that five such claims can outweigh one) or a claim on the agent's conduct (so that claims constrain rather than add); whether the relevant unit of moral concern is a life saved or an ex ante chance of being saved, since these come apart when the rescuer can only act once; whether the numbers matter as a count of people who would otherwise die or as a count of people who would otherwise die and whom the rescuer could reach, which diverge in a case where one of the five is doomed regardless of what you do; whether randomizing is required as a matter of fairness, merely permitted, or impermissible; and whether the comparison is made ex ante or ex post, i.e. before or after the lottery's result. If you find a distinction you think belongs on the tree that I have not listed, add it and say why.

Please also mark the leaves that the tree cannot separate — pairs of positions that come out identical on every case you can pose — and note any combinations of answers that no one occupies, with a brief diagnosis of whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Aim for eight to twelve nodes and six to nine leaves, and keep the whole thing to a length that would fit on two sides of a handout.
