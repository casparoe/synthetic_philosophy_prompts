In hospitals, pain gets turned into a number. You ask the patient to rate it 0 to 10, you chart it next to the blood pressure, and you dose the morphine against that number. The whole quality-improvement apparatus of the last few decades — pain as the fifth vital sign, treat it aggressively, ask and reassess — sits on top of that scale, and the textbook line is that pain is whatever the patient says it is, that self-report is the gold standard.

But the same hospital scores pain in people who can't talk — babies, intubated patients, late-stage dementia — by watching them: the grimace, the tensing, the pulling away, or going quiet and still. Vets do the same thing with dogs and cats. So in one room the number out of ten is the authority and in the next room the flinching is.

Then there are the asymbolia patients, usually after a stroke that eats part of the insula (some bit of cortex in the middle of the brain, I think). They'll often say yes, that hurts, they feel it fine, they can point to where it is and rate it — but it doesn't bother them. No wincing, no guarding, sometimes they laugh or try to shake your hand mid-procedure. Whatever they've lost, it isn't the sensation, it's the part that minds. Note also that the official definition of pain builds unpleasantness right in, as a sensory and emotional experience, which makes these patients a headache for the definition too.

So what does the practice presuppose about why pain is bad? Does the 0-10 scale assume the badness of a pain is just its intensity, so a 7 is bad to degree 7 no matter whose it is? Or does it presuppose that whats bad about pain is the affective bit — the suffering, the wanting it to stop — with the number serving as a rough proxy for that? If it's the first, then the asymbolic patient's pain is exactly as bad as yours and we should be dosing them the same, and the missing flinch is a red herring. If its the second, then a lot of what gets charted and medicated in a hospital isn't bad at all, and the whole enterprise is aimed at a proxy that can come apart from the thing that matters. And which is it in the baby's case, where we've quietly decided the flinch is the real evidence — did we switch proxies because the baby can't talk, or because we secretly think the flinch is closer to the badness than the report ever was?

Tell me what theory the practice actually presupposes, and separate what it strictly needs from what it could swap out. Could you run the same wards on the rival theory, or would something have to change — what gets treated, who gets asked, whose pain counts as an emergency?
