Our reading group of twenty has been offered a real deal, and we have to hand in our slips on Friday. A benefactor will place $20,000 in escrow. Each of us writes a whole number of units, from 0 to 2,000, on a slip and hands it in sealed. He opens the slips and totals the units. Each member who wrote a positive number then receives $20,000 multiplied by that member's units divided by the total units written. In addition — this is his wrinkle, and I have not seen this exact version of the puzzle before — each member pays him $1 for every unit he or she wrote, win or lose. If every slip reads 0, he keeps the money. We are free to discuss the game as much as we like beforehand, but each slip is written in private and no agreement among us can be enforced. All of this is common knowledge.

Two of us, Devi and Oskar, have spent two meetings arguing about what to write, and I have stopped trusting my own reaction to either of them. Their exchange went roughly as follows; I have tried to reproduce the substance rather than the wording.

Oskar: The answer is to write a large number — 600, say. Look at it from where I sit: whatever the other nineteen write, my units only raise my cut, and the fee is pocket change next to what I stand to gain. There is no state of the world in which writing 1 is better for me than writing 600. Counting on the rest of you to write 1 is not a plan, it is a hope, and if it fails I will have given away a fortune to be polite. Rationality is about what I can actually affect.

Devi: You can affect it. There are twenty of us with the same slip in front of us, the same rules and the same arithmetic. If 600 is the right thing for you to write, it is the right thing for me to write, and for all twenty of us — and then we each gross $1,000, pay $600 in fees, and go home with $400. Your argument, if it works at all, works for every one of us. It cannot be a proof that singles you out as the exception.

Oskar: That is not an argument, it is a slogan. My writing 600 does not move anyone else's hand; there is no mechanism in it. And your principle undermines itself. If we all write 1, I have a strictly better reply: writing 600 against nineteen 1s nets me about $18,800. So everyone writing 1 is not a stable answer to the question of what each of us should write, and you cannot call a number rational if the person writing it would abandon it the moment he thought it through.

Devi: What you call stability is a definition you have imported from economics and will not examine. Run it out: the stable outcome is everyone writing 950 units and clearing $50 apiece. That is what your kind of rationality buys this group — fifty dollars each, plus a rule declaring it irrational to do better. My claim is narrower than you think. The question what a person in my position should write has one answer, and it cannot depend on which of the twenty of us is asking, because we are in the same position. Whatever that answer is, all twenty of us give it.

Oskar: Then it depends on something you have not shown — that we are in the same position in the sense that matters, namely that we will all reach the same conclusion. Suppose it is likely that most of you write 1 and I am the one who thinks it through; then 600 is simply correct for me. Your principle needs the rest of us to be reasoning exactly as you are, which is an empirical bet about twenty people, and I am not making it. Notice also where your answer comes from: if we could sign an enforceable contract, or draw lots to choose one member to write 1, then 1 would plainly be right. But we cannot. So your answer is an answer to a different question — what a group with a working enforcement mechanism should do — and we do not have one.

Devi: The binding is supposed to be the reasoning itself; that is the whole idea. And your last move cuts against you. You say the argument works only if the rest of us reason as I do — but that is exactly what I am entitled to assume, because we are the same case. You keep describing nineteen strangers who happen to be in my game. There are no strangers here. There are twenty of us, and any answer we give is an answer all of us give.

Oskar: Suppose I concede the whole thing and write 1 out of solidarity. Then the one member who writes 600 walks away with about $18,800 while the nineteen faithful ones take home about $31 each — less than they would have had if they had never trusted me. A principle that holds only when everyone follows it, and that is punished by anyone who does not, is a description of a hope, not a reason to act.

Devi: You have just described why the reasoning has to be shared rather than merely hoped for, and then treated the difficulty as a refutation. I will grant the sting: my answer is not robust, and it cannot be, because the game is not robust. No strategy both lets each of us write 1 and protects the nineteen against a defector; that is not a defect in my answer, it is the shape of the payoff structure. If you want a reason to write 1 that survives your writing 600, you are asking for a reason that cannot exist. The question is what to do given that, and my answer is to be the sort of person whose reasoning the others can see and share — which is something we can manage, since we are all sitting in the same room.

That is where they left it. I would like you to referee them. To be clear, I am not asking what we should write; I am asking for the argument itself to be adjudicated as they actually made it.

First, take the eight moves in order. For each, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and justify your rating briefly. Where either of them uses a figure, or makes a claim about what the numbers would be in a given scenario, check it and say plainly if it is wrong.

Second, say what each of them is getting right that the other is not, and then — more useful to me — what both of them are missing that neither has said.

Third, give me a verdict on the question actually in dispute: what should a member of this group write, and does that question have a determinate answer at all? Commit to a position and give your reasons for it. I would much rather have a verdict I can argue with than a survey of the options with the choice left to me; if your verdict turns on a judgment call, say so, but make the call.
