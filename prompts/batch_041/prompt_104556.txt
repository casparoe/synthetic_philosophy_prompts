Imagine T. H. Huxley, who argued that consciousness is a collateral by-product of the brain's machinery rather than a cause of behavior — the famous simile being that mind is to the body roughly as a steam whistle's noise is to the locomotive's working — were confronted with the modern objection that epiphenomenalism cannot account for our capacity to form and utter true beliefs about our own experience. The objection runs: if conscious states do nothing, then my report that I am in pain, or my belief that I am conscious at all, cannot be caused by the pain or the consciousness it purports to be about; yet such reports are frequently accurate, which the view must somehow explain without granting the phenomenal any causal role. What would Huxley say? Reconstruct his likely response as a position, not a verdict: identify which parts of his automaton picture he would hold fixed, which he would concede or amend, whether he would treat the accuracy of introspective report as a genuine problem for him or as an artifact of how the objection is framed, and what he would offer in place of a causal explanation — for instance, some correlationist or selectionist story, or a deflationary account of what such reports are. Then say where his reply is strongest and where it looks like evasion, and whether the position that results is still recognizably his or has quietly become something else.
