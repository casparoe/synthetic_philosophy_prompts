I'm a retired materials scientist — 35 years of crystallography, mostly alloy work — and since I packed it in I've been teaching myself philosophy of mind, which is a strange way to spend a retirement but there you go. A old lab colleague of mine and I have been going back and forth for weeks about functionalism: the view that what makes a state a pain, or a belief, is its causal role — what brings it on, what it sets off, how it hooks into behaviour — rather than the physical stuff doing the work. Thats the usual reason people say an octopus, or one day a silicon chip, could be in pain even though the hardware is nothing like ours: same pattern of transitions, same mental state. I like the view. Ray likes it too, I think, but he built the following out of it to see whether I'd still like it, and I can't get out from under it:

(1) If a state's being a pain isn't a matter of it being some particular physical thing, then it must be a matter of its causal role — what brings it on, what it brings about, how it hooks into behaviour. (2) A causal role is just a pattern: a bunch of states with rules about which one follows which, given which input. (3) Any pattern of that kind can be realised by more than one physical system, which is the whole point about the octopus. (4) Push it further. Take any pattern at all, and any lump of matter at all. Every physical system has a colossal state space — every arrangement of its atoms is a point in it — and a pattern is only a finite set of states with arrows between them, so there is always some way of assigning the lump's physical states to the pattern's states that makes the arrows come out right. (5) So the lump realises the pattern. (6) So a rock, a cloud, a bucket of water realises the pattern for pain, and by (1) it is in pain. (7) A rock is not in pain.

I'll give up anything before I give up (7), and (1) is the only reason I find the whole view attractive in the first place, so I'm wedged between two things I don't want to drop. Where does it break? Is (4) simply wrong — and if so, what exactly is missing from that assignment, given that the arrows do come out right? Or is the trouble earlier, in what "realises" is supposed to mean, or in (1) itself? And whichever step you throw out, tell me what the functionalist has to pay for throwing it out, because my suspicion is that the price is some notion of a correct or natural realisation that quietly lets the biology back in through the back door, which would undo the multiple realizability business that got us here in the first place.
