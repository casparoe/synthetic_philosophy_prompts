Two of my mates had a right arguement in the pub last night and I've ended up as the referee, but honestly I'm out of my depth. It kicked off because a podcast we were listening to played a clip of a politician using a racial slur, and then the presenter said the word out loud himself while discussing it.

Jenna's line: the word does the damage by itself. It doesn't matter that he was quoting, or that he obviously meant no harm, or that he was condemning it — the contempt is part of what the word means. Her test: you can't say "he's an X, but there's nothing wrong with Xs" without contradicting yourself, the way "he's a git, but he's a decent bloke" works fine. And when someone from the group uses it about himself, that isn't the same word in a friendlier tone — it's a different act altogether.

Marcus's line: the word just labels the group, and the contempt is something the speaker does with it, like a sneer or a hand gesture. Same word, affectionate between two people in the group, lethal in a football chant. He reckons the group policing who's allowed to say it is a social rule about a speech act, not a fact about what the word means, and that her contradiction test proves nothing, since "I promise, but I don't intend to" clashes too and nobody thinks that's a matter of meaning.

Jenna came back with: but the same word turns up in a sociology paper and in a pub brawl, and we don't say those are two different words. The contempt rides along with it whether you intend it or not. Marcus said "rides along" is doing all the work in that sentence, and that if she means the contempt is carried by convention rather than stated outright, then fine — but a convention about how to use a word isn't the same thing as what the word means.

Neither of them will budge and I've been told to pick a winner. Can you go through it move by move, say who's right about what, and tell me what they're both missing? British English, please.
