I'm running a session at a student classics thing next month, and the guest is a historian who's spent his whole career defending the Athenian assembly — his line is basically that "mob rule" is a slur invented by the losers, and that the ekklesia was actually a decent machine for pooling what a lot of ordinary people knew. I've read enough of his stuff that I know his standard moves, so I dont want questions that just let him do the greatest hits. Write me eight to ten questions that really squeeze him, plus a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he's most likely to give.

In particular I want:

- Something on Mytilene, and I want the ugly details used against the flattering reading. The vote to spare the city was nearly a tie, and the whole rescue came down to a boat race — the first ship made no haste on its errand, and the second arrived only just as the commander was reading out the decree. So ask him straight out whether the "the assembly corrected itself" story is really just luck. If he says the second debate is the point, press him on why a body that had to be talked out of a massacre within a day deserves credit for that rather than suspicion.
- Something on Sicily, where I want the difference between "reasonable given what they knew" and "reasonable, full stop" put to him. I suspect he's quietly leaning on hindsight bias, so make him say what an assembly would have had to do differently for him to call the decision reckless.
- Something on Socrates, since that's the case where the democracy killed a philosopher for what he did in public. Does he quarantine it as an outlier or admit it's a symptom?
- Something on his evidence, because everything we have about how the assembly actually deliberated was written by people who weren't exactly fans of it, and mostly not by ordinary citizens. How does he get from those texts to a claim about the crowd's collective competence?

One more thing. A colleague of mine has a half-serious theory I'd like tested: that the interesting thing about Mytilene isnt the reversal but the fact that the assembly was willing to reopen a question it had already decided, instead of treating yesterday's vote as binding. Get him to say whether that's the institution working as designed or just proof that a crowd's decisions are unstable. And finish with the question that would actually hurt — what would he have to find in the sources to give up the whole view?
