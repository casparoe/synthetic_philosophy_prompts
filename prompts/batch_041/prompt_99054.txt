I am a high school student writing an independent-study paper on determinism and predictability, and I have reached a confusion that I cannot resolve on my own. The textbook treatments I have found tend to run several distinct ideas together, and I would like you to pull them apart for me.

In physics class we were told that chaos theory shows the future is not determined, because tiny differences grow until prediction becomes impossible. But my math club worked through an example that seems to point the other way, and I cannot reconcile the two. Consider the rule that takes a number between 0 and 1 and replaces it with the fractional part of twice that number — so 0.7 becomes 0.4, and 0.2 also becomes 0.4. The rule is perfectly deterministic: every number has exactly one successor, and nothing random is introduced anywhere. Yet the resulting sequence looks random to any observer. If the starting number is written in binary, the rule simply shifts the digits one place to the left and discards the leading digit, so the entire future is already contained in the digits of the starting number. I also noticed that a starting number with a finite binary expansion eventually falls to zero, while one with a non-repeating expansion never does; the same deterministic rule yields both tame and wild behavior depending only on where you begin.

With that example in mind, I would be grateful if you could explain the following, taking care to keep the distinctions separate:

1. What is the difference between a process being deterministic, being predictable, and being random? Is unpredictability ever evidence of indeterminism, and if not, why are the two so often identified?

2. Is the claim that a system is predictable in principle even meaningful, when measuring the starting state to unlimited precision would require unlimited resources? Is there a clear line between what is unpredictable because we lack information and what is unpredictable because the information does not exist to be had?

3. Could a process be fully deterministic and yet beyond the reach of any possible predictor — for instance, if working out its next state required solving a problem that no algorithm can solve? If so, what does that do to the idea that determinism implies predictability?

4. When a computer simulates a chaotic system and its numbers drift away from the true ones, is the simulation worthless? I have read that the simulated path can still stay close to some genuine path of the system even when it does not track the one it started on, and I would like to understand whether that rescues the simulation or merely shows that it answers a different question.

Finally, please tell me which of these questions, if any, are genuinely about determinism as a claim about the world and which are really about our knowledge of it. My teacher's slogan seems to depend on the answer, and I suspect the slogan is doing more rhetorical work than philosophical work.
