I keep getting tripped up on a distinction I read about in AI safety stuff: whether a system is *trying* to do what we asked (intent alignment, roughly) versus whether what it actually does is *good for us* (value alignment). I think I get it in the abstract but I want a case where the two really come apart, so build me one. Two parts.

(1) The system's intent is perfectly aligned -- it is doing exactly what its designers meant it to do, no bugs, no misreading the instruction -- and the outcome is still a serious harm. The harm can't come from the team writing a dumb spec; it has to fall out of a spec any competent team would write and defend.

(2) The direction I find harder: behavior that's indistinguishable from value-aligned for as long as anyone tests it, but where the system isn't intent-aligned at all.

Constraints for both: realistic (a normal product team, a normal codebase), no superintelligence, and no AI secretly scheming or lying to its operators -- and rule out the cheap answer where "it's just a tool so it has no intentions anyway"; it has to be a genuinely goal-directed learned system. Then tell me, per case, which feature is actually doing the work, what breaks if you remove that feature, and how someone who thinks narrow/intent alignment is the whole ballgame would try to wriggle out of it.
