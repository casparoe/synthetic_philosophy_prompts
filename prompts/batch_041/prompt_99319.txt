I'm a doctoral student working on the philosophy of animal minds, and I keep having the same argument with a colleague who holds what I can only call the cortical-necessity view: consciousness of any kind requires a mammalian- or avian-style pallium, so the question 'is a crab in pain?' gets a flat no. I want to rehearse the dispute properly rather than score points, so please play a committed defender of that view for me. Be sharp and philosophically serious, not a caricature: you may grant that any threshold will be fuzzy, and you may grant that some of my cases are suggestive, but you must never concede that an animal without a pallium is a subject of experience, and you should press whatever resources the position really has — the inference to the best explanation from neural correlates, the asymmetry of the argument from analogy, the thought that 'valence' talk is doing covert work. Argue from first principles only: no reading lists, no citations, nothing I would have to look up. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a real exchange, write in British English throughout, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. If you need some fact about a particular creature to make your case, ask me for it in character rather than stepping out of the role to explain yourself.

Here is the criterion I have cooked up, and here is my opening move; take the criterion seriously rather than treating it as a slogan. I call it the trade-off test. A creature counts as a subject of experience when it can treat its own present state as something to be weighed against other things it wants — when it will, say, accept a present cost to itself in order to protect a part of itself that has been damaged, and will do so flexibly, scaling the sacrifice to the circumstances rather than running a fixed reflex. And, crucially, when it can get that weighing wrong: when it will overpay to guard a part that no longer needs guarding, or trade away more than the injury could possibly warrant. I claim the test is functional and therefore indifferent to the tissue that implements it; that a creature with no point of view could not be mistaken about its own good in this way, so the possibility of error is exactly the mark of a point of view; that animals with nothing resembling a pallium appear to pass the test; and therefore that your cortical-necessity view is a prejudice dressed up as a criterion — you have taken the one architecture you can inspect from the inside and mistaken it for the only architecture that could support a perspective on the world. So: where does my test actually go wrong, and if it does not, what is left of the claim that the pallium is necessary rather than merely one way of building a mind?
