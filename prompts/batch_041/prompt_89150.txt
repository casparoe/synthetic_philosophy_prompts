A colleague in our consciousness reading group has produced an argument that has become a small local scandal: nobody can say exactly where it fails, and several of us suspect it fails somewhere. The setting is the color phi phenomenon. Two small dots are flashed in succession at two different positions on a screen. At suitable spacings and intervals, subjects do not see two dots; they see a single dot traveling smoothly from the first position to the second. If the first dot is one color and the second another, subjects see a single dot whose color changes, and the change is seen to occur abruptly somewhere in the middle of the path, that is, at a moment before the second dot has appeared on the screen. Subjects cannot be trained to report the change at either end of the path; the middle is what they see.

For any such episode there appear to be two families of stories about what happens inside the subject, and they agree on every report and every measurable response. On the first story the experience itself is late: the brain holds the first dot's representation, waits for the second dot, then constructs a single experience of a moving dot with the color change built into the middle of its path, and delivers that experience to the subject. Nothing is misremembered on this story, because the subject's experience really does contain the change at the midpoint; it simply occurs after the second flash. On the second story the experience is on time and the record is late: the subject has an ordinary experience of the first dot and then of the second, and only afterward is the stored record of that experience edited so that it now represents a moving dot changing color at the midpoint. The experience itself never had the midpoint change as its content; the memory, or whatever produces the report, is falsified after the fact. These are the two options usually labeled Stalinesque and Orwellian respectively.

The argument:

(1) In the color phi case, for any episode of apparent filling-in, we can construct a late-experience story and a late-record story that agree on every observation the subject could make or report.

(2) No observation, by the subject or by anyone else, could distinguish the two stories; they are empirically equivalent, not merely hard to tell apart.

(3) If two hypotheses about a mental episode agree on every possible observation, then there is no fact of the matter as to which is true; they are two notations for the same facts, not two competing descriptions of one reality.

(4) So there is no fact of the matter about when the color-change experience occurred, nor about whether the experience itself contained the change at the midpoint.

(5) Nothing in (1) through (3) is special to color phi. The same pair of stories can be told about any episode in which the perceptual system appears to fill in, complete, or interpolate — blind-spot filling-in, the completion of partly occluded objects, the resolution of an ambiguous figure — and can be told about the content of an experience as easily as about its timing.

(6) So for any feature of any experience — its content, its spatial character, its timing, even whether it occurred — there is no fact of the matter.

(7) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about what any subject experiences, including whether the subjects in the color phi experiments experience anything at all.

(8) But (7) is absurd, and it also destroys the evidence for (1): if there is no fact of the matter about what subjects experience, their reports cannot be evidence about anything.

I would like to keep (1) and (2); they look to me like plain descriptions of what the experiments show, and I do not wish to deny them. I cannot accept (7). Everyone in the group blames a different step, and I do not trust any of the diagnoses. Which step should go, why did it look innocent, and what does giving it up cost? If the honest answer is that I must choose between the deflationary conclusion and something I would rather keep, tell me which, and tell me what I lose on each side.
