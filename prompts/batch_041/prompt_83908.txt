I run an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of economics — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want to spend the session on the no-trade theorem. My students are fluent in expected utility, Bayes' rule, and the private, mutual, and common knowledge distinctions, and a few have met Aumann's agreement theorem, but none has encountered the no-trade result itself, and I would rather they arrive at it than hear it recited.

Please design a runnable activity — a market they actually play, not a lecture with a game bolted on — that makes them feel the theorem's engine: when traders share a prior and their willingness to trade is observable, the bare fact that a counterparty will transact at the posted terms is itself evidence about the asset, and once each side treats that evidence as evidence, speculative trade can unravel until the market empties. I want them to reach the empty market by trading in it and finding that no one will meet their price.

Stage at least these contrasts, whatever mechanics you invent. First, a round with hidden order flow and a round with public order flow, so that they see the difference lies not in information about the asset but in information about who wants the asset. Second, order flow that is merely mutual knowledge versus order flow that is common knowledge — each trader has seen the book but cannot tell whether the others are attending to it, against a book that is unmistakably common knowledge — because I care most about this gap between everyone knowing and everyone knowing that everyone knows. Third, a control with no private information at all but heterogeneous risk tolerance, in which trade reappears and holds, so that they see the theorem forbids only trade resting on nothing but a difference in belief, not trade as such.

Keep the theorem's own conditions in view while you design: it needs a common prior, a market already in efficient equilibrium, and common knowledge of the structure by which traders acquire information, and it is close to empty as an empirical claim about real markets, which trade constantly.

Give me the rules, the materials, a scoring scheme in points rather than money, and a timeline that fits the ninety minutes. Then a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the corresponding assumption of the theorem; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it; and a frank note on which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Keep the whole design under 900 words, and write it in American English.
