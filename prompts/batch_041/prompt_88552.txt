I am setting the final examination for a master's-level course on cooperative game theory and bargaining. My students are at ease with Pareto frontiers, convexity and the axiomatic method, and they have already seen the Nash and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions developed side by side, so reciting the two characterization theorems would earn them almost nothing. I want a single long question, worth 25 marks and intended for about 60 minutes of a closed-book exam, that tests three things at once: whether they can compute, whether they can compare axiom sets, and whether they notice what the construction quietly presupposes. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

Build the question on the two bargaining problems below, using exactly these numbers and no others.

Problem 1. Two parties divide a surplus. Utility is cardinal and comparable across parties only up to independent positive affine transformations. The disagreement point is (0, 0). The feasible set is the convex hull of (0, 0), (12, 0), (10, 4) and (0, 9), together with every point dominated by a point of that hull, so that the set is compact, convex and comprehensive.

Problem 2. The same disagreement point, and again a comprehensive set, but this time the union of the rectangle with corners (0, 0), (6, 0), (6, 12) and (0, 12) with the triangle with corners (0, 0), (10, 0) and (0, 10). This set is not convex.

The question should have three parts, in this order.

In the first part, students must compute both the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution of Problem 1, showing their working, and then state what fraction of its own ideal payoff each party ends up with under each of the two solutions.

In the second part, they must say which standard axiom each of the two solutions has to give up (the two are not the same axiom), state both axioms in full so that nothing turns on recalling a label, and explain why no solution can satisfy both, given the other standard requirements that the two solutions share. A student who merely cites the two characterization theorems must not be able to score full marks here; the argument has to be made, and a top answer should be able to supply a concrete two-party case in which the Nash solution moves a party's payoff in the wrong direction when the feasible set grows while the other party's ideal payoff is unchanged.

In the third part, students must apply the Kalai–Smorodinsky construction to Problem 2 and say what happens. The number they obtain is the least interesting thing about this part; what matters is what the breakdown reveals about what the construction presupposes, and what a defender of the solution ought to say about it.

Constraints on the question. It must be entirely self-contained: no citations, no titles of papers, no dates, nothing that presupposes the student has read a particular source, and nothing that presupposes internet access. Any axiom used must be stated in the question rather than named and left to memory. The three parts should be weighted so that the computation is the cheapest and the third part the most expensive, and the marks must add to 25.

For the model answer, write as an examiner would: correct, complete, arithmetic shown, fractions stated exactly. Make the counterexample in the second part explicit. In the third part, say plainly what the construction requires and what the defender's options are.

For the marking scheme, list the must-hit points with the marks attached, totalling 25, and give for each point one line on the mistake that most often costs students that mark.
