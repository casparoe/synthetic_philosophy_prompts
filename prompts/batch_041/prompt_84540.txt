I do property and trusts litigation for a living — chain of title, void vs voidable conveyances, bona fide purchasers, adverse possession, limitation periods, the whole unglamourous apparatus. My sister is an art historian who's spent the last year on the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, and every argument we have ends with me saying "fine, but who has title?" and her saying that's not the point. I'd like to actually run the exercise properly instead of scoring points off her.

So: take the main arguments people make for restitution of those two collections and restate them, as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary. For each argument I want the chain-of-title treatment — who held what when, whether the transfer that moved the thing out was void or merely voidable, whether any later holder could count as a bona fide purchaser for value without notice, where the clock started on any limitation or prescription period and whether it's run, what laches and the act of state and sovereign immunity doctrines do to the claim, and what a court could actually order if it found for the claimant. Keep the Benin material and the Parthenon sculptures separate — I have a strong suspicion they don't come out the same way, and I want to see whether that's right and where the difference actually lives. Note that Elgin took his lot off the Acropolis with Ottoman permission and that the Benin objects came out of a punitive expedition in 1897, so the two starting points are nothing alike.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What in the restitution case has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — the claim that some things belong to a culture or a people rather than to any legal person, the idea that the wrong is ongoing rather than a past conversion, the "universal museum" line, the fact that the state that would receive the objects back may itself be a colonial artifact, the thought that some objects shouldn't be ownable in the first place. For each one, tell me whether it's my framework failing — and if so whether that failure is a bug or the entire point of a system built to stabilise possession — or whether the moral claim is the thing that's underspecified, as when the answer to "who's the rightful owner" turns out to be four entities at once. Thats the distinction I want you to actually commit to.

Don't be diplomatic. If the legal translation makes one of the moral arguments look like a non-starter, say so, and if it makes one look stronger than the moral literature admits, say that too.
