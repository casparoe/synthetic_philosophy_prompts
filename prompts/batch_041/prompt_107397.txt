Having spent thirty-five years in a physiology department before retiring into philosophy, I have found the literature on machine and animal sentience oddly unsatisfying: it seems to alternate between arguments from behaviour and arguments from architecture without ever asking which of the two ought to be doing the evidential work, and the result is that every discussion of moral status re-litigates the same handful of positions. I have been trying to discipline my own thinking on the matter, and I would value your help. Below is a list of candidate criteria for the presence of morally relevant experience in a system. Please sort them into five tiers, from the criteria that should carry the most weight to those that should carry the least, when we are deciding whether to attribute moral status to a system we do not yet understand — a cephalopod, a corvid, a cultured neural organoid, or a large language model. Give a single sentence for each explaining why it lands where it does, and do not worry about being even-handed: I would rather have a definite placement than a hedge.

(a) Re-entrant or recurrent processing — the system's states are fed back into the process that produced them, rather than the system being a feed-forward cascade. (b) Global broadcast — the state is made available at once to many otherwise independent subsystems. (c) Irreducibility — the system's causal structure at that moment cannot be decomposed into independent parts without loss, in the manner of the integrated-information measures. (d) A self-model maintained by interoceptive prediction error, that is, a representation of the system's own body as its own. (e) Valence-tracking — the state carries information about what is better or worse for the system and is routed back into the system's own control policy. (f) Offline replay — the system can re-token states of the relevant kind in the absence of the eliciting stimulus, as in planning or in dreaming. (g) Metacognitive monitoring — the system represents its own reliability or uncertainty about its own first-order states. (h) Reportability — the state can be expressed in a language-like format, so that the system can say that it has it. (i) Re-entry depth, which is my own coinage: the number of times a state is re-described by the system's own higher-order models before it issues in action, on the suspicion that a deep stack of such re-descriptions is closer to what we mean by a felt state than any single loop. (j) Behavioural economy, also mine: the system will forgo an otherwise attractive reward, or incur a risk, in order to terminate or avoid the state — that is, it treats the state itself as a cost.

Once the tiers are drawn, I would like you to answer four further questions, briefly. If you could retain only one criterion from the list and had to discard the rest, which would you keep, and what would be lost by discarding the others? Which two criteria come apart most often in actual systems, and what would a system that satisfies one but not the other look like? Are my two coinages genuine additions to the list, or are they covert restatements of criteria already on it? And finally, tell me where you would place the functionalist's catch-all — the bare claim that the state plays a sufficiently rich role in the system's cognitive economy — which I have deliberately left off the list in order to see whether it survives contact with the others or collapses into all of them.
