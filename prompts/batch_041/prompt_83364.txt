**Question (40 marks).** Frankfurt's counterexample to the principle of alternate possibilities runs roughly as follows. Black wants Jones to perform action A and is prepared, should Jones show any sign of deciding to do otherwise, to intervene and ensure that Jones decides to do A after all. As it happens, Jones decides to do A on his own and Black does nothing. Frankfurt concludes that Jones is morally responsible for A even though he could not have done otherwise, and hence that the principle of alternate possibilities — the claim that a person is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise — is false. (a) Set the argument out in premise-conclusion form, making explicit which premise the case is meant to falsify and what further assumption is needed to get from the description of the case to that falsification. (b) The best-known reply is that the case leaves Jones with a "flicker of freedom", some alternative possibility surviving Black's presence. Explain how that reply is supposed to work, and say exactly what it must establish if it is to succeed. (c) Does the reply succeed? Defend your answer.

**Student's script.** (a) The principle of alternate possibilities, PAP, says that someone is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's case is meant to show this principle is false. The argument is: P1, if PAP is true then Jones is morally responsible for A only if he could have done otherwise; P2, Jones could not have done otherwise, because Black would have intervened had Jones shown any sign of deciding otherwise; P3, Jones is morally responsible for A; therefore PAP is false. The premise the case is meant to falsify is P2 — or rather, the case is meant to show that P2 and P3 can be true together, so that the only way to keep PAP is to give up P3. To get from the description of the case to that conclusion you need the assumption that Black's merely counterfactual intervention really does remove Jones's ability to do otherwise rather than just making it very likely that he will do A. You also need the assumption that Black's presence does not undermine Jones's responsibility, since Black never actually does anything.

(b) The flicker of freedom reply says that even in the Frankfurt case Jones retains an alternative. He cannot perform the alternative action, because Black would stop him, but he can still try to perform it, or decide to perform it, before Black intervenes. The alternative is therefore relocated from the level of action to the level of will or choice. The reply is not a denial that Jones could not have done otherwise; it is a denial that this matters, since PAP ought to be stated in terms of what the agent could have willed rather than what the agent could have done. For the reply to succeed it must show that the residual alternative is a real possibility and not just something we imagine, and that it is the sort of possibility that can ground moral responsibility.

(c) I think the reply succeeds. If Jones could still have decided otherwise, then the case has not produced an agent who could not have done otherwise in the relevant sense, and the counterexample fails against a properly stated PAP. Frankfurt himself accepts that his case does not show determinism to be true, and compatibilists have used the case to argue that free will and determinism are compatible; that is exactly why the flicker of freedom matters. The only way the reply could fail is if we insist that the relevant alternative must be an alternative action rather than an alternative choice. But that looks like a stipulation rather than an argument: the difference between doing and deciding is not the sort of thing that ought to decide whether someone is responsible. On the whole the reply is sound, though it shows that PAP needs restating rather than that Frankfurt's case is worthless.
