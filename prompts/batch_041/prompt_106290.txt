In my philosophy of mind seminar, two classmates, Mara and Dev, got into an argument about pain asymbolia and whether pain can be bad for someone who does not mind it. The instructor had characterized pain asymbolia as a condition in which patients report feeling pain, can often locate it and rate its intensity, but show little or no distress and may even smile or joke while reporting it. She then introduced a hypothetical variant she called inverted asymbolia: a patient who reports no pain at all but displays the behavioral and autonomic signs of intense suffering, and later describes the episode as bad but not painful. The question was whether either case shows that the badness of pain is separable from the sensory experience itself.

Mara argued that pain asymbolia shows the badness lies entirely in the affective or motivational component, not in the sensory quality. If the aversion is absent, the sensation may remain, but it is not bad for the patient; the patient is not suffering. She said inverted asymbolia would be the mirror image—suffering without pain—and therefore confirms that badness tracks affect rather than sensation.

Dev replied that this is too quick. Pain asymbolia patients still have pain, and it remains bad for them in at least one respect: it signals bodily damage, and it would be rational for them to want it gone even if they feel no distress. Their indifference may be a deficit in affective response, not proof that the sensation is neutral; a person can fail to be bothered by something that is nonetheless bad for them. He added that inverted asymbolia would not be suffering without pain; it would be suffering with a different sensory or bodily component, and the patient's report that it is not painful may reflect their concept of pain rather than the experience itself.

Mara responded that if the patient does not mind the pain and is not motivated to stop it, calling it bad for them is paternalistic: badness for a subject must connect to what the subject cares about or what is unpleasant to them. Dev answered that we routinely call things bad for people even when they do not care about them, such as a tumor or a false belief; and if asymbolia patients still withdraw from the painful stimulus when asked, that suggests some residual badness. Mara said withdrawal could be a reflex, not evidence of badness.

I could not decide who had the better of it. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: which claims are right, which are mistaken, and what, if anything, both are missing. In particular, address whether the two cases actually support the separability thesis, and whether being bad for someone should be tied to aversion or can outrun it. Finally, say who has the stronger overall position.
