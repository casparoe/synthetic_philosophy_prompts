I am a novelist at work on a book about the slow decline of a fictional maritime empire, and I have found myself caught for two years between two friends whose argument never advances. My historian friend insists that one empire's fate cannot be forecast from the fates of others, because each is constituted by its own institutions, contingencies and self-understandings, and any generalisation loose enough to cover them all is worthless for prediction; my economist friend replies that this is the inside view dressed up, and that if one wants to know what is likely to happen one must place the case in a reference class and reason from the base rate, however imperfect the class. When the historian asks which empires belong in the class — Rome, Ming China, the Ottomans, the Habsburgs, the British — the economist calls that an empirical question; when the economist asks what would count as evidence that this empire is unlike the others, the historian says the question is backwards. Neither will move. My own suspicion, offered as a suspicion, is that they are not really disagreeing about history at all, but about what a prediction is for and when an explanation has succeeded. Please diagnose the impasse: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what 'decline' or 'collapse' means, a hidden empirical or formal question about reference classes and selection, or two people answering different questions under one heading? Tell me what each side is actually committed to, whether anything — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — could count as progress, and if not, why not. Kindly answer in British English.
