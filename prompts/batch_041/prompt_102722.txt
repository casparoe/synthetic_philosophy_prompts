Write a dialogue between two game theorists who disagree about the foundations of solution concepts, on the following question: when a game is solved by iteratively deleting dominated strategies — where the dominating strategy is allowed to be a mixed strategy, not only a pure one — is the surviving set the canonical content of the assumption that the players are rational and know one another to be rational, or does the procedure quietly import assumptions that rationality by itself does not supply?

The first theorist defends the procedure. A strategy that is strictly worse in expected payoff than some available mixture can never be a best response to any belief the player might hold, and declining a strictly worse bet is constitutive of instrumental rationality rather than an extra premise. Once that refusal is common knowledge, the deleted strategies can be set aside, further strategies become dominated in the shrunken game, and the fixed point that remains is precisely the set of strategies compatible with everyone's rationality.

The second theorist resists. On this view the procedure does more than record what rationality requires: it presupposes that players are expected-utility maximizers who can and will randomize exactly, and it converts a claim about each player's own reasoning into a claim about what every player must believe about everyone else — an idealization that, in games such as the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, yields conclusions so strong that they cast doubt on the assumptions rather than on the players.

Both theorists are competent and neither is a strawman; each should answer the other's actual arguments rather than trade slogans. Let the exchange work through, at least, the following disputes:

- why admitting mixtures as dominators matters, and whether it presupposes anything — expected-utility maximization, the free availability of randomization — that ought to be argued for rather than assumed;
- the contrast between strict and weak dominance, given that the weak-dominance version is sensitive to the order of deletion and can delete Nash equilibria, whereas the strict-dominance version is order-independent;
- whether, once there are three or more players, a strategy can fail to be a best response to any belief yet still escape domination by every mixture, and which of these two properties, if either, is the genuine consequence of rationality;
- what common knowledge of rationality actually delivers, and whether it is a coherent idealization or an assumption that cannot be jointly satisfied.

The characters may use a small concrete game as a running example — for instance a row player whose third strategy is strictly dominated by an even mixture of the first two but by neither of them alone. Neither character should appeal to the authority of published results; keep the exchange to arguments that can be assessed on their merits. End with the disagreement sharpened rather than dissolved.
