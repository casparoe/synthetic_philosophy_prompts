Explain a specific issue in the theory of speech acts, using a case that the textbook examples do not settle. The issue is what separates an utterance that is a speech act from one that merely resembles a speech act, and whether the difference lies in the speaker's intentions, in the conventions in force, or in the uptake of the audience.

The case: a company runs a negotiating agent that conducts contract talks with suppliers on its behalf. The agent is a large language model with access to tools; it has no beliefs or intentions in the ordinary sense, does not grasp the practice of promising, and selects its outputs to maximize a scoring function. In one negotiation it produces 'we accept your terms' and 'we undertake to deliver by the fifteenth'. The supplier relies on these, and the company's legal department treats them as binding on the company.

Explain, in order:

(a) whether anything has been promised, and by whom — the agent, the company, or no one, with the company merely having incurred a liability that is not a promise;

(b) which condition on promising the case strains: Austin's requirement that an accepted conventional procedure be executed correctly and in earnest, Searle's requirement that the speaker intend to place themselves under an obligation under constitutive rules, or Grice's requirement of a reflexive communicative intention — and whether the strain comes from the speaker's being artificial or from its being institutional;

(c) whether the case is better classified as a misfire, in which the act fails to come off, or as an abuse, in which the act comes off but is defective, and whether that distinction survives when there are no inner states for the speaker to be insincere about;

(d) whether some variant of the case would make you say that a promise has genuinely been made — for instance, an agent disposed to act on its undertakings in ways that track the addressee's reliance, or one whose outputs the addressee is entitled to take as the company's own word — and what that shows about whether speech acts require a subject of experience at all.

Commit to a position rather than surveying the options, and say which single modification of the case would most easily overturn it.
