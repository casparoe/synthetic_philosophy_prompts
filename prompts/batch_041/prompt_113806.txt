I cover science and ideas for a living — features, a newsletter, the occasional radio hit — so I end up writing about philosophy from the outside all the time. But my actual training is as a courts and insurance reporter; six years of torts and claims files before I switched beats. I can walk you through the elements of a negligence claim in my sleep, and I've spent alot of hours reading claims manuals, but I never took a philosophy class and I dont pretend otherwise.

Here's what I keep circling. There's this literature on moral luck — Nagel, Williams, and a mountain of follow-up — and I want you to restate it in the vocabulary I actually think in. Not a summary with legal analogies sprinkled on top; an honest translation. My side of the ledger: duty, breach, the reasonable person standard, cause in fact and the but-for test, proximate cause and foreseeability, intervening and superseding causes, the eggshell skull rule, comparative fault, joint and several liability, compensatory versus punitive damages. And from the insurance side: expected loss versus realized loss, actuaries who price risk rather than outcomes, moral hazard, adverse selection, subrogation, and the fact that the adjuster reading your file is asking whether the loss is covered, not whether you were a good person.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What in the moral luck debate comes across cleanly? I have a hunch that our habit of blaming people more when things turn out badly is just strict liability wearing a hat, and that the control principle everyone argues about is doing roughly the work fault and foreseeability do for us. What comes across only with a distortion? What has no counterpart at all? Two things I'm stuck on. First, Williams's agent-regret — the driver who did everything right and still can't stop thinking about the child he hit — seems to have no home in my vocabulary, and I can't tell whether that's the law being callous or the concept being mushy. Second, the whole apparatus assumes a competent adult who could have met the standard, so we seem to bracket exactly what the constitutive-luck people say is the deepest problem. Same feeling about the med-mal cases where some states let you sue over the lost odds rather than the death itself — the law quietly pricing risk instead of outcome.

And then the verdict I'm after. When the residue shows up, is it my vocabulary that's deficient, or is it evidence that the philosophers' fight is less substantive than it sounds — that the paradox is just two practices coexisting, which the law does without embarrassment every day? And is there anything my side can say about responsibility that theirs can't? If so I want to hear it.

Write it as one long email to me. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write an actual letter. I want to read it straight through.
