I host a small comparative-politics reading group, and for the third time this year we have ended a session deadlocked over a question I had assumed was merely a pub argument: is Sweden or Norway the more liberal country? What interests me is that the deadlock is not for want of facts. One side marshals the Swedish case — the choice reform of the early 1990s that opened schooling to publicly funded independent providers, a comparatively large private sector in welfare services, membership of the EU single market. The other side marshals the Norwegian case — a more comprehensive state school, a position outside the EU (inside the EEA), heavy state ownership, and petroleum revenues channelled into a sovereign wealth fund so that a large share of the country's capital is publicly held. Each side then adds smaller exhibits — both countries run state alcohol-retail monopolies, both have generous parental-leave and sickness regimes, both score well on press freedom — and reaches opposite verdicts from the same material. I have a suspicion about what is going on, but what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I want the suspicion tested rather than adopted.

My suspicion is that the argument is verbal at the surface and substantive underneath. At the surface, 'liberal' is doing at least three jobs in the room: the market-liberal sense (limited government, private provision, low taxes), the civil-liberties sense (personal and expressive freedom), and the sense that has been prominent in political theory since the revival of republican thought, on which a country is liberal to the extent that its institutions protect people from arbitrary power — a sense on which generous, universal provision might count in favour of liberty rather than against it. If that is right, then part of the deadlock is simply that the two sides are answering different questions under one word, and the remedy is to say which sense is at issue. But I doubt that is the whole story, because I notice that even when we stipulate a single sense the disagreement survives, and it then seems to turn on something else: whether a state that provides a benefit has thereby reduced the recipient's dependence on the market or increased her dependence on the state, and whether the Nordic model is best understood as the institutional form of a certain kind of freedom or as a well-cushioned form of unfreedom.

Please diagnose this. Concretely, I would like you to tell me whether the dispute is best classed as verbal, as a disagreement about which dimensions of the concept matter and how they should be weighted, as a hidden empirical disagreement about what the institutions actually do, or as a clash of bedrock intuitions that no argument will shift; whether the question as posed — a single scalar ranking of two countries — is well-posed at all, or whether it is more like asking which of two people is taller when one is taller sitting down and the other standing up; and, given your diagnosis, what if anything would count as progress — a stipulated definition, a disaggregated multi-dimensional comparison that abandons the single ranking, a piece of evidence, or a conceptual distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict. Where you rely on a claim about what the institutions actually do, keep it to things you can state confidently from what you already know, and flag anything you are unsure of rather than asserting it. Please write the answer in British English.
