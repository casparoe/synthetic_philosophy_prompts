Design a thought experiment that isolates a single variable — the accuracy of an agent's higher-order representation — while holding its first-order sensory content, its verbal reports, and its behaviour fixed. The case should be one in which a first-order theory of consciousness and a higher-order thought (HOT) theory agree that the agent is conscious but disagree about what it is conscious of, because the higher-order representation misdescribes the first-order state it targets. The case has to generate this divergence rather than stipulate it: no philosophical zombie, no brain in a vat, and nothing whose description already decides the verdict. Use a recognisable clinical or everyday phenomenon — masking, confabulation, and the like are fair game — and describe the mechanism; the higher-order state must be of a kind the theory actually posits (unconscious, belief-like, and about the first-order state), not a device invented for the occasion. Confine the scenario to a paragraph. Then: state what the first-order theory says the agent experiences and what HOT says; identify the feature doing the work; say whether making the higher-order representation accurate would collapse the disagreement or merely relocate it; and give the strongest reply available to a HOT theorist who wants to deny that a misdescribing higher-order state can confer consciousness. The exercise is meant to test whether the two theories can be made to disagree about content alone, with everything else held equal.
