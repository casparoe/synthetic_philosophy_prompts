My closest friend and I have spent the better part of a month arguing about the right to organize, and I keep losing track of where the real disagreement lies beneath the shouting. He has one analogy he returns to whenever he thinks I am being sentimental: a union is a cartel. On his telling, workers who organize are sellers of labor who agree among themselves to restrict the supply of what they sell — by striking, by picketing, by refusing to work alongside those who will not join — and to set a common price for it, which is precisely what a cartel of firms does when it fixes prices and allocates quotas among its members. He notes that we treat the firm cartel as a serious wrong, at times a crime, and he says the only reason a labor cartel escapes the same treatment is a legal exemption that labor won politically and has defended ever since. He adds two refinements he thinks make the case airtight: that a union security clause is the cartel's enforcement arm, the device that keeps members from defecting and undercutting the agreed price, and that the cartel picture predicts unions will price some workers out of jobs, which he takes to be exactly what we observe. From all this he draws a practical conclusion — that the right to organize ought to be pared back to whatever any other combination of sellers is permitted, and not much more.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than referee the politics. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what is supposed to map onto what — the cartel's members, its product, its price-fixing, its output restriction, its internal discipline, the buyer, the state that enforces the rules — and then say where the mapping genuinely holds and where it breaks down. I am most interested in the features the cartel picture quietly imports that labor markets may not possess: assumptions about how many employers a worker can sell to, about how freely labor can move or withhold itself, about whether the thing being priced is a commodity or a person with a life attached, and about who controls the organization and toward what end. I have objected that employers may themselves hold market power over wages, and that a union might be better understood as a counterweight to that power than as a cartel; he replies that if that were the real problem, the remedy would be more competition among employers, not a counter-cartel, and that two wrongs do not make a market. Tell me whether that reply lands. Distinguish the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque, and then say whether the conclusion — treat the union as we treat a price-fixing cartel — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole way. If some better comparison captures what a union does, name it and explain what it does that the cartel picture cannot.
