I teach a ninety-minute upper-level seminar in formal epistemology to twelve students who are comfortable with credences and conditionalization but who have only ever met van Fraassen's reflection principle as a one-line formula: your present credence in a proposition ought already to equal your expectation of the credence you will hold in it later, at least when the later credence is one you expect to arrive at without any cognitive failure on your part. On the page it looks either vacuously true or plainly false, and neither reaction is the one I want. I want them to feel the pull of the principle from the inside — to be put in a position where deferring to their own future judgment seems obviously right, and then to watch that obviousness come apart in cases involving self-doubt, foreseeable forgetting, or a future self they know will be compromised — before we ever argue about whether the principle is a coherence constraint or a substantive claim about trusting oneself. Design a runnable activity for this group and this time slot. I am happy to use an urn, a deck, a set of sealed envelopes, or a small prediction market with play money, but the payoffs must not require real stakes, and the students should construct the relevant credences themselves rather than being handed numbers. Specify the rules, the materials, a timeline that fits ninety minutes including the debrief, and any roles I need to assign. The idea should show up in what happens rather than in what I say afterwards: I want the structure of the activity to push them into the reflection-respecting move, then into a variant where the same move is clearly the wrong one, so that the debrief has something to point back to. In the debrief, map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the idea — the expectation formula itself, the Dutch-book-style case for the principle, the self-doubt case in which an agent is unsure what she will later think, and the case in which she expects her later self to be irrational, distracted, or to have lost relevant information — and tell me honestly which feature of the principle the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply in exposition. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, since I would rather hear it now than discover it in front of the class. If the activity as I have imagined it cannot actually make the principle feel forceful — if the setup collapses into a trivial arithmetic exercise, or if the rules guarantee by construction that the reflective credence is always the right one, so that the students learn nothing they did not already assume — then say so plainly and tell me what to change, rather than handing me a polished lesson plan that flatters the premise.
