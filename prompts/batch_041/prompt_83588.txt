I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about explanation, and I came away realising that I don't actually know what my own position is exposed to. Here is what I hold. Explanations in biology and psychology come in two kinds. Either the thing is assembled from parts and processes that are themselves already understood — selection, iteration, mechanism, structure accumulated step by step — or it is simply stipulated: a primitive is planted at the bottom of the story and permitted to do work it has not earned. The second move is never legitimate. Where it appears to succeed, it is either the first kind not yet understood or a failure of nerve. I also hold that the charge of greedy reductionism names a real error rather than a matter of taste: there is a difference between decomposing a system into parts that preserve the phenomenon and decomposing it so finely that the phenomenon quietly disappears, and the latter is a mistake, not a preference.

My friend made three moves against this, and I want to know which of them I should feel.

First, he said the dichotomy is not exhaustive. Some explanations in biology and cognition cite neither an assembly history nor a stipulated primitive but the form of a system — constraints, symmetries, self-organising dynamics — so that the correct answer to "why this pattern?" is "because this is what such systems do". If that is a genuine third kind, my dichotomy is a false dilemma.

Second, he said my notion of the greedy is circular: I call a reduction greedy only once it has failed to explain something, so no possible result could show the category to be empty, and a distinction applied only after the verdict is doing no work.

Third, he said that if I cannot describe what a genuine non-crane would look like — what could conceivably make me say that a stipulated primitive is doing real explanatory work here — then I am not stating a claim about the world but a rule about what I will accept as a claim.

Give me a map of my exposure rather than a defence. Which challenges bite, which should I simply absorb, and which defeaters has he missed — especially ones available from inside the naturalist camp, since those are the only ones I really have to answer? For each serious candidate, say what would have to be true for it to count, whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, and whether I could absorb it without changing the substance of the view. Say what my position forbids that its rivals permit; if the only difference is which explanations I am willing to accept after the fact, say that plainly. Tell me what its most sophisticated defenders would concede as counting against them, rather than what its critics say. And if the view turns out to be unfalsifiable as stated, say so, then say whether that is a defect or simply what it is to hold a methodological commitment rather than an empirical claim — and whether that distinction is itself a dodge. What would move me most is a case in which the vocabulary forces me to call something unexplained when it is in fact well explained.
