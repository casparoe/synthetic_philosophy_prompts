I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company and I sit on our engineering investment board — we decide whether to keep funding big internal projects. Next week we're deciding on moving billing off our old mainframe: 18 months in, roughly double the original estimate, team reckons another 8-10 months. If we kill it now we have to run the old system alongside the half-migrated one for at least two years, which is definately worse than either finishing or never starting.

A mate once explained the dollar auction to me: someone auctions a £20 note, and the trick is that the top two bidders both pay, but only the top bidder gets the note. So people bid past £20 and carry on, because dropping out means paying for nothing. I can't stop thinking thats us, and I want it in the memo but I don't want to sound like I've just read one pop-economics article.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday: recommend approve, kill, or approve with conditions, with a time frame and what the conditions would be. Take the case for finishing seriously — "we've spent £9m and stopping now leaves us worse off than never starting" isn't obviously just a fallacy. And tell me honestly whether the dollar auction is even the right comparison, or whether the differences matter (our prize's value isn't fixed, and unlike the bidders we can change the rules mid-game — e.g. hand the decision to someone who hasn't bid).
