The question was:

"If P = NP, then finding a proof would be no harder than checking one. Does the P versus NP problem therefore bear on the limits of human reason — in particular, on whether mathematical discovery could be mechanised?"

And the student wrote:

"P is the class of problems a computer can solve in polynomial time, and NP is the class of problems whose proposed solutions can be checked in polynomial time. Checking is normally easier than finding, so P sits inside NP, and the question is whether the containment is strict. Cook and Levin showed that SAT is NP-complete: every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time. A problem is NP-complete, then, when it is at least as hard as every other problem in NP — solve it quickly and you can solve them all quickly. That is the sense in which P = NP would abolish the gap between having an idea and checking that the idea works. Baker, Gill and Solovay showed that there is an oracle relative to which P and NP come apart, and this is normally read as strong evidence that they come apart outright. If P = NP, encryption would be pointless, since any key could be recovered in polynomial time. As to reason: if P = NP then, given a formal statement, a machine could find a proof of any given length as quickly as it could check one, so mathematical discovery would be a mechanical procedure, and the question of whether mathematical insight is something special about us would just be the question of whether P = NP."
