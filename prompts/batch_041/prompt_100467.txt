I'm preparing a lecture on linguistic relativity and want to teach Nineteen Eighty-Four with the Newspeak appendix treated as an integral part of the novel, not a curiosity appended to it. Give me a reading of the book through the Sapir–Whorf debate that takes both the fiction and the actual empirical literature seriously. Where does Orwell dramatize a defensible thesis — that a lexicon shapes what is easy, salient, or default to say and think — and where does he dramatize the much stronger thesis that abolishing a word abolishes the concept, the one most linguists and psychologists now reject? Attend in particular to the appendix's framing: it describes Newspeak in ordinary English, in the past tense, as a project not yet completed and possibly abandoned, and it can name the very concepts the Party intended to make unthinkable. Does the appendix undercut the novel's own premise, and if so, is that an oversight or a deliberate irony? Syme's boasts about destroying words, the Party's slogans, and the apparatus of doublethink and crimestop all bear on this, since the Party's need for a special cognitive discipline suggests that the words alone were never going to be enough. Then bring the novel into contact with what is actually known: which findings on language and thought — colour, spatial frames, number words, grammatical gender, time — would Orwell have found vindicating, which would force concessions, and which of his imagined mechanisms turn out to be unnecessary given how modest the real effects are. Conclude by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that the empirical work does not, and what the empirical work shows that the novel gets wrong.
