I am setting the final exercise for a third-year seminar on divine foreknowledge and creaturely freedom, and this year I am asking for fiction rather than for an essay. Please write a short story of roughly 2,500 words — anything between 2,000 and 3,000 will do — which dramatises the structure of the Molinist doctrine of middle knowledge without ever naming it.

The set-up I want you to work from is this. An institution of your choosing — pick its century, its country and its internal culture, and make those choices do some work — holds what its staff call the Ledger. For any person, and any fully specified set of circumstances, the Ledger records what that person would freely do in those circumstances. Its entries cover people not yet born, and people the institution has decided never to bring into being. They cannot be altered: the founders once tried to amend an entry and found that the amendment would not take. The institution does not cause what the entries record. It reads them, and then arranges circumstances to suit. Its theologians distinguish the Ledger, which they say is knowledge that precedes the decision, from the arranging, which they call the decision itself.

Within that set-up I want the story to turn on at least two of the following three pressures, and to be precise rather than suggestive about whichever two you choose.

First, the grounding. What makes an entry true? It cannot be the person, who may not yet exist; it cannot be the circumstances, since the entry is there before they are arranged; and it cannot be the arranger's will, since the entries are unalterable. I would like someone in the story to press this point hard, but do not let the story settle it by assertion, whether in that character's favour or against.

Second, the arranging. The institution gets what it wants by setting the scene, and insists that the people in the scene act freely. Show what that looks like from both sides: from inside a life that is being arranged, and from the desk of someone who arranges.

Third, self-defeat. Someone obtains her own entry, or is told the entry concerning her, and sets out to falsify it. Be exact here about whether, and under what description of the circumstances, such an entry can be defeated at all, rather than merely staging a paradox.

Make the entries for the never-to-be-created a live institutional practice, and let the moral cost of that practice emerge through the plot rather than through commentary.

Some constraints. Do not use the technical vocabulary of the doctrine anywhere in the story: the words Molinism, middle knowledge, counterfactuals of freedom and the like must not appear, and no character is to explain the position in the manner of a textbook. The story must be followable by a reader who has never heard of any of it, and must reward a reader who has. Do not quote or cite real works — no scripture, no theologians, no philosophers — and if a character needs to invoke an author, have her state the position in her own words. No character is to function as the author's mouthpiece; objections should be voiced by people who have something at stake in them. The story must not contain an essay. I shall be reading for whether the plot would collapse if the doctrine were false; if it would survive intact, it is not doing the job.

Finish with an author's note of 150 to 200 words. There you may name the doctrine and its usual author, identify which features of the story carry which element of it, and say where you take the position to be strongest and where it is most exposed. The note should be your own assessment rather than a survey.

Write in British English throughout, spelling and idiom included, and give the story a title.
