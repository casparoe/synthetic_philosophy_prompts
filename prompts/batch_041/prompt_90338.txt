The Platonia dilemma and the luring lottery are ordinarily presented as showcases for superrationality, on the assumption that they have a superrational solution. I want to test that assumption. To keep us off the terrain of contested recollections, I will stipulate the game rather than describe the original.

Stipulated game. There are N ≥ 2 agents, and it is common knowledge among them that they are identical reasoners, each running the same decision procedure on the same information. Each agent independently and simultaneously either abstains or writes a positive integer on a card. Let m be the smallest integer written, if any. If m is written by exactly one agent, that agent receives a prize of 1/m units and everyone else receives nothing; if m is written by two or more agents, or if no one writes anything, every agent receives nothing. There is no communication, no repeated play, and agents may use randomized procedures if they wish.

Question. Give me your credence that the following claim is true, and decompose it rather than defending a bare number:

For this game, the superrational requirement — evaluate each action by the payoff it would yield on the supposition that every identical reasoner takes that same action — does not single out a unique prescription. The game is a case in which superrationality is not a well-defined decision theory, not merely a case in which it is indifferent.

Decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, assigning a rough probability and one line of justification to each before combining them: (a) whether the phrase "identical reasoners" is well-defined at all, given the self-reference problem of a procedure that must predict the output of a copy of itself; (b) whether the superrational requirement should be quantified over pure actions, over mixed or randomized procedures, or over programs — in which last case the relevant object is a program equilibrium rather than a symmetric action; (c) whether the indeterminacy is really a property of the game, since every symmetric profile pays zero, rather than a property of superrationality, and whether a variant whose symmetric profile paid a positive amount would dissolve the problem; (d) whether permitting a public randomization device available to all agents, so that their choices can be correlated without being identical, restores a unique prescription, and whether that should count as a repair of the theory or as a change of subject; and (e) whether "no unique prescription" should be scored as a failure of the theory or as an ordinary case of indifference in which any action is permissible.

Then give the combination, identify the term to which your number is most sensitive, and say what single result — a formalization, an impossibility theorem, a worked analysis — would move the number most, and in which direction. Finish by saying whether your number would be higher or lower for the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and why.
