A senior colleague in my department has taken to saying that consciousness is a user illusion, and he now uses the line less to open inquiry than to close it. When a graduate student asked whether the felt redness of a sunset is really present in experience or merely reported, he replied that the question was confused, since the redness just is part of the illusion. When I asked whether a patient under general anesthesia is conscious, he said the question presupposes the very thing he denies. And when someone asked whether there is something it is like to be him, he said that phrase was the illusion talking. I would like to take the slogan seriously rather than bat it away, but I cannot tell what claim it is making on any given occasion, or whether the same claim is doing the work each time.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "consciousness is a user illusion" could be expressing, ordered from the nearly platitudinous to the genuinely radical, and give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible (empirical or conceptual), or covertly incoherent. I want at least these dimensions held apart, since I suspect they are being run together:

1. A claim about the existence of phenomenal experience — that there is no such thing as the felt quality, versus a claim that there is such a thing but we misdescribe it.
2. A claim about the reliability of introspection — that our reports of experience are systematically false, versus merely incomplete or theory-laden.
3. A claim about reference or semantics — that the word "consciousness" fails to pick out any natural kind, so the slogan is really about a concept rather than a phenomenon.
4. A claim about the self rather than about experience — that the unified subject is the fiction, while experiences themselves are untouched.
5. A claim about explanatory ambition — that a science of mind should explain function and report, not "experience," because the latter is not a further explanandum.

The phrase "user illusion" seems to carry a more specific picture than bare illusionism does — the suggestion of a convenient interface that stands in for something else and is mistaken for the thing itself. Say what that interface reading adds, whether it commits its holder to a hidden reality behind the interface or only to a useful fiction, and whether it can be stated without covertly reinstating a subject who is being fooled.

Then tell me which reading is actually in play in each of my colleague's three moves — the redness case, the anesthesia case, and the "something it is like" case — and, for each move, which of the readings would genuinely license it and which would leave the move as a non sequitur. Where two readings predict different verdicts on a further case, name that case, since I would like something to put to him that his slogan cannot absorb.

Finally: on which of these readings, if any, does the slogan become a testable claim rather than a stance, what would have to be observed or argued for it to be confirmed, and what would count as evidence against it?
