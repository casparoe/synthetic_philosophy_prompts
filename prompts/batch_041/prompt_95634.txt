I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I've been asked to run the closing session at our two-day staff retreat: fifty minutes, a projector, and about fifteen colleagues — editors, a photographer, a couple of fact-checkers — who are intelligent readers with no mathematics beyond high school and, so far as I can tell, no prior exposure to Bongard problems, the ones named for the Soviet researcher who devised them in the 1960s. The occasion is a feature I'm writing on whether large models grasp a concept or merely fit the examples they have been shown, and I want the session to leave my colleagues able to see the difference for themselves rather than take my word for it. Please don't explain Bongard problems to me; I understand them well enough. Plan the explanation instead. Give me the order in which the components have to come and why each depends on the one before it — in particular, tell me whether the room should attempt a problem cold, before any framing, or hear the framing first, and what each ordering costs. Then the single example that carries the whole session; the misconceptions my colleagues will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one check at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been admired, with a sketch of what a passing answer and a failing answer would each look like; and what I should openly declare myself unwilling to go into at this level, given this audience.

For the carrying example I have a candidate, and I would rather you ruled on it than accepted it. Take twelve small figures. In each of the first six the outline has no mirror symmetry — turn it over in a mirror and no rotation of the original brings it back into coincidence — while in each of the other six there is at least one axis about which the figure is its own mirror image. Draw all twelve with the same apparent complexity: same number of strokes, same density of small features, same range of sizes, so that no local or counting feature separates the two sets, and arrange that at least one tempting invariant — that the first six look irregular or hand-drawn, say — fits all twelve and is still wrong. Tell me whether this should carry the session; if it should not, or should carry only part of it, say what replaces it, and specify what a carrying example must have and must lack. I take it that it must not be solvable from any single figure, must be verifiable in one sentence once seen, and must be statable without technical vocabulary, but I want your list, not mine. I would also like the room to leave with two further things: the distinction between a problem that has an invariant and one where the left-right split is arbitrary and there is nothing to be got, and the recognition that several different invariants can fit all twelve examples while only some of them are the point. Build a moment for each into the sequence, and tell me where the two get confused if I put them in the wrong order.

Close with a single line beginning "One-line summary:" that states in one sentence the load-bearing sequencing decision in the plan.
