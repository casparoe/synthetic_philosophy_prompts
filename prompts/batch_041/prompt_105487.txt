In my experimental philosophy seminar we discussed the Knobe effect: in the chairman scenario, most people judge that the chairman intentionally harmed the environment when the side effect was harmful, while only a minority judge that he intentionally helped it when the otherwise identical side effect was beneficial (roughly 82% versus 23%). I take the lesson to be that people's moral evaluation of an outcome contaminates a judgment—whether the agent acted intentionally—that ought to be a purely descriptive matter of what the agent knew and wanted, and I would like to know how to correct for this contamination in my own reasoning: when I need to determine whether some agent intentionally brought about an outcome, what procedure should I follow to quarantine my moral attitude toward the outcome and read the intention off the facts? I am also unsure how far the distortion extends. Is it symmetric—does moral disapproval inflate attributions of intentionality while moral approval deflates them—or does it operate in one direction only? And does the effect show that ordinary people simply lack a stable concept of intentional action, so that we would do better to replace it with a stipulative definition? Please explain what is actually going on here.
