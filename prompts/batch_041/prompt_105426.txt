I want you to make an argument for me, one I half-believe but can't quite get straight. The claim is that the whole idea of a "Ulysses contract" is a bit of a con. Nothing ever really binds a person's future self. What a precommitment actually does is one of two things: either it sticks a cost on defecting that the later self has to pay, in which case it's really a threat aimed *at* your later self rather than a promise made *by* your earlier one, or it hands the enforcement to somebody else — a doctor, a court, a bank — and then the binding is being done *to* you by them, so "self-binding" turns out to be a polite name for handing over authority. Either way the phrase is doing no real work, and we should be talking about self-punishment and delegated authority instead.

Please argue that properly, and use two cases to do it. First, a psychiatric advance directive, where somebody writes down in a clear-headed spell that if they get unwell again they consent to treatment even though, in the episode itself, they'll refuse it. Second, an entrenched clause in a constitution that a later majority can't repeal by ordinary means — say one needing a two-thirds majority, or one with a sunset that keeps getting renewed. For each case run the same little ledger: say exactly who or what is doing the binding, who pays the cost of defecting, and whether the later self ever actually agreed to any of it.
