I'm a novelist — three books, the last one did okay — and for the past two years I've sat on the allocations panel of a small writers' hardship fund: one-off grants of a few hundred to a couple thousand, for writers who've hit a wall, a medical bill, a landlord, a divorce, an advance that fell through. We meet four times a year, we have roughly £40k a round and usually three or four times that in requests, so most rounds we're saying no to people who badly need it. And I've noticed we decide by mood — whoever's case is most vivid in the room, whoever the chair happens to have read, a vague feeling that this one is a "serious writer" — and I hate it, partly becuase I'm a novelist and I know exactly how good we all are at making the vivid case beat the deserving one.

Someone at a dinner tried to explain Harsanyi's aggregation theorem to me and I think it's the thing I want, if I could only use it. As I understood it: if you want a group's choice to be rational and impartial, you should pick whatever maximizes the average welfare of the people affected, and "impartial" means imagining yourself equally likely to be any one of them — the same veil-of-ignorance setup as Rawls, except Harsanyi puts probabilities behind the veil and Rawls doesn't, which is why one lands on averaging and the other on protecting the worst off. That contrast is the whole reason I'm writing to you, because instinctivly I'm a Rawls person and the theorem says I shouldn't be. So: turn it into a procedure our panel can actually run. I need the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is values and not arithmetic. Bear in mind we have about an hour, nobody on the panel has read any philosophy, one member is a retired accountant who will demand numbers we don't have, the chair has a veto, and because we're publicly funded every rejection has to come with written reasons — so the procedure has to produce something I can defend in a paragraph of plain English to someone I've just turned down.

Then run it on three cases. First, a poet with a chronic condition needing two grand for treatment that isn't covered, no dependents, says she'll manage either way. Second, a mid-career novelist, book under contract, needs £1,500 to buy out a day job for three months — no crisis, just time. Third, the one I'm dreading: one applicant facing eviction and a medical catastrophe who needs the whole pot, versus eleven applicants each needing a few hundred for rent, childcare, a broken laptop. I have a horrible feeling your procedure spreads the money across the eleven and I think that's monstrous, so tell me whether the theorem really commits me to it, and if it does, whether that's a reason to abandon the procedure or just to grow up. Tell me straight where the axioms break against our actual data — where we're ranking needs rather than measuring them, where the "equally likely to be any of them" move is doing work it can't justify given that our applicants aren't randomly drawn but self-selected and some are just better at filling in the form — and where the whole thing collapses into me deciding by mood again with extra steps. I'd rather have a procedure with honest holes in it than a clean one that quietly lies.
