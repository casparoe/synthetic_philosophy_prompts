I'm a graduate student in ethics working mainly on the particularism/generalism debate, and I keep returning to the passage in Nicomachean Ethics Book V where Aristotle introduces the leaden rule of the Lesbian builders. In the translation I'm reading it runs: "For what is itself indefinite can only be measured by an indefinite standard, like the leaden rule used by Lesbian builders; just as that rule is not rigid but can be bent to the shape of the stone, so a special ordinance is made to fit the circumstances of the case." I follow the surrounding argument well enough — equity corrects the letter of the law, and the defect lies "in the nature of the case: the material of conduct is essentially irregular" — but the analogy itself is what I can't get a grip on, and I suspect it is doing more work than the usual gloss allows.

Two things I want help with. First, an ambiguity I can't resolve from the text: is the standard indefinite because the subject matter is irregular in itself, or because a general formulation, however accurate, cannot anticipate every case? Aristotle seems to assert both in consecutive sentences, and I want to know whether the image of the bending rule commits him to one rather than the other. Second — what I actually care about — does the analogy support reading Aristotle as a proto-particularist, on the view that a "rule" remade to fit each stone is not really a rule at all, or does it support a generalist for whom principles are genuine but flexible? I have also been told that the image quietly relocates normativity out of the rule and into the craftsman's eye, and I can't tell whether that is in the text or read into it. Please work carefully through what the leaden-rule analogy does and does not license, tell me which reading the passage leaves underdetermined, and flag where I am importing a contemporary distinction the text cannot bear.
