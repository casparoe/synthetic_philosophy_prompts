I run a 200-level seminar on knowledge, science and society -- 20 students, one 60-minute meeting -- and I want to use the whole hour on the social construction of kinds (money, race, gender) in a way where they feel it in their gut instead of hearing me say "socially constructed" forty times. I have a rough idea but I want you to actually design the thing.

The core I'm chasing: I want them to leave seeing three things that people constantly mash together. (1) A constructed thing can be perfectly real -- not fake, not "just a story." (2) It can be objective, in the sense of not being up to any one person, while still not being natural or inevitable. (3) "It's constructed" does not mean "we can just decide to change it," because the construction lives in other people's expectations and in material arrangements, not in your head.

My rough sketch, please feel free to scrap or restructure it:

Phase one, a fake currency. Everybody gets some slips of paper, I declare that slips initialed by me count as points, points buy candy (or bonus points, whatever's cheap) at the end, and they can earn more by doing trivial tasks or trading with each other. Then I let them haggle and hoard for a few minutes so the slips actually start to feel like something. Then I pull a rule change -- devalue them, or stop honoring one group's slips -- and watch the room freak out. The point being that the value was never in the paper, it was in everyone's expectation that everyone else would keep accepting it, and that while it lasted it was real enough that they made real sacrifices for it. Also: once I yank it, no individual can just privately decide the slips still have value -- that's the "objective but not natural" bit, I think.

Phase two, a classification of the people. I hand out arbitrary stickers (or use something trivial like birth month) and declare that one group gets a better exchange rate, or earns double, or gets first pick. Let it run a few minutes. What I'm hoping happens is that they start to sort themselves along the line, form little alliances, and -- this is the part I really want -- start *rationalizing* it, treating the distinction as if it tracked something. Then I reveal it was arbitrary. That's the looping/sedimentation idea, that once a classification has resource effects it becomes self-reinforcing and starts to look natural.

Then a debrief that maps the moments onto the ideas, and is honest about the difference between the classroom version and the real thing.

What I need from you: actual rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour, roles (do I need a couple of student "bankers" or a co-facilitator?), a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money but still gets them to care, the debrief with the specific mappings, and a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each. Also please be honest about which features of real social kinds this whole thing *can't* reproduce -- I have a worry that a 60-minute game makes construction look thin, reversible and purely conventional in a way that real money and real race aren't, and that they'll walk out thinking change is easier than it is, which is its own mistake. If you think the two phases fight each other, say so and cut one. Keep it to one session, no prep they'd have to do outside class, and nothing that puts anyone on the spot about a real identity.
