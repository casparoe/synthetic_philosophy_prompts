Ok so this is a weird way to start, but I have to explain why I'm asking. My dad had a stroke back in March. The doctors gave us two doors: a surgery that might buy him a few more months but with alot of pain and confusion, or keeping him comfortable, which probably means less time with him. I sat in that for two weeks waiting for one door to win, for the scales to tip, and they definately never did. It wasn't just that I was scared and couldn't choose. It was that more time with him and less of him suffering didn't seem to have an exchange rate at all. Nobody could tell me how many extra months a unit of suffering was worth. It really did a number on me and I've been reading about it ever since.

So of course now I'm teaching the upper-level ethics seminar this fall and I have to write the final. I want one essay question on this whole cluster — incommensurable values, hard choices — that a student can't skate through by saying values are plural and sometimes you can't rank them, and then name-dropping the usual suspects.

Here's the exact knot I want them to have to untie. A friend tried to convince me incomparable is a trick. Take the two options, surgery (S) and comfort care (C). Now take S+, a slightly better S — same thing plus one extra lucid day. S+ is clearly better than S. Is S+ better than C? He says it can't be: one extra day doesn't vault over C, not when S and C are in completely different currencies. So S+ beats S and yet doesn't beat C. And that, he says, proves S and C were never equal — because if they were equal, then S+ beating S would make it beat C too, by transitivity. So they're not equal, and neither is better, and now you need some spooky fourth thing, on a par, to even describe them. But then he twists it: keep adding lucid days to S and eventually you cross into better than C. No single extra day can be the one that does the crossing. So the whole picture is a sorites mess and on a par is just a fancy name for not knowing where the line is. I half-believe him and half-think he's cheating.

I don't want the answer from you — I want the exam question that puts students through this, plus the answer key.

Please write me:
- one essay question for a 75-minute, closed-book final for upper-level undergrads;
- a model answer of about 650–750 words, written at the level of a strong student, that actually does the reasoning instead of gesturing at it;
- a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, with the marks for each, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically blow it — I want to separate a real A from a B that just knows the vocabulary.

Two things I care about for the question itself. First, it has to force them to pull apart I can't tell which is better from neither is better, and to say what actually follows for regret and for calling a choice rational if two options are genuinely incomparable. Second, it has to make them commit on whether there's really a fourth relation sitting between better and equal, not just describe the debate from a safe distance. Don't let it turn on remembering who said what — no names required, just the reasoning. If my friend's argument is secretly some famous argument with a name, let them reconstruct it in their own words; don't make the question hinge on them already knowing it.
