I retired from a biology department a few years ago and have been teaching myself philsophy since, mostly through a reading group that meets above a bookshop. Last month we did the two old Confuscians who disagree about human nature — Mencius, who thinks we come with moral sprouts, and Xunzi, who thinks we're born selfish and that only ritual and hard effort make us decent. Two members of the group went at it for forty minutes and I couldn't referee them, so I'm outsourcing it.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Ruth said Mencius has to be right, and pointed to his child at the well: nobody with a working nervous system watches a toddler crawl toward an open well without a jolt of alarm, and that jolt is the seed of morality right there. (2) Dan said a jolt isn't a virtue, it's a reflex; Xunzi's point is that an untrained person will shove his own brother aside for the last bowl of rice, and whatever decency we have is built up by rules, the way you steam a plank and clamp it in a jig until it sets in a new shape. (3) Ruth said Dan had just argued her side for her: only a plank that will bend can be steamed, so the fact that ritual takes at all shows there's something in us for it to take hold of, which is Mencius's sprouts under another name. (4) Dan said that's like saying iron is already magnetized because it can be magnetized. (5) Ruth came back with a worry I hadn't thought of: if the sages invented the mold, then good and bad are just whatever shape the mold gives, and Xunzi has no ground to stand on when he says one shape is better than another. (6) Dan said the sages didn't invent the mold, they found one that works, the way we found out which crops grow; the mold is a tool for getting somewhere we already want to go. (7) Ruth then said something that made Dan pause: both of them think anyone at all can become a sage, so they agree about the destination and only differ about the road. (8) Dan said fine, then it's a nature-versus-nurture argument, and that's an empirical question neither of them was in a position to settle.

Someone else at the group said afterwards that the whole thing might be a word game, since the two of them probably meant different things by 'nature' in the first place.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins overall? I especially want to know whether moves 7 and 8 are right that the disagreement shrinks into something smaller than it looks, or whether something real is left over — and what both of them are missing.
