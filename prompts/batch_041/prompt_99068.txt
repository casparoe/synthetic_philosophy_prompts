ok so before i read more theories of consent i want to know what an adequate one actually has to do. not a definition -- the desiderata. the cases any theory of "this consent was valid / it wasn't" has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the stuff it has to stay consistent with.

purpose first, since i know "adequate" is relative: i want a standard a hospital ethics committee or a data-protection regulator could apply, so it has to be usable by someone who can't read minds and often has to judge before the fact rather than after. keep that in mind for anything about feasibility.

here are the cases i keep tripping on. tell me which are genuine constraints on any theory and which are just me being confused:

- the threat nobody issues. someone takes a dangerous job because they're broke. no coercer exists, there's no threatener to point at, but it feels wrong to call it free. a theory either counts this as valid or it has to explain why the absence of a threat-maker doesn't matter.
- consent to a bundle. i agree to a study; the protocol quietly shifts a year later to something i'd have refused; i never withdraw. valid or not?
- the unverifiable condition. i consent "provided the surgeon is the most qualified available," but neither i nor the committee can check that. can a valid consent rest on a condition nobody can evaluate? if yes, what's left of the information requirement; if no, almost no real-world consent survives.
- consent that changes what i'll want. i agree to a treatment that will, as a side effect, make me more pliable about future treatments. can consent to X be valid when X undermines the standing of my later consent -- and do we judge the first consent by my before or after values?
- the person who can't be wrong about their own competence. we call someone incompetent to consent if they can't understand what they're agreeing to, but "understand" how well, and who sets the bar without already smuggling in a standard of competence?
- act vs. state. i sign but don't want it; or i want it but never say so. which one is the consent?

then the requirements list proper -- each requirement with the case or consideration behind it -- and please seperate the ones that are real constraints on any theory from the ones that are really just house preferences of one camp (the autonomy people vs. the welfare people). what i most want is the pairs you think can't be had together. i have a hunch that "fully informed" and "decidable by a committee in advance" pull against each other, and maybe "tracks the agent's actual will" vs. "legible/checkable from the outside" too. if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so -- that's the actual result i'm after.
