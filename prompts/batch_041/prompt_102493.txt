I'm in an ancient political philosophy seminar, and I've been stuck on Plato's treatment of drunkenness in the Laws. The part I find genuinely strange is not the well-known move — that the Athenian Stranger rejects the Cretan and Spartan hostility to wine and defends the supervised symposium as a legitimate institution — but the justification he gives for it. Wine, on his account, is not chiefly a pleasure to be rationed; it is a drug that loosens the grip of shame and calculation, and precisely for that reason can serve as a test of character and an exercise in courage against fear and temperance against pleasure, provided the drinking happens in company, under rules, with a sober overseer, and with the participants knowing the occasion is a test. He even seems willing to let a city use compulsory drinking as part of civic formation, which is why I want to set his theory against a real case that looks like its inversion. The Spartans, on the standard account, did not drink in order to be tested; they compelled helots to drink to excess and displayed the results to their young, so that the boys would see what a slave looks like with the wine in him.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis running in both directions. Take the Spartan practice of displaying intoxicated helots to Spartan youth as the episode, and Plato's account of wine in the Laws as the theory. Map it carefully: who is the drinker, who is the overseer, what exactly is being tested and on whom, what the spectators are meant to learn, and whether Plato's conditions — voluntariness, rough equality among the drinkers, the presence of a sober director, the shared knowledge that this is a test — are simply absent here or present in a perverted form. Then tell me where the theory is silent or strained. Does the Spartan practice count as a counterexample to Plato's claim that communal drinking can be a legitimate instrument of civic education, or does it actually confirm the claim by showing that the very same mechanism, once those conditions are stripped away, turns into an instrument of domination rather than of self-knowledge? And what does the case do to Plato's implicit assumption that the drinker's self-revelation is knowledge the drinker himself can possess and use — the helot is made to reveal himself to others, not to himself, and I suspect that is the real fault line. I don't want a survey of the secondary literature or any citations; I want the analysis itself, with the mapping kept honest and the points where Plato's account runs out marked as such.
