A small foundation has a three-year budget sufficient to support three to five researchers, and it has committed that capacity entirely to accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory: the program that identifies epistemic rationality with the maximization of expected accuracy and formalizes this using scoring rules and epistemic utility functions. The researchers are philosophers and formal epistemologists; the work is to be conceptual and formal — proofs, counterexamples, distinctions — rather than empirical or experimental; and they will have no internet access and no library beyond what they already know. I want a defended research agenda, not a survey, and not a reading list.

Begin by identifying the open questions that matter most in this program — the ones whose settlement would change how other parts of the program are done — and rank them. Candidate territory you should consider, and are free to reorganize or replace: the strength and scope of the dominance-style arguments for probabilism; whether propriety (the requirement that no credence function expect itself to be scored worse than some rival) is the right constraint on the scoring rules the program uses; how to score imprecise or indeterminate credences; whether accuracy is the sole epistemic good or one good among several; what the program can say about the norms of conditionalization, coherence, and evidence-gathering; whether it extends to non-credal attitudes and to group credences; and the objection that these arguments show only what to believe if accuracy is the sole good, and so change the subject. I care less about the list than about the reasoning: for each item, why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is with the formal tools the program already has, whether it is neglected, and whether settling it would actually change anything downstream. Say explicitly which items you would refuse to fund, and why.

Then bring in the following construction, which I want treated as a live option for the portfolio whether or not it is already discussed in the literature. The program's central formal achievement is an account of states: a proper scoring rule penalizes a credence function by its distance from the truth, and propriety supplies a self-endorsement constraint on such rules. Suppose we try to run the same machinery one level up, over inquiry rather than opinion. Model an inquirer as holding a prior plus a fixed budget of effort that must be divided among a set of open questions, where a question is a partition of the algebra; an inquiry policy maps the prior and the budget to a distribution of effort and a stopping rule. The obvious candidate for epistemic utility over policies is the expected accuracy of the posterior the policy yields, with costs paid in effort rather than in accuracy. That candidate has a consequence worth checking and worth building on: under a proper scoring rule the expected accuracy of the conditionalized posterior is never below that of the prior, so every inquiry has non-negative expected value and, absent effort limits, gathering everything is optimal. Once effort is genuinely scarce and questions compete, the theory licenses a verdict that looks wrong. The question I want in the ranking is whether there is a coherent analogue of propriety for inquiry policies — a constraint that both rules out policies that could only leave the agent's doxastic state worse by its own lights and ranks policies when effort must be divided. If there is no such constraint, that is a boundary of the program; if there is one, it is plausibly its next major development. Place this item in your ranking and defend its position, including how much turns on the value-of-information claim just stated.

Deliver the ranking with the criteria you used made explicit, the trade-offs between them, and what would move items up or down. For each item, say what a successful outcome would look like — a theorem, a counterexample, a distinction — and whether three years is enough. Finish with the two items you would fund first if only two could be funded, and what would change your mind. Do not cite works or supply a reading list; where you need to refer to an idea, describe it rather than name it, and flag any place where you are unsure of the details.
