I am a high school senior and the editor of my school's literary magazine, and I have volunteered to write the closing piece for our philosophy club's end-of-year reading. This semester the club has been working through higher-order theories of consciousness, and I want the poem to be something a reader could enjoy without already knowing the debate, while still being accurate enough that our faculty advisor, who works on philosophy of mind, would recognize the theory in the poem's bones rather than merely in its vocabulary. The part of the debate that has stuck with me is the idea that a mental state becomes conscious when a higher-order representation—a thought or an inner perception—takes it as its object, and the objection that such a higher-order representation might be targetless: it could represent a first-order experience that is not actually occurring, in which case the theory seems to give us consciousness of a state that isn't there. I would like the poem to make that possibility feel like a genuine psychological predicament rather than a technical curiosity.

Please write the poem as a three-voice round, or canon, of roughly eighteen to twenty-four lines total. The first voice should be a plain first-order experience—something like a sting in the palm or the smell of rain on hot pavement—and it should speak in short, unadorned lines. The second voice should enter one line late and represent that experience to itself; it is allowed to be more articulate and more confident than the first voice, and it should sometimes get the first voice wrong. The third voice should enter one line after the second and represent the second voice in turn, so that the poem's staggered entries enact the way higher-order theories pile representation upon representation. The ending should let the three voices overlap in a way that makes it impossible to say which one, if any, is the conscious one, and whether the first voice was ever there or whether the second and third voices generated it between them. Free verse is fine; the constraint is the staggered entries, not a rhyme scheme. The voices should not announce their theoretical roles, and the poem should not explain the theory in footnotes or asides; let the staging do the work. The form should carry the philosophical weight: the round's late entries should track the structure of the theory, and the final overlap should leave the targetless possibility open rather than resolving it. I am not asking for citations or a bibliography—just a poem that a reader could take seriously on its own.
