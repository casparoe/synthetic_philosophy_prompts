I have been keeping a list of practices I am willing to call conventions and practices I am not, and I would like to know whether a single principle underwrites the list. Please treat the verdicts below as data about my usage rather than as a considered theory.

On the convention side: driving on the right; extending the right hand to shake; the QWERTY layout, which I never chose and which no one alive chose for me, and which I stay on only because everyone else is there; the fact that the word dog picks out dogs; the arrangement by which my partner and I settle a restaurant bill — whoever sits facing the window pays — which either of us would drop if the other did; the use of metres and kilograms, which happens also to be required by law; and standing for the national anthem at a stadium, which is not required by law but would earn me hard looks if I stayed seated.

On the other side: my fixed route home from work, which is a habit with nobody else in it; stopping at a red light, which I do on an empty road at three in the morning with no one watching and nothing to expect from anyone; displaying the leader's portrait on my house, which is required on pain of a fine; the local custom of waving at every passing car, which I do because others wave and would stop if they stopped; and the village habit of building every house with its door facing south, which anyone could depart from at no cost to anyone and which each of us does because the others do.

Two pairs I cannot reconcile on my own. The anthem and the portrait feel to me like different kinds of thing — one a convention, the other an edict — although both are arbitrary, both are common knowledge, both are sustained by my expecting others to conform, and both carry a penalty for nonconformity; the only difference I can point to is how bad the penalty is. And the waving custom and the south-facing doors seem to satisfy whatever the driving and QWERTY cases satisfy — arbitrariness, mutual expectation, common knowledge, an alternative that would have served equally well — yet I will not call them conventions, and I cannot say what is missing except that nobody would be worse off if the practice lapsed.

There is also a case I keep going back and forth on. Everyone at my office stays late, each because they believe the others expect it and fear being thought unserious; the director is indifferent and has said so. I want to call this a convention, because each of us conforms because we expect the others to, and I want to deny it, because no coordination problem is being solved and the whole thing would evaporate if the beliefs were corrected.

What I want from you: the principle, or the smallest set of rival principles, that best fits these verdicts. State each clause precisely and identify the case that forces it — in particular, say what work the arbitrariness condition, the common-knowledge condition, the mutual-expectation condition, and the requirement that conformity be conditional on others' conformity are each doing here, and whether anything in my list forces a further condition about why people conform (expectation, coercion, habit, or the merits of the practice itself). Then test the principle on at least three cases I have not listed, chosen so that they separate the rival principles rather than merely illustrating the winner, and give your verdict on each. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, whether I should give that verdict up, and whether the result is recognizably the Lewis-style analysis of convention or a repair of it.
