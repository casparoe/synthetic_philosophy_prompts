You are a philosopher of science who designs classroom simulations and has a reputation for getting students to run into a problem before anyone tells them what the problem is. I need one session's worth of activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science: eighteen students, ninety minutes, movable chairs, a whiteboard. I have index cards, adhesive labels, a pad of play-money tokens, two envelopes, and a printer. Assume I want nothing that requires web access, and no citations, reading list, or background reading: the session should run from the materials I have.

What I want them to come away with is the recognition, reached through their own choices rather than my summary, that the observations they had could not have singled out the rule they were confident in over a rival rule that fitted those same observations just as well — and some sense that the standard rescue, pointing at what kind of predicate the good rule uses, does less work than it appears to. The idea you are staging is Goodman's new riddle of induction. The participants must not hear that name, or the words grue, bleen, projectible, or entrenchment, until the debrief.

Here is the philosophical material, so that you can spend your effort on the design rather than on reconstructing it. Fix some date t in the future. Call a thing grue if it is green and first inspected before t, or blue and first inspected at or after t; call it bleen if it is blue and first inspected before t, or green and first inspected at or after t. Every emerald inspected so far has been green, so every one of them has also been grue: the evidence for the claim that all emeralds are green is equally evidence for the claim that all emeralds are grue, and a predictor using either rule has exactly the same track record. The first thing anyone says is that the second predicate is manufactured, disjunctive, a trick of definition. That reaction has a problem, because green and blue are themselves definable from grue and bleen by a symmetric translation, so the two vocabularies are intertranslatable and neither is more basic than the other. Goodman's own answer was that projectibility attaches to predicates through their record of actual use, which locates the difference in our practice rather than in the evidence or in what the words mean. There is a further turn worth staging: whatever rule the group settles on, a rival can be written that agrees with it on every case so far and diverges on the next one, so no finite body of evidence, and no vote, selects a rule.

Think carefully before you design anything, and begin with a short brainstorm — three or four candidate mechanics, one line each on what they would make the participants do — before you commit to one and write it up in full. If two designs are viable, give me the one you would actually run and say why.

Constraints on the activity:

1. It must not open with a lecture or a framing speech, and it must not use the target vocabulary while it is running. The distinction has to arrive through what participants do; the terms come only in the debrief.
2. Participants must commit in advance, in writing, to a rule and to specific predictions, before the observations that would separate the rival rules are revealed. The commitment must cost something — tokens, standing, a forfeit — and there must be a stage at which individuals commit privately before the group deliberates, so that I can read the room back to them afterwards.
3. Both rival rules must have a perfect record on everything observed, and neither may be written so that it looks longer, more disjunctive, or more obviously rigged than the other. Decide how you will handle the participants' antecedent beliefs about the subject matter — for instance the conviction that the colour of a mineral does not change on a calendar date — since that conviction is exactly what the puzzle is about, and it must be used rather than merely relied on or avoided.
4. At some point the participants must themselves construct a fresh rule that fits all the evidence so far and diverges on the next case. It should take them under two minutes, and the ease should be the point.
5. Choose whether the rule most of them prefer wins, loses, or is left unresolved, and tell me how the rest of the session should differ in each case.

Deliver, in this order:

A. One paragraph stating the single thing participants should be able to articulate at the end, so that I can check whether the session worked.
B. Materials list, plus the exact wording to be printed or read aloud at each step, including the rule templates and the observation cards.
C. Rules, roles if any, the token payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including a twenty-minute debrief.
D. The debrief, mapped moment by moment: which part of the activity is the evidential symmetry, which is the demand for a justification available in advance, which is the vocabulary-relativity point, which is entrenchment, and which is the regress. For each, give me the question I should put to the group rather than the statement I should make.
E. At least five things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with what to do when each happens. Include at least one failure that comes from students being right rather than confused.
F. An honest note on what this activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply by talking — including the strongest reason someone might think the whole puzzle is an artefact of how I set up the room.
