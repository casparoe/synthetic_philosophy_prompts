I teach a third-year seminar on toleration and free expression, and I'm drowning in the same essay every term: the paradox of tolerance proves we're allowed to shut the intolerant down, done, next question. I want a handout that makes them work harder than that, and I'd rather have you write it than me.

So: take on the role of someone who has thought about toleration, exclusion and the ethics of no-platforming for a long time — the kind of philosopher who can tell a real objection from a slogan. Before you write a word, work out privately what the strongest version of each side's case actually is. I don't want to see your notes or a plan; I just want the dialogue. Think it through properly first, though, because it'll show.

Here's the setup. Last term a student in the seminar floated what she called the mirror test. The idea: a body that controls a platform — a university, a conference programme committee, a student union — may exclude a speaker or a group only if that speaker's or group's own declared programme would, were they in a position to do it, remove the same liberty from their opponents. If the answer is yes, exclusion is permitted; if no, it isn't. She thought this was the only non-arbitrary way out of the paradox, since it makes exclusion turn on reciprocity rather than on how much we happen to dislike the speaker. Half the room loved it, half the room thought it was a machine for laundering censorship.

Write me a dialogue between two people who know what they're doing. One defends the mirror test, the other thinks it's incoherent or worse. Neither is a fool and neither is a caricature — I want the reader to be genuinely unsure which one is winning at the halfway mark. Ravi can be the defender and Jo the critic, or pick your own names. They should be replying to the actual last thing the other one said, not to a stock version of the opposing view, and they should be willing to press each other on small distinctions rather than trading big pronouncements.

I want the exchange to get properly into the weeds on at least these four things:

- the counterfactual at the heart of the test. "What would they do if they were in power?" has to be assessed somehow, and Jo will want to know whether we're predicting behaviour under different incentives, reading off the doctrine's own commitments, or just guessing. If Ravi says it's dispositional rather than predictive, what does that buy him and what does it cost?

- doctrines versus tactics. The test as stated looks at what a group says it wants. Jo should push the thought that the actual harm — disruption, heckler's veto, coordinated harassment, capture of the committee itself — isn't a doctrine at all, so the test is blind to precisely the cases that made us want an exit from the paradox in the first place. Ravi needs a reply that isn't just "that's a different problem."

- self-application. Whoever applies the mirror test has to be protected by the rule while applying it. Is that a regress, a straightforward fact about any rule needing an enforcer, or a quiet admission that the test can't be the foundation of anything?

- what's being tolerated. Tolerating a view and tolerating the silencing of a view are different objects, and it's worth having one of them notice that the paradox only bites on one of them. Let them fight about whether that distinction is real or a dodge.

No citations, no "the literature says", and please don't name real philosophers — I don't want you guessing at who said what, and I'd rather the arguments stand on their own legs anyway. Invent your examples (a cancelled talk, a society's speaker policy, a committee vote) but keep them small and concrete rather than dramatic.

Don't resolve it. Also don't let it fizzle out into "well, it's all about power in the end." I want at least one of them to give up something specific — a formulation, a claim, a concession about a case — while holding the line on the main point, and I want the remaining disagreement to be visible in the last few lines of dialogue rather than summarised for me.

Formatting: continuous prose only, please. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists. Just the speaker's name, a colon, and what they say, with paragraph breaks. Around 1500 words. And when you're done, read back through your own dialogue for the tell-tale move where a character answers a point the other one never made — if you spot one, rewrite it before you hand it over.
