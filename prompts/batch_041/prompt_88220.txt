A friend in my comparative-politics seminar keeps leaning on a therapeutic analogy when we argue about how nations come to terms with a shameful past, and I would like you to audit it rather than write an essay about memory politics. Her picture is this: a country that has committed or permitted something terrible is like a patient carrying a trauma; Germany, after the war, eventually did the work — it stopped repressing, it mourned, it built the memorials and put the history into the schools — whereas Japan, Turkey, and the American South are still, on her account, in denial, which she illustrates with the Yasukuni shrine and the textbook fights, the official line on 1915, and the Lost Cause with its monuments and its school curricula. The analogy is doing real work for her: it licenses the conclusion that the others ought to follow the German path, and that if they did they would be better off for it in the way that a patient who faces the truth is better off than one who does not.

What I want is the correspondence table first — what plays the patient, the trauma, the therapist, the repression, the working through, the relapse, the cure, and the point at which treatment ends — together with a straight answer to whether any single entity on the national side can occupy the patient's role at all. Then the places where the mapping breaks, sorted by whether each break matters for the use she is putting the analogy to. Does the disanalogy about agency damage her conclusion? Nobody consents to this kind of treatment, and in the German case the therapy was largely administered from outside and above, by an occupying power and a successor state, after a total defeat — so how much of that recovery is available to a Turkey or a Japan that would have to choose it for itself? What does the analogy quietly import that the target may lack: a unified psyche, a memory capable of being repressed, a wish to get well, a clinician with authority, and above all a terminus at which the past is worked through and one is permitted to stop? And what happens to the fourth case, where the contested memory is not of a crime against an external victim but of a domestic order whose beneficiaries are still present and still arguing over statues?

Finish by telling me whether the normative conclusion survives the audit, and whether some other analogy does better work here — a legal settlement, a trust, a treaty, a contested archive — saying in each case what the alternative buys and what it costs. Assume I know the broad history of all four cases; I want the structure of the comparison, not a survey, and no reading lists or citations, which I will look up myself.
