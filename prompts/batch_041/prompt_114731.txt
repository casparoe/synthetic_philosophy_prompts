I keep running into the same puzzle in Mill's record on empire. He spent thirty-five years administering British India from the Company's London office and publicly opposed the transfer of Indian government to a minister answerable to Parliament. In On Liberty he confined the principle of liberty to those he judged mature, and allowed that despotism over a less advanced people may be legitimate provided the end is their improvement. And he treated British rule in India as a trust held on the Indians' behalf, defensible only as a stage on the way to their governing themselves.

Now put to him a case he never faced. A poor, faction-ridden territory has for a generation been administered by an outside authority — an international body, say, staffed and financed by a wealthy neighbour. By every measure Mill cared about, the inhabitants are better off than under the government it displaced: longer lives, more schooling, higher incomes, impartial courts, and no revenue drawn off for the administrators or for their country. There is, however, no exit timetable and no elected legislature, and the administrators say plainly that the inhabitants are unlikely to be fit for self-government for a century, if ever; polls suggest that most inhabitants would rather keep the present arrangement than risk the alternatives. Would Mill call this rule legitimate? Give me your best guess, the rule you take him to be running, at least two other rules that fit his record just as well together with what each predicts about this case, and the further case that would best tell those rules apart.
