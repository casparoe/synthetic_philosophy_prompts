You are a political philosopher who works on corruption, public office, and the anthropology of exchange. Please help me work out what the international anti-bribery regime presupposes, and then commit to a reading of it rather than surveying the options.

Begin by generating for yourself a list of candidate presuppositions before you settle on any. The practice: multinational firms now run compliance programs that cap gifts to public officials at a nominal value (often a few tens of dollars), require them to be recorded in a gift register, treat "anything of value" as covered — travel, meals, internships, scholarships, charitable donations — and vet the intermediaries who might pass money on. Two statutory anchors diverge. The US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, as amended in 1988, exempts "facilitating" payments made to speed up routine, non-discretionary official action, and reaches only foreign public officials; the UK Bribery Act 2010 contains no such exemption and covers commercial and employment functions as well as public ones. The Act also settles the question of local standards in a way that looks deliberate: section 5 provides that "the test of what is expected is a test of what a reasonable person in the United Kingdom would expect in relation to the performance of the type of function or activity concerned", and that where the function is not governed by UK law, "any local custom or practice is to be disregarded unless it is permitted or required by the written law applicable to the country or territory concerned".

What conception of public office, and of the person who holds it, does this apparatus presuppose? Is the ban on giving a customs officer a bottle of whisky intelligible only on a view of the office-holder as a trustee of an impersonal order, for whom personal advantage is a category error rather than a mere incentive problem — or can a plain principal-agent conception of corruption do the same work? Why would a firm that bans a forty-dollar gift to an official allow a four-hundred-dollar dinner for a procurement manager at a private client, and what does that asymmetry presuppose about the difference between public and private roles? Then the harder question: does the regime presuppose that corruption is a single, culture-independent kind of act, or is it compatible with the anthropological insistence that gift exchange and patronage constitute social relations, so that "corruption" is a category exported from one moral economy into another? Say whether section 5's refusal to let local custom serve as a defence is a commitment to moral universalism, a merely jurisdictional choice about whose officials may be punished, or something in between, and defend your answer.

The deliverable is a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely fits well with, which rival views it quietly rules out, and what would have to change in compliance practice if the presupposed view were replaced by the most plausible rival. Take a definite position and give your reasons, and close by naming the step in your own reconstruction you are least confident about and what would move you off it.
