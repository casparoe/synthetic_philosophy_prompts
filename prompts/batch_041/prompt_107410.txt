I am a self-taught sign painter and type designer, not a philosopher, and I have been turning over a question that I suspect has a long academic history I do not know. What makes every a an a? I can draw a lowercase a with a single story or a double story, with a bowl that is almost circular or nearly rectangular, with a straight stem or a curved one, with a tiny spur or none at all, and readers still call it an a; yet if I change the proportions just a little in the wrong direction, it stops being an a and becomes a d, a q, or a blob. I have my own working vocabulary for this: I think in terms of skeletons and outlines, of Bézier control points, of x-height and ascenders, of counters and terminals, of masters and interpolation axes in a variable font, of hinting and rasterization, of the difference between a glyph and a character code, of sidebearings and the way spacing can rescue or ruin a shape. I would like you to take the main positions on what makes a letter the letter it is — the idea that there is an essence or set of necessary features, the idea that we sort shapes by resemblance to a prototype, the family-resemblance idea that no single feature runs through all instances, the conventionalist idea that a community of readers simply agrees to count certain marks as the same letter, and any other position you think belongs — and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. Treat the skeleton, the outline, the designspace, the master, the interpolation, the rendering, the reading context, and the spacing as your terms. Then give me the residue report: which parts of each position translate cleanly, which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how a type designer or font engine thinks about a letter.

To make this concrete, imagine a variable font with a single-story a at one end and a double-story a at the other, and a weight axis that thickens the strokes; at what point along those axes does the glyph cease to be an a? Or imagine a display face where the a is so stylized that only the surrounding words tell you it is an a — a sign painter's trick. I am especially interested in whether the residue shows that my practical vocabulary is missing something — some normative or abstract dimension that only the philosophers can name — or whether it shows that the philosophical dispute is less substantial than it looks once you have to specify what actually stays fixed across a family of glyphs. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the translation and the report on what does not survive it.
