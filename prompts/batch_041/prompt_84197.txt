I'm doing a PhD on safety standards for self-driving vehicles, and my supervisor has an argument I can't get out from under, mainly because I cant find the loose brick. Everyone in the lab calls it the responsibility gap. Here it is, the way she puts it.

A logistics firm runs autonomous lorries. One of them, on a route it had done thousands of times, met a child running out between parked cars and a motorcyclist overtaking, and did something its engineers had never anticipated. Someone died. Now:

(1) You're morally to blame for a harm only if you could have avoided it — if at some point you had the capacity and the opportunity to do otherwise.
(2) The death came out of a choice the lorry made in circumstances that none of the humans involved had foreseen, or could have foreseen in the relevant detail.
(3) So none of them could have avoided that death.
(4) So none of them is to blame.
(5) And blame is the only thing that can justify making a person answer for a harm — pay for it, be punished for it, be named for it.
(6) So nobody is answerable for the death at all.
(7) But nothing in (1) to (6) turns on the lorry being autonomous. Any harm that comes out of a long enough chain — a firm's decision, a regulator's approval, a surgeon following a protocol that later turns out to be wrong — comes out of circumstances no single person fully foresaw, and the particular harm is one nobody individually chose.
(8) So nobody is ever to blame for anything that comes out of a complex process, which is plainly false.

Which of (1) to (5) do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd sooner keep (1) than (8), but if I keep (1) I seem stuck with (8), and if I drop (1) I don't know whats left of the thought that you answer only for what you could help. Tell me what the repair does to the case for keeping a human in the loop, since that's my whole thesis. Answer in British English.
