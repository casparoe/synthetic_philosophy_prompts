I'm a novelist, about 60,000 words into a book set on a made-up island where the villagers settle everything by drawing lots, and I've hit a wall that's less to do with plot than with what my Lot-keeper is allowed to say when someone calls a draw unfair. So: here are my gut verdicts on a pile of draws, and what I want back is the principle behind them, if there is one — or the two or three rival principles, with the case that forces each clause. No reading lists, please. I won't read them, and what I need is the argument, not the shelf.

The draws, and how I feel about them:

Spinning a needle on a dial to pick a direction to walk: fair, no one could object.

Picking a spot in the circular common by choosing a direction from the middle and then a distance along it, both at random: not fair. Everyone ends up milling about the middle of the field.

Picking a spot by choosing a random easting and a random northing inside the square that boxes the common, then throwing out any that fall outside the circle: fair.

Picking a spot by choosing a random easting and taking the top edge of the circle at that easting: not fair. The east and west sides get picked far too often.

Stretching a fence between two random points on the rim of the common: I want to say fair. Dropping a pin at a random spot inside the common and running the fence through it at a random angle: I want to say fair too. But those two rules disagree about how often the fence passes close to the well at the middle, and about how often it cuts off a strip smaller than a third of the field, so one of them must be wrong. I definately can't see how both can be fair, and this is the one that keeps me up. (I gather it's the old chord business — Bertrand's?)

Picking a distance from the middle and running the fence at right angles to that line at that distance: feels like cheating in the same way the direction-and-distance spot does.

'Split the wine at a random ratio between the two houses': I don't think that's a draw at all until you say whether you mean indifferent over wine-to-water or water-to-wine, and the two give seperate answers.

'Draw a random whole number': there's no such draw. A character who says the chance of it being even is one half should be made to say what physical thing they're doing.

The tile workshop makes square tiles no wider than two spans. 'Draw a tile at random' — how likely is it to be no wider than a span? Indifferent over the side gives one answer, over the area another, over the perimeter another. My gut says the fair draw is whatever the actual cutting does, and that with no process there's no fact of the matter.

A random point on the globe, and then you're told it's on the equator: fine by me, provided I know how the news would have reached me. Ask 'is it on this meridian?' and I'd say one thing; ask 'is it at this latitude?' and I'd say another.

A random point on an endless plain, and how likely it is to be within a mile of the well: no answer. The question's broke, not hard.

My half-formed rule is that a draw is fair if it's indifferent between exactly the things the villagers couldn't tell apart — which I take to be the symmetries of the shape you're drawing from. But that seems to let both fence rules through, since the rim has its own symmetry and the field has its own, so it can't be the whole story.

What I want: the principle, or the rivals, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and why that one rather than another; and what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — a random point on the surface of a ball picked by choosing a random latitude and a random longitude; a random great circle on a ball picked by choosing a random point and a random direction at it; a triangle made by picking three points on a rim at random, and whether it contains the middle; and a random point on a straight road that runs for ever. If the honest answer is that half these questions have no answer until you name a physical process, say that plainly — I can write a character who says it.

Please do it in British English. I'm fussy about spellings and my copy-editor is worse.
