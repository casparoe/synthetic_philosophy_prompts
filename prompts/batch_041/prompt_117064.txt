Next month I present at a two-day workshop on value and personal relationships, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: the two goods Moore crowns at the end of Principia Ethica — the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of human intercourse — are not on all fours, and the value internalism he states there (the claim that the only things intrinsically good are certain states of consciousness) can carry the first but not the second. What makes a friendship good seems to depend on the actual existence and actual character of the other person, and on the good's being hers as much as mine; Moore's doctrine of organic unities lets him obscure that gap without closing it. I claim this unresolved pressure, rather than mere fashion, is why the friendship literature still divides between internalist accounts (the good lies in the experience of relating) and relational ones (the good depends on facts about the relationship itself).

The audience: mostly contemporary value theorists who work on friendship and love and treat the history as a distraction; two senior historians of early analytic philosophy who know Principia Ethica closely and will defend Moore's consistency; one consequentialist who will ask what turns on my being right; and one philosopher who holds the unfashionable view that friendship is not valued for reasons at all.
