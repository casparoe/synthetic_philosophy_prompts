Ok so this is a weird one. My mum has ALS — the kind where your mind stays sharp but your body stops working — and last month she got offered a slot in a trial thats testing what they call "progressive neural prosthetics": as her motor neurons die off, they replace them with synthetic ones that do the same job, so the idea is she keeps going. She's asked me to sit down with the consent form and help her pick, and there are five different ways they can run it. Medically they're all meant to be about the same. What I actually care about is which one leaves me with my mum at the end of it, and the form is definately no help on that at all.

Roughly as I understand them:

1. The slow swap, awake. One neuron at a time over a few years, she's conscious and herself the whole way through, the synthetic ones just quietly take over the job as the real ones fail.

2. The slow swap, asleep. Same thing but they keep her under for the entire process, so there's no continuous experience running across it — she goes to sleep now and wakes up on the other side, same memories, same personality as far as any test can show.

3. The weekend rebuild. Scan everything, build the replacement in one go, switch the original off. Nobody can tell the difference afterwards, including her.

4. The double. They build the synthetic version first and run it alongside her for a year, both awake, both her, and they can talk to each other — then the biological one is retired and only one carries on.

5. The patch job. They only replace the regions that are failing and leave the rest, so she ends up mostly original with synthetic patches — and those patches take manufacturer firmware updates over the air, meaning someone else can change bits of her later without her agreeing each time.

Can you rank these from most to least "still her"? Give each one a number out of 100 for how much I should count it as her surviving, plus a sentence or two on which feature of that setup is doing the work. Just the list, exactly five items, no intro, no wrap-up.
