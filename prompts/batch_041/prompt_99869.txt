I am a retired earth scientist who has spent the last several years on decision theory, and next month I am giving a paper at a departmental work-in-progress seminar. My thesis is that the familiar objection to expected-value maximization — that it is fanatical, licensing the pursuit of minute probabilities of enormous goods — mislocates the problem. The cases that generate the fanatical prescriptions are precisely the cases in which the mathematical expectation is either undefined or is carried almost entirely by outcomes so improbable that no finite sampling process could reveal their contribution; there the expectation fails to describe what an agent can expect, much as the sample mean of a heavy-tailed quantity fails to converge on its expectation. I argue that the decision-relevant quantity is instead the behaviour of the outcome distribution over the ensemble of situations an agent actually faces, and that once this shift is made the fanatical prescriptions lose their force without invoking risk aversion, diminishing marginal value, or any bound on how good outcomes may be. The argument runs in three steps: (i) expectation is a functional on a distribution, and its decision-theoretic authority rests on an implicit appeal to what repeated or ensemble behaviour would reveal; (ii) that appeal fails in just the cases the fanaticism debate is about; (iii) what survives is a comparison of distributions, which is not fanatical but is also not yet a complete decision rule, and I say something about what fills the gap.

The audience will be about fifteen analytic philosophers: several epistemologists, one of whom works on imprecise probability; a decision theorist who will defend the standard axioms of expected utility and the sure-thing principle; someone in formal ethics who works on population axiology; and a visiting economist who is a committed Bayesian. None of them is a statistician, and most are skeptical of importing results from the sciences into decision theory.
