I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm working up a piece on juries and rational disagreement, using Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957) as the hook. Here's the knot I can't untie. Aumanns agreement theorem says that two agents who share a prior and who both know each other's posterior probabilities cannot agree to disagree — if they're rational, one of them must be wrong about something, and so they converge. The jury in that film starts 11-1 to convict and ends 12-0 to acquit, and the whole movie is the road between those two numbers. So is the film a dramatization of the theorem, or a counterexample to the assumption that makes the theorem work?

My hunch, and I'd like you to tell me if it's a bad one, is that the juror's don't share a prior and couldn't, because a prior in that room isn't a probability, it's a biography — Juror 3 and his son, Juror 5 growing up in a slum and knowing how a switchblade is actually held, Juror 10's bigotry, Juror 4's faith in respectable witnesses. If that's right, then the film's real subject is the collapse of the picture in which a jury is twelve agents drawing their types from the same urn, and what we're watching is not Bayesian updating on evidence (the evidence was all aired in the courtroom, it's shared) but the slower and costlier business of making each other's reasons common knowledge — and I mean the iterated thing there, I know that you know that I know. Which raises the question I most want answered: is the theorem even the right tool here, given that the jurors plausibly differ in information (what each of them happened to notice, what each happens to know about el trains and knives) rather than in prior? That distinction seems to be carrying a lot of weight and I want it carried honestly.

So walk me through the film and tell me where the theory bites and where the film is doing something the theory has no room for. Which scenes are genuinely agreement-theoretic — the secret ballot, the identical knife, the marks on the witness's nose, the old man and the passing el train, Juror 3 finally breaking — and which are just evidence, or just drama? Aumann tells you where rational agents end up and nothing at all about the road; what does the film know about the road that the theorem can't say? And where does the film push back on the framing itself — does it suggest that what moved those men wasn't an argument but a person, and if so, does the common prior start to look like a claim about rationality or just a claim about who we happen to be?

Two last things. Say how confident you are in what you claim, and flag the single point you're least sure of — probably something about what the theorem does and doesn't imply, since that's where I'd most likely embarrass myself in print. And skip the citations; I'm filing from a place where I can't check them anyway.
