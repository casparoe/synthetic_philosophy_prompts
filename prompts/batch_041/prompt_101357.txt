I write about language, speech, and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been asked to draft our newsroom's standing procedure for handling slurs — in headlines, in quoted speech, in reader comments we excerpt, and in transcripts we publish. Writers and copy editors make this call several times a week, usually in minutes, and the current guidance ("use your judgment") has produced both needless offense and pointless euphemism. Acting as a philosopher of language who has also worked a copy desk, write me a decision procedure a writer with no linguistics training can actually run in order: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a hard no, and where it hands the call to a human because the question is evaluative rather than linguistic. Ground it in what the semantics and pragmatics literature genuinely establishes about derogation — that a slur's force may be part of what it conventionally encodes, or may ride instead on what the speaker insinuates, presupposes, or echoes, or may depend on who is speaking to whom and in what register — and be explicit about which of these distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Before you commit to the procedure, brainstorm the candidate distinctions so I can see what you considered and rejected.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a senator caught on tape using a racial slur, which we feel we must quote; a member of the targeted group using the same word in an interview about reclaiming it; and a novelist's use of the word in a book we're reviewing. For each, say what the procedure yields, and flag any case where it gives the desk an answer we won't like. Include one short test a writer can apply to a single sentence — for instance, whether swapping in a neutral description preserves the sentence's force — and tell me what that test cannot detect. Finally, mark clearly where the procedure must stop and defer to a person, and what qualifications that person needs. No reading lists or citations, please; I need something the desk can use today.
