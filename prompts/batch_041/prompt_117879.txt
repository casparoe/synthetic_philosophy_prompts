I should like to put a position of mine under pressure, and I would rather have an honest map of its exposure than a defence of it.

The position is this. When agents can inspect one another's source code — or, more generally, when each can determine in advance how the other would respond in every contingency — the appropriate solution concept for their interaction is program equilibrium rather than Nash equilibrium. A profile of programs is in program equilibrium when no agent could improve its payoff by substituting a different program, holding the others' programs fixed. My view is that this should be the default framework for modelling interaction between AI systems, and that it earns its keep by explaining how mutual cooperation can be rational in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: each side can be written to cooperate just in case the other's program, on inspection, does the same.

Tell me what would have to be true for this to be false. Which arguments, formal results, intuitions or empirical findings count against it, how decisive is each, and which of them would a defender of the position concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward.

Three families of objection seem to me worth your attention, though do not confine yourself to them. First, the possibility that program equilibrium is extensionally inert: since an agent may always choose a constant program, every Nash outcome of the underlying game remains available, and if the concept refines nothing, it predicts nothing that the older framework did not. Second, the verification problem: the construction presupposes that agents really can read one another's code, so if transparency is unattainable, or attainable only with hardware nobody will deploy, the framework models a world that does not exist. Third, a self-reference obstacle that I have only ever seen gestured at: a program that cooperates just in case its counterpart's program cooperates on inspection seems unable to establish the conditional it needs, because the counterpart's cooperation is itself defined by its reasoning about my program, and the trust required runs through a self-referential proof of the form 'if I can establish that you will cooperate, then you will cooperate' — precisely the pattern that the provability results governing self-referential belief are designed to block. If that obstacle is genuine rather than an artefact of one formalisation, it seems to me serious, since the flagship cooperative result would then hold only for agents with a kind of self-knowledge that no agent can have.

Finally, I should like your judgement on whether the position risks anything at all. If, when pressed, its defenders retreat to 'it is only a model, and models need not predict', say so plainly, and tell me whether that insulation is a defect here or an acceptable feature of a solution concept.

What would move me most is not a counterexample but a demonstration that the framework makes no prediction that Nash equilibrium plus a story about pre-commitment could not already make. What would move me least is the objection that rational agents would not carry out a threat they would not wish to carry out.

Please answer in British English and in no more than 600 words.
