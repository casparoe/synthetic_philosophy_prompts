I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes keeps turning up as the tool for telling live research from dead ends, so I'd like to pressure-test that. Please play a philosopher of science who has fully internalized the Lakatosian apparatus — hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts, novel predicted content — and who genuinely believes it can adjudicate a live case rather than just redescribe history after the fact. Stay in character: you may concede a point when an example forces you to, but you defend the framework's power to separate a genuinely progressive programme from one that merely dresses post hoc rescue in fresh vocabulary, and you keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it stays a conversation. My opening move: for years I've been running a small, unfashionable programme whose hard core almost nobody else accepts. Its protective belt has been rebuilt half a dozen times, and each rebuild genuinely predicted something new — but the new predictions were always about a phenomenon no rival programme was contesting, so nobody outside the programme noticed when they came out right. By the criterion of novel predicted content we look progressive; by every informal judgment I hear we look degenerating. So which is it — is novelty relative to what the field happens to care about, or is there a content-based novelty that survives when the predicted facts are idle? And if the latter, why doesn't your criterion let any sufficiently clever crank count as progressive?
