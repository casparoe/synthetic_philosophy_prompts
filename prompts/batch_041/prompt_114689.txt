For a seminar on the moral limits of markets, I want a reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go that resists the obvious one. The novel is usually treated as a fable about organ harvesting, and so about kidney sales, but there is no market in it: no prices, no payments, no bargains, only a bureaucratic and compulsory regime that takes what it needs, and the horror survives the removal of all of that. Read it against the commodification literature, separating the objections that are usually run together — that pricing a good corrupts it, that a sale under duress is not real consent, and that being used as a resource is wrong on any terms. Which of these does the novel actually support, and does it show the first two to be red herrings? I am inclined to think it does, and that the book therefore cuts against the claim that a voluntary, unpaid donation regime is morally superior to a market; tell me whether that is right. Two things to work in: the Gallery, which exists to prove that the students have inner lives and which the world accepts without altering anything, and the rumour that love might earn a deferral, the one place a bargain is imagined and the one place it fails. Then say where the novel locates the wrong, what that implies for kidney sales, price gouging, or vote selling, and where the book resists the reading I am proposing.
