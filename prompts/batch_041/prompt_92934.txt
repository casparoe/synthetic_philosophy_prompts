I have been following the anthropic doomsday argument on and off since I first ran into it in a LessWrong comment thread years ago, and I keep a small file of debates that seem to me stuck in the same way this one is. The shape is always the same. Someone lays out the birth-rank reasoning — you should be surprised to find yourself this early in the human sequence, and that surprise, run through an indifference principle over ranks, ought to shift your posterior toward a short future — and someone else answers that there is nothing to be surprised about, that the reasoning treats your position as a random draw from a well-defined urn when no such urn exists, and that any conclusion you reach is a function of a reference class you have no principled way to fix. The first person then says the objection proves too much, because it would equally license ignoring your rank in cases where the answer is obvious; the second says those cases are disanalogous; and the thread dies. Both sides know all the moves, and as far as I can tell nothing in the last couple of decades of discussion has moved anyone who was not already moving. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would rather have your reasoning than a summary of who holds what; a reading list would be useless to me, since I have read most of it.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but a stack of three, and that participants keep sliding between them without noticing: a formal question about whether the probability calculus extends to self-locating propositions at all; a substantive question about which selection principle is correct, given that the two live candidates — the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, to use the standard labels — return opposite answers in toy cases where each looks nearly self-evident; and a downstream question about the reference class, which I suspect is not empirical at all, since the class is chosen rather than discovered. What makes me think the formal question is load-bearing is a wrinkle I have not seen handled in a way I find satisfying. In the standard setup the total number of observers is treated as a fact about the world, but consider a world in which a large share of observers are digital: minds that can be run faster or slower, copied, paused, forked, or run in parallel. On the counting conventions that seem most natural to me, the number of observer-moments then depends on engineering choices, and in the limit on choices made by the observers themselves. Run the argument on observer-moments and its answer looks like a function of a bookkeeping convention rather than a fact about how long humanity lasts — and worse, one that the subjects could in principle manipulate, which cannot be right. Run it on some coarser class and I cannot see a non-arbitrary way to draw the boundary, at which point the argument's force seems to drain away into the choice of class.

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side says rather than a taxonomy imposed from outside. Is the formal question real, or am I confusing a genuine limitation of the framework with my own failure to specify the right sample space? Does the digital-minds wrinkle genuinely break the argument, or is it just the old reference-class problem wearing new clothes, or have I simply misdescribed what the argument is doing? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result showing one principle incoherent, a redesigned setup that makes the toy cases agree, or the admission that the question is ill-posed and no progress should be expected. If the last of these is the right answer, I would like to be told so plainly and told why.
