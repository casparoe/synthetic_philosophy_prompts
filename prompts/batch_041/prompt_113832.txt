I took my doctorate in geochemistry and spent thirty-five years in industry and government laboratories; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science on my own, and I have gotten stuck on the problem of verisimilitude. Popper's promise — that although our best theories are strictly false, one false theory can still be closer to the truth than another, so that inquiry can progress toward a goal it never reaches — is the part of his program I most want to keep, and I understand that his own formal account of it was shown in the mid-1970s, by Tichý, Harris, and Miller, to have the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other. I would like you to gather the serious accounts offered since for measuring how close a false theory comes to the truth — those built on a truth factor and a content factor, those that insist only relevant consequences may count, and those that take the word likeness literally and compare the possible worlds in which a theory would be true — and to include, if you think it earns a place, a proposal of my own: fix in advance a finite list of questions the theory is meant to answer, and score the theory by the weighted share of those questions it answers correctly. Sort all of them into tiers by how well each delivers what Popper wanted, namely that greater truth content together with lesser falsity content should yield greater closeness, without the comparison collapsing either into mere logical strength or into arbitrary bookkeeping. Give a sentence or two for each entry on what it buys and what it costs, and where two accounts are close enough that you would not separate them, say so rather than forcing a gap. Please write this as continuous prose in American English — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way you would write a long letter to a colleague.
