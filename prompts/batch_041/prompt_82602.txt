Take the decipherment of Linear B — Michael Ventris's 1952 argument, developed with John Chadwick and building on Alice Kober's analysis of recurring inflectional patterns, that the Mycenaean tablets record an early form of Greek — and analyze it as a case of radical translation under unusually austere conditions. The decipherer has no informant, no pointing, no way to elicit assent or dissent, and no prospect of new evidence: only a closed and finite corpus of palace inventories, plus whatever the archaeology and the later history of Greek will supply. Kober's triplets, the numerals and commodity ideograms that anchor the readings, and whatever the resemblance to the later Cypriot syllabary, itself read as Greek, is worth — judge that for yourself — constrain the hypothesis space, and none of them is a native's utterance.

Make the mapping explicit. What stands in for stimulus meaning and observation sentences when the evidence is a dead corpus rather than a living speaker? Which of Quine's analytical hypotheses are carrying the weight — word division, sign values, the case system, the reading of the numerals — and what would a rival manual that fits the same corpus look like? Can you even describe the shape a permutation or proxy-function rival would take here, swapping the referents of certain logograms while compensating elsewhere in the manual, and could Mycenaean scholars tell the difference? Take the ideogram beside a numeral that everyone reads as sheep: sheep, mutton, or a sheep-stage?

Then run the case back against the theory. Note that the phonetic values of most signs are now settled while the meanings of many words remain contested; is that the shape indeterminacy should take, sentences determinate and reference inscrutable, or merely ordinary incompleteness of evidence? Quine insists that indeterminacy of translation is not underdetermination of theory by evidence, and Chomsky replies that the distinction collapses. Say which reading of the episode supports which side, and what the episode shows about the thesis: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated.

Keep two boundary cases in view. Linear A is still unread, which suggests indeterminacy can be permanent. The Maya glyphs were eventually read with the help of surviving Mayan languages and a colonial-era sign list, over the objection of an influential scholar who took them to be largely ideographic — ask whether access to something like a living informant is what made the difference.

I am inclined to think Linear B refutes the thesis by showing that a manual can be killed by the evidence, but I would rather be shown what that costs than be agreed with.
