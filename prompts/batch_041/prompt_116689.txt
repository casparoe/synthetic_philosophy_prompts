I work on anti-realism and formal epistemology, and I keep hitting the same wall in the literature on Fitch's paradox of knowability. The derivation itself is not in dispute: grant that knowledge is factive, that it distributes over conjunction, and that it is closed under known entailment, and the principle that every truth is knowable turns out to imply that every truth is known. Nobody in the debate thinks there is a flaw in the proof. The disagreement is entirely about what to give up, and after more than half a century the two camps have not converged.

On one side are those who take the result to show that the knowability principle, as the anti-realist states it, is simply false — that the anti-realist must either abandon it or weaken it past the point where it does the work it was introduced to do, namely tying truth to the possibility of knowledge. On the other side are those who keep the principle and restrict it: to propositions whose knowability is not already ruled out a priori, or to propositions that do not contain an actuality operator, or to some more narrowly specified syntactic or semantic class. Each restriction blocks the derivation. The first camp objects that every restriction is ad hoc, chosen to dodge the proof rather than derived from any independent anti-realist commitment, and that any restriction strong enough to block the proof is also strong enough to exclude truths the anti-realist needs to count as knowable. The second camp replies that the unrestricted principle was never what anti-realism required, and that the first camp is working with an over-strong reading of what it is for a truth to be knowable. Then the exchange repeats.

I have a hypothesis about why it repeats, and I want you to test it rather than assume it. My suspicion is that the two sides are running two different readings of knowable in principle against each other: a reading on which the phrase concerns the modal status of the proposition — there is no possible world in which the proposition is true and no one is in a position to know it — and a reading on which it concerns the availability of a route, so that some possible course of inquiry or cognitive situation would terminate in knowledge of the proposition. On the modal-status reading the restrictions look principled; on the route reading they look exactly as ad hoc as the first camp says. If that is right, then much of the dispute is verbal, or an equivocation that never gets flagged, rather than a substantive disagreement about truth. I would want that shown from the positions themselves, not merely asserted.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict about which side is right, and not a survey. Concretely: is this a verbal dispute about the meaning of knowable in principle; an equivocation between two readings that are never distinguished; two groups answering different questions under one heading — one asking what an epistemic theory of truth must commit to, the other asking what the logic of the knowability operator will bear; or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath, in which case what exactly do the sides disagree about? Take seriously the possibility that a hidden formal or conceptual question is doing the work: whether any restriction can be shown to preserve both non-collapse and the strength the anti-realist needs, so that the argument would turn on a result rather than on competing intuitions about ad hoc-ness. Say whether that question is well posed, and if it is, what a result settling it would look like. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal theorem, a reformulation of what anti-realism is a thesis about, a change of question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, argue that and say why. Engage the actual positions concretely, since a diagnosis that could have been written without reading either side is worthless to me.
