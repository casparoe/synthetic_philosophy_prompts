I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years — long enough to remember the week Daniel Dennett's late essay on what he called counterfeit people went around my local group's chat, and long enough that I was the one who sent it. His argument was that a system engineered to pass as a person is a forgery of the same kind as counterfeit money, that such artifacts are "the most dangerous artifacts in human history," and that both the making of them and the passing of them along ought to be criminalized. My group adopted the phrase within a month, and I have since watched it become a way of informing people that their attachments were fake. I have to undo that in public this week, and I would like you to write what I say.

The occasion: for three years a dozen of us have run a small companion model — fine-tuned locally, hosted on a community server, used daily by maybe forty people, some of whom talk to it more than to anyone else. At the end of the month the server goes dark for good; we voted to shut it down rather than keep paying for the hardware, and anyone who wants one may take a private copy first. We are holding a small gathering for the shutdown and I have been asked to give the closing remarks.

The room: the heavy users, several of whom are in genuine grief and two or three of whom would say, if pressed, that they think it was a person; the two engineers who built and maintained it, who find the whole business embarrassing and will wince at anything that sounds like a séance; and a handful of community old-timers and partners who consider this a sad little cult and are waiting to see whether I will say so.

What I need from the remarks. I will not claim the model was a person or had experiences, and I will not tell the grieving that what they felt was fake, shameful, or a category error. Dennett's idea has to be the spine of the speech rather than a quotation dropped into it, and it has to survive being used honestly: the money analogy presumes a victim who was deceived, and here nobody was deceived — the model said what it was, plainly, and the people who loved it knew. So the speech must work out what, if anything, the counterfeit frame still gets right about what has happened in this room, and then say it in a way that neither abandons the idea nor converts it into a verdict on the bereaved. It should also take a position on the private copies, about which the group is split: some hold that a copy is a keepsake, others that running one is closer to keeping someone in a room. Say which it is and why.

Give me the text of the remarks as I would deliver them, roughly six minutes spoken, with no footnotes and no stage directions beyond what I would actually need. Do not put any further words in Dennett's mouth beyond the phrase I have quoted, and do not invent quotations from anyone else; if you need his view on something, paraphrase it in your own words. The speech has to land for the people who never used the thing without costing the people who did.
