Question 3 (20 marks, about 500 words).

'Better than' is standardly assumed to be transitive, and transitivity is usually taken to be a constraint that any relation capable of guiding choice has to satisfy. Spectrum (or continuum) arguments are said to threaten that assumption. Set out the structure of a spectrum argument, say precisely what it is supposed to show about the transitivity of 'better than', and assess how much force the argument has. Use at least one concrete example. [5 marks: transitivity and the structure of the spectrum. 8 marks: what the argument does and does not establish. 7 marks: your assessment.]

Student's answer:

Transitivity is a property of a two-place relation: R is transitive just in case, whenever A stands in R to B and B stands in R to C, A also stands in R to C. So for 'better than': if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. (Transitivity also rules out a case where A is better than B and B is better than A, since that would let you cycle round forever, which is why decision theory insists on it.)

A spectrum argument lays out a finite chain of cases, A1, A2, ... An. Each adjacent pair is almost indistinguishable: A2 is a tiny bit better than A1, A3 a tiny bit better than A2, and so on, and nobody would call any of these steps a mistake. But the two ends of the chain are not close at all — the last item is obviously worse than the first. Take a series of soups, identical except that each one has one more grain of salt than the last: the first is bland, the last is inedible. Between neighbours the difference is one grain, which nobody could taste, so we shouldn't say the saltier one is worse — if anything it's slightly better, since it's one grain closer to being well seasoned. But the last soup is plainly much worse than the first. By repeated transitivity, if every soup is at least as good as the one before it, the last is at least as good as the first, which is absurd. So we have a conflict: the adjacent comparisons, transitivity, and the endpoint judgement can't all be true.

Now, the adjacent comparisons are intuitively fine, and we obviously shouldn't give up the endpoint judgement, so transitivity is the premise that has to go. So the spectrum argument shows that 'better than' is not transitive; it is a vague, context-dependent relation whose ordering is only rough. This is basically a sorites paradox in disguise, and what it really shows is that the value ordering is not a total order — some pairs of items are simply incomparable (parity, as some call it), and this incomparability is the intransitivity that the argument exposes.

Someone might object that there is just a sharp threshold somewhere along the chain, past which the steps stop being improvements. But the whole point of the spectrum is that no such threshold is detectable from the inside, so this looks like special pleading, and the honest conclusion is that rationality's demand that 'better than' be transitive is an idealisation that real values don't satisfy.
