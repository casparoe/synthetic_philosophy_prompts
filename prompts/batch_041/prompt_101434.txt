I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of a decade — LessWrong, the old Slate Star Codex and its successors, the comment threads that spawn from them — and for the past two years I have run a fortnightly discussion group that grew out of a local meetup. The members are capable non-specialists: a few engineers, two doctors, a lawyer, one lapsed philosophy graduate student. They are at home with thought experiments and have absorbed the popular consciousness literature, but they have no formal training in philosophy of mind. The next session runs ninety minutes, and the topic is epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment.

I do not want the argument explained to me; I want a plan for teaching it. Let me fix the target. Epiphenomenalism is the claim that experiences are caused by brain events but cause nothing in turn — the physical order is causally closed, so the pain is a byproduct rather than a mover. The paradox is what surfaces once you ask how our judgments and reports about experience are themselves produced. If the sentence "this hurts" and the belief that red looks a particular way are caused by the brain event and not by the experience, then the view appears to saw off the branch it is sitting on: it is no longer clear in what sense such a judgment is about the experience, and the epiphenomenalist's own conviction that epiphenomenalism is true seems to inherit the difficulty.

So: give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and why each depends on the one before — where the hinge is, the single moment at which the paradox has to become visible rather than merely stated. Give me the one example that should carry the whole session, and tell me what it must have and must lack; I had been planning to lean on the Victorian steam-whistle picture, and, for the second half, on a barometer that reliably announces a storm because both are effects of the same falling pressure, so tell me whether either carries the weight or collapses the puzzle by making the judgment look perfectly fine. Map the misconceptions the group will bring or form, in the order they will surface — I predict they will hear "epiphenomenal" as "not real" or "illusory," and I expect the room's reflex of asking whether any of this is falsifiable — and tell me the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Give me a single question or move for the end that will distinguish a group that has felt the pull of the paradox from one that can merely recite the position. And tell me what I should refuse to settle at this level, and say so out loud — the replies, the verdict on whether the paradox is fatal, the surrounding metaphysics of causation. Do not send me readings.
