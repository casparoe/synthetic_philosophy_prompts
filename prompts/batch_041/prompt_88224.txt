I am a high school student, and I am writing up an argument for my school's philosophy club that has left me stuck: every way out of it seems to cost more than the problem it solves. I would be grateful if you could tell me which step to give up, and what giving it up commits me to.

The setup is the toxin puzzle. A billionaire owns a machine that detects, with perfect accuracy, whether a person sincerely intends to perform a given action. He will pay you one million dollars if and only if, at this moment, you sincerely intend to drink a vial of toxin at noon tomorrow. The toxin will make you violently ill for a day and do no lasting harm. The money is paid the moment the intention is detected, and the payment does not depend on whether you actually drink. Once the money is in your account, drinking the toxin brings you nothing but a day of illness.

My argument has five steps.

(1) A sensible person can collect the million. Forming the intention costs nothing and is the only way to obtain the money.

(2) The same billionaire now offers a second million to anyone who, at this moment, sincerely believes the toxin is harmless. He detects belief as reliably as he detects intention, and he pays on the spot.

(3) A sensible person cannot collect the second million. I cannot believe the toxin is harmless simply because believing it pays. Belief answers to evidence — the labelled vial, the billionaire's evident seriousness — and not to money.

(4) The two cases are structurally identical. In each, a reward is attached to a mental state rather than to an action; in each, the state is one I would not have for its own sake; in each, the machine rewards sincerity.

(5) Since (1) and (3) cannot both be true if (4) holds, one of them must go. Either the first million is uncollectable — forming an intention is not something I can do for the sake of a payoff — or the second million is collectable, and belief is something I can adopt at will. Both look unacceptable. Forming an intention is something I do and not something that merely happens to me: I can decide this moment to raise my hand, and the decision is mine; so I do not see why I could not make the same kind of decision for the sake of a million dollars. Yet no amount of deciding seems able to make me believe a proposition merely because believing it would pay me.

The obvious reply is that intention, unlike belief, is under my direct control, so I can simply decide to drink. I do not see how this helps. The machine pays only for a sincere intention, and an intention adopted purely as a means to money is exactly the kind that seems insincere; and if the reply is that I can sincerely intend now and change my mind later, then I need to know why changing my mind does not simply amount to never having had the intention.

So which claim should I abandon? If the fault lies in the parallel between the two cases, state the disanalogy precisely and tell me what it costs — in particular, whether it lets me collect the first million, and if so, how an intention formed for the sake of the money can be sincere. If the fault lies in the original puzzle rather than in the comparison, tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what I lose by rejecting it. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than (3), but I want to know what that choice costs me.
