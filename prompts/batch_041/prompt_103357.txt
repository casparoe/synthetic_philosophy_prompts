I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I apologise in advance for whatever I am about to get wrong. For the past few years I have been reading around the Post Office Horizon scandal, and it is the epistemic dimension of it that will not leave me alone, rather than the fraud or even the wrongful convictions. The essentials as I understand them: an accounting system called Horizon, built by Fujitsu, repeatedly reported shortfalls in subpostmasters' branch accounts; the Post Office treated those figures as authoritative and prosecuted hundreds of people for theft, fraud and false accounting on the strength of them; the subpostmasters said the system was wrong and were not believed; the Post Office maintained for years that Horizon was robust; and it was eventually established in court that the software contained bugs, errors and defects, after which convictions began to be quashed and a public inquiry followed. I am not asking you to litigate the history. I want to know what a particular philosophical framework makes of it.

The framework I have in mind distinguishes testimonial injustice, where a hearer gives a speaker less credibility than the evidence warrants, from hermeneutical injustice, where someone is wronged because the shared vocabulary needed to make sense of their experience does not yet exist. I believe both terms come from Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice; if I have attached the wrong names to the wrong ideas, correct me rather than humouring me.

Please do two things, and keep them separate.

First, run the episode through the framework properly. Identify who is speaking and who is hearing, where the credibility deficit actually sits and what produces it, and where the hermeneutical gap lies. I am especially interested in that second half: the subpostmasters were not only disbelieved but seem to have had no usable way of saying the thing that was true. Is that a genuine hermeneutical injustice in Fricker's sense, or does it stretch the concept, given that the relevant expertise existed and was simply not admitted into the room? Be exact about which features of the case the framework explains and which it quietly misdescribes.

Second, and this is the part I care about most, tell me whether the framework needs something it does not have. Here is a half-formed extension of my own, which I would like you to assess rather than adopt. What strikes me about Horizon is that the machine was the more credible witness. The system's record was treated as the disinterested account; the subpostmaster was the one with a motive. The institution did not merely disbelieve the humans; it believed the software, and it ranked the software's output above the humans' reports as a matter of course. I have been calling this procedural credibility: the weight an organisation gives to an account because of the process that produced it rather than the person who gave it, where an auditable, traceable process is presumed honest and an unaided human recollection is presumed self-serving. Is that a distinct phenomenon that the testimonial and hermeneutical pair cannot capture, or is it ordinary testimonial injustice with a machine sitting where the hearer should be? Does the case show that the framework needs an extra category, or that I have been reading it too narrowly and it was always general enough?

Then be honest about the limits. Where is the theory silent rather than merely incomplete — what about the persistence of the injustice does it fail to explain? In particular, I have read that some philosophers treat this kind of ignorance as active rather than passive: not a gap in the shared vocabulary that no one happened to fill, but a refusal by the powerful to acquire concepts that were already available. If that is right, does it sit inside the framework or does it require a different kind of explanation altogether, one about institutional incentives rather than about knowledge? And say plainly which of the moves you make above are supported by the theory and which are your own extension of it, because I would not be able to tell the difference.
