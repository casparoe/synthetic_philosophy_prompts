I run a biweekly reading group for our MA students and this term we've been crawling through Dennett's Kinds of Minds, specificly the part where he sorts creatures into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian grades. Last week two of them — Mira, who works on animal cognition, and Dev, a visiting postdoc from cog sci — got into it at the whiteboard and honestly neither of them was obviously wrong, and I said I'd come back with a proper verdict this week but I've been buried in marking. I'm moderating, so I dont want to just pick a side; I need to know what's actually defensible at each step.

Here's the exchange, roughly as it went. (1) Mira said the four grades are a real ladder, each defined by where the selection happens: in the gene pool, in the individual's reinforcement history, among inner simulations that let the hypothesis die in the head instead of in the animal, and finally among culturally transmitted thinking tools. She said each grade needs everything below it plus one new thing, so the joints are genuine. (2) Dev said the middle joint is mush — anything with a nervous system that learns does a bit of inner trial and error, so Skinnerian and Popperian are just points on a continuum of how much simulation you do, not two kinds of creature. (3) Mira said fine, but the top joint is different in kind and not degree: a Popperian creature is stuck inside its own skull, whereas a Gregorian one can run part of its thinking outside the skull — on paper, in a notebook, in techniques inherited from others — so the cognitive unit becomes a population with a shared library rather than one brain. (4) Dev said that's just the extended-mind line and it proves to much, because a beaver's dam and a chimp's stick-learned-from-mom are external props and socially transmitted tools too, so either a lot of nonhuman animals get promoted to Gregorian or the whole distinction is really about language, in which case calling it a fourth grade is rhetorical packaging; and anyway a Popperian with a pencil is still a Popperian, just better equipped. (5) Mira said he's missing the point of the taxonomy — the grades aren't species-level natural kinds and a creature can sit in two at once, they're grades of design meant to mark where new selective pressures show up, and learning a language or a numeral system rebuilds the inner environment rather than just adding storage.

Can you adjudicate this move by move for me — who's right about what, what each of them is missing? Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one per move, each a sentence or two, and put your overall who's-more-right verdict at the end of the fifth one. No intro or conclusion, I'll just read it out at the start of the session.
