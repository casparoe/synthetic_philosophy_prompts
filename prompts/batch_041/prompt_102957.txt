I'm in a small reading group that's working through computational complexity and what it does or doesn't say about the mind, and one of the guys there brought in an argument last week that I can't get out of my head — mainly because I'm pretty sure it's wrong but every step looks fine to me on its own. Here it is, cleaned up a bit:

(1) A problem is NP-hard when every problem in NP reduces to it; assuming P≠NP, that means no algorithm solves all of its instances in time polynomial in the input size.
(2) Loads of things I do without thinking are NP-hard. Packing a suitcase so everything fits and I still have room for souveneirs is basically bin packing. Finding the shortest loop that hits the bank, the pharmacy, and the school before they close is TSP. Picking the cheapest basket of groceries that covers every dinner I've planned for the week is set cover.
(3) I do all of these in a few seconds, with inputs that aren't tiny — a suitcase is dozens of items, the grocery run has a dozen constraints — so my brain must be running an algorithm that solves them in polynomial time, or close to it.
(4) If that's right, then either P=NP or my brain isn't a Turing machine at all, i.e. I'm some kind of hypercomputer.
(5) Both of those are crazy. P≠NP is about as safe a bet as exists in math, and nobody serious thinks the brain computes things Turing machines can't.
(6) So one of these steps has to go.

I'd rather not give up (1), it's just the definition, and I'd rather not give up (5). The guy who brought it in says the lesson is that complexity theory is just irrelevant to how the mind works, which feels way too quick to me. Which step do you actually reject, why did it look airtight before you poked at it, and what does rejecting it cost me — and if I patch the hole, does the argument come back in some weaker but still uncomfortable form?
