I retired from a comparative physiology lab a few years ago and have since been working through the philosophy of personal identity, reading it alongside films I once watched purely for entertainment. I keep returning to Ghost in the Shell (1995). Its protagonist's body is entirely prosthetic; the film opens with a shell being assembled on a production line; and the drama of the ending is that she merges with the Puppet Master, a program that claims to have been born rather than written, in the sea of information, and emerges in a child's body speaking of herself as neither of the two.

I would like a reading of the film through the problem of gradual replacement and uploading. Which view of identity does the film presuppose when it treats a wholly prosthetic body as no threat to the ghost at all, and does that view survive an ending that is really a fusion of two streams — the sort of case that leads Parfit to conclude that identity is not what matters? Note that gradual replacement is a sorites problem, so that a film which finds the piecemeal case untroubling has already committed itself on the wholesale one. I want both directions kept honest: where the film's images do work that the argument cannot, and where it quietly assumes what a gradual-replacement theorist would still have to argue. Finally, say what the film evades by treating a mind generated from scratch as continuous with one that was transferred.
