I have been trying to work out on my own what an emotion actually is — whether it is a kind of judgment, a kind of perception, or simply a feeling — and I keep getting stuck on a passage from Spinoza's Ethics, Part III, that I cannot get clear on. He writes: "By emotion I mean the modifications of the body, whereby the active power of the said body is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, and also the ideas of such modifications." A little later he adds: "If we can be the adequate cause of any of these modifications, I then call the emotion an activity, otherwise I call it a passion, or state wherein the mind is passive."

What confuses me is the following. The definition seems to make an emotion partly a bodily event — a change in the body's power of acting — and partly an idea of that event. But the activity/passion distinction seems to turn on whether the mind is the adequate cause, which sounds like a claim about where the state comes from rather than about what the state feels like or what it represents. So when Spinoza says the emotion includes "the ideas of such modifications," is he saying that the emotion is a perception of the body, a judgment about the body, or something else entirely? And does the activity/passion distinction change what kind of thing the emotion is, or only how it was brought about?

I would like help understanding the passage in its own terms, and then some help connecting it to the present-day question of whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings. In particular, I want to know whether Spinoza's view fits any of those three categories cleanly, or whether it is doing something different that the categories obscure. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want to understand the thought itself.
