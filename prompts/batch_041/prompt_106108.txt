For a piece I'm writing on the transatlantic argument over inequality, I've come to hold a view I'd like you to test rather than endorse. The dispute is almost always framed as a clash of values — a solidaristic Europe against an individualistic America — but I think that is a misdescription: the moral premises on both sides are close to identical, and what actually differs is the causal picture people carry around, chiefly their beliefs about how much movement there is between the bottom of the distribution and the middle, and about whether market rewards track desert. If that is right, the European attitude is the more defensible one, not because its values are superior but because its empirical picture is better calibrated; the American attitude stands or falls with factual claims about mobility that hold for some rungs of the ladder and fail for others, and that look quite different depending on whether one reads mobility in absolute or in relative terms. What would count against this? Give me the evidence, arguments, and findings that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including anything that would show the disagreement is genuinely about values after all — and address in particular whether beliefs about desert can be classified as empirical in the first place. I'll say in advance that I won't be moved by anything that can itself be redescribed as a difference in factual belief, so I need to know whether that leaves the view unfalsifiable; if it is insulated, say whether that is fatal or merely a cost, and what a sympathetic defender of my position would concede as against what they would wave away.
