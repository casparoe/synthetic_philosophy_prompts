I'm trying to get a grip on Ranke's preface to his 1824 book on the Latin and Germanic nations, specifically this bit (in the standard English rendering): "To history has been attributed the function to judge the past, to instruct ourselves for the advantage of the future. Such a lofty function the present work does not attempt. It aims merely to show how it actually took place."

I get the words but not the force. Everyone trots this out as the birth certificate of naive positivism — the historian as a neutral camera — but that reading seems to make nonsense of the fact that Ranke was a Lutheran who thought you could see God's hand in history, and that he spent his life doing source-criticism, which is a very interpretive buisness. Also the German is "wie es eigentlich gewesen," and "eigentlich" can mean "actually" but also "essentially" or "properly speaking," which feels like it changes things. And then there's Carr, who says "The historian and the facts of history are necessary to one another. The historian without his facts is rootless and futile; the facts without their historian are dead and meaningless" — which sounds like a straight denial that "showing how it actually took place" could ever be a seperate activity from interpreting. Hayden White's line about historians emploting the past pushes the same way.

Help me understand what the passage is actually claiming, and whether it survives the Carr/White pressure. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro, no conclusion. I want points that cover: what the contrast class is — who's the "history" that's been assigned the job of judging and instructing, and what Ranke thinks is wrong with that; whether "merely to show" forbids selection and narrative shaping or only forbids shaping in the service of a moral or a lesson; whether reading "eigentlich" as "essentially" makes the claim more or less defensible; whether the Lutheran God's-hand background is a bug or a feature for the "neutral camera" reading; and finally what a reading of the passage would look like that takes Carr and White seriously without just collapsing into "Ranke was naive."
