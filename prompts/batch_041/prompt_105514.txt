I am a philosophy professor, and I was recently drafted into an argument between two of my colleagues — one a decision theorist, the other a normative ethicist — about how moral uncertainty ought to guide action. They were testing their views against a concrete case, and I would like your help adjudicating the exchange move by move rather than settling it by fiat. Here is the case and the substance of what each of them said.

The case: a small foundation holds a fixed budget of $100,000 and must commit it to exactly one of two grants for the coming year. Grant P would fund a public-health intervention that, by the lights of an impersonal consequentialist theory, yields a very large aggregate gain in welfare; but by the lights of a rights-based theory, P also enrolls a modest number of participants in a way that fails to secure their genuine consent and so constitutes a serious wrong. Grant Q funds a less effective intervention that raises welfare far less but violates no one's rights. Both colleagues agree that these two theories are the live candidates, and both are willing to grant, for the sake of argument, credence of roughly one-half to each.

The exchange:

(1) The decision theorist: Under moral uncertainty one should maximize expected choiceworthiness. Take each theory's ranking of the options, convert it to a numerical score, weight the scores by one's credence in each theory, and choose the option with the higher expected score. On this case, that means weighing the welfare gain of P against the rights violation of P, in proportion to the two credences, and selecting the winner.

(2) The ethicist: That procedure presupposes something the theories do not supply — a common cardinal scale on which their verdicts can be added. A welfare gain and a rights violation are not quantities in the same unit, and the theories themselves do not specify how to trade them off; if they did, there would be no conflict to adjudicate. One can always choose a scaling on which P wins and another on which Q wins, so the expected-choiceworthiness verdict is an artifact of an arbitrary choice of units rather than a deliverable of one's credences. The rule is therefore not merely difficult to apply; it is ill-defined.

(3) The decision theorist: Suppose I concede that, for the sake of argument. Then we should adopt a rule that requires no intertheoretic units at all. Take the probability that each option is the one the true theory endorses, and select the more probable option. That is scale-free, and it returns a determinate answer.

(4) The ethicist: That rule has the opposite defect. It is sensitive only to credences and entirely insensitive to stakes: it would have you flip a coin between an option that a theory calls mildly discourteous and an option that another theory calls a massacre, provided you were equally confident in the two theories. It also treats every departure from the chosen option as equally regrettable, which is precisely what we wanted the numbers for in the first place.

(5) The decision theorist: Then combine the two. Choose the option that minimizes the ratio of worst-case regret to best-case regret across the theories. This normalized measure requires no common unit, because it divides each theory's regret by that same theory's own scale, and it does not ignore stakes, because regret grows with how much worse one option is than the other.

(6) The ethicist: That maneuver smuggles the scale back in through the denominator. Dividing by a theory's own scale presupposes that the theory has a well-defined internal scale and that the zero point of regret is comparable across theories. If the theories' internal scales are merely ordinal, the ratio is undefined; if they are cardinal but their zeros are not aligned, the ratio again depends on an arbitrary alignment. You have not escaped the problem, only relocated it.

(7) The ethicist, pressed for a positive proposal: When theories genuinely conflict and no intertheoretic comparison is available, the situation is one of underdetermination, and the agent is permitted to choose either option, so long as she does not represent the conflict as settled.

(8) The decision theorist: That is not a procedure but an abdication. It renders the choice immune to criticism, so an agent could select whichever option better suits her convenience and then describe the selection as permissible.

They then turned to me, and I gave the unhelpful reply that each of them was right about something. Please do better. Go through the eight moves and say, for each, whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and why. Then say which of the two has the stronger overall position, and what both of them are missing. I am particularly interested in whether the exchange at (5) and (6) admits of a resolution, or whether it shows that any scale-free rule must fail on one of the two desiderata — sensitivity to credences and sensitivity to stakes — and, if it must, which desideratum should give way and on what grounds.
