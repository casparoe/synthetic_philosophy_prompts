The question read: "Consider the standard sequential-learning model. The state of the world is A or B, equally likely a priori. Six agents move in a fixed order. Each privately observes a binary signal that matches the state with probability two-thirds, independently across agents. Each agent observes the actions — not the signals — of all her predecessors, and chooses A or B so as to maximise the probability of matching the state; an agent who is exactly indifferent follows her own signal. (a) Work out the reasoning of the first three agents, and say at which agent a cascade begins, and why. (b) In what sense is the resulting behaviour rational, and how does an information cascade differ from herding? (c) Suppose the same agents move in the same order, but a platform now displays a running tally of the choices made so far by the agents in the sequence, all of whose actions are already observable. Does the tally make a cascade more likely, less likely, or neither, and what would break a cascade once it has begun? (d) What does the model imply about the epistemic performance of markets and electorates, and how should the 'fragility' of cascades be understood?"

The answer I am looking at reads, in full: "(a) The first agent simply follows her signal, since a signal of A moves the posterior from one-half to two-thirds. Her action is therefore informative, and the second agent's posterior given that action has a likelihood ratio of two — exactly the likelihood ratio of her own signal. So when her signal agrees she follows it, and when it disagrees she is exactly indifferent. On the stated convention that a tied agent follows her own signal, her action coincides with her signal, and after two agreeing actions the public likelihood ratio is four. A contrary signal, with likelihood ratio one-half, then leaves a posterior of two-thirds in favour of A, so the third agent chooses A even on a B signal, and every later agent joins her. The cascade therefore begins with the third agent, and this is exactly the point at which an agent stops using her private information: the signal is given no evidential weight at all, since the accumulated public evidence swamps it. (b) The behaviour is rational in the plain decision-theoretic sense: each agent maximises expected utility given her observations, and nothing in the model turns on a mistake or a shortcut. A cascade is the specific case in which it is optimal to disregard one's own information; herding is the broader term for imitation, much of which is not informational at all. (c) The tally makes no difference, since it is a function of actions that are already observable; what makes cascades possible is that predecessors' actions can be seen in the first place. What breaks a cascade is new public information about the state, not a further private signal. (d) The model implies that the epistemic quality of a market or an electorate depends on the order in which information arrives and on who moves first rather than on how much information is in circulation, and that a wrong cascade is as stable as a right one — which is what is meant by calling cascades fragile and idiosyncratic. In practice, though, cascades are short-lived: as more agents draw their signals, the contrary private evidence accumulates and eventually breaks the cascade."
