I am preparing a seventy-five-minute session for a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind. The members are fluent in the vocabulary of the hard problem, qualia, and the explanatory gap, and several of them work on perception or cognitive neuroscience, but none has seriously engaged with illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that phenomenal consciousness, as it is ordinarily conceived, is a kind of introspective illusion rather than a feature of the mind still awaiting explanation. I do not need you to explain the position to me. I need a plan for teaching it.

Two commitments of mine should govern the plan. First, I take the pivot of the session to be a distinction that is easy to state and hard to hold onto: the reality of experience versus the reality of its alleged phenomenality. An illusionist does not deny that we undergo experiences; the denial falls on the further claim that those experiences possess phenomenal properties — a felt what-it's-likeness over and above their sensory, functional, and representational character. Second, I want the session to arrive at the meta-problem rather than rest on the hard problem: the question is not why experience is so hard to explain, but why we are so powerfully inclined to posit phenomenal properties in the first place, and whether that inclination can be accounted for without them.

What I need from you:

1. The order. Set out the sequence in which the components must be introduced, and say of each why it must precede the next. I am particularly interested in where the phenomenal/access distinction belongs — prerequisite or distraction at this level — and in whether the intuitive case for phenomenal consciousness should be built up before it is dismantled, or whether doing so entrenches the very intuition we are trying to expose.

2. The carrying example. Give me the single case or scenario that should hold the session together, and state what it must have and must lack in order to do that work. I have been tempted to build the session around the transparency of experience — the fact that when I try to introspect what it is like to see a red tomato, I seem to encounter only the tomato's redness and never any separate mental item — with the twist that the illusionist must explain how that very transparency is what conceals the misrepresentation. Adopt this if it can bear the weight; if it cannot, say why and supply the example you would use instead. Either way, specify the moment at which it enters the sequence and what it is doing there.

3. The misconception map. List the misconceptions this audience will bring or form — I expect at least "illusionism denies that we have experiences" and "the illusion is like a visual illusion" — and for each, say precisely where in the sequence it should be headed off and how, without the correction itself generating the next misconception.

4. The check. Give me one question to put to the group at the end that would distinguish someone who has genuinely grasped the position from someone who has merely learned to repeat its slogans.

5. The exclusions. Tell me what to leave out at this level — which of the live disputes among illusionists and their critics would cost more confusion than they are worth in seventy-five minutes — and how I should flag the omissions so that the group knows the picture has been simplified.

Take definite positions throughout. Where two sequenceings are plausible, choose one and defend the choice rather than laying out the alternatives for me to decide between. And where you think my framing is a mistake — in particular, if you doubt that the reality/phenomenality distinction will survive contact with this audience, or that ending on the meta-problem is wise — say so directly and tell me what to do instead.
