I'm midway through a term paper on bounded rationality, and a confusion that began as a throwaway footnote has swallowed the whole thing. As the contrast was presented to me, it is tidy: an optimizer scans the options until it identifies the best one available, whereas a satisficer scans until it encounters an option that clears a threshold — the aspiration level — and then stops, on the grounds that further searching costs more than it is worth. The tidier I make this, the less it seems to say. If the agent takes search costs into account, then stopping at good enough looks like nothing more than the optimal stopping rule for a search problem with a known cost structure, and the satisficer turns out to be a maximizer whose utility function has been written in an unusual notation. I have heard defenders of satisficing insist that this is a misunderstanding, that the point was never to describe a different stopping rule but to describe agents who cannot represent the option space in the first place, or whose ends are formed and revised in the course of acting rather than fixed before deliberation begins. I can follow each of those remarks on its own, but I cannot see how they add up to a single position, or which of them is doing the real work.

What I want is a careful conceptual explanation of what is actually at stake between the satisficing picture and the optimizing picture, written for someone who already has the standard vocabulary (utility, optimal stopping, aspiration level) and wants the anatomy rather than the history. Four threads, if you can keep them apart. First: is there a version of satisficing that is genuinely distinct from maximizing even for an agent with no computational limits at all, or does the distinctness always have to come from somewhere else — from the agent's inability to enumerate the options, from the costliness of search, or from the fact that its preferences are not yet settled? Second: the aspiration level is usually said to be endogenous, drifting upward after success and downward after failure; is that a genuine prediction of the theory or a device that lets it accommodate any data, and what would it take to tell the difference? Third: there is a regress nearby that I cannot close. If the agent is choosing how hard to search, by what standard is that choice made, and does invoking a second-order standard simply reinstate the optimizer one level up? Fourth: I have been toying with a distinction of my own and would like to know whether it is already implicit in the tradition or whether it collapses — the distinction between satisficing over options, meaning accepting a good enough candidate, and satisficing over procedures, meaning accepting a good enough rule for how to choose, on the thought that an agent might do the first while still optimizing the second, or the reverse.

Please tell me which of these threads is load-bearing and which is a distraction, and flag any place where I have imported an assumption that the satisficing tradition would reject outright. I am not asking who said what or where to read further; I want the conceptual structure, with the weakest joints marked, and, where you think the two pictures can be made to coincide, an explicit statement of the bridge principle that would make them coincide and of what that principle costs.
