I belong to a small reading group that has spent the term on the sociology of scientific discovery, and one of us — a former molecular biologist now working in science policy — has been arguing a comparison that has quietly taken over our Thursday meetings. Her claim is that the phenomenon of multiple discovery is convergent evolution applied to ideas. The camera eye arose independently in vertebrates and in cephalopods, the wing in bats and birds and insects, echolocation in bats and dolphins: the selection pressure was the same, the available materials were the same, and so the same solution was arrived at more than once without anyone copying anyone. On her telling, calculus is that same story for Newton and Leibniz, natural selection for Darwin and Wallace, the telephone for Bell and Gray — the problem space had narrowed to a single opening, and whoever happened to be standing in front of it collected the credit. She draws a strong normative conclusion from this. The heroic theory of discovery, the picture on which a particular person is the cause of a particular idea, is on her account a category error of the same kind as crediting one dolphin for the shape of a fish; and our entire apparatus of priority disputes, eponymy, patents, and prizes is therefore busy allocating credit for something that no individual did. Another member of the group, who actually works on evolutionary biology, insists she has the biology wrong and that the analogy, drawn properly, supports nearly the opposite conclusion. I would like an audit before I take a side.

Please give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the lineage, the ancestral state, the selection pressure, the adaptation, the common ancestor, the gene flow, and the patent office that biology does not have — then the places where the mapping breaks down, separating the breaks that are load-bearing for her conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Two points I would especially like pressed. First, whether convergent evolution is even the right biological analogue here, as against parallel evolution from a shared ancestral state, or a single lineage with rampant horizontal transfer, given that Darwin and Wallace were reading the same books and writing to the same correspondents; if the two discoverers are better modelled as branches of one stock than as unrelated lineages that hit the same solution, say what that does to her argument. Second, what the analogy quietly imports from its source that the target case lacks — deep time and many trials, an environment of selection external to the selected organism, a graded rather than exact resemblance between the two solutions, and the total absence in biology of anyone who loses a priority fight. My own hunch, which I would like tested rather than indulged, is that the honest biological analogue is neither convergence nor common descent but recombination inside a communicating population, and that this predicts something neither of them wants: as the speed of communication rises, the ratio of multiples to singletons should rise with it, and the window in which a priority claim is even coherent should shrink accordingly.

Argue it out on the merits — I do not need citations, reading lists, or a survey of who has said what, and I would rather you reason from the structure of the two cases than from anyone's authority. Tell me in the end whether her conclusion survives the audit, whether any weakened version of it does, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.
