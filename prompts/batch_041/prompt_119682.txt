I'm a second-year PhD student in a cognitive science program, and for a seminar on cognitive architecture — nominally about the frame problem — I've been asked to write a short position statement: my working assumptions about how minds work, which the group will then try to pull apart. I would much rather find the weak joints myself than discover them in front of everyone, so I'm asking you to audit the set for internal consistency. Please take the role of a philosopher of cognitive science who has thought carefully about the relationships among representation, inference, and relevance. Before you assess anything, work through each of my commitments on its own terms and spell out what it commits me to; only then start looking for conflicts. I'm not looking for references or a survey of the literature — I want an audit.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) I am a computationalist: cognition is computation over structured representations, and a mind is, at least in principle, implementable with a finite stock of representations plus rules that operate on them. (2) I hold that a representation means what it does in virtue of the inferences it licenses — its content is its role in the system's economy of inference, not any privileged causal hookup to a piece of the world. (3) I take the mark of a mind, as opposed to a calculator or a lookup table, to be its capacity to cope with situations its designers never anticipated. (4) I believe the reason we still lack common sense in machines is fundamentally an engineering problem of representation: equip a system with enough explicit knowledge and the right inference rules — including defaults and non-monotonic machinery, so that conclusions can be retracted when new information arrives — and the apparent difficulty will dissolve. (5) I believe that nothing is relevant in itself; relevance is always relative to a task or a goal the agent currently has.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which pairs merely look like they are, and rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitment would have to be revised, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost. I'd also like you to flag any commitment that, on reflection, is doing no real work or is unfalsifiable as stated. Think it through carefully before you commit to a verdict.
