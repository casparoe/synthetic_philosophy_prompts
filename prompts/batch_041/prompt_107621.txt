I'm a novelist — 200 pages into a book whose main character is a neuroscientist who is slowly, and not unhappily, convincing herself she has no free will — and I'm worried I've built the whole thing on a misreading of the science. A friend of mine who writes for a science magazine sent me this crib sheet when I asked her to explain Libet, and I've been leaning on it pretty hard:

"Libet's experiments back in the eighties were the last nail in the coffin for free will. He wired people up and had them flick their wrist whenever they felt like it, and asked them to note the moment they *decided* to move. The brain's readiness potential showed up about half a second before the subjects reported having made that decision — so the choice was already underway, unconsciously, before they were ever aware of it, and the conscious feeling of 'I chose to move' is just a story the brain tells itself after the fact. Later fMRI work pushed the lead time out to something like ten seconds, which is even worse than Libet thought. Libet, being a good scientist, accepted that this refutes the idea that we author our own actions, though he held out hope for a sort of veto. And once you see that the physical causes of a choice are fully in place before you ever experience choosing, moral responsibility starts to look like an illusion, and the only honest position left is hard determinism."

That reads beautifully and it's exactly the mood I want for the book. But my brother-in-law is a postdoc in cognitive neuroscience, and when I read it to him he rolled his eyes and said "it's not wrong so much as it's *wrong*," which is not the feedback I paid for. Can you go through it for me sentence by sentence, in plain language for someone who doesn't have the literature memorized? I want to know which parts a working neuroscientist would nod along to, which are the kind of thing that's technically defensible but quietly misleading, and which are flatly false — and I'd especially like to know what the actual findings leave open that the crib sheet treats as settled, since that's the space my character probaly lives in. Then, seperate from all that: does anything in the *accurate* version of the story license the jump at the end, from "the brain does things before you're aware of them" to "nobody is responsible for anything"? I'd much rather write a character who's wrong for interesting reasons than one who's wrong because I didn't do the reading.
