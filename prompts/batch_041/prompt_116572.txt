I'm a science journalist — I write features about ideas, mostly the philosophy-of-biology beat — and I'm working on a piece about whether Darwinian natural selection is a scientific theory by Popper's own lights. Not the creationist version of that question, the real one: Popper himself suggested at one point that natural selection might be better described as a metaphysical research programme than as a testable scientific theory, and then later partly walked that back. I want a sidebar for the piece, and I've decided the sidebar should be an imagined dialogue rather than me explaining it, because the explaining always goes flabby.

So: write me a dialogue of about 900 words between two people, both of whom are competent and neither of whom is a strawman. Call them Vera and Osei, or whatever you like, but they're invented — no real living philosophers.

Vera is a philosopher of science trained in the Popperian tradition. She takes the demarcation problem seriously and is not embarrassed to say the word "falsifiabilty" out loud. Her worry is the old one: if selection just says that the variants that leave more offspring leave more offspring, it looks close to a tautology, and if it can be fitted to any outcome after the fact — the trait spread, so it was selected for; it didn't spread, so it was selected against or constrained — then it isn't doing the work a testable theory does. She should be honest that this is a worry about the *principle*, not about whether evolution happened.

Osei is a working evolutionary biologist who also reads philosophy and is not going to let Vera caricature Popper. He should press the standard replies: that selection hypotheses aren't just "survivors survive," they're quantitative claims about differential reproduction under specified conditions, that the environment and the trait's costs and benefits can be characterized independently of the outcome, that optimality and comparative models make predictions that could come out wrong, and that drift and selection are separable given the right data. He should also grant the real point where it bites — that a lot of adaptationist storytelling in the wild is post hoc and untestable, and that his own field is guilty of it.

Now here's the bit I actually want to see them fight over, because it's the thing that made me want to write the piece. Have one of them float a criterion they've just made up on the spot — let's say Osei calls it the swapped-environment test: a selection explanation counts as testable if you can state in advance what should happen to the trait's frequency under a specified change of environment, or under a specified change to a rival variant's payoff, such that the prediction could fail. Vera should then attack this in a way that isn't cheap: her line is that whatever is doing the falsifiable work in the swapped-environment test is the ecology and the genetics, not the principle of selection, so the test tests the auxiliary assumptions and leaves selection itself untouched — the same structure she'd point to in any Duhem-Quine complaint. Osei should answer that this is true of every theory worth having and that the demand for a principle that is falsifiable all by itself is a demand no science can meet, including physics. And Vera should have a comeback to *that* which isn't just restating herself — maybe she concedes that selection-as-mechanism is testable while holding that selection-as-universal-principle isn't, and asks whether that distinction does any real work or is just a relabeling to save the framework.

A few rules for the dialogue:

- They should actually respond to each other's points, line by line, rather than taking turns delivering monologues. Real back-and-forth, including at least one place where one of them says something like "wait, that's not what I meant" and rephrases.
- Each should concede something real at some point. Not a fake concession that immediately gets retracted.
- Don't resolve it. End on the live disagreement, not on a synthesis or a both-sides-are-right note.
- Don't put words in Popper's mouth verbatim. Paraphrase his positions, but no quotations from him or from any text — I can't check wording and I don't want the sidebar to hang on a phrase that might be apocryphal. Same for citations: no titles, no dates, no reading list. Just the arguments.
- Don't let either of them win by citing authority. If Vera says Popper thought X, Osei should ask what the argument for X is, and vice versa.
- Set it somewhere specific and slightly mundane so it doesn't read like a Platonic dialogue — a conference coffee queue, an airport bar, a hotel breakfast room at 7am. Pick one and stay in it, and let the setting intrude a little (someone's phone, a bad pastry) without it becoming comedy.
- Around 900 words. Keep it tight — these two are smart and a bit impatient with each other.
