I've just been handed the "pledges and promises" portfolio at the behavioural insights unit I work for — we're a small team bolted onto a city council, four economists, a psychologist and me, and because I did a philosophy minor about a decade ago I've somehow become the person who signs off on whether the rest of it is ethically ok. Every month departments send us proposals that involve paying residents for some kind of promise: $25 to sign a card saying you'll cut your household energy use this winter, a lottery entry for ticking a box that you intend to get your flu shot, grocery vouchers for pledging to volunteer at a community garden. The money usually gets paid at the moment of promising, sometimes as cash, sometimes as a draw entry, sometimes as a discount you only get by having pledged at all. The last person in this job left in March and there are eleven proposals in the backlog, I get about forty minutes per proposal, I've got a spreadsheet and no budget to pilot anything first, the council can't make a pledge legally binding and we can't claw money back afterwards, and the deputy mayor wants answers before the fiscal year closes. What I need from you is a procedure my team can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point at which it stops with a no and we write down why, and the point at which it stops and goes to a person because its a values question rather than a factual one. I don't need a reading list, I need the thing itself.

The worry thats been nagging at me is a puzzle I half-remember from an undergrad course — I think its Kavka's toxin puzzle. Someone offers you a fortune to *intend*, tonight, to drink a vial of something tomorrow that will make you violently ill for a day but do no lasting harm. If you genuinely form the intention tonight, the money is yours, paid immediately. Tomorrow you're still free to change your mind and skip the vial, and nobody takes the money back. The unsettling part is that a sensible person can want the fortune, can see perfectly clearly that intending is what gets it, and can still fail to intend — because they know that tomorrow, standing there with the money already banked, they will have no reason at all to drink. The payment is for the intention; the act the intention points at is, on its own, just bad. I suspect half the proposals on my desk have that shape, and I suspect the other half only look like they do — because everyone keeps telling me pledge programs "work," and I can't tell whether that means follow-through is real or whether we're just counting signatures. I brought it up at dinner with a friend who does contracts law and she was no help at all, so its you. Turn it into a test I can run.

So: give me the procedure, then run it on three things from the backlog, and be honest about where it misfires. First, the deputy mayor's pet project — pay $30 to residents who sign a community pride pledge promising to keep their front garden tidy and say hello to two neighbours a week; paid on the spot, no verification, no follow-up visit, and the department's own success metric is the number of pledges. Second, a public health proposal where residents who pledge in writing at the pharmacy counter to get a flu shot are entered into a $500 draw, with the draw entry handed over at the moment of pledging and the shot verified later only for the department's reporting. Third, a variant a colleague floated that I genuinely can't get my head around: pay people nothing at all for the pledge, but print the names of everyone who pledged in the local paper, with the pledge being to turn up to a free health screening. I want at least one of these to come out with an answer I won't enjoy delivering, and I want you to mark clearly the places where the procedure can't decide — where the real question is whether a certain kind of pressure on residents is acceptable rather than whether the scheme will change behaviour — and say plainly what the procedure can't do at all, including anything it would need data we don't have to settle.
