Commercial aquaculture applies sharply different standards to the welfare of fish and to that of crustaceans. Fish raised for food are increasingly subject, in many jurisdictions, to rules requiring that they be rendered insensible before slaughter, and slow asphyxiation in an ice slurry or death in carbon-dioxide-saturated water is widely condemned as inhumane. Shrimp and prawn hatcheries, by contrast, routinely induce spawning by ablating the eyestalk of broodstock females — pinching, cutting, or cauterizing it off — and crustaceans are commonly killed by chilling, boiling, or desiccation. Even where the law has begun to acknowledge that decapod crustaceans can suffer, hatchery and kitchen practice appears to have changed very little.

What theory of mind, or of the conditions under which a creature can be wronged, does this bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account as fully as you can. Is the practice committed to a particular claim — that suffering requires a centralized brain with cortex-like integrative structures, or vertebrate-type nociceptors — or is the distinction compatible with a range of views about what makes something a subject of welfare? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then take up the rival account, on which the capacity to suffer is fixed by functional and behavioral criteria rather than by lineage — avoidance learning, trade-offs between noxious stimuli and competing goals, modulation of responses by analgesic or opioid-like mechanisms — and say what would have to change, in regulation and in daily practice, if that account were adopted. Finally, ask whether the bifurcation is better explained by a genuine theoretical commitment than by administrative convenience, the historical reach of animal-welfare statutes, or the commercial interests at stake — and whether, once those factors are discounted, it still makes sense to say the practice presupposes a theory of sentience at all.
