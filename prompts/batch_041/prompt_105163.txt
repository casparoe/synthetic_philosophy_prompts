I've been reading about vagueness again lately, partly because a side project of mine needs a defensible story about borderline cases and partly because the sorites keeps nagging at me, and I've noticed that almost every treatment I find rushes straight to defending a preferred formalism — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism, the nihilist line that nothing is really vague — before anyone has said what a theory in this area is even supposed to accomplish. Before I read another one, I want the specification itself. Write me the desiderata any adequate theory of vagueness must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the pre-theoretic judgments it is not allowed to simply declare mistaken. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Some candidates I already suspect belong on the list: the apparent validity of the sorites step (the heap, the beard); the intuition that there is no sharp point at which, say, red gives way to orange; higher-order vagueness, the fact that borderline cases themselves seem to come in borderline cases; the behavior of vague predicates in compounds and comparatives, so that a patch which is a borderline case of red and a borderline case of orange still has to be determinately one or the other rather than both or neither; context-sensitivity, since tall shifts with the comparison class; and the question whether the vague facts can be known at all. Tell me which of these are genuine constraints on a theory and which are merely house rules of one camp, and — this is the part I most want — whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that the demand for no sharp boundaries, the demand that the sorites step come out valid, and the retention of classical logic cannot all be honored at once; if that is right, I want the exercise to end by stating it as its central result.

Then make the list bite on a case of my own. Picture a grid of colored patches varying continuously along two axes, hue and brightness, such that any two adjacent patches — horizontally or vertically — are indiscriminable at a glance, while one corner is unambiguously red and the opposite corner unambiguously green, so that a path of single imperceptible steps carries you from clear red to clear green, and a diagonal path gets there in fewer steps still. A theory of vagueness should tell me, for any patch on that grid, whether it is red, not red, or borderline, and whether any boundary is a determinate or an indeterminate matter — and whether the answer changes when I walk the path in the opposite direction, or when I compare a patch with one only a single step away. Which of my requirements does this case pull apart, and which theories quietly fail it? Finally, run the main families — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree-theoretic accounts, contextualism, and the nihilist option — briefly against the list, and name the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of them. I don't need a full scorecard; the criteria are what I'm after.
