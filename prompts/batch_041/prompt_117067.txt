Escher's Drawing Hands depicts two hands, each drawing the other; the usual gloss is that such a tangled hierarchy is held together by an inviolate level—the level at which the drawing is actually executed, which is not itself depicted as drawn. Write an essay on this controversy: is the inviolate level a genuine structural feature of self-referential systems, or an artifact of describing them in a language that imposes a hierarchy? Your central test case should be the arithmetization of syntax: once a formal system's metalevel is coded into the object level, the old inviolate level seems to be swallowed by the tangle, and a further metalevel must be invoked to interpret the result. Does this regress show that no level is privileged—only an endless ladder, each level inviolate relative to the one below—or does it show that some level must always be taken as uninterpreted and outside the tangle for the whole to mean anything? Weigh both sides, then reach and defend a verdict. If you conclude the inviolate level is relative, say what, if anything, stops the regress; if you conclude it is absolute, say where it lives and why the regress does not reach it.
