Question 3. Illusionists about phenomenal consciousness claim that the felt, qualitative character of experience — what it's like — is itself an illusion manufactured by our introspective systems, rather than a real feature of the mind. In lecture I separated two readings of that claim:
(a) the strong reading: there are no phenomenal properties at all, and our introspective judgments that we have qualia are straightforwardly false;
(b) the weak reading: there are states with phenomenal character, but they are nothing over and above their functional/representational roles — it's our concept of phenomenal properties as intrinsic, ineffable, private and immediately given that is the illusion.
Assess whether the weak reading really earns the name illusionism or whether it quietly collapses back into realism about phenomenal properties. Then say which reading is better placed to answer the meta-problem — why creatures like us are so disposed to believe in phenomenal properties — and why.

Student answer: Both readings are attacking the same thing — the traditional picture of qualia as intrinsic, ineffable, private and immediately given — and both are physicalist in spirit, which is the standard illusionist line going back to Dennett. The only real difference is how much gets eliminated. On the weak reading, phenomenal properties survive as functional or representational properties, so all that is actually rejected is a certain conception of them. I'd argue the weak reading is still genuinely illusionist, because what the realist about qualia actually cares about is precisely the intrinsic, non-relational feel, and once you've denied that, calling the leftover functional properties "phenomenal properties" is just relabelling. So it doesn't collapse into realism — if anything it shows that the whole dispute is verbal.

The meta-problem is the question of why we have the hard problem at all, i.e. why we're built to find consciousness mysterious. The weak reading is better positioned to answer it, because it can tell a functional story about the introspective representations that generate the intuition while still granting that there is experience there. The strong reading has a harder time, since if there are no phenomenal properties then the introspective reports it leans on are just reports, and a report can't be the evidence for its own falsity. And in any case, solving the meta-problem would basically settle the issue in illusionism's favour, because once you've explained why we believe in qualia without needing them, the realist has nothing left to point to.
