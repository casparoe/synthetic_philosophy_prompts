I am writing a feature on how the vocabulary of social construction has migrated out of the seminar room and into governance, and I would like to pressure-test the premise my draft rests on before I commit to it.

The premise is this: to say that a category such as race, gender, or money is socially constructed is to say that it is not a natural kind but an artifact of collective practice, and what is an artifact of collective practice can be remade by collective decision. Constructedness, on this reading, is what makes a category arbitrary, and arbitrariness is what makes it abolishable — nothing outside our conventions holds it in place. What we made, we can unmake.

My reporting keeps complicating the premise. A city removed race from its intake forms on the ground that the category is a social invention, and the disparities those forms had been used to track did not oblige by disappearing. A cooperative voted to retire its internal scrip after agreeing that the currency was nothing but a convention, and within a season the members were trading it again. The money case seems to cut the other way: nobody disputes that the dollar is a convention, and yet the convention binds as firmly as anything in my life.

So: if these categories are just social constructions, why can they not be abolished by the same agreement that sustains them? When I find a constructed kind refusing to die, am I looking at a fact about the world or a fact about us — inertia, coordination, vested interest? Does the distinction between a category being constructed and a category being arbitrary do any real work, or is it the move academics make when they want the rhetorical force of the first claim without the uncomfortable consequences of the second? And if the distinction is real, what would dismantling a constructed kind actually require, and which of my three cases — race, gender, or money — comes closest to licensing the easy-abolition conclusion I have been assuming?
