I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book set in a made-up port city in the 1620s, and there's a pawnbroker in it — a woman who lends at interest and keeps having to defend herself to a preacher who's convinced she's damned. I want her to have the better arguments, so I've been reading up on where the Christian ban on usury actually comes from, and everything keeps pointing back to Aristotle. A friend of mine did a classics MA and now writes for a magazine, and over dinner the other night he gave me his rundown, which I wrote down on the back of an envelope more or less as he said it. Here it is: "So Aristotle on money. Basically he thinks money is a human invention — it exists by convention, not by nature, which already tells you he's suspicious of it. He draws a line between managing a household, which is natural because it aims at supplying what the household actually needs, and what he calls wealth-getting, which is unnatural because it has no limit, it just aims at more money. Trade is the obvious case: swapping goods to satisfy need is fine, but buying in order to sell at a profit is a perversion, because the point of a shoe is to be worn, not to be a vehicle for profit. And the worst version is lending at interest: interest is money begetting money, the most unnatural thing there is, since money was invented to be a medium of exchange and not to reproduce itself. His real objection to usury is that it's exploitation — the lender grows rich off the borrower's need without contributing anything to the goods being exchanged — and that's why it's the direct ancestor of every later attack on finance, the medieval church's ban, Marx on interest-bearing capital, all of it. The one soft spot is that in the Ethics he notices that exchange requires the two things being swapped to be commensurable, and since he can't see what makes a bed commensurable with a house, he falls back on money as the thing that measures value. That's a bit of a fudge, and it's why he never really solves the problem of what value is — he's stuck because he doesn't have the concept of demand."
