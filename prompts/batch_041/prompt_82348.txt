For the last few months I've been sitting in on a fortnightly discussion group that a friend runs out of his flat — eight or nine of us, mostly graduate students in biology and philosophy, and the standing rule is that whoever brought the paper has to keep quiet for the first twenty minutes while everyone else tears into it. A couple of weeks ago the topic was whether culture is a Darwinian process at all, and two of the regulars, Devi and Owen, spent most of the evening going at each other while the rest of us worked through the biscuits. I'd like someone to sort out what actually happened, because I came away unable to tell whether they genuinely disagreed or were talking past each other.

Devi's line was this. A Darwinian explanation needs more than variation, differential persistence, and some statistical resemblance between one generation and the next; it needs that resemblance to be there because information was copied down a lineage. But almost nothing in culture is copied in that way. When I learn a joke from you, what I end up with is largely a product of my own memory, my own sense of what's funny, and conventions we already share, with your telling acting as a nudge rather than a template. Owen said that's a difference in fidelity, not in kind — genes are only statistically heritable too, and a leaky channel is still a channel; weak heritability means weak selection, not no selection. Devi said that misses the point: where reconstruction does the work, two people resemble each other because they share a mind, not because they share an ancestor, and if shared descent isn't doing any explaining, then calling the process Darwinian is decoration. Owen then put a point about selection not having to favour the host. A practice can spread because it is good at getting itself passed on, even when it costs the people who carry it; that, he said, is the whole interest of treating cultural items rather than people as the units, and nothing about reconstruction touches it. Devi answered that this only works if there is a lineage to select among. If learners reliably re-derive the practice from a fragment, what you have is a fixed point of the reconstruction process, not a lineage — and the two stories predict different things, because the fixed-point story predicts convergence from unrelated starting points, which is the opposite of what a descent story leads you to expect. Owen said convergence is just a steep landscape with a single peak, and evolution does that constantly. Devi said only where you already have transmission, and in the convergence case there is none: she had seen the same joke turn up independently in two office kitchens with no contact between them, and each time it settled into the same short, punchy form. Owen said fine, then descent supplies the initial conditions and reconstruction supplies the dynamics, and that's a division of labour rather than a refutation — she was asking the Darwinian framework to be the whole story when it only ever claimed a part of one. She said that if a framework needs a second framework bolted on before it can say anything about the interesting cases, that is worth saying out loud.

Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about which specific claim. For each move I'd like a verdict — right, wrong, or right but overstated — with a brief justification. Then say whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal: whether they are using heritability, selection, and descent in the same senses, and where a word is quietly shifting under them. Finally, tell me who has the better of it on the balance, what both of them are missing (including anything they agree on that they shouldn't), and whether any of their disputed claims could be settled by a concrete observation or experiment rather than by more argument — if so, what that observation would be. Don't split the difference to be safe. Where you think one of them is simply mistaken, say so plainly and give your reasons; where you are genuinely unsure, say what would move you.
