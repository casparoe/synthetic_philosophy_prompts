Some context about who is asking. I am a novelist, and for the past several years I have sat on the planning board of a mid-sized American city. I spend my working life imagining how people live inside decisions that are made about them, which is probably why this case has gotten under my skin.

The case: a developer proposes to replace a surface parking lot and a shuttered mid-century social hall with a seven-story, roughly 240-unit apartment building — 15 percent of the units deed-restricted affordable for forty years, ground-floor retail, no parking minimums. The neighborhood is a historically working-class district that is appreciating fast. Opposition is led by the neighborhood association, and a substantial part of it comes from long-time residents, including elderly homeowners and a tenants' group, who fear that the building's new amenities and new arrivals will raise land values and rents and push them out — even though the project adds supply, and even though many of the opponents concede that abstractly.

Two wrinkles matter. First, our city recently adopted a pilot "neighborhood consent" provision: a recognized association may pass a resolution of objection, which is not a veto but obliges the board to apply a standard of heightened scrutiny, under which we must find that the project's public benefit outweighs the harms the neighborhood identifies. The ordinance deliberately leaves "outweighs" undefined, and the association has now passed such a resolution. Second, a community land trust has submitted a competing proposal for the same site — permanently affordable co-op housing — but it cannot match the price the developer holds under contract.

One commissioner has floated a condition I'd like you to engage with seriously: approval should require the developer to fund a neighborhood-governed "stability levy" — money that would cover long-time owners' property-tax increases, endow a tenant right of first refusal, and finance an elected neighborhood assembly with real design authority over the ground floor. I am genuinely uncertain whether this is an honest attempt to share power or a purchase of consent, i.e., whether it converts a claim of right into a price and whether that is corrupting or simply how democratic bargains actually work.
