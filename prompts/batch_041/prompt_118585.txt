I've been reading a lot of the old archaeological-theory wars in my spare time (I did a archaeology degree years back, then defected to data work, and it keeps dragging me back in), and I want to make a decision tree for a small reading group — half ex-archaeologists, half rationalists who've never held a trowel — that takes someone from "you've got a pit full of objects" to a named position on how far you can legitimately get from things to minds. The row I keep having with my mate is that the standard versions of this debate get bogged down in -isms and labels, so I want the thing built the other way round: questions first, positions located afterwards in the space of answers. My angle, which I think is the actually interesting bit, is to split the target of inference into four separate things — what bodies did (behaviour), what was expected or permitted (norms), what people took the world to be like (beliefs), and what the practice meant to them (meaning) — and then make every branch node a question about which of those you're after, what licenses the step from object to target, and how much a new find could make you take the answer back.

So: a tree, six to nine nodes, each branch labelled with the question that splits it from its siblings, phrased so a person can answer without already knowing which position they'll land on — no node that just asks "are you a processualist or a post-processualist?", that's cheating. Leaves should carry a name where there is one (ethnographic analogy, middle-range theory, the contextual reading of the record as already meaning-laden, cognitive archaeology's appeal to shared human cognition, the symmetry-minded line that objects aren't merely evidence of people, and the flat refusal to talk about past beliefs at all), plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment each has to swallow. Where your tree leaves a combination unoccupied, say so and give a one-line diagnosis — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? — and flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate, I'd much rather have that stated than fudged. It has to handle at least two cases: a bog hoard of unworn bronze axes that were plainly never meant to be swung, and a house floor with everything left where it sat as if the people just walked out. Don't bother with a reading list or references, I'll bung those in myself.
