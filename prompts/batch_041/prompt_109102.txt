I am a journalist who has spent much of the last decade reporting on archaeology, and for a couple of years now I have been keeping a private file of a move that I have never seen named, which I keep trying to explain to a friend who is a field archaeologist and who tells me, a little impatiently, that I am simply describing sloppy practice. The move concerns how excavators get from objects to beliefs and institutions. A pit holds a deliberately bent sword, three dog skulls, and a quern snapped in half; the report calls the assemblage ritual. A pit a few valleys away, from the same century, holds an intact quern, a whole pot, and the skeleton of a goat; the report calls it domestic refuse. So far this is only the familiar habit of giving the strange a ceremonial label. What I keep noticing is the second half of the move. The ritual label then comes back as the explanation of the facts that provoked it, the sword was bent because the rite required a killed weapon and the pit was dug that way because the ceremony demanded it, and, more troublingly, the label can absorb almost anything found later: a comparable pit with no metal in it at all simply becomes a different rite. The same shape turns up with votive, ceremonial, symbolic and foundation deposit, and with buildings rather than pits, where the largest structure on a site is called a temple and the scale of the structure is then cited as evidence of an organized priesthood. I have noticed that the labels behave this way when the subject is belief or institution and hardly ever when the subject is diet, climate or trade, where a failed prediction seems to cost the hypothesis something.

What I want to know is whether there is an established name for this, not for unfalsifiable interpretation in general, which I take to be old news, but for this particular figure: a residual term, defined largely by what it is not, whose application grows more confident as the evidence fits a practical account less well, and which then turns around and serves as the explanation of its own trigger. If there is a term of art for a label that works this way, I want to know precisely what it covers and what it leaves out; if the nearest existing thing is the standard warning that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, I want to know why that warning seems too weak here, since in the cases I collect the absence is being read as positive evidence for a variant of the very belief in question. I would also like the relation spelled out to the respectable version of the same manoeuvre, in which a living people is shown to bend swords at funerals and the pit is thereupon pronounced ritual, and to whatever it is that makes the label a genuine hypothesis in the good cases, when the invocation of ritual actually forbids something rather than merely filling a gap.

Please do not simply hand me the name. I need the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are, so that when I read my next excavation report I can tell whether I am looking at the real thing or only at its shadow. Write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you would write a letter.
