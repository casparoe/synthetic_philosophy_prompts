I teach a 300-level philosophy of science seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute meeting a week — and I'm midway through a unit on natural kinds. My students can all recite the greatest hits (water and H2O, the periodic table, the species problem, the fight about race and medicine) and I'm bored stiff, because I don't think they feel the problem at all, they just know the vocabulary. Last week I tried a debate on whether species are real and it collapsed into everyone agreeing that it "depends on your purposes," which is exactly the answer I don't want them to be able to give so cheaply. So what I want from you is a class-long activity — not a lecture, not a debate — where they build something with their hands and get burned by it before I say a single word about joints or pluralism. Take the role of someone who does both philosophy of science and classroom game design; start by brainstorming two or three possible spines for the activity and telling me why you'd pick one over the others, then write up the winner properly.

Here's what it has to do. They have to produce a classification under constraints, and the constraints have to come from roles I hand out rather than from me: the same pile of things, several groups, each group with a real purpose of its own, and every purpose has to be legitimate — no obvious crank in the room. The pile shouldn't be the usual specimens; I dont want "here are fifteen animals, make a tree," partly because they've seen it and partly because I want them arguing about something they can't recall a textbook answer to. Two things I need planted in that pile: at least one item every group has to classify the same way no matter which purpose they're serving, so that when someone says "it's all just our interests" in the debrief they have to reckon with the thing that didn't budge; and at least one item that one group treats as a single kind while another splits it in two, where the honest description is not "the boundary is fuzzy" but "there are two different things here being run together." I want them to conflate vagueness with plurality, and then find out they did. Round one has to force exactly one official list per group, signed off by everybody, because the moment they have to commit to a single answer is where the fight gets real. Then round two: they may keep as many classifications as they like, on the condition that each one be defended as non-arbitrary — that's the part I care about most, I want them to feel how expensive pluralism gets once you actually have to pay for it.

Then the practical stuff. Give me the rules in a form my TA could run cold, the materials (index cards, a whiteboard, and roughly zero photocopying budget, so keep it cheap and reusable), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including how long each round should run, and the debrief: map specific moments from what actually happened onto the philosophical moves, and only name names there — I'm thinking Boyd-style property-cluster kinds, Dupré-style pluralism about classification, the species-are-individuals line, whatever else fits, but nothing they need to have read first and nothing that would require me to cite a source. I also want your honest prediction of how this falls apart the first time I run it — I suspect at least one group will game the rules and produce a list that satisfies the letter of their instructions and nothing else — plus what I should do when that happens, and whether to lean into it. Finally, be frank about what the activity can't reproduce: which part of the realism-versus-pluralism issue they'll still only be able to talk about rather than feel, and what I have to supply myself in the last ten minutes. Think carefully about the failure modes, and don't pad it with generic advice about "encouraging discussion."
