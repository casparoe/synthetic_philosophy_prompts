I've been arguing about this with two friends for weeks now and I still can't get the shape of it straight in my head, so I'm writing it out. Here's whats bugging me. The way international law and international bodies actually behave about secession looks, from the outside, like it's holding two things that don't fit together. On one side there's a right of self-determination that supposedly belongs to all peoples -- it's in the UN Charter, in the big 1970 declaration on friendly relations, in the human rights covenants -- and colonial territories got their independence on the strength of it without anyone thinking the colonial power's consent was needed. On the other side, when a group that isn't a former colony wants out, the answer is basically always: not without the parent state agreeing. Scotland got its referendum only because London legislated to hand the Scottish Parliament the power to hold one. Catalonia held one in 2017 and Madrid's position, backed by the constitutional court, was that it was simply void, because the Spanish constitution declares the unity of the Spanish nation indissoluble, and the EU more or less lined up behind that. When Canada's Supreme Court was asked whether Quebec could go it alone, it said no -- unilateral secession is illegal under both Canadian and international law -- though it added that a clear majority on a clear question would create an obligation on the other side to negotiate. And then there's the other half of the pattern that I only noticed recently: when the empires came apart, the new states mostly kept the old colonial administrative lines, even where those lines cut a language group or a tribe in half, and international bodies have treated those lines as the legally real ones (the doctrine is called uti possidetis, I had to look it up, the usual justification being that any other rule would mean permanent war over every border). Meanwhile the same bodies will cheerfully describe internal autonomy, or just being able to vote in the existing state's elections, as a people exercising its self-determination.

So what I'm staring at is a practice that seems to deny both of the obvious theories at once. It denies that a majority of the people living in a territory can change their sovereign just by voting -- no consent of the governed. And it also denies that a nation has a claim to a state of its own just because it's a nation with a language and a history. So what does it actually presuppose instead? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory. Start with who counts as the relevant self: does the practice presuppose that the people who hold the right are defined by the existing state's territory and its colonial pedigree, rather than by culture or by consent? Then the theory of territory underneath it -- is this a property-like picture where the state owns the land and nobody can take it without the owner's agreement, or something more like a fiduciary or trust picture where the state holds the territory for the inhabitants and can lose it by betraying them, and does the practice actually need one of those rather than the other? And what does it mean to say a people has already exercised its self-determination by being allowed to vote in the state's elections -- what view of the right does that presuppose, and can a right be discharged once and for all like that? I also want to know whether the colonial/non-colonial line presupposes that self-determination is a one-time remedy for a particular historical wrong (overseas empire) rather than a standing right that any people holds at any time, and if so whether the practice can even state that principle out loud without embarrassment.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: tell me which rival views the practice actually rules out and which it merely happens to fit, since I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined and would be licensed by several theories. Is there one coherent view behind the whole package, or is this an unstable compromise between a statist theory (borders change only when the incumbent says so, full stop) and a liberal one (legitimacy requires the ongoing consent of the governed)? And if you swap in one of those clean views instead, what concretely changes -- what would courts, referendum rules, the EU, the UN have to do differently if we took a straight consent-based view, and what would change under a straight statist view where there is no international right to secede at all? Be honest about which bits of the practice you have to give up either way.
