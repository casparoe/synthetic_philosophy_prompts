I am in a running disagreement with a colleague who reads a good deal of Spengler, and he keeps resting his case on one comparison: a civilization is an organism. It is born, it grows, it ripens, it ages, it dies. The West, on his telling, is somewhere in its late autumn or its winter, and the decline he anticipates is therefore a natural process, like growing old, which no amount of policy, reform or technology can do more than postpone at the margins. The comparison is not decoration for him; it is what makes the conclusion feel forced. It is why he treats the decline as inevitable, why he thinks talk of reviving a civilization is a category mistake, and why he thinks we should stop treating the present as a choice. I would like the comparison audited rather than waved away.

Begin with the correspondence table. In his picture, what plays the role of birth, of growth, of maturity, of aging, of death? What plays the role of the species with its fixed lifespan? What corresponds to the organs, to the genes, to the immune system, to the physician? Then say where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into those that matter for the conclusion he draws and those that do not. Tell me what the source quietly imports that the target may lack: a lifespan fixed by the species rather than by circumstance; a single moment of death; sharply individuated members that neither overlap nor reproduce; parts with no access to the diagnosis; an organism that cannot read a prognosis about itself and then act on what it has read. On that last point, say whether the fact that the participants in a civilization can read the analogy and adjust their behaviour weakens his case or strengthens it. Then press on whether the West is an individual of the relevant kind at all, or whether the analogy manufactures the unit it then applies itself to.

Keep the other deployments of the same picture in view as a control. Polybius's anacyclosis runs the constitutional forms around a cycle and treats the movement as natural. Ibn Khaldun builds the rise and fall of dynasties on group solidarity, which is strongest at the nomadic edge and decays over roughly three generations, the cycle closing when a fresh group arrives from the periphery with solidarity intact. Toynbee insists that civilizations break down from within, through the failure of a creative minority that has stopped being creative, and he means this as a diagnosis of failure rather than a sentence of fate. If the organic analogy were really carrying the fatalist conclusion, why does it license something different in these hands? Consider also the older and related use of the same picture, the polity as a body whose members must not withhold their service, since a body cannot survive the secession of its parts, and say whether the disanalogies that sink my colleague's fatalism sink that use too, or whether it fares better.

Close with a verdict: what this analogy can and cannot carry, and, if there is a better comparison for whatever is actually going on, an ecosystem with succession and abrupt regime shifts, an aging that medicine can slow, a language, a fire, say what it is and what conclusion it would license in place of his.
