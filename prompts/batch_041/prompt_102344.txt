I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've spent the last few evenings arguing with two friends — one a grad student in epistemology, the other a product manager — about something that started as a joke about our navigation app. The app routes you across the country flawlessly, and from there we got onto the old point that someone who merely has a correct opinion about the road to Larissa will get there just as reliably as someone who actually knows the way, and yet none of us could shake the feeling that the two are not on a par, that the one who knows has something the other lacks. That widened into the general question of why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief at all — the Meno problem, as I gather it's called — and I've noticed that every answer we try seems to buy one intuition at the price of another. Before I read any more arguments, I want the specification laid out first: what exactly must a theory of the value of knowledge deliver in order to count as adequate? Please take the role of an expert epistemologist who has thought hard about this exact question, and think carefully before writing — brainstorm the candidate requirements first, then prune them down.

Treat this as a design-spec exercise, the way I'd approach writing an API contract: I want the full list of desiderata, and for each one the case or consideration that motivates it. That means the phenomena the theory has to explain (for instance, why knowledge is prized even when a true belief would serve action just as well; why we treat someone who knows as a more trustworthy source than someone who is merely, accidentally, right; why inquiry seems to aim at knowledge rather than at true belief alone), the distinctions it must not collapse (between the value of a belief's being true and the value of the state of knowing; between being valuable and being more valuable; between what makes a belief true and what makes it knowledge), and any result or platitude it cannot contradict — including the familiar worry that if truth is the sole source of a belief's value, then nothing added to a true belief could raise its value, so that the very idea of knowledge being worth more looks impossible. Be explicit about which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, and flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; that tension is the part I care about most, since I'd rather have the conflict identified than papered over.

Then, briefly, say how the main families of response do against the list — I've vaguely heard of the idea that knowledge's value is different in kind rather than greater in amount, and of the idea that what's really valuable is the concept or the ability to know, but don't assume I know the labels, so explain as you go. Keep the emphasis on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on who said what, write it so a non-philosopher can follow the reasoning without a glossary, and skip any bibliography or citations — I can't look anything up and don't want guesses at sources. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the one requirement that rules out the most otherwise plausible theories.
