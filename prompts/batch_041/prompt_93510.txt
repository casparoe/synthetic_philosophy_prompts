For the better part of a year I have been writing a novel set in Dublin in the 1730s, and my protagonist — a printer's widow who has taken over her late husband's press — has fallen in with a circle that reads and argues over the philosopher George Berkeley's *The Querist*, the long series of questions Berkeley first published in 1735 on money, credit, and the Irish economy. What has lodged in my mind, and what I want to get right on the page, is the analogy at the heart of the thing. Berkeley asks whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," and in a later query proposes that "gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof." The work this comparison is made to do is considerable. If money is merely a ticket, then it is not itself wealth; wealth is the power to command the labour and industry of others; gold and silver carry no value intrinsic to them; and a nation may therefore issue credit — paper included — to stir up its industry rather than hoard bullion. My characters find this liberating, and one of them has begun to talk as though a state could print its way to prosperity. I suspect the ticket is being made to carry more than it can bear, but I cannot yet say precisely where the load exceeds the beam.

I would like you to audit the analogy for me the way a good structural engineer audits a bridge: not to pronounce it worthless, but to say exactly which loads it holds and which it does not. Tell me, first, what corresponds to what — what plays the role of the ticket, the box office, the performance, the seat, the doorkeeper, the money taken back at the end of the night — and be explicit about the table of correspondences, since that is where my confusion starts. Then show me where the mapping breaks: the respects in which money is unlike a ticket or a counter, and, just as important, the features of the ticket-and-counter world that Berkeley quietly imports into his picture of money even though money lacks them. After that, sort the fractures into those that genuinely threaten the conclusion he wants — that money is not real wealth, that bullion is dispensable, that a nation may run on issued credit — and those that are merely picturesque and do not touch it. I am happy to be told that some of my worries are pedantic; I only want to know which ones are not. Close by saying whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not survive intact, sketching the analogy you would reach for instead.

Please set out your answer under five headings, in this order and at roughly these lengths: "The Ticket and What It Is Doing" (about 150 words, restating the analogy and the conclusion it is meant to license); "The Correspondence" (about 250 words, with a table mapping the ticket-world onto the money-world); "Where the Ticket Tears" (about 350 words, the disanalogies, including what the analogy smuggles in); "Which Tears Matter" (about 200 words, separating the disanalogies that bear on Berkeley's intended conclusion from those that do not); and "Verdict, and a Better Ticket" (about 200 words, on whether the conclusion survives and what analogy would serve better).
