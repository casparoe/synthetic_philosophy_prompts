Please explain a conceptual issue in the theory of epistemic injustice that I find genuinely unresolved: the relation between its structural and its agential dimensions. The familiar picture separates a hearer's failure to grant a speaker credibility — an interpersonal wrong, locatable in a particular exchange — from a gap in the shared interpretive resources that leaves a speaker unable to render her own experience intelligible even to herself. My difficulty is that the two look less independent the more closely I examine them: the hearer's withholding of credibility is itself sustained by the same background resources that generate the interpretive gap, and the gap persists only through countless individual acts of withholding. So please do the conceptual work rather than the exegetical work. Set out what must be true of concepts, of speakers, and of hearers for the two dimensions to be genuinely distinct; give the strongest reason for thinking the agential dimension collapses into the structural one; then assess a criterion one might propose for keeping them apart — namely, that a structural failure cannot be repaired by any single hearer, because the requisite concept is not yet in circulation, whereas an agential failure could be repaired by the hearer alone. Does that criterion survive scrutiny? And what does its fate imply about which of the two is the more basic category? I want the anatomy of the distinction, not a survey of the literature or a list of readings. A single illustrative case is welcome if it earns its place, but the weight should fall on the analysis.
