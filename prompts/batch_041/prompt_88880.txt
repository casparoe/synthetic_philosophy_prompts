I'm a novelist — fourth book, and my first one with a real historical setting (a small port city, roughly the 1890s, though I'm making up most of the people in it). I'm about two thirds through and I've noticed something worrying: I keep switching between three completely different relationships to the past and I have no idea which one each scene wants. Sometimes I'm writing a scene because some old figure was magnificent and I want my reader to feel that magnificence and take courage from it. Sometimes I'm lovingly cataloguing how people kept their accounts, what was in the pantry, what the harbour sounded like at four in the morning, purely because I've spent two years learning it and I can't bear to throw it away. And sometimes I'm writing to put a period on trial — to show that a thing my characters treat as natural and eternal was in fact made by people who could have made it otherwise, and that it was rotten.

It only occured to me recently that this is basically the three ways Nietzsche says we can do history for the sake of life — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and that he thinks each one has a disease it slides into if you don't watch it. Which is very flattering to me, except I have no idea how to turn that into something I can actually use at the desk, at 11pm, on a Tuesday, when I've got a scene half-written and I'm not sure if I'm writing it for the right reason.

So: give me a procedure. A reusable one, that I run per scene or per historical element before I commit to it — questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and where the thing stops with a no and tells me to cut it. Where the procedure reaches a question that's genuinely about what kind of book I want to write rather than about the material, it should say so and hand it back to me instead of pretending to decide. I'd like it to run in ten minutes, tops. I'm not a philosopher and I'm not going to re-read the essay while I'm drafting. My editor sees the manuscript in March and I'd like to be able to defend each choice in one sentence, so build in whatever it takes to produce that sentence.

Then run it on three things that are actually in my draft:

- A battle scene I'm very attached to, where a character's grandfather is at his finest. I think this is my best writing in the book. I suspect you're going to tell me something I won't like about it.
- A chapter that is basically a long inventory of a household — objects, recipes, the way people addressed their servants — that I love and that does almost nothing to move anything forward.
- A subplot where I'm using a real institution of the period to show my characters that its cruelties were contingent and changeable, which I now worry is me delivering a verdict rather than telling a story.

For each, tell me which mode the procedure thinks I'm in, whether that's the mode the scene needs, and what the procedure says to do — and where it just shrugs. And at the end, be honest about what this procedure can't do: Nietzsche was talking about a culture keeping itself healthy, not about a novel, and I don't want to kid myself that his categories fit my problems more neatly than they do.
