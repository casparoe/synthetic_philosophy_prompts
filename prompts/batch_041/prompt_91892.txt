I'm in a decision-theory reading group that has spent the last few sessions on games whose expected values misbehave, and I've invented one of my own that I'd like you to reason about. It's played with a coin that lands heads with probability q and tails otherwise, tossed until the first heads appears; if the first heads falls on toss n, the payout is (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n divided by n, and if the coin never lands heads the payout is zero. With a fair coin (q = 1/2) the expected payout is the alternating harmonic series, which converges but only conditionally, so the game's expectation is not well defined — the positive and negative parts of the payout each have infinite expectation, and by rearranging the outcomes you can make the series of expected contributions come out to any real number you please. What interests me is that nudging the coin's bias upward tames the game completely: when q exceeds 1/2 the terms shrink geometrically, the expectation becomes an ordinary finite, order-independent number, and the well-defined value approaches a definite limit as q decreases back down toward 1/2 from above. My own calculation gives the value at bias q as q/(1-q) times the natural logarithm of (3 - 2q), whose limit as q tends to 1/2 from above is the natural logarithm of 2 — precisely the value you get by summing the alternating series in its written order; do check that algebra before you rely on it.

I want you to argue for a specific verdict: argue that the unbiased game's expectation is genuinely undefined, and that the well-defined values of its biased cousins converging on the natural logarithm of 2 is an artifact of the limiting procedure rather than a discovery about the unbiased game itself. The natural objection you'll need to defuse is that the limiting route never presupposes an ordering of the outcomes — each biased value is order-independent, so the limit looks like a principled, non-arbitrary way to assign the unbiased game a value — and you should also make something of the asymmetry in the bias parameter, since for q below 1/2 the series of expected contributions diverges outright, so the well-defined value does not vary continuously with the bias in any neighbourhood of the symmetric coin. I don't need a survey of the literature; I want the argument itself, laid out step by step and honest about where it is weakest.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, setting out the argument in order, with no introduction and no concluding summary.
