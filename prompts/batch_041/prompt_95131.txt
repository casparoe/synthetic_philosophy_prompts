A reading group I attend has been arguing about partiality, and the slogan one thought too many keeps doing heavy lifting in ways I doubt are consistent. It first surfaced in a discussion of a rescue case: a man whose spouse is drowning alongside a stranger swims to his spouse without first consulting the principle that one should save one's spouse, and someone said that any theory requiring him to entertain that principle before acting would give him one thought too many. That seemed right to me. But the same phrase then reappeared in two arguments I found far less convincing. In the first, a classmate used it to reject any demand that a doctor disclose a conflict of interest before recommending a treatment, on the grounds that the honest doctor simply treats the patient and that deliberating about disclosure is itself one thought too many. In the second, someone used it to reject the practice of asking people to justify their attachments at all, claiming that the request for a justification is the extra thought.

I want a precise map of what the slogan could be claiming, because I suspect these three uses are not the same claim and that only some of them are defensible. Lay out the distinct theses the phrase could express, from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each say what it commits you to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or self-undermining. Pay particular attention to what the allegedly superfluous thought is supposed to be a thought about — the value of the person saved, the permissibility of the act, the need for a reason at all, or merely the timing of deliberation — and to whether the objection is to having the thought, to having to have it, or to its being the thing that moves you. Then say which reading, if any, actually supports the doctor case and which supports the anti-justification case, and whether the slogan can bear all the weight my reading group is putting on it. Present the readings as a numbered menu I can bring back to the group rather than arguing for a single one.
