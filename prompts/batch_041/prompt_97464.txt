For the handout at a small reading group I run, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether the numbers count when lives are at stake — the sort of case where you can either save one person or save five, the five are strangers to one another, and nobody has any special claim on you. I've sat through enough threads on this to be tired of how quickly it turns into a shouting match, so I want the dialogue to be sharper than the threads are. The first speaker holds that there is no reason to save the five that isn't either a purely impersonal consideration no individual could complain about being ignored, or a disguised way of treating the five as a single claimant when they are in fact five separate claimants each with a life to lose; on his view, once you see that, the fair procedure is a lottery giving all six an equal chance, and saving the five outright is an injustice to the one. The second speaker holds that the numbers do count, and I don't want her resting on the thought that more lives is simply more good; have her argue that each of the five has a claim not to be left to die, that each such claim is exactly as strong as the one's claim, and that counting is what respect for the separateness of persons demands rather than what violates it. Neither should be a dummy: each should answer what the other actually said, concede what has to be conceded, and aim at the weak point rather than repeating a slogan.

I'd like them to get into the weeds rather than trade intuitions. Let them press on what a claim is and what it means for one claim to be outweighed by another; on whether the first speaker's view can really be indifferent between a certainty of saving one person and a one-in-six chance for each of six, and if so whether that is a reductio or the whole point. Above all I want the following exchange. If, as the first speaker says, one claim against one claim is a tie, then where exactly does the second speaker get a tipping point? One against two is presumably still a tie by the same reasoning, and one against three, and so on — so she owes an account of what makes five claims outweigh one when four do not, and it had better not be that five simply feels heavier. The first speaker, for his part, should have to say why his lottery isn't itself a procedure that quietly aggregates, given that six equal chances is after all a fact about all six of them, and to say what he thinks is actually wrong with wanting the most lives saved. End with the disagreement sharper than it started, not with a synthesis or a declared winner — I'd rather my group argue about it than be told how it comes out. A page or two of dialogue is plenty.
