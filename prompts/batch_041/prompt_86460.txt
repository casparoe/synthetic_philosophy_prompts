I am presenting next month at a small workshop on the evolution of human cooperation. My paper argues that the handicap principle — the idea that honest signals must be costly to produce — is misapplied in the human sciences because it misidentifies where the honesty comes from. Honest separation, I claim, requires only that the marginal cost of signaling fall differentially across signalers, the condition economists call single-crossing; it does not require that the signaler bear that cost as waste. In the human cases usually offered — ritual feasts, public charity, conspicuous consumption, religious sacrifice — the differential cost is generated less by the signaler's superior capacity to bear waste than by his superior capacity to make others bear it. The costs are offloaded onto kin, subordinates, and commons, and the separating trait is the power to externalize, not the fortitude to suffer. Prepare my question-period sheet.

The audience will be: behavioral ecologists who study nonhuman signaling, who treat the handicap logic as settled and cost as its core; two evolutionary game theorists who will want the formal condition stated exactly and will ask what, if anything, my proposal adds to it; anthropologists and human behavioral ecologists who use costly signaling to explain ritual and religion and who hold most of the empirical cases; and one philosopher of biology interested in whether cost here is a causal or a functional notion.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede at the outset and what to hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
