Right, so I'm a science journalist working on a long read about how we make decisions when we don't know the odds, and I want to present it as a proper dialogue rather than the usual chin-stroking essay. Two people, one pub table, geniunely going at each other.

On one side: a card-carrying subjective expected utility person — Savage, sure-thing principle, the lot — who thinks "ambiguity aversion" is basically a posh name for a muddle, and that a rational agent has a credence for everything. On the other: someone who reckons ambiguity is a distinct attitude from risk, and that picking the option with the best worst case (or simply refusing to trust an unreliable source of odds) is perfectly rational, not a cognitive failure.

Give me the dialogue. I want them to actually respond to each other's moves, not trade monologues, and neither should be a straw man.

Set the scene on three urns. Urn 1: a hundred balls, fifty red, fifty black, you bet blind on a colour. Urn 2: a hundred balls, red and black, proportions unknown to you. That's the classic setup and I know roughly where it goes, so here's where I want you to push it: Urn 3 is like Urn 2, except there's a card on the table giving the exact rule that fixed its contents — a deterministic recipe, fully specified, but one you'd have to sit and crank through for a week to know how many red balls are in there. So the composition of Urn 3 is a plain fact about the world, knowable in principle, just not by you, cheaply.

Does Urn 3 count as ambiguous, or merely uncertain? Does the answer change what a rational person should do? And what does each of them say when the other demands they put a number on it?

Somewhere in there I want the SEU person to press the point that if you won't assign probabilities your choice isn't even well-defined, and the ambiguity person to come back with the sure-thing violation as a reductio, and then for both to have to say something about paying a small fee to peek inside the urns. Also — do they think the disagreement is about the world, or about what's going on in the chooser's head?

Keep it sharp and concrete. No citations, no name-dropping of papers, just the argument.
