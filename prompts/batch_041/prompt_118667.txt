I sit on our jurisdiction's inheritance and gift tax commission. The governing statute is short: a rate schedule, an exemption threshold, and a preamble that names two purposes without ranking them — to prevent the growth of a hereditary propertied class with power over the jurisdiction's institutions, and to secure equality of opportunity among its residents. We hear cases in panels and publish our reasons, so our real law is our own record. Six rulings bear on what follows.

1. A founder left a controlling block of shares in the company he had built to one of his three children, and cash to the other two. We taxed the shares at full value, rejecting the argument that the tax would break up a family enterprise.
2. A decedent left everything to a spouse of forty years who had been a co-owner of most of the assets. We held the transfer exempt, reasoning that no one new had come into the property in any sense that concerned the statute.
3. A decedent left her estate to a hospital foundation whose trustees were drawn from the local medical school, with no family member serving as a trustee and none employed by it. Exempt.
4. A decedent conveyed a portfolio to an irrevocable trust for his grandchildren twelve years before his death, naming an independent trust company as trustee with full discretion over distributions, but reserving to himself the power to substitute assets of equal value and a reversion if the trust failed. We taxed the corpus.
5. A decedent transferred a large sum outright to a son three months before his death, while terminally ill, and his lawyer recorded that he wished to get the money out of the estate. We taxed the transfer.
6. A decedent left a modest bequest to a second cousin he had met twice. Taxed.

The case before us next month has divided the commission. The decedent owned sixty per cent of the voting shares of the jurisdiction's largest private employer and of its only daily newspaper, and left those shares by will to a foundation she had created. The foundation's income is dedicated to scholarships for local students, and its charter forbids any distribution to her descendants. That same charter, however, makes the board self-perpetuating, requires that a majority of the trustees be her descendants, and allows the trustees to set their own compensation and to appoint the company's board.

Predict our ruling. Set out the competing rules our record is consistent with and what each of them predicts here, then give the prediction you would make and your reasons for it. Identify the closest analog among the six rulings and say why it is closer than the others. If we tax the shares, say which of the statute's two purposes has become controlling and what that commits us to in later cases; if we exempt them, do the same. Finally, name the case you would bring next to tell those principles apart.
