I'm teaching E. M. Forster's The Machine Stops in a class on epistemic dependence on AI, and I want a reading of it that does real work in both directions. The story is obviously about people who can't do anything without the Machine, but I don't want the obvious take that tech is bad and unmediated experience is good. Here's the distinction I want you to use: reliance is when you use a tool but could in principle check it or do without it; deference is when you've lost the ability even to formulate the check, so you can't tell whether the output is right and can't repair the thing that produces it. Read the story through that distinction. Where does Forster dramatize deference rather than mere reliance, which scenes, which details? Is Vashti's endless circulation of second-hand ideas the erosion of understanding, or is the real loss somewhere else, like the loss of the ability to tolerate not-knowing or to do anything with your hands? And where does the story resist the reading: does it stack the deck by making the surface and the body into obvious goods, or does it show that the Machine also enabled kinds of understanding that would otherwise be unavailable? End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate about AI dependence that the arguments don't. Also tell me where your reading is most likely to be wrong, and what in the story or in the distinction would change your mind.
