Ok so I've been trying to write this essay on cognitive penetration and I want your honest comments, not just encouragement. Let me give you the argument in outline and a rough draft of the key sections, then tell me what you think.

The claim: the whole debate about whether cognition can penetrate perception is stuck because everybody is arguing about one relation when there are at least three, and the evidence people trot out almost never supports the one they think it does.

The three, as I'm carving them:

1. Content penetration. Cognition changes what the experience represents. The tomato looks redder, the ambiguous figure looks like the face you were primed with, the hill looks steeper when you're tired. The content itself is different.

2. Gain penetration. Cognition doesn't change what the experience represents, it changes how heavily the rest of the system weights it. Same content, different precision or salience. The thing you're looking for isn't seen as more X; it's just that the same X now drives your downstream judgments and actions harder.

3. Report penetration. Nothing about the experience changes at all. What changes is the judgment or the verbal report. The person says it looks different because their beliefs shifted what they're willing to assert, not because anything looked different.

My move: the old memory-color results (the fire-engine red and banana yellow stuff), the priming studies, the hungry-people-find-food-more-appetizing results, the various fear and expertise effects -- I want to argue these at best establish gain or report penetration, and that treating them as content penetration is exactly what generates the pile of contradictory findings. Every time someone replicates a content effect they get a null, and every time they get an effect someone else says it's just response bias. I think that's a symptom, not a series of failures.

Then the positive part: gain penetration is the interesting one, because it's compatible with the content of perception being encapsulated. A Fodor-style module can be right about content and still sit in a system that reweights its output. If gain penetration is real, cognition gets a genuine perceptual role without anyone having to break encapsulation, and a lot of the rhetoric about perception being theory-laden collapses into a much smaller, more defensible claim about how much we lean on what we see.

The test I want to propose: find a case where a cognitive state shifts a detection threshold or a same/different discrimination -- an actual psychophysical measure -- while leaving the represented content fixed. Call it gain-only penetration. I claim if you can show that, you've shown cognition matters perceptually with zero content penetration. If you can't show it, my distinction is doing no work and I should drop it.

Two worries I already have. (a) Is the three-way split actually exhaustive? Could there be a fourth relation I'm ignoring -- penetration of the format or the temporal structure of experience rather than its content or its weight? (b) My test might be circular: any threshold shift could just be redescribed as a content shift (the stimulus literally looks more contrasty), so how do I keep gain and content apart operationally without begging the question?

What I want from you:

- Where is the argument just wrong or overstated? Specifically, do the standard experiments really only show gain or report, or am I strawmanning them to make my carve look clean?
- Is the three-way distinction a real joint in nature or just a verbal redescription of the usual direct/indirect distinction dressed up?
- Is my proposed test diagnostic, or is it circular the way I fear? If it's circular, is there a version that isn't?
- What's the strongest objection to the whole thing that I'm not seeing?
- And seperately: is this interesting, or is it the kind of thing that sounds deep and turns out to be bookkeeping? Be blunt, I'd rather hear it now.
