Exam question:
The simulation argument is often stated as a trilemma: either (1) almost all civilizations at our stage go extinct before becoming posthuman, or (2) almost all posthuman civilizations choose not to run simulations of their ancestors, or (3) we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation. Suppose we accept the argument as establishing this disjunction. Does that dampen fanaticism about the far future—that is, does it give us reason to reduce our estimate of the astronomical value of a long-lasting future, or to be less willing to maximize expected value over astronomical stakes? Write an essay answering this question. Be explicit about which premise of the argument you are relying on and what follows from it.

Student's answer:
The simulation argument shows that we are probably living in a simulation. Since we are probably simulated, the far future of our civilization is not really our future; it is a simulation run by posthuman beings. This dampens fanaticism about the far future because the astronomical value that longtermists assign to the future is based on the real lives of real people. If we are simulated, those future people are not real, so their welfare does not count. Therefore the expected value of working to improve the far future is much lower than longtermists claim.

The argument also dampens fanaticism in a second way. Fanaticism about the far future depends on the idea that there could be a very large number of future beings, perhaps 10^50 or more. But if we are in a simulation, the simulation might be shut down at any time, or it might be limited in duration. So the expected number of future beings is smaller, and the case for maximizing expected value over astronomical stakes is weaker.

However, the simulation argument does not completely destroy longtermism. Even if we are simulated, we might still be able to influence the simulation in ways that affect simulated beings. But since simulated beings are not real, this is not as important as affecting real beings. So the simulation argument gives us a reason to focus on more modest, near-term goals rather than fanatical far-future goals. The argument thus supports a kind of anti-fanaticism.
