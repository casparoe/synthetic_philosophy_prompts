So I'm giving a job talk in three weeks — it's a metaphysics-heavy department, plus one philosopher of language who works on structured propositions and beleif reports, and one historian of early analytic philosophy who pretty much thinks the whole Bradley's-regress industry is a 1900s artifact nobody ever cleaned up. My paper argues something fairly in the weeds, so let me sketch it: I claim the regress is normally run against the wrong target. The standard story is that it kills any relational analysis of instantiation — you need a relation to tie a and F together, then a further relation to tie that relation to a and F, and off you go. What I want to say is that the regress only bites against what I'm calling the *combinatorial* picture of unity, on which a proposition is a collection of items plus a binder, and the binder is itself one of the items in the collection. On my view relations are strongly syncategorematic: not denizens of the domain at all, but modes of predication, so there is no extra item to be tied down. And unity isn't an ingredient of a proposition, it's a status — a proposition is unified just in case it poses a determinate question, so its parts are held together by being answerable-to something rather than by any third thing. So I don't solve the regress, I try to dissolve the demand that generated it. The part I'm actually nervous about is the cost: if unity is just posing-a-question, then two propositions with the same constituents in different arrangements — this thing loving that one, versus that one loving this thing — come out unified in exactly the same way, and my philosophy-of-language colleague is going to want to know what individuates them, because she needs structured contents for attitude ascriptions and for compositionality. There's also a version of the worry where my view makes the *number* of constituents do no work at all.
