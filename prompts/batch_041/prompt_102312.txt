Explain the conceptual issue that arises when historians attribute many causes to a single event, and how it bears on the metaphysical notion of overdetermination. Begin from a puzzle a careful student might raise about a standard historical explanation — for example, that the First World War had many causes, among them the alliance system, nationalist rivalries, imperial competition, and the assassination at Sarajevo. If each of these was on its own sufficient to bring about the war, then removing any one of them would have left the outcome unchanged, and it becomes hard to see how any of them caused it; if none was on its own sufficient, then no single factor caused the war, and the claim that there were many causes looks like a roundabout way of saying there was no cause at all. Either way the familiar explanation seems to dissolve.

Explain what is genuinely at stake. Distinguish overdetermination as it is understood in the metaphysics of causation — two or more independent causal processes, each sufficient to produce the same effect, so that the effect would have occurred even had any one of them been absent — from the looser way historians speak of multiple causes. Address whether the historian is committed to the metaphysician's notion, or whether the appearance of commitment comes from an ambiguity between causes that are sufficient for an outcome and causes that merely contribute to it. Then say what a defensible account of multiple causation looks like: in particular, whether it requires construing a cause as an insufficient but non-redundant part of a sufficient condition, and whether that reconstruction preserves the explanatory force historians want. Consider, finally, whether the overdetermination worry is a genuine problem or an artifact of importing a difference-making criterion of causation into a domain where explanations are often relative to a contrast — why this outcome rather than some other — and characterize what a real case of overdetermination in history would look like and why such cases are rare or contested.
