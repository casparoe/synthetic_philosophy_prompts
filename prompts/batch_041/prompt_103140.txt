My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma can be defused by having the players submit decision programs rather than choices, and I want you to make the case for the position I ended up defending.

First, the setup, so that we do not talk past each other. Two players each submit a program to a referee. Neither program can observe the other's move in advance; the programs are run simultaneously, and their outputs simply are the players' moves. What each program can do is read the other program's source code, which the referee supplies as input, and simulate it. The payoffs are the ordinary Prisoner's Dilemma payoffs: mutual cooperation is better for both than mutual defection, unilateral defection against a cooperator is best of all, and being defected on while cooperating is worst. Call a pair of submitted programs an equilibrium when neither player could have obtained a higher payoff by substituting any other program, holding the other player's submission fixed. The construction I have in mind is the program that cooperates exactly when the other program, run against the first program's own source, returns cooperate, and defects otherwise.

My friend's objection is that this is a conjuring trick. His first charge is that the trick works only because the players are given a chance to commit before the game proper begins, so what we really have is a pre-play or repeated game wearing a one-shot costume, and the original question about what rationality demands in a genuinely single encounter is left untouched. His second charge is that the cooperative program only cooperates with programs already disposed to cooperate with it, so it begs the question rather than answering it.

I want you to argue that he is wrong: that the program-equilibrium construction is a genuine and non-question-begging resolution of the one-shot dilemma, and not a commitment device or a repetition in disguise. Engage the strongest version of his objection rather than a strawman, and say clearly what work the source-code transparency is doing and whether the resolution would survive without it. Then address a wrinkle that occurred to me only after the argument ended. Suppose the two players must each submit a single program that will be used across an entire tournament of different one-shot games, so that neither can tailor a program to the Prisoner's Dilemma specifically. Does the cooperative program still earn its keep under that demand for generality, or does generality push the equilibrium back toward defection? Tell me what the generality requirement does to my argument and whether the resolution survives it.
