I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of science, and I would like a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on whether the patterns described by the special sciences -- and by our everyday mentalistic, biological, and economic vocabulary -- are real. Every reader entering the tree is assumed to accept a common starting point: that a single system can be described truly in more than one vocabulary, and that at least some of those descriptions support genuine prediction rather than mere redescription. The tree should carry them from that starting point to a named position.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors. I want the branch nodes to be the questions that actually separate the views; the positions should then be located in the space of answers those questions allow. The distinctions I care about most, though you may rephrase and reorder them, are these: whether calling a pattern real says anything over and above saying that the pattern supports the compression and prediction we happen to care about; whether there is a fact of the matter about which description compresses best, independent of our interests and purposes; whether a pattern must be grounded in, or realized by, some lower-level goings-on in order to be real; whether the reality of a pattern admits of degrees, and whether a degree view can count as realism at all; whether the physical, design, and intentional stances are independent of one another or nested, so that adopting the intentional stance commits one to the design stance and that to the physical one; whether the availability of a stance is itself a fact about the world or only about us; and whether two incompatible but equally predictive descriptions of the same system can both be true.

At least two nodes should turn on concrete cases rather than general questions. Candidates you may use or replace: whether the temperature of a gas, the boundary of a species, and the decision of a firm exist in the same sense, or in merely analogous senses; and whether a system that is best predicted by treating it as an agent -- a market, an insect colony, a language model -- is thereby shown to contain one.

The leaves should include, at a minimum, and under whatever names you find clearest: a robust realism on which patterns are real and reality comes in degrees; a deflationary view on which real marks nothing beyond predictive usefulness; a realism relativized to a stance; the view that only the fundamental physical description is real and that higher-level patterns are indispensable projections; the view that patterns are all there is and require no further ground; fictionalism about higher-level pattern talk; and one further position that you judge defensible but that currently has no settled name. For each leaf, give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Phrase every node so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position the answer leads to. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say why it cannot. Note the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the instrument to roughly six to eight nodes and no more than eight leaves, and make it fit on two sides of a handout.
