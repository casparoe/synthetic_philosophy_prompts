The discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation is the phenomenon whereby a group whose members each hold individually consistent sets of judgments can nevertheless arrive at a collectively inconsistent set when it decides each proposition separately by majority vote. The standard illustration involves three propositions — A, B, and C — with C stipulated to hold just in case both A and B hold, and three judges whose votes run as follows: the first accepts A, B, and C; the second accepts A and rejects B and C; the third rejects A and accepts B and C. Majority voting then yields A accepted, B accepted, and C rejected, so the collective set is inconsistent even though each individual set is consistent. The two familiar repairs are premise-based aggregation, in which the group votes on A and B and derives C, and conclusion-based aggregation, in which it votes on C alone and leaves A and B undecided.

Now consider a variant that I stipulate rather than draw from any source. The three judges agree on A and B — each accepts both — but they disagree about the connecting rule itself: the first holds that C requires both A and B, the second that C requires at least one of them, and the third that C requires A alone. Compare this stipulated variant with the standard discursive dilemma. I want a genuine comparison rather than a summary of either: are the two the same problem in different clothing, or does the disagreement shift to a different level — from the premises to the inference — with consequences for how the problem should be framed? Address in particular whether the standard repairs survive once the connective is contested, whether premise-based aggregation even has determinate content in that case, and what the variant exposes that the standard formulation conceals. Close by taking a stand on whether the variant is a distinct puzzle or merely a restatement of the original.
