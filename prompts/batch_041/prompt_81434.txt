(2) Reason itself claims authority: it claims the right to judge all other claims and to bind anyone who would be rational.
(3) By (1), reason's own claim to authority must have adequate justification.
(4) But any attempt to justify reason must either use reason to justify reason (circular), appeal to something outside reason (which would then be an unjustified authority, contradicting (1)), or launch an infinite regress of justifications.
(5) There is no fourth option.
(6) So the Enlightenment's demand cannot be satisfied by the Enlightenment's own standard.
(7) Its authority therefore rests on a circle, an unacknowledged dogma, or an endless deferral—none of which is adequate justification.
(8) An authority that cannot justify itself by its own standard, yet presents itself as universal and rational, is the structure of the unjustified domination the Enlightenment set out to abolish.
(9) Hence the Enlightenment is not the remedy for domination but a disguised instance of it.

If this argument is sound, it seems to convict every critical inquiry, science included, since each rests on ultimate standards it cannot justify without circularity or regress. I would rather keep (1) and (6) than (9), and I am not frightened by regress arguments as such—I spent years on the problem of justifying induction. But I suspect I am missing something.
