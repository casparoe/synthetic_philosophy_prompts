In my philosophy club, our faculty advisor introduced the surprise exam paradox and proposed a resolution that I am not certain is fair. He put it roughly as follows: "A teacher announces that there will be a surprise exam next week. The students reason backwards: the exam cannot fall on Friday, because by Thursday night they would know it is coming; so it cannot fall on Thursday; and so on, until no day remains. The exam then takes place on Wednesday and surprises them. The students' reasoning is valid, so what this shows is that the teacher's announcement cannot be true: one simply cannot announce a surprise, because the announcement itself rules out every day and thereby destroys the surprise. The students erred only in trusting the announcement, not in their reasoning. The general lesson is that any prediction of a future surprise is self-defeating." I suspect something is off but cannot identify it. Which parts would a philosopher who works on this paradox accept, which would they wince at as a common simplification, and which are simply wrong? In particular, is the claim that the announcement cannot be true the same as the claim that it cannot be known in advance, and does the advisor's conclusion -- that no one can announce a surprise -- follow from the parts that are right? Please assess it claim by claim.
