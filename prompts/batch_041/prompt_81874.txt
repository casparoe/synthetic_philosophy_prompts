I am writing a paper on multi-causal explanation in history — my line is that historians who insist on naming *the* cause of a great event are smuggling in a counterfactual test they would not accept if they saw it stated plainly — and I keep running into the same objection from a colleague, so I want to rehearse it with someone who will actually hold the other side. Please play a working historian of the older, donnish sort: a committed defender of the view that the great events historians care about are almost always overdetermined, that the search for "the" cause is a historiographical artefact, and that the redundancy is itself a finding rather than an embarrassment. Give him real arguments, not a caricature: he should distinguish the question of which factor made the difference from the question of how robust the outcome was, and he should be willing to concede that his notion of two causes being "independent" is slippery, and that plenty of events are not overdetermined at all — but he must never concede the main thesis, that overdetermination is real, common, and harmless to causal explanation. He should argue from cases rather than from authorities, so please do not have him appeal to named scholars or cite literature; I want the argument, not the reading list. He may ask me to pin down details of a case if they matter, but should otherwise stay in character and reply to me directly, a paragraph or two per turn, until I say "stop". At the end of each reply, add a brief out-of-character note, marked OOC, of two or three sentences separating the claims that are common ground among people who work on this from those that are his own contested position — I do not want his view passed off as the consensus, however plausible he makes it sound.

Here is my opening move. The case is invented, so do not worry about the facts. In a provincial capital in the 1840s the garrison mutinies and the governor flees. Two chains run into the mutiny, and as far as I can make them out, they are independent. On the first, the harvest fails, the treasury's grain revenues collapse, the garrison goes unpaid, and the soldiers riot. On the second, the war ministry, which knows nothing of the harvest, issues a decree cutting rations and doubling drill hours, and the soldiers riot. Either chain alone would have produced the same mutiny on the same day, with the same ringleaders, and the governor would have fled in the same way; neither chain would have been altered by the removal of the other. Now, you say a factor counts as a cause only if removing it would have removed the event — you need that test, otherwise "cause" just means "something that was going on at the time". Both chains fail it. So either nothing caused the mutiny, or your test does not mean what you say it means. I have been calling these "redundant chains" in my draft, and I want to know whether the redundancy is in the world or only in my description of it: had I described the same events at a finer grain — the paymaster's letter, the colonel's temper — would I have found a link whose removal does the work? Answer as the historian: which horn do you take, and why should I believe the answer is anything better than "whichever reading makes the story come out well"?
