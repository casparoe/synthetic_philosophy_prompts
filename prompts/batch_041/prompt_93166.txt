I'm writing a short story for a friend's reading group, and the theme she's given me is reasons without reasoners — the idea that some bit of behaviour can genuinely be there in order to do X, or for the sake of X, when nothing anywhere wanted X. The stock illustration: something chews on a plant, the plant starts putting out a volatile that pulls in parasitic wasps, the wasps kill the caterpillar, the plant carries on. No caller, nothing was asking for anything, and yet it seems flat out true that the chemical is there in order to recruit the wasps. I definately don't want the story to explain that; I want it to happen to the characters.

Here's the setup I've got, please use it rather than swapping in something you know better. A PhD student is doing fieldwork on a small island, recording a two-note alarm call in a little brown songbird. There's a second species there, a ground-nesting bird, that bolts for cover whenever the alarm goes off. Her supervisor wants to write it up as cross-species communication, becuase the reserve's funding review has a line in it about acoustic communication networks and he knows which way that review will go. What the student thinks is going on is more like the second bird exploiting something that was never addressed to it — which is where the cue/signal line in behavioural ecology comes in: a signal is there because receivers responded to it, a cue is just something a receiver happens to be able to use. She isn't sure that line survives being asked whose cue it is, and that's the bit I want the story to sit on.

Some constraints, otherwise this drifts into a lecture. Under 1,000 words. No character may use the words rationale, teleology, function, design or adaptation — it all has to come through what they do, say, and scribble in notebooks. There should be at least one moment where a character correctly attributes a purpose to something that has no mind, and one where she's wrong to; I want to be able to tell those apart from the writing alone, without being told which is which. No epigraph, no closing paragraph that explains what the story was about. British English please, the anthology is UK-published. And end on something concrete — a sound, an object, a gesture — not a reflection.
