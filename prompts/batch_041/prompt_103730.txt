At the 2005 World Summit, the UN General Assembly adopted a formulation of the responsibility to protect that is standardly read as a compromise between two value systems: one organized around state sovereignty and non-interference, the other around the claims of individuals against their own governments. The agreed text commits the international community to collective action through the Security Council, "on a case-by-case basis," and only where national authorities "manifestly fail to protect their populations" from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity. The sovereignty side, on this reading, surrendered the claim that intervention against a state's will is never permissible; the human-rights side surrendered automaticity and unilateralism by routing any response through a body where a single permanent member can block it.

Please treat this episode as a test case for a particular theory of moral compromise: that a compromise between value systems is best understood as a species of exchange, justified in the same way trade is justified — by the mutual gains that voluntary exchange produces when each side values what it receives more than what it surrenders. For the analogy to be apt, four conditions must hold: each party must hold an entitlement it is free to alienate; the goods exchanged must be commensurable enough to admit a rate of exchange, or the swap must at least improve each party's position on its own terms; the exchange must be voluntary and enforceable; and the parties doing the trading must be the holders of the claims being traded.

Do four things. First, map the 2005 agreement onto that structure: identify the parties, the entitlement each surrendered, and the good each received, and say whether the deal was a Pareto improvement over the pre-2005 status quo of Rwanda and Kosovo or merely a redistribution of risk — and whether the Security Council is a party to the bargain or the marketplace in which it is struck. Second, run the case forward: what do Resolution 1973, the NATO campaign in Libya, the reading of that mandate as licensing regime change, and the subsequent paralysis over Syria each do to the exchange, and was the sovereigntist side's suspicion vindicated? Third, run it backward: what does the episode show about the trade model itself — does it confirm the model, expose a defect (that the populations whose protection is the object of the deal are not parties to it, or that the good on one side is a promise for which no one holds collateral), or show that the correct frame is not exchange at all? Fourth, give a verdict on whether moral compromise is better justified by gains from trade or by the avoidance of a shared loss, and say what that verdict implies about when a compromise of this kind slides into complicity with the next atrocity. Be disciplined about the mapping, and say plainly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
