They were arguing about whether a nation can be guilty for something it did generations ago.

Maya: A nation is a continuing entity. Think of it like a monorepo. The current maintainers didn't write the bug, but they own the service. If the bug is still causing harm, they have to fix it. And if the code was stolen, the repo is tainted. The nation is the repo, the citizens are the maintainers, and the guilt is on the nation.

Dev: A repo isn't a moral agent. The code is just text. The maintainers are responsible for fixing it, but they're not guilty of writing it. You're confusing responsibility with guilt. Responsibility is forward-looking; guilt is backward-looking. You can be responsible for a mess you didn't make. You can't be guilty of an act you didn't do.

Maya: But the nation isn't just a repo; it's a legal person. It can sign treaties, own property, be sued. If it can be liable, why can't it be guilty? And Jaspers, the German philosopher after WWII, distinguished four kinds of guilt -- criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. Political guilt attaches to you just for being a citizen. That's collective, and it's still guilt.

Dev: Political guilt is just a fancy name for liability. Jaspers was writing about Germans after the Holocaust, where the people alive had either done something or stood by. It's a special case. And even he said moral guilt is personal. You can't build a general theory of national guilt on it. Also, if you say the nation is guilty, the punishment lands on individual people, usually the wrong ones. That's why the concept is dangerous.

Maya: So what do you call it when a nation holds onto stolen land and refuses to give it back? That's not just a debt; it's a wrong that's ongoing. The nation is still doing it. So it's not inherited guilt; it's present guilt.

Dev: Then it's the current citizens doing it, through their institutions. Blame them for what they're doing now, not for what their ancestors did. If they vote to keep the stolen land, they're guilty of that. But that's not inherited guilt; that's current guilt. You don't need the inherited kind.

Maya: But the current citizens didn't choose the institutions. They were born into them. And they benefit from them. If I inherit a house built with stolen money, I'm not guilty of the theft, but I'm implicated. And if I refuse to make it right, then I become guilty of something. So the guilt can be inherited in the sense that the obligation is inherited, and refusing it makes you guilty.

Dev: Now you're switching again. The obligation is inherited. The guilt is not. You become guilty only of your own refusal. That's a current act. So the past doesn't transmit guilt; it transmits a situation that can generate new guilt. That's completely different.

Maya: That seems like a distinction without a difference. If the obligation is inherited, and refusing it makes you guilty, then the nation is guilty as soon as it refuses. And it has refused for generations. So it's guilty.

Dev: But the refusal is made by people, not by the nation. The nation doesn't refuse; the government refuses, or the voters refuse. Those are individuals. You keep reifying the nation into a person when it suits you.

Maya: And you keep reducing it to individuals when it suits you. But we say 'Germany invaded Poland.' We don't mean every German invaded Poland. We mean the nation, as an organized entity, did it. If that's meaningful, then the nation can be the subject of guilt too.

Dev: It can be the subject of action, maybe. But guilt requires more than action. It requires a guilty mind, or at least a capacity for moral judgment. A nation doesn't have a mind. It's a lot of people with different minds. So the most you can say is that some people did wrong, and others benefit. That's it.

Maya: But corporations are legal persons with no mind, and we convict them. We fine them. We say they're guilty. So the law already accepts collective guilt.

Dev: The law accepts collective liability. The word 'guilty' is doing legal work there, not moral work. And a corporation has a decision structure -- a board, officers, policies. A nation is much looser. Also, corporate guilt is controversial. People argue it's just a way to punish shareholders who did nothing. Same problem.

Maya: So you're saying collective guilt is never legitimate, even for corporations?

Dev: I'm saying it's a legal tool, not a moral truth. And when you use it morally, it becomes dangerous. It leads to blood guilt, to treating people as guilty because of their membership. That's the thing Jaspers was trying to avoid.

Maya: I think Jaspers was trying to avoid individual evasion, not collective guilt. He wanted Germans to accept that they were part of a guilty nation, even if they personally didn't commit crimes. That's the whole point of political guilt.

Dev: Then we disagree about Jaspers. But even if he said that, it was a specific historical context. You can't just universalize it.

Maya: Why not? If it works there, it works elsewhere. The structure is the same: a nation commits a wrong, the citizens inherit the benefits and the guilt.

Dev: Because in the German case, the wrong was ongoing and the citizens were alive. The people who committed the crimes were still there. It wasn't intergenerational. You're extending it to cases where everyone involved is dead. That's a different problem.

Maya: But the nation is still alive. That's the point. The nation is the same entity. It doesn't die when the citizens do.

Dev: Then the nation is an immortal person, which is a theological idea, not a political one. And I don't accept it.

Maya: Fine. But you still haven't told me what to do about the stolen land.

Dev: I told you: responsibility, benefit, repair. Not guilt. Guilt is the wrong tool.
