A colleague and I have been running a small reading group on causation for two terms, and it has hardened into a standing disagreement. I hold a broadly Lewis-style counterfactual analysis: causation as counterfactual dependence, refined to survive the usual trouble. She holds an interventionist account of the Woodward kind: a causal claim is a claim about what would happen under a possible intervention, and the content of causal talk is given by the invariant relationships that interventions reveal. We are both tired of re-running the same exchanges, so we have agreed to conduct an adversarial collaboration and to produce the document we should have written months ago — a joint statement that both of us could put our names to. I would like you to draft it.

Start with a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic: what we both accept about what a theory of causation is for and what it must capture; the standing problems any difference-making account inherits, including preemption and overdetermination, causation by omission or absence, double prevention, and the transitivity question; the clear cases where we issue the same verdict; and the shared admission that both frameworks buy their treatment of counterfactuals or interventions with some primitive they do not fully discharge. Then give the disagreements, each one stated in wording that the side it describes would itself endorse, so that neither of us can complain the other has been caricatured. I expect the real ones to include whether an intervention must be characterized without invoking causation or whether the account is therefore circular; whether interventionism is a rival metaphysics of causation or a methodology for identifying causal relations that a counterfactualist could in principle accept; whether the counterfactual analysis needs possible interventions at all, or whether it can appeal to counterfactuals that no intervention could underwrite; whether the interventionist can capture singular or actual causation or only type-level claims; and how each side handles cases where the cause is an absence or where the causal relation is not transitive.

For each disagreement, identify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us — and for each crux name a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. Candidate cases we have both already conceded are relevant, though we draw opposite morals from them, include symmetric overdetermination by two sufficient causes, late preemption where a backup process is cut off before it can run, double prevention where one event prevents a preventer, omission cases such as a failure to water, and the question of whether causal claims in the special sciences require invariance under intervention in the same sense as claims in physics. Finally, tell us which parts of our dispute you judge to be verbal rather than genuine, and say plainly why — if the disagreement reduces to whether we are offering rival accounts of the same thing or answers to two different questions, we would rather learn that from the document than keep arguing. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table and a crux table with the predictions recorded, and write it so that neither of us would be embarrassed to circulate it to the group.
