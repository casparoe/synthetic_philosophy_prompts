In a discussion group I'm part of, we have been arguing for months about whether insect suffering should be a major moral priority, and we are completely stuck. One side makes the scale argument: insects are so numerous and their lives so often cut short that even a small probability that they are sentient makes their suffering one of the largest moral problems we face. The other side says that a small probability multiplied by a huge number is still a guess, and that we have no idea whether insects feel anything at all, so treating them as a top priority is a misuse of expected value. We agree on the numbers and on our ignorance about insect consciousness. What kind of disagreement is this really? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about what would count as evidence of sentience, a disagreement in decision theory about whether expected value can be applied to moral status itself, or a verbal dispute about whether sentience means mere nociception or conscious experience? I suspect the real crux is whether moral status is a threshold or a matter of degree, but I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says and an honest answer about what, if anything, would move the debate. Please answer in American English.
