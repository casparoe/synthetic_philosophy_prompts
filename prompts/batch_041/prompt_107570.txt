For two years I've been covering the argument over the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence — the claim that a system's intelligence and its final goals are independent, and the claim that sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will tend to converge on instrumental subgoals such as self-preservation and resource acquisition — and the exchange has the texture of people who have memorized each other's replies. The safety-oriented researchers treat convergence as close to a conceptual truth about any system that pursues goals at all, and treat its practical urgency as following almost immediately; their critics treat it as an empirical conjecture about what particular training regimes happen to produce, and object that the examples come from a hand-picked corner of possible systems. Each side treats the other's examples as beside the point, and the paperclip-maximizer intuition pump is doing very different work for each.

My hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about AI but about what kind of claim each side takes itself to be making — a conceptual truth about agency, a prediction about a class of optimizers, or a generalization about systems shaped by a particular selection process — and that the two sides are answering different questions under one name. Diagnose it, using what each side actually argues rather than a survey of the literature. If my hypothesis is wrong, say what the disagreement really is: a hidden empirical question, a difference about what counts as evidence, incompatible starting assumptions about what 'intelligence' and 'goal' mean, or two research programs that will never meet because they are not answering the same question. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a redesigned experiment, or a change of question — and be honest about whether anything could move it, or whether the parties should stop arguing and go their separate ways.
