I want to take the multiple personality / DID episode as a test case for Hacking's looping-effect picture of psychiatric kinds, and I want the analysis to run both directions, theory on the case and case back on the theory.

The shape of it as I have it: barely a handful of cases before the 70s, then the Sybil book and film, then an explosion of diagnoses through the 80s concentrated in a few clinics and mostly in women, then the recovered-memory wars, the lawsuits, the parents backlash, and by the 90s the diagnosis draining out of practice while the category itself sat in the manual under a new name. Don't spend the answer on the history, but tell me if I've got the shape wrong.

What I want is the loop drawn precisely: who is classified, by whom, and what exactly feeds back into what -- patient self-understanding, clinician expectation, criteria, the manual, the courts, the talk shows -- and which of Hacking's several mechanisms each piece belongs to (making up people, interactive versus indifferent kinds). Then the part I actually care about. Looping is supposed to make a kind more entrenched: people come to fit the category and the category bends to fit them. This one went extinct in practice. Hacking has a second story for kinds that vanish, the transient illness that needs an ecological niche, which is what he says about the late-19th-century fugue epidemic. Is that doing real work here or just renaming the puzzle? If the loop ran in reverse, say what the mechanism was. And say whether the framework can even pose the question of whether the diagnosis was tracking a real disorder -- I don't want a verdict on DID, I want to know where the theory goes quiet.

Use the rival accounts only where they earn their keep -- old labeling theory and Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy on one side, the sociocognitive versus trauma camps among clinicians on the other -- and finish with one honest paragraph: confirmation of the looping thesis, counterexample, or a case it never claimed to cover.
