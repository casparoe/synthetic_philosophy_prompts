I'm in this applied ethics seminar and we just spent two weeks on consent — the usual stuff: deception and coercion in medicine and sex, whether coercion is really about threats vs. offers, whether consent is a mental state or a performative act. My roommate and I have been arguing about it at dinner all week (he's a law student, so everything turns into contracts with him), and I think I've got an angle for the final paper, but I want to run the notes past you before I commit, because I keep flip-flopping on whether it's real or just a distinction in search of a paper.

The idea: nearly everyone treats consent as doing one job, which is changing permissions — you consent, and something that was off-limits for the other person becomes permitted. I want to argue that consent does two jobs that can come apart. First it changes permissions (the standard story). Second it changes reasons: it gives the other person a reason to act that is *yours*, one that runs through your will rather than through their own interests or some general duty. Call these the permission function and the authorship function. I think seperating them gives a cleaner diagnosis of the three classic defeaters. Deception wrecks permission: you can't waive a right you didn't know you were waiving, so the act that got licensed isn't the act you actually performed — but authorship survives, because you really did put your will behind something. Coercion is the mirror image: permission may well survive (saying yes under a threat isn't saying nothing), but authorship is gone, because the reason the other person is acting on isn't properly yours — it's theirs wearing your clothes. And competence isn't a third defeater at all, it's the precondition for either function running at all, which is why incompetent consent feels void in a different way than coerced consent does. The payoff I'm most excited about: this predicts that we should treat coerced-but-informed consent as still doing something normatively — the person has a complaint, but the other party isn't simply an offender the way a deceiver is — and I think that lines up with intuitions about bad plea deals or exploitative but fully disclosed contracts.
