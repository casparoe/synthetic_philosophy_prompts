I am an undergraduate in my third year, splitting my time between philosophy and comparative literature, and next week I have to present a paper at our department's aesthetics workshop, which is the closest thing we have to a job talk. The paper grew out of a seminar I am taking this term on nineteenth-century Russian prose and its critics, where we spent several weeks on Tolstoy's late writings about art. My thesis is that the account of art in What Is Art? — art as a human activity in which one person transmits to others, by means of external signs, feelings he has himself lived through, with infectiousness rather than beauty as the mark of the genuine article, and with individuality, clarity, and sincerity as the conditions of that infectiousness — is a better starting point for a theory of art's value than the formalist and Kantian inheritance most of us were trained in, and that the familiar dismissals of it misfire. I also argue that the sincerity condition should be read as a claim about the structure of the work rather than about the artist's private biography, since insincerity leaves traces — the pursuit of effect, the borrowed device, the detail that is merely striking — and that once sincerity is read that way, the objection that awful people make great art loses much of its force. I am willing to concede that Tolstoy's own verdicts on Shakespeare and on Wagner look embarrassing, but I claim they follow from the theory only when it is joined to his further claim that the feeling transmitted must answer to the religious understanding of his time, which for him comes down to the consciousness of the brotherhood of all people, and that this further claim can be detached from the rest without loss.
