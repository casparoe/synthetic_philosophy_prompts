I run the quantitative risk group at a mid-size engineering consultancy -- we do the probabilistic risk assessments that regulaters and insurers make clients produce before they can build a chemical plant, keep an aging dam running, launch a medical device, that kind of thing. Every one of these jobs boils down to the same ugly task: somebody has to put a number on the probability of a failure event and then defend it in a room full of people who will ask where it came from. I've read enough LessWrong and stats blogs to know that probability is in the mind, and I know about base rates and reference classes and calibration, but none of that is a procedure I can hand to my analysts, who are good engineers and mediocre statisticians and who are always on a deadline because the client wants the report by Friday and a regulator has to sign off on it. Right now it's a mess: the old-timers want to lift numbers straight out of industry failure-rate tables, our one Bayesian wants to elicit priors from experts, and a lot of the events we assess have literally never happened anywhere, so the tables have nothing to say and nobody fully trusts the experts either. I want to fix this with an actual written procedure, not another lecture.

So please write me a decision procedure an analyst can run on a single probability claim, from the moment they're told "assign P(event)" to the number they write down and how they record it. The key thing I want it to force is that they first work out what kind of thing the probability is supposed to be -- a long-run frequency over a population of similar items, a propensity or physical tendency of one particular system, or a degree of belief somebody holds -- because I'm convinced most of our arguments are people using different senses of the word without noticing, and I want the procedure to drag that into the open and commit to one sense per claim, with reasons. For each step I want: the question to ask, what counts as a good enough answer, what to do when the honest answer is we don't know, where the procedure stops and hands it to a human with "this one is a judgment call and here's who owns it", and how to tag the final number so the next person can see which sense it was in. Then run it end to end on three claims: (a) the failure rate of a bog-standard centrifugal pump in normal service, (b) the probability that a brand-new process chemistry has a runaway reaction during its first year of operation, and (c) the probability that one specific 60-year-old dam -- one of a kind, no population of dams quite like it -- fails sometime in the next thirty years. I fully expect it to give a clean textbook answer on (a) and to end up telling me something I won't like on (c), so don't paper over that. One more thing that keeps biting us: these numbers get multiplied together in fault trees, and I suspect we're quietly mixing senses when we do that -- a frequency from a table times somebody's degree of belief -- and nobody notices. Say whether that's actually a problem and what the procedure should tell an analyst about combining numbers that were tagged with different senses. And be straight with me about what the procedure fundamentally can't deliver, no matter how carefully we follow it.
