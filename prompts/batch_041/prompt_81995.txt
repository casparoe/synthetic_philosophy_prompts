Until last month I would have said the difference between lying and merely misleading was a philosopher's question with no bearing on my job. I am the research-integrity lead at a mid-sized medical centre, and I now sit on the committee that signs off on the language we publish about our studies — patient handouts, press releases, the plain-language summaries attached to consent forms. We are deadlocked over one sentence in a draft. Word by word it is accurate: nothing in it is false to the data, and our lawyers have confirmed as much. But when we tested it on a panel of patients, nearly all of them took it to mean something stronger than the data support, and several said they would have agreed to a treatment on that basis. The misleading impression comes from two places: what we chose to leave out, and one word we use in a narrow technical sense that readers hear in its ordinary sense. Our general counsel's view is that this settles the matter — "It is not a lie if it is technically true," he says, and our disclosure policy speaks only of "materially false statements."

I do not want a ruling, I want a map. Set out the distinct precise theses that this one line could be asserting, from the platitudinous to the radical, and for each say whether it is true and whether it is really a claim about lying or a claim about something else — blame, trust, or what the reader has grounds to complain about. Keep the definitional readings separate from the moral ones: does literal truth settle what kind of act this was, does it settle whether the writer may be blamed, does it settle what the reader is owed. Include the readings on which our sentence — true as asserted, misleading as received — counts as a lie after all, and the readings on which it is not a lie but is nonetheless a wrong of a different kind. Then tell me which reading counsel is most likely invoking, whether that reading actually supports releasing the sentence unchanged, and which of the remaining readings imply that lying and misleading do not differ morally, that misleading is merely the lesser wrong, or that they are wrongs of different kinds altogether. Lay the readings out as a numbered list I can circulate before the vote, with a one-line verdict on each, and keep our sentence in view throughout; I do not need the history of the debate, I need each thesis in the form our policy would have to adopt it.
