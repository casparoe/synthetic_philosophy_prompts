I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a character who misleads people without ever, in his own estimation, lying to them; to get his self-justification right I have been reading Augustine's On Lying (De mendacio), and I have run aground in the third chapter. The sentence I cannot get past is this: "Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived." A little earlier in the same chapter Augustine also writes that "he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it."

I would like help understanding this. What exactly is the "double heart," and why should it be the thing that makes an utterance a lie rather than the falsity of what is said? Is the wish to deceive a further requirement on top of the doubled heart, or is it just another name for it — and if it is a further requirement, can the two come apart? I am especially interested in whether this old analysis gives me any grip on the distinction between lying and merely misleading, since my character's whole defence is that he says only true things. Please explain the passage in plain terms, unpack the contrast Augustine draws between believing and opining, and say where his account is doing real work and where it looks vulnerable. I do not need citations or further reading; I want to understand the passage itself.
