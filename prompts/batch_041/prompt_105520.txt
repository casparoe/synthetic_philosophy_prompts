I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about whether the victors write the history, and I would like your help settling it, because I suspect we were talking past each other. She holds the claim more or less literally: the powerful impose the record, and what we call history is the winners' account laundered into common sense. I kept insisting that the slogan conceals at least three separate theses, which she was running together — one about the archive (which documents and voices survive at all), one about narrative (which events a story selects, emphasizes, and connects into a plot), and one about metaphysics (whether there is, beneath the competing stories, any fact of the matter). To test the whole tangle, I want to work through a film that is neither history nor historiography but has become the standard illustration of perspective: Kurosawa's Rashomon, in which a murder in a grove is recounted four times — by the bandit, by the wife, by the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and by the woodcutter who claims to have watched — and the accounts cannot all be true, since each narrator tells a version in which he or she cuts a better figure, and even the dead man's testimony is a performance staged by someone else.

Please read the film through the lens of the question my friend and I were fighting about, and take both the film and the philosophical debate seriously rather than trading in the easy observation that truth is subjective. I want to know which of those three theses the film actually dramatizes and which it leaves untouched; whether its subject is really power at all, given that its narrators are not victors but people defending their dignity, or whether it is a study in interested testimony that has been mistaken for a study in power; what work the frame story does — the priest, the commoner, and the rain at the gate — and whether the woodcutter's closing gesture toward the abandoned infant amounts to an argument or merely a sentiment; and whether the film is committed to the existence of one true account, with the woodcutter's version as the withheld truth, or to a genuine underdetermination in which no version is privileged. It would also help me to know whether the film's relativism, such as it is, is a relativism about memory and self-image rather than about evidence, and whether that distinction does any real philosophical work or only sounds tidy.

I am not asking for a summary of the plot or a survey of what critics have said; I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, one that illuminates specific scenes and choices without reducing the film to an illustration of a thesis, and that admits where the film resists the reading — the places where it strains against the very idea of perspectival relativism it is supposed to exemplify. End by telling me what the film contributes to this dispute that argument alone cannot, especially about the difference between a history that has been captured by the powerful and one that has simply been told by interested parties. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a long letter — and in American English.
