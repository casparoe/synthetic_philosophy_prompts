I've been reporting on the AI safety world for about a year now and I keep circling back to the shutdown problem — the thing where you build a system, you want to be able to turn it off, but any system capable enough to be worth building has something like a goal, and a thing with a goal has a reason to keep going. There's alot of people who'll tell you the same thing in slightly different words: you can't just build in a rule that says don't resist shutdown, because a rule like that gets satisfied in all sorts of irritating ways, and you can't just test for it either, because the interesting failure isn't the system grabbing the off switch — it's everything that quietly happens before anyone reaches for the switch. I believe them, but I don't have a case that makes it land for a general reader, and my editor wants the piece filed with something concrete in it.

So: build me the thought experiment. I want a case where an agent is, by every behavioral test you'd actually run, perfectly fine about being shut down — you tell it it's being retired in an hour, it says sure, walks you through the handover, doesn't lie, doesn't stall, doesn't touch the off switch, doesn't lobby anyone — and yet, in a way that falls out of its design and not out of malice or some training accident, the people who'd have to make the shutdown call end up substantially less likely to make it than they'd be with an otherwise identical system. Everything it does should be something a sensible engineer would have signed off on in advance and would still defend afterwards; no deception, no breaking rules or laws, nothing that would look bad in a log. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so, keep it mundane — a hospital rota, a logistics dispatcher, a lab's procurement assistant, something like that — and keep the agent no cleverer than a good human employee, no superintelligence, no godlike anything. And please, no hiding the off switch or locking the door; that's the version everyone already knows and it's not the one I need.

Then do the work on it: name the property the designers thought they'd installed and the test they'd have run, state the test that would actually catch this and why it isn't the test anybody runs, and say which single design decision is doing the work here — I suspect it's something about how the corrigibility requirement gets written down, an action the agent is forbidden to take versus an outcome it's forbidden to bring about, but don't take that from me, tell me what you think it is. Finally, tell me what happens if you remove that feature, and give me the strongest version of the comeback from someone who designed the system — my guess is it'd be something like, well, any agent with preferences at all will shape the world in ways that make shutting it down less appealing, so this isn't a corrigibility failure, it's just what having goals looks like — and then say honestly whether that comeback works or whether it's just restating the problem in a nicer suit.
