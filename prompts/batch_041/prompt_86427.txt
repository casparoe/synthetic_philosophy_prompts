I'm in the second year of a philosophy MA and I've spent the term in a reading group on the philosophical essays of the physicists — Bohr, Heisenberg, Pauli, and above all Schrödinger, whose later, off-duty writings keep circling back to the Upanishads and to the claim that consciousness is finally one. I've written a short piece for the group, about a thousand words, so there is no room to hedge, and I'd like your comments before I circulate it, because I suspect the central move is either the whole point of the thing or a cheat, and I genuinely cannot tell which.

My thesis is that his oneness claim is best read not as a claim about how many minds there are — which would be empirically absurd, since your experiences and mine are plainly not shared — but as a claim about the limits of the concept of number: that 'how many' simply has no grip here, in the way it has no grip on the question how many colours the number seven is. On this reading the plurality of persons is not an illusion to be explained away; it is a description that serves perfectly well for every practical purpose and yet fails to pick out a fact about the subject of experience. He also takes the question of why one should be this particular person rather than another to be a sign that something has been misdescribed, and I use that as evidence for my reading, since on the counting reading it is merely a hard question about which person one is, whereas on mine it dissolves. The piece has four moves: the grammatical reading; the claim that this is why the plurality of persons needs no explaining away; the dissolution of the why-this-person question; and finally the step I am least sure of, that this reading is what lets him treat the Upanishadic identification as a statement of fact rather than a piece of piety, since on my account it denies nothing we know, it only refuses a description, and the refusal is itself a kind of knowledge claim. A friend in the group says the move merely relocates the problem. If there is one subject and two streams of content, one that remembers writing my essay and one that remembers writing hers, then the subject/content distinction is doing all the work, and the partition has to live somewhere: either it lives in the subject, and we are back to two, or it lives in the contents, and then we owe an account of why contents come packaged in exactly the mutually inaccessible bundles we call persons — which is a fact about the world after all, and one that the modest oneness claim was supposed to be too modest to have to explain. I think there is a reply, something about contents being individuated only relative to a perspective, so that asking for the packaging before you have a perspective is like asking for the shape of a shadow before you have a light, but I am not confident it is more than a restatement of the view in a new metaphor.

I want comments on three things, in order of how much I care: whether my reading is fair to a physicist's off-duty metaphysics or is a philosopher's tidy-up that has quietly replaced it; where the actual weakness lies — is the grammatical move sound, and if so does the subject/content reply answer the packaging objection or only postpone it; and whether the whole thing is interesting enough to circulate, or whether the grammatical reading is exactly the kind of deflationary move that makes the Vedantic commitment decorative, so that a reader could accept every word of my essay and lose nothing that mattered to him. Please don't hedge everything equally; tell me which part I should be defending and which I should cut. I'm not after reading suggestions or who has said what — I want your own diagnosis in your own terms. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write a letter to a colleague who has asked you to be honest with them.
