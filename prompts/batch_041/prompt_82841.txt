For thirty years I ran controlled experiments on living systems, and I have spent the last four reading moral philosophy. I keep returning to the dispute between neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicists, who hold that durable traits of character are the proper unit of moral assessment and explanation, and situationists, who hold that behavior is better predicted by features of the immediate situation and better improved by institutional design than by character. Each side, it seems to me, answers the other's strongest point with something that functions as a rescue clause, and I would like the two of them to produce the document I would have demanded of two laboratories that had spent a decade contradicting each other in print.

Draft that document. It should contain the following.

A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Include the findings neither disputes (the familiar battery in which ordinary helping and honesty shift substantially with hurry, ambient noise, an unexpected small windfall, or the mere presence of bystanders), what each side's positive position actually asserts rather than what its critics say it asserts, and the methodological points both accept about how single-occasion correlations, behavior averaged across many occasions, and person-by-situation interactions should be read. If a claim would be signed by anyone at all, cut it.

A disagreements table: one row per genuine disagreement, with the question at issue and then each side's position stated in wording that side would accept as its own.

The cruxes, ordered by how much turns on each. For each crux, say why settling it would move at least one side; give a study, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on it; and record each side's prediction in advance. I want the sharp ones — whether a trait that shows up only in aggregate behavior counts as a trait, whether a virtue acquired in one setting must transfer to a structurally different one, and how many occasions of observation the virtue ethicist needs before evidence could count for or against a trait's existence. Where a number is possible, give me the number and the design that would estimate it.

A section separating the disagreements that are real from those that are verbal, saying which piece of vocabulary is doing the work — in particular whether "virtue" names a standard for what a good person would do or a prediction about what people in fact do.

Finally, the single experiment both sides agree is worth running, and what each would count as evidence against its own position. If either position can be made immune to every possible result, say so plainly, and say what would have to be fixed for it to be falsifiable. I do not need a bibliography; I need the logic, the formulations, and the designs.
