I've been reading G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica on my own — a 1903 reprint I picked up secondhand, mainly because a friend in my book club kept going on about it — and I've got completely stuck on the last chapter, the one called The Ideal. I don't have any philosophy training, so bear with me. The bit I can't get past is where he finally says what the best things in life actually are. As I understand him, after pages of insisting that good is simple and indefinable, he comes out with something like: the most valuable things we can know of are certain states of consciousness, and above all two of them — the pleasures of human intercourse, and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. He then seems to say that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful are the two great goods, and that affection comes out on top, partly because loving a person properly involves appreciating their good qualities, so beauty-appreciation gets folded into friendship as an ingredient. And there's something earlier about wholes being "organic" — that the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts, and can be greater than that sum, which he needs for the friendship point I think. He also opens the whole book with a line borrowed from Bishop Butler to the effect that everything is what it is and not another thing, which I mention only because it seems to sit oddly with the idea that you can pile things together and get more value than the pile contains.

So what I want help with is this: can you walk me through that final stretch of the argument in plain English, in British English please, and tell me what exactly Moore is claiming and what he's not? Specifically — is he saying beauty is only good when someone actually enjoys it, or is beautiful stuff good in itself whether or not anyone's there to look at it? Does friendship count as a "pleasure of human intercourse" in some crude sense, or is he using pleasure in a much wider way than I'd normally mean it? And how is the organic-whole business supposed to work here — what does it even mean to say a whole is more valuable than its parts added up, and does he think that's a general truth about value or just a special trick he needs for the friendship case? If you can also say which of these questions Moore himself would have thought was confused, that'd be a bonus, because honestly I suspect at least one of them is me asking the wrong thing rather than him being unclear. I'd rather have a careful, patient explanation than a summary — I want to actually follow the moves, not just get the conclusion, and I don't mind you telling me where I've mangled what he's saying.
