Next month I present to our departmental reading group on infinite tasks and the foundations of analysis a paper whose thesis is that Thomson's lamp is a reductio not on supertasks as such but on a principle I call state-by-limit — the assumption that a system's state at an accumulation time is the limit of its states on the approach to that time. My line is that the lamp has no state at t = 2, and that this is not the innocuous observation that the task description underdetermines the lamp (of course it does; descriptions underdetermine lots of things) but the stronger, metaphysical claim that reality itself fails to settle the matter, because the switching schedule converges to no state and state-by-limit is the only candidate principle that could have settled it. I say in the paper that the bouncing ball, which bounces infinitely often in a finite interval and comes to rest, is no counterexample, since the ball's rest state is fixed by the contact mechanics and not by taking a limit of earlier states. I open with the switching schedule and the parity argument and I have twenty minutes, so the Q&A is where this paper will live or die.

The group is neither friendly nor uniform, and I want to be ready for it specifically. Two members work on determinism and the direction of time in philosophy of physics and have argued for years that supertasks are physically idle — no lamp switches infinitely fast, no ball rebounds elastically forever, so the whole genre is science fiction dressed as mathematics. One is a set theorist who will want to know in exactly what space I am taking the limit and will observe that a richer state space makes the lamp's sequence converge, so that the indeterminacy is an artifact of my choice of state space. One is a constructive mathematician who suspects the entire debate trades on classical assumptions about the completion of infinite sequences and who will tell me that I have rediscovered intuitionistic negation and given it a physics costume. And two are metaphysicians: one is a well-known enthusiast of no-fact-of-the-matter arguments who will press the bouncing ball harder than I have answered it in the paper, and the other is our host, who has a taste for dilemmas and likes to ask the question that makes a position choose between being trivial and being expensive.
