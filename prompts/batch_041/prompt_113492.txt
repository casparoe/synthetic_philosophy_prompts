I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy — my thesis is on the metaphysics of classification, mostly species and psychiatric kinds — and this term I'm TAing a big first-year survey module, 'Europe 1000–1800', which is divided into three blocks (Medieval; Renaissance/Early Modern; Enlightenment), each with its own convenor, reading list and exam paper. What's nagging at me is that the same department, in its research seminars, is full of people saying the periods are constructs: that 'the Renaissance' is a label later centuries stuck onto a stretch of time that didn't experience itself that way, that the medieval/modern break is an artefact of a Europe-centred story, that there's no day on which the Middle Ages ended. Institutionally, though, we act as though they're real. We advertise a chair in medieval history, not in '1150–1420'. Journals have period remits. My supervisor won't let me write a chapter that straddles two of them. Its definately a bit odd, and I can't tell whether the two halves of the department are contradicting each other or just using the word 'real' in different ways.

So: take the role of an expert in the philosophy of historiography who also knows the metaphysics of classification, and think it through carefully — I'd rather you laid out the candidate views first and then committed, than gave me a tidy answer straight off. I want to know what our periodising practice actually presupposes. Does it require realism about periods, i.e. that a period is an entity with some internal unity — a shared character, features that co-vary — such that the boundaries are boundaries of something? Or could the practice get by with something much thinner, a convention for dividing labour and shelf space, in which case all the realist-sounding talk is just loose speech? Two friends in the pub offered me analogies and I'd like to know if either gets the practice right: one said periods are like constellations, groupings we impose on a continuum that the continuum itself doesn't care about; the other said they're more like biological species, real but vague-edged and only identifiable in hindsight. (A third friend works on the Anthropocene and keeps pointing out that geologists have an international body that votes on whether a unit counts as real and drives a marker into an actual rock face to fix its boundary, which she thinks makes their periods a completely different kind of thing from ours.)

Then please tell me what is strictly presupposed here as opposed to what the practice merely fits nicely with, and where the practice is underdetermined — which rival accounts of historical categories would license exactly what we do. And if the presupposed view were swapped out, say for a thoroughgoing instrumentalism about periods, what would actually have to change in a department like mine: would the period chairs, the period journals and the survey module be indefensible, or would some other rationale quietly take over the work they're doing? I'm not after citations or a reading list — I can find those myself — I just want the philosophical scaffolding laid out, in British English please.
