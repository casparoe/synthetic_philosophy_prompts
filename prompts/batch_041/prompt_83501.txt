I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I have been reading about the identity of indiscernibles and the two-spheres thought experiment usually credited to Max Black. The two sides of that dispute seem to talk past each other, and I would like a document that stops that: a joint statement that a defender of the principle and a critic of it could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two camps I have in mind are these. Camp A, the grounded-distinctness camp, holds that numerical distinctness always requires a difference in properties, so the two spheres must differ somehow, if not in their intrinsic features then in their relational or identity-involving properties. Camp B, the ungrounded-distinctness camp, holds that two things can be numerically distinct while differing in no property at all, so the principle is false, and that appeals to identity-involving properties either presuppose the very distinctness they are meant to explain or make the principle trivial. Please treat these as the two parties. If you think the labels need adjusting, adjust them, but keep two camps.

A note on vocabulary, since I am still learning it: by identity-involving properties I mean ones that mention a particular individual, such as the property of being identical to this sphere or of being two miles from that sphere, as opposed to purely qualitative properties, which can be stated without naming any particular thing.

I have also tried to construct a case that closes the obvious escape routes, and I want the document to address it directly. Take a universe containing exactly two objects, both iron spheres of the same size, mass, and temperature, arranged so that every relational fact about one is also a fact about the other: they are the only two things there are, each is the same distance from the other, and there is no other asymmetry anywhere in the universe. Call this world W. Now consider W*, which is W with the two spheres exchanged. I want both camps to say, in advance and in their own words, whether W and W* are the same possible world or two different ones, and whether an ideal observer who knew every fact about W could have any basis for saying which sphere is which. I also want them to say what happens if we add a third, asymmetrical object to the universe, so that one sphere is nearer to it and the other farther; my guess is that both camps will agree the principle is untouched in that version, and that the real dispute is only about the fully symmetrical case. If that guess is wrong, I would like to be told why.

Please write the joint statement with these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

1. Preamble (about 100 words): who the two camps are and the rules both accept for the collaboration.

2. Joint Claims (numbered, eight to twelve claims, one or two sentences each): the claims both camps can sign, about the logic of the principle, about what the two-spheres case does and does not show, about what would count as a refutation, and about anything else they can agree on. These should not be vacuous; each should do some work.

3. Each Side's Position in Its Own Words (two paragraphs, about 150 words each): the view of each camp, written so that camp would accept the wording, with a note on the phrases each side insists on.

4. Cruxes (three to five numbered items, about 150 words each): for each, the sub-question at issue, why settling it would move at least one camp, the case or argument both camps agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction about how it comes out, labelled by camp.

5. Disagreements Table (four to six rows): columns for the crux, Camp A's answer, Camp B's answer, and what both camps agree would settle it.

6. Genuine or Verbal (about 150 words): which parts of the dispute are merely about words, for instance whether property is to include identity-involving properties, and which parts are substantive, with your reasoning.

7. Closing Statement and Signatures (about 80 words): a paragraph both camps could put their names to, and, for each camp, one thing it would count as evidence or argument against its own position.

I would rather have a shorter document that is exact than a longer one that hedges. Where a formulation would be accepted by one camp only because it is vague, say so instead of using it.
