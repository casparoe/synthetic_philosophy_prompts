I was diagnosed with bipolar II last year and my therapist has been pushing me to write one of those Ulysses contracts before my next episode (she calls it a psychiatric advance directive) where I spell out in advance what I want done if I start going manic -- no new credit cards, call my sister, that kind of thing. I mentioned it to a friend who did a masters in political theory and he gave me his whole spiel, and now I'm second-guessing the whole thing. His version, as best as I can reconstruct it:

Ulysses contracts are named after the Odyssey, where Odysseus has his crew put wax in their ears and tie him to the mast so he can hear the Sirens without jumping overboard. The idea is that you bind your future self now, because you know that future self is going to want to do something you don't want them to do. Constitutions do the same trick -- rights and rules that a majority can't just repeal whenever it feels like it. But the standard objection is that it doesn't work and shouldn't: if the contract can be undone then it isn't really binding, and if it can't be undone then you've handed control of your life to a past version of yourself, who isn't the one who has to live with it. In your case a third party is doing the tying, so it's really just paternalism with extra steps -- someone else deciding for you, only doing it on your earlier instructions, which is arguably worse because you can't even argue with your past self. And the constitutional version is anti-democratic for the same reason: a dead majority ruling a live one. So the whole idea is either trivial (a fancy name for a promise) or incoherent (you can't bind the person who is free to untie themselves).
