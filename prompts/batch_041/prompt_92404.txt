I am a PhD student in intellectual history, and I would like help diagnosing an argument that I have been carrying around since a workshop on historical explanation. The argument is meant to show that no historical narrative can be a genuine causal explanation, but it seems to generalize to every causal explanation, which I take to be a reductio. Here it is, with the steps numbered:

(1) A causal explanation of an outcome is adequate only if it identifies causes that operated independently of the fact that the outcome occurred, in the sense that their causal role can be specified without assuming the outcome.
(2) Any historical explanation of an outcome must select and arrange antecedent events into a coherent sequence that terminates in that outcome.
(3) Such selection and arrangement require a criterion of relevance, and the only available criterion is the outcome itself; otherwise the historian would have no way to distinguish causally relevant antecedents from the infinite field of preceding events.
(4) But selection by the outcome is exactly what hindsight bias distorts, and the narrative fallacy consists in mistaking the coherence of the resulting story for evidence that the outcome was inevitable.
(5) Therefore every historical narrative is shaped by the outcome it purports to explain and cannot satisfy (1).
(6) Every causal explanation, including those in the natural sciences, selects and arranges antecedents into a representation that terminates in the explanandum.
(7) Therefore no causal explanation can satisfy (1), and the distinction between historical and natural-scientific explanation collapses.
(8) Since (7) is unacceptable, one of (1) through (4) must be rejected.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (6), but I want to know what I am paying. I suspect the problem lies somewhere in the inference from a claim about how narratives are built to a claim about what they can justify, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. I have also tried to use contrastive explanation, in the form of why this outcome rather than some other, to block the inference from (3) to (5), and Mackie-style INUS conditions to block the inference from (5) to (7), but I cannot tell whether these are genuine repairs or merely redescriptions. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere? If there is more than one defensible target, please say which rejection is cheapest and which preserves the most of the original argument.
