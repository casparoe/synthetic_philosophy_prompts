Two colleagues of mine spent most of a seminar arguing about what the handicap principle actually claims, and by the end I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. (1) Nadia said the principle is that a signal is honest because it is costly: the peacock's tail, the gazelle's stotting, the decade spent on a useless credential — the sender burns resources it cannot recover, and only a genuinely high-quality individual can survive the burn, so the cost is precisely what stops the signal from being faked. (2) Ben said that cannot be the whole story, because a cost that every sender pays equally separates nothing; what makes a signal honest is that the same cost is cheap for high-quality senders and ruinous for low-quality ones, so the principle is really about the slope of cost against quality, not the size of the cost. (3) Nadia said that just restates her position, since a differential burden is simply a handicap. (4) Ben said no, it shows the handicap principle is only one route to honest signaling: a signal that is free but reliably correlated with quality — a fixed trait, an index — is just as honest, so the handicap is a special case rather than the essence. (5) Nadia then offered a case she thought broke his version: two sender types, and a signal that costs exactly two units to either of them, but one type has ten units to spare while the other has only two and needs most of that to survive. The cost is identical for both; only the budget differs. She said the signal is plainly honest, so the principle cannot require a differential cost, only a differential burden — which is just cost again. (6) Ben replied that she is conflating the price of the signal with its price relative to the sender's resources, and that once cost is defined that way his formulation already covers her case, so it is a clarification rather than a counterexample. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (1) through (6), say whether it is right, mistaken, or partly both, and briefly justify the rating. Then say what, if anything, the two of them are jointly missing about what makes a signal credible, and who is more nearly right overall. Answer in American English.
