My two housemates got into a long arguement at the kitchen table last night about a call our other housemate made, and I still can't tell who came out on top. The situation: at a charity raffle you could either take a guaranteed $500, or take a ticket with a 90% shot at $600 and a 10% shot at nothing. He took the guaranteed $500. Then there was a second round with the same kind of deal but the odds sliced down to a tenth — a 10% shot at $500 versus a 9% shot at $600 — and this time he went for the 9% shot at $600.

Housemate A says he contradicted himself. His reasoning: the second choice is just the first choice with every probability multiplied by 1/10, and scaling all the odds by the same number shouldn't flip what you prefer, so one of his two answers has to be a mistake. Housemate B says that's a slight of hand. A guarantee, she says, isn't just a very big probability — 'I get $500 no matter what' and 'I very probably get $500' are different in kind — and the whole multiply-everything-by-1/10 trick quietly assumes a sure thing is nothing special. A shot back that it isn't about vibes, it's about consistency: picture the second round as a game where a coin first decides whether you even get to play, with only a 1-in-10 chance of playing — then his second-round pick is the same as his first-round pick in the world where the coin says play, so he can't have it both ways. B said that move assumes it makes no difference whether the randomness comes all at once or in two steps, which is the exact thing they're fighting about, so he's begging the question.

Can you go through this point by point and tell me who's right about what, and what both of them are missing? I'm not an academic, just curious, so keep it in plain language.
