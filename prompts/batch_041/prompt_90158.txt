I teach a seminar on the socialist calculation debate, and I've got a problem: my students can recite Mises and Hayek and Lange back at me chapter and verse, but none of them can say what any of it implies for a situation they haven't already been taught. So I want to run a case in class. My own university is about to do something I think its a mistake. The dean wants to pull every department's budget into one central pot and have the provosts office allocate the whole thing by formula — space, TA lines, travel, the lot — on the grounds that since everything now lives in the same data system, the centre can just optimise the whole university at once instead of letting departments compete for scraps. When I pushed back he said not to worry: his office will compute internal shadow prices for everything, departments can just respond to those, and if the numbers look off they'll revisit them each year. A colleague says this is the calculation debate all over again with worse coffee, which I think is right, but I want the actual implications rather than the slogan. What are the most important implications of the socialist calculation debate for a proposal like this — and be honest about which parts of the classic argument actually transfer to an institution like a university and which don't. Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, most important first, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
