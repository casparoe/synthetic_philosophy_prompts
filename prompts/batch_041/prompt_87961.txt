I'm in a seminar on ancient economic thought and I keep having the same argument with a friend who's doing a masters in finance. She thinks Aristotle's whole attack on chrematistics — the unnatural kind of wealth-getting, money breeding money, lending at interest as the worst case of it — is just sour grapes from a guy who never saw a capital market, and honestly I half agree with her, but I'd rather actually test the thing than trade vibes. So take the 2008 financial crisis and run it through Aristotle's account of exchange and money in the fifth book of the Nicomachean Ethics and his distinction between household management and wealth-getting in the first book of the Politics. Not the economics — I know the economics. I mean the mapping, done properly: who counts as what in his scheme, where money is doing its proper job as a measure and a medium of exchange and where it's been made into the thing that's sought, and what he would say each party was actually doing. The chain I want mapped is the one from the subprime mortgage originators to the securitizers slicing pools of those loans into tranches, to the credit default swaps AIG was writing on the securities it didn't have the reserves to cover, to Lehman in September of '08, to the bailout. The bit that keeps snagging me is that a mortgage to buy a house is about as natural a transaction as he could ask for, and it was the raw material of the whole disaster, so tell me whether his line between natural acquisition and unlimited acquisition tracks anything real here or whether it cuts straight through the middle of the case.

Then I want the honest part, which is where he runs out. Does he have anything to say about a chain of counterparties none of whom need anything the others have and all of whom are only holding the position because they expect to sell it? His line is that exchange rests on a common measure, and that the measure is grounded in need, which is why money can stand in for need at all — what happens to a theory of exchange when nobody in the room has a need, only an expectation about resale? Same question for credit that's genuinely productive, the farmer who borrows for seed and comes out ahead, which his "currency is barren, interest is money breeding money" move seems to rule out a priori. And don't force him to speak where he can't: I want to know where he's simply silent — sorting culpability across that chain, telling a bad loan apart from a fraudulent one, saying anything about risk that moves through a system rather than sitting in one pair of hands, or the state showing up as a borrower and a guarantor rather than as a household writ large.

Finally, give me the verdict in both directions, because that's the part my friend and I can't settle. Does 2008 vindicate the chrematistics critique, or does it show that the natural/unnatural distinction can't be drawn at all once credit exists and the answer is just that Aristotle was right about the disease and wrong about the diagnosis? And say plainly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to have a view — if he would have had nothing useful to say about, I don't know, systemic risk or the difference between a homeowner and a speculator in the same instrument, then say that and tell me what it costs the framework. I don't need the Greek or anything, just the analysis.
