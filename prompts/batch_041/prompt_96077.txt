I spent most of a long argument with a friend trying to say whether it means anything to claim that an AI system believes something, and I came away defending a package of views that I suspect I cannot actually hold together. I would like an audit before we take it up again. Here is where I stand. (1) The intentional stance is the correct account of belief talk in general: to attribute a belief is to adopt a predictive strategy toward a system, and the attribution is vindicated when it lets you anticipate and explain that system's behavior. (2) It is therefore entirely legitimate to say that a model believes the meeting is on Tuesday, whenever that description predicts its output better than talk about weights and tokens. (3) I am nevertheless a realist about human beliefs: there is a fact of the matter about what a person believes, and it is fixed by what is going on in them, not by how useful the attribution is to us. (4) The way to settle whether a model really believes something is to look inside it for states that play the right functional role, namely representations that drive behavior across contexts and that get revised when the evidence changes. (5) A model cannot be held morally responsible for what it says, even when attributing beliefs to it is exactly the right thing to do, since responsibility requires a kind of self-governance that predictive success does not by itself establish.

I assume at least one pair here is in genuine conflict. Tell me which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and how severe each real tension is, ranking them rather than treating them as equally costly. For each real tension, say which commitment has to give and what I would be losing by giving it up.
