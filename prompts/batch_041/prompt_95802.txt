A mid-sized city has adopted a futarchy-like procedure — the scheme Robin Hanson proposed, in which the ends are settled democratically and the means by market — for one class of decisions. For its discretionary capital budget, a randomly selected citizens' assembly first fixes a single welfare metric, currently a weighted composite of three indicators: life expectancy, median household income, and a subjective-wellbeing survey. The council is then bound by conditional prediction markets rather than by debate. Two markets open on whether that composite index will be higher three years after adopting a given proposal than after declining it, traders stake real money against a city-funded liquidity subsidy, and the proposal takes effect if its conditional price clears a stated threshold. Two carve-outs were written into the ordinance: decisions bearing on civil rights are excluded from the procedure entirely and revert to the ordinary council vote, and the council may override any market verdict by a two-thirds majority.

I want an account of what this institution presupposes philosophically, and I want you to be precise about the difference between what it strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit.

Begin with the division of labor at its core. The design treats "what we want" and "what will happen" as two separable inputs, the first delivered by the assembly and the second by the market. What view of the relation between value and fact does that require? Is the separation available, or does the choice of metric already encode empirical beliefs and the market price already encode evaluations, so that the two halves contaminate each other? Say whether the institution needs the separation or only finds it convenient.

Then take the metric. A single scalar, with fixed weights, evaluated at a three-year horizon, presupposes that the relevant goods are commensurable and substitutable at a known rate. Which theories of value does that rule out — pluralism about value, incommensurability, a capabilities approach, a rights-based view — and which does it leave open? Does the procedure strictly need a scalar, or could it run on a vector of indicators with a rule for partial orderings? Does it commit the city to welfarism, or only to the weaker claim that whatever matters must be measurable?

Then the legitimacy of the output. The procedure draws authority from two sources at once: the metric is legitimate because it was chosen democratically, and the verdict is authoritative because the price is epistemically better than deliberation. What theory of political legitimacy does that combination presuppose, and is it coherent? Note the asymmetry the design creates — the assembly is answerable to no one for the metric, while the market is answerable to no one for the verdict — and say whether the two sources can conflict in a way the institution has no resources to adjudicate.

Then the price itself. What must be true of market prices for a conditional contract to constitute evidence about a policy's causal effect — about the traders' incentives, the liquidity, the isolation of the counterfactual, the difference between a price that tracks credence and one that tracks a bet? What theory of evidence does the practice assume, and how much of it is load-bearing?

Finally, read the two carve-outs as evidence about the designers' implicit theory. The civil-rights exemption appears to concede that some goods cannot be placed on the scale at all; the override appears to concede that the market can be wrong in a way that a supermajority is entitled to correct. Are those concessions consistent with the main procedure, or do they concede the very premises the procedure rests on — that everything relevant is measurable and that the price is the best available estimate? If a good is exempt from the market, what work is the exemption doing: marking it as lexically prior, marking it as politically too hot to bet on, or marking it as a case where the metric is simply incomplete?

Close by reconstructing the implicit theory in a few sentences, saying where the practice is underdetermined in the sense that several rival theories would license it equally well, and describing what the institution would have to look like if it were rebuilt on one of those rivals — say, a pluralist theory of value, or a view on which legitimacy is procedural all the way down.
