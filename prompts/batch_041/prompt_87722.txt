A friend of mine in a phil of science program has an argument that the standard Bayesian fix for the problem of old evidence proves too much, and I've been staring at it for two weeks without finding the exit. I want a diagnosis, not a reading list.

Background so we're on the same page. For a Bayesian, E confirms H just in case learning E would make you more confident in H. In probabilities that's P(H|E) > P(H), which by Bayes is equivalent to P(E|H) > P(E). Now the old evidence problem: usually the facts a theory predicts are already known by the time the theory arrives. The anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion had been measured for decades before anyone could account for it; the broad shape of the fossil record was known before Darwin had a mechanism to offer. But if we already know E, then P(E) = 1, and nothing beats 1, so no known fact can confirm anything -- which would mean the celebrated episodes in the history of science weren't confirmations at all. Everyone agrees that can't be right, so there's a standard fix: don't use your present credences, use the ones you would have had before E entered your stock of knowledge. He calls this the ur-prior move.

His argument that the fix is too powerful:

(1) E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H), iff P(E|H) > P(E).
(2) If E is already known, P(E) = 1, so no known fact confirms anything.
(3) Fix: rerun the calculation from an ur-prior that leaves E out.
(4) Before you have any evidence, no hypothesis on the table is privileged over any other; the ur-prior is symmetric across them.
(5) So once the fix is in place, the only thing that can favour H over a rival H* is the likelihood, P(E|H) against P(E|H*) -- the Bayes factor.
(6) So for any fact E I already know, a hypothesis that entails E beats a rival that doesn't.
(7) And there's always such a hypothesis: take 'E holds, and otherwise the world is however it likes'.
(8) So every fact I know is fresh evidence for some hypothesis cooked up to fit it.
(9) So the fix makes old evidence confirm anything you like, including theories assembled backwards out of the data -- which is the one thing confirmation was supposed to stop.

I'd much rather keep (3) than lose it -- without it Bayesianism can't say the good episodes in science were confirmations at all -- but I want to know what I'm paying.
