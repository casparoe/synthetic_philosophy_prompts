I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Thomson's lamp, and by the end we were defending two different diagnoses of the puzzle rather than two different answers to it. He holds what he called the last-term principle: an infinite sequence of flips cannot be completed, because completing it would require a last flip, and where there is no last term there is nothing to fix the lamp's state; on his view the puzzle is a reductio on the completed supertask, and the question of the lamp's state at the two-minute mark is not difficult but empty. I hold that the flips are all completed — the timings are fully specified and every flip occurs before the two-minute mark — and that my description fixes the history of the lamp without fixing its state at the limit; nothing in the sequence settles whether it is on or off, and any answer we give is a stipulation smuggled into the description rather than a consequence of it.

I would like these two readings compared on their merits, not a survey of what has been written about the puzzle. Specifically:

(i) Is the impossibility reading doing any work the indeterminacy reading cannot do, or does it simply assume that a state can only be settled by a last term — an assumption the indeterminacy reading denies?

(ii) Suppose the switch is not a toggle but a setter, and that every instruction after some finite stage sets the lamp to off. The sequence is then infinite, has no last term, and yet the lamp is off at every time after that stage, so its state at two minutes is settled. If that variant is coherent, what becomes of the last-term principle, and does it leave the indeterminacy reading untouched or show that indeterminacy was never really about the missing last term?

(iii) Take the urn case: at each stage before noon ten balls go in and the lowest-numbered ball comes out, so the ball numbered n leaves at stage n and the urn is empty at noon. Again there is no last stage, yet the final state is determined, because each ball's fate is decided at some finite stage. Is the real contrast, then, between histories in which every element's fate is decided somewhere and histories in which the limit state is left open? If so, which reading does that contrast favour?

(iv) If the indeterminacy reading is right, does it imply that there is no fact of the matter about the lamp, or does it collapse into the claim that the lamp is on or off but that its being so is a brute fact with no explanation?

End with a recommendation: which diagnosis I should adopt, and what the other one sees that I would be giving up.
