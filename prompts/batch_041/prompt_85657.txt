Write a dialogue between two theorists of perception — an Orwellian and a Stalinesque — each of whom thinks the other has mislocated the moment at which experience is fixed. The labels are Dennett's, borrowed from Orwell's rewriting of the historical record and from Stalin's show trials, in which the verdict is settled before the proceedings begin.

Set the phenomenon out precisely, because the argument turns on its details. In the standard demonstration, a blue dot flashes near the top of a screen and, 200 milliseconds later, a red dot flashes near the bottom. Observers report a single dot that travels from the first position to the second and turns red about halfway along its path. The awkward fact is that the midpoint of the path is occupied, in real time, before the red dot has appeared; so the reported color change at the midpoint cannot be a report of anything the observer was in a position to witness as it happened. The Orwellian holds that the observer did undergo a genuine experience, which was subsequently edited in memory, much as a regime might alter the archive. The Stalinesque holds that nothing was ever experienced and later falsified: the perceptual process itself constructed a tidy, already-resolved content and presented only that.

Then have them argue about a hypothetical experiment I have invented, not about the familiar one. On each trial, after the second dot has vanished, a tone sounds at a delay of 0, 800, or 2000 milliseconds and instructs the subject to answer one of two questions: (a) what color the traveling dot was when it reached the midpoint of its path; or (b) whether the dot turned red before or after a small white square that was flashed midway between the two positions 100 milliseconds after the first dot. Suppose the experiment returns the following pattern: the color probe yields the interpolated midpoint color at every delay, essentially unchanged; the order probe is uninformative at the shortest delay but drifts steadily with longer delays, so that subjects increasingly place the color change before the white square, even though the square physically preceded the red dot.

The two characters should do the following. Each should state, in mechanistic terms, what his hypothesis commits him to about what the brain does and when, and should say whether the stipulated pattern was already predicted by his view or is an embarrassment to it. Each should give the strongest available reply to the other's reading of the drift. At least once, one of them should press the objection that the dispute presupposes a single finish line at which processing becomes experience, and is therefore ill-posed; the other should answer rather than deflect. They should end by either designing a test that genuinely discriminates between the two hypotheses or explaining, on principled grounds, why no test can — and each should concede whatever the exchange has actually cost him.

Neither character may appeal to the literature by name; the argument must stand on its own. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should talk past the other: each reply should engage the specific point just made.
