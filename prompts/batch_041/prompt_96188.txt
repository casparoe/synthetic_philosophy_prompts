A colleague in my strategy seminar reaches for the same analogy whenever an escalation comes up, and before I go on arguing with him I want a proper audit of it. The analogy is the dollar auction: a one-dollar bill is auctioned to two bidders who raise each other in fixed increments, the highest bidder pays what he bid and collects the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what he bid and receives nothing. He applies it to a race we both follow, in which two firms spend heavily to be first to win a single exclusive licence, the winner taking the market and the loser's spending being stranded. His reading is that the escalation is neither a failure of nerve nor a failure of arithmetic: once the money is in, quitting means it buys nothing, while staying in preserves a chance of a return, so each further step is locally justified and the two of them are carried past the point at which the prize was worth the outlay. He concludes that it is a mistake to call either firm irrational or blameworthy, and that the only real remedies are to change the rules of the game or to decline to enter it. Against my objection that this is just the sunk cost fallacy in a new suit, he insists that the dollar auction is no illustration of that fallacy, since the runner-up's bid is owed whether or not he keeps bidding.

What I want is the audit, not a verdict on the two firms. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the auctioneer, the two bidders, the increment, the dollar, the second-highest-pays rule, the rule that the rules themselves are fixed and externally given, and the terminal condition — and then tell me where the mapping breaks: which of those elements has no counterpart in the race, which has a counterpart that behaves differently, and which of the differences are load-bearing for his conclusions rather than decorative. In particular, say whether his two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the dollar auction actually settles the question he takes it to settle about the rationality of continuing. I would also like to know which single rule of the auction would have to change for the trap to disappear, and whether the corresponding change has any analogue in the race. If the dollar auction turns out to be the wrong model, name the right one and say what changes when it is swapped in.
