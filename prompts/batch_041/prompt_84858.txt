I cover cryptography and security for a science desk, and something about the whole enterprise nags at me. When a cryptographer says a scheme is secure, what they mean is that no adversary running in polynomial time can break it — thats the definition, thats it. So the secrecy of bank transfers and messaging apps rests on a claim about which computations are hard, plus a bet that something like factoring stays hard forever. What I want to know is what philosophy that commits them to. Is computational hardness supposed to be a fact about the world, like the mass of an electron, or a fact about us and our machines — and if its the latter, how is that different from saying a lock is safe because nobody has built the bolt cutters yet? Is "polynomial time" doing real work here or is it a stand-in for "feasible" that just happens to be mathematically convenient? Same question for pseudorandomness: a stream that no feasible test can distinguish from random is treated as random for all purposes — is that operationalism about randomness, and is it honest? And when someone proves that breaking the scheme would solve a hard problem, why should that reassure a bank? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me which parts are load-bearing and which are just convention, and say where the practice would fall apart if you swapped the theory out. No citations, I just want the argument.
