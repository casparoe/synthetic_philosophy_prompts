I sit on the ethics committee at the teaching hospital where I work as a critical care nurse, and we have spent our last three meetings rewriting the protocol that governs donation after circulatory death — the pathway in which life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn, the patient's circulation stops, and, if it has not restarted within a fixed interval (ours is five minutes), death is declared and organ retrieval begins. Drafting it has made me realise that I hold a handful of views about death that I have never tried to line up with one another, and I would like you to tell me whether they can all stand together. (1) Whether someone is dead is a fact about a biological organism, not a decision that a committee or a family makes; there is a right answer even when we cannot agree what it is. (2) The right criterion is the irreversible cessation of all function of the entire brain, brainstem included — which is what our hospital's brain-death testing is designed to establish. (3) What death takes from a person, and the reason it is a harm rather than a merely biological event, is the loss of the capacity for consciousness; that is the thing I take myself to be tracking when I ask whether someone is dead. (4) The dead donor rule is absolute: we may never bring about a patient's death in order to obtain organs, and organs may be taken only from someone who is already dead. (5) Our donation-after-circulatory-death protocol is ethically sound and I would want it offered to a member of my own family. (6) A patient in a persistent vegetative state, with a working brainstem but no evidence of awareness, is alive, though it is often right to stop feeding them.

What I want is not a summary of the literature on brain death but an audit of my own position. I suspect that at least one of these commitments has to go, but I cannot see which, and I would rather find out now than in front of the committee. Which of them pull against each other, and how badly? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally troublesome, and for each one say precisely what would have to be given up, what that would cost me, and whether there is a cheaper repair that leaves the rest of the set intact. Tell me also which of the apparent conflicts are not real conflicts at all — I would much rather have one or two of them dissolved than have everything flagged as a problem. Our medical director has been needling me on one point in particular, that a word like 'irreversible' cannot do the work I want it to do in a protocol that declares death five minutes after the pulse stops; I have been treating that as a verbal quibble, but say so plainly if I am wrong about it.

Finally, throughout, separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is contested, and do not let a contested position pass as the consensus. Where your own verdict is itself contestable, say so and give the strongest line against it. Please write in American English.
