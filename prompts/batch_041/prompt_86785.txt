I run pricing at a mid-size consumer-goods firm, and our largest rival now runs a model that anticipates us: within hours of our posting a price, theirs is set at whatever level takes the margin out of it. My analysts keep making two arguments I cannot adjudicate — the first says that posting the price they will punish is bad news about what is about to happen to us, so we should avoid it; the second says their response is already fixed by their forecast, which our posting does not cause, so we should simply pick the price that is best given the response we expect. This is the structure of the Newcomb-style problems, and of the case in which the appointment with death is wherever you end up, so that fleeing the city is itself the news that death waits where you are going, and I want to know what that literature implies for a Tuesday afternoon.

Write me a decision procedure my team can run in twenty minutes before we post: the questions to ask in order about a single pricing decision, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the pricing committee because the question is which decision rule the firm is committed to rather than a fact about the market. The diagnostics should be things we can actually check — whether their price moves before ours or only after, whether both move only after a common signal such as an input-cost or demand shock, whether their model is trained on our past behavior, and what becomes of our analysis if it has been trained on this procedure as well. I have no decision-theory background, though I am comfortable with regressions and lead-lag comparisons, and the committee will not accept a procedure that requires probabilities we cannot estimate in the time available.

Run it on three cases: a rival who reliably undercuts us a day after we post; a rival whose price tracks ours only because a common supplier cost moves us both; and a rival whose model is so good that every price we post is met with exactly the response that makes it the wrong price. In that last case I want to know whether the procedure can do anything except report that the two arguments diverge and that the choice between them is a standing commitment we have to make once and live with. Say plainly where the procedure misfires — where it needs data we will not have in time, where the two arguments coincide and the apparatus is pointless, and where it will recommend something my sales director will read as surrender.
