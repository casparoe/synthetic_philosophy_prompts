I'm in a small reading group that has been working through the anthropic-reasoning literature, and we keep stalling on the Boltzmann-brain argument: someone lays it out in the usual way, everyone feels the vertigo, and then we realize we cannot tell whether the conclusion is being driven by the cosmology or by the self-locating principle we quietly imported at the start. I'd like you to build us a single thought experiment that pulls those two apart. The specification is this: give me a case with exactly two hypotheses about the world's large-scale structure, both physically serious (finite, no appeal to an infinite or eternally inflating universe, nothing beyond ordinary statistical mechanics plus standard cosmology), such that under both hypotheses the ratio of Boltzmann-brain observers to ordinary observers is the same number, and yet the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption yield different answers to the question "how confident should I be that I am a Boltzmann brain?" The divergence has to fall out of the two principles themselves, not out of a stipulation buried in the setup, and the case should be describable in a paragraph a non-physicist could follow.

What I want isolated is one variable: the total number of observers, or observer-moments, in the reference class. Hold everything else fixed across the two hypotheses — the fluctuation rate that produces brains, the proportion of brains to ordinary observers, the content of the apparent memories, the fact that I find myself having them. If the variable cannot be isolated cleanly, tell me so and explain what blocks it rather than papering over it, and be explicit about what reference class you are using and why.

After the scenario, do the check: state each hypothesis's numbers, state what the self-sampling assumption says and why, state what the self-indication assumption says and why, and give the credence each yields for the Boltzmann-brain hypothesis. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work; what happens to the disagreement if that feature too is held fixed across the hypotheses; and how a committed defender of whichever principle comes out worse would try to defuse the case — by restricting the reference class, by denying that the two hypotheses are genuinely distinct, by shifting to observer-moments rather than observers, or otherwise. Finish by flagging any point at which the case is really just the Doomsday argument in costume.
