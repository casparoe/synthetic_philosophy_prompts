A friend of mine is building a little program that has to design a whole alphabet in one consistent style -- you hand it a handful of letters and it has to invent the rest so that the whole set looks like it came from the same hand. It's basically that Hofstadter Letter Spirit thing, and the puzzle underneath it is the one that's been eating at me: what actually makes every 'a' an 'a'? We've been going back and forth about it. His view is the prototype story -- somewhere in your head there's a fuzzy average 'a', and anything close enough to that central blob counts as an 'a', while "style" is just decoration smeared on top of a core shape that exists independently. I don't buy it. I think a letter is defined by the job it does and its place in the system -- the fact that it's this vowel, that it contrasts with these particular other glyphs, that it has to be able to be the first letter of a word and so on -- and I think the style isn't a layer on top at all, it's part of what the identity even is. Could you actually compare these two pictures for me, on the hard cases rather than in the abstract?

Here's what I'd want the comparison to do real work on. Take a glyph that sits right between an 'a' and an 'o' -- is it a bad 'a', a strange 'e', or not a letter at all? Or a typeface where the 'a' is drawn exactly like an ordinary 'e' in a normal font: is that a badly made 'a', a weird 'e', or evidence that the whole idea of a fixed shape-identity is broken? Or handwriting, where the very same squiggle reads as 'a' in one word and 'o' in another depending on what's around it -- does that sink the prototype view, or just mean the prototype is context-sensitive? And the one I keep coming back to: an alphabet where every single letter is slightly off, nothing is a clean instance of anything, and yet the set hangs together and you can still read it fluently. My friend says that's just noisy prototype matching; I say the identity lives in the relations among the letters, not in any one shape. So -- which of these does each view actually handle, where does each one have to cheat or quietly add machinery, and is there any version of the prototype idea that survives all of it, maybe one where the prototype turns out not to be a shape at all? Skip the citations and the who-said-what, I just want the two positions taken seriously and a verdict on which cases are decisive and which both sides can just absorb.
