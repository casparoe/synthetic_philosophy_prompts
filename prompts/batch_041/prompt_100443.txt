I cover science and ideas for a magazine, so I end up sitting in on a lot of expert panels — hospital ethics committees, scientific advisory boards, that kind of thing — and I keep hitting a pattern I don't have a word for. Take a hospital ethics committee I shadowed last year. They had to settle four things: whether the patient had capacity, whether the patient was refusing treatment, whether that refusal was properly informed, and then whether to treat anyway. They voted on each one separately. Majority said the patient had capacity. Majority said the refusal was informed. Majority said that if someone has capacity and refuses treatment in an informed way, you don't treat them. And then a majority voted to treat. Nobody in the room batted an eye; I only saw it when I went back through the minutes. A different board did the opposite — put a single bottom-line question to the room and got an answer that none of their separate votes would have predicted, so the "same" body gave two different verdicts depending on how the question was put. When I ask people about this they either shrug and say its just Arrow's theorem or say the group was simply being inconsistent, and neither feels right: every member was perfectly consistent on their own, and voting issue-by-issue vs. voting on the conclusion doesn't look like one right way and one wrong way, more like two different things being asked.

Start by brainstorming what might be going on before you commit to an answer. Then: is there a name for this? I don't want just the label — tell me what the phenomenon actually is, how it differs from Arrow-style results and from an ordinary Condorcet cycle and from a flat "the group was incoherent," which of the nearby concepts fits best, whether it makes a difference if you aggregate the sub-questions or only the final verdict, and where the edges of the thing are — cases that look like it but aren't. Answer as a philosopher who works on group decision-making and is good at telling near-neighbours apart.
