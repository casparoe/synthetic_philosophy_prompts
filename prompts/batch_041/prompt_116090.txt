My cousin's kid had that operation where they cut the cable between the two halves of her brain — the corpus callosum — to stop her seizures. It worked pretty well, she's back in school, and honestly everyone talks about her the same way they did before: same kid, same jokes, one person. But then I fell down a rabbit hole reading about the experiments people run on patients like her and it got weird. If you flash a picture into just one side of her vision, she can reach out with her left hand and pick up the matching object while saying out loud that she has no idea what she's doing or why. Sometimes the two hands go after the same thing and fight over it, one trying to put the sock on and the other pulling it off. And apparently one half of the brain will just make up a story explaining what the other half did, and believe it. So in the lab it's two minds in one head, or at least two streams of experience running side by side. In the clinic it's one patient: one chart, one consent form, one set of pain meds, one anaesthetic plan. And when the two sides disagree the mouth wins — whatever she says out loud is what goes down as what she wants, and when she says she feels like one person, that settles it. Her mum asked the surgeon whether her daughter was still "in there" and he said of course she is, she's the same girl.

What I can't work out is what the whole setup is actualy committed to. Treating the talking half as the patient — does that mean we're assuming consciousness goes with language, or just with whatever can report on itself? If so, is the other half a person, or a zombie, or a bit of machinery with nobody home? And taking her word for it that she's one person, does that presuppose being unified is the sort of thing you can just look inside and check, i.e. that there's nothing it's like to be split? Or is the unity that matters a fact about the person rather than about the stream of experience, so that it survives the surgery by definition?

The part that really bugs me is that we seem to run two completely different books. Two minds when there's a lab and a grant, one mind when there's a chart and a prescription. Is that just an inconsistency, or is there a story where both are true at once — maybe the lab manufactures the split and ordinary life doesn't, or maybe the two halves share enough through the brainstem and the outside world to count as one thing? I'm not after a reading list, I want the implicit theory spelled out: what this practice strictly has to believe, what it merely fits nicely with, where it's actually neutral between rival views of consciousness, and then what would have to change day to day if we took the two-minds line seriously — who signs the consent form for the operation that might create the second one, do you have to put both under for surgery, does the hand that isn't speaking get its own pain relief, whose name goes on the chart.
