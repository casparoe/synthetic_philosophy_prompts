I've been reading about how the NHS in Britain decides which medicines to pay for. They work out a cost per quality-adjusted life year and if it comes out above some threshold, thirty thousand pounds or so, they usually just say no. So a pill that gives a hundred people one extra decent year each gets judged the same as one that gives a single person a hundred years, and nobody's age or how well off they are comes into it at all. It's just years, added up.

Somebody told me theres a proof by a man called Harsanyi that if you accept a couple of innocent sounding things, that we can put how well off different people are onto one scale, and that when you're deciding for a whole society you should do it as if you didn't know which of the people you'd turn out to be, then you're basically forced to just add everyone's wellbeing up equally. No extra weight for the badly off, nothing.

So here's what I want to know. When a health service rations care by cost per life year like this, what is it actually committed to? Does it really need Harsanyi's assumptions, or could you run the exact same policy while genuinely caring more about the worst off, the way most of us say we do? And the thing that really bugs me is the rescue business. The same system will spend basically whatever it takes to save one identifiable patient who's been in the news, way over the threshold, and yet refuse to fund a cheap drug that would help a thousand people nobody's heard of. If the practice really did presuppose that you just add up the life years equally, that looks like a flat contradiction. So tell me which parts are the real commitments and which are just habits or politics. And what would the whole thing have to look like if we took the opposite view, that we shouldn't be adding wellbeing up across people at all?
