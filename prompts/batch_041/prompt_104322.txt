I write a monthly column on philosophy and the sciences for a general-readership magazine, and for an upcoming piece on the philosophy of mathematics I want to build an interactive decision tree that lets readers discover which response to Benacerraf's dilemma they actually hold. The reader I have in mind knows some undergraduate philosophy but has never read the technical literature, so the instrument has to work off what people are willing to say about cases, not off which school they already claim to belong to. Please write that tree.

Start from a commitment almost everyone will grant — that ordinary arithmetical sentences such as 2 + 2 = 4 are true or false in much the way that sentences about tables are — and let each branch node be one of the further commitments on which the available positions genuinely divide: whether there is a domain of abstract objects for such sentences to be about; whether mathematical discourse must be given the same semantics as discourse about ordinary objects; whether knowledge of an object requires some causal or otherwise contact-like relation to it; whether a single determinate subject matter is needed for arithmetic to be objective; and whether the dilemma is a real problem at all or a pseudo-problem generated by a bad theory of meaning. Phrase every node as a question or a concrete case that a reader could answer without already knowing where it leads. Use at least these cases somewhere in the tree: a neuroscientist who claims that your brain reliably tracks the fact that 2 + 2 = 4 while no causal chain runs from the number 4 to your head, and who asks whether that is enough for mathematical knowledge; a platonist and a fictionalist who agree on every theorem they will ever prove, so that the question becomes whether they disagree about anything; and the fact that the standard set-theoretic reductions of the natural numbers deliver different sets for the number 3, which raises the question of what fixes arithmetic's subject matter.

The leaves should carry the names of positions — platonism, ante rem and in re structuralism, modal or eliminative structuralism, fictionalism, if-thenism, neo-logicism or abstractionism, formalism, and the quietist or anti-realist option that denies the dilemma — each with one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the names in it afterward, and where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say so and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Keep it to roughly seven branch nodes and no more than a dozen leaves, and please do not send me reading lists or citations; my readers cannot follow them and I cannot check them.
