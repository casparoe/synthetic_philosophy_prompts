In three weeks I am to deliver a lecture at an interdisciplinary seminar, and I mean to use the occasion to state and defend a thesis I arrived at while writing my last book. The audience will be roughly a dozen moral philosophers who work on human dignity and the foundations of human rights, two social psychologists whose research treats honor as a measurable cultural syndrome, one anthropologist who has done fieldwork on Mediterranean and Inner Asian honor, and a handful of literary critics. I am a novelist, not an academic, and I will be speaking without notes.

The thesis is this. The familiar ranking in which dignity cultures are the moral improvement upon honor cultures, and victimhood cultures are either the further improvement or the rot that follows, is not a ranking of moral worth at all; it is a ranking of which grammar of answerability happens to fit the institutions we already have. I distinguish the three not by how much they value the person but by where each locates the seat of a wrong, what each treats as the term of its repair, and whether the wrong can ever be closed. On my account, honor conceives a wrong as a subtraction from standing that must be answered in kind; dignity conceives it as a breach of an entitlement that must be answered by the recognition of a right; victimhood conceives it as an inheritance that must be answered by being witnessed, and that can therefore be answered without ever being discharged. Each grammar, I argue, has a characteristic pathology — the feud, the wrong that leaves no name, the debt that cannot be paid — and the moral question is not which grammar is best but which one can metabolize a wrong without manufacturing a permanent creditor. I expect the philosophers to hear a relativism I do not intend, the psychologists to hear a metaphor where they have measurements, and the anthropologist to hear a tidy taxonomy that the field will not bear.

Prepare me for the question period. Tell me the objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones I would get from a general audience or from readers who already know the book — ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them; phrase each one the way the person who will raise it would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine; give me a two-minute reply to each; and mark honestly where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections share a single root, so that I can see whether one strong answer would collapse several of them at once. Tell me what I should concede up front, so that I am not defending more than I need to. Tell me the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the hour, and the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
