I run a biweekly lunchtime reading group at the software company where I work. I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and the group is about sixteen people — mostly engineers and data scientists, plus one product manager who took a game theory course in college and will not let anyone forget it. We get 75 minutes in a conference room with a whiteboard, a projector nobody uses, and whatever I can carry in from the supply closet; there's no budget for anything, so any incentives have to be points, bragging rights, or the privilege of choosing the next session's topic. This month the topic is Aumann's agreement theorem, and I've concluded that talking about it is worse than useless. I explain that two agents who share a prior, are both rational, and have common knowledge of each other's opinions cannot agree to disagree; everyone nods; and then the same people spend the next twenty minutes disagreeing about whether the theorem says anything about real disagreements. What I want instead is an activity that makes the theorem's engine visible in the room: they should converge when they don't want to, and then, when I change one thing, they should keep disagreeing and be unable to fix it — and only at the end should I name the thing I changed.

Please design that activity in full. The shape I have in mind is a two-condition run. In the first condition everyone is given the same prior about the setup — a bag of balls, an urn, a factory spec, whatever you judge cleanest to run with index cards and no computer — and each person gets a private piece of evidence; then they repeatedly write down and reveal their probability of some target event and update on everyone else's revealed numbers. In the second condition the identical protocol runs, except that the room is split and each half is given a different prior. I care that they feel the first condition as a trap closing, the numbers dragging toward one another whether or not anyone wants them to, and the second condition as a release valve: the same honest, rational updating, and the disagreement simply sits there, stable. If the timing allows, I'd like a short third beat in which one participant is secretly instructed to break a different assumption, so the group can see which assumption had been doing the work. Include the exact rules, the materials and how to set them up in advance, the payoff scheme (no real money — I was thinking points scored against a ground truth revealed at the end, with the top scorer choosing the next session's topic, but I'm open to something better), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the theorem's moving parts: the common prior, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of the protocol, why the protocol operates on announced posteriors rather than on raw evidence, and the fact that the theorem guarantees agreement and not accuracy — I want them to notice that they may all march confidently to a wrong number, and I want the ground truth revealed only after they've converged, so the punchline is about what the theorem does and does not deliver. It would also help if the debrief says something about which of these assumptions a person is implicitly rejecting when they insist, in ordinary life, that reasonable people can disagree forever.

Two things I specifically need from you. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the ways people will miscompute a posterior, anchor on the loudest person, refuse to move off a number, or converge in a single round and leave twenty minutes of dead air — and what to do about each. Second, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I assume there is something about this theorem that depends on an idealization of knowledge and information — partitions of possibility, what it takes for a fact to be common knowledge rather than merely known by everyone — that a room full of people with index cards simply cannot instantiate, and I would rather learn that from you now than discover it when someone in the back asks and watches me flounder. Assume the facilitator is me: competent at running a room, not trained in any of this. Write it so that I could hand the rules to a colleague and have them run it without me.
