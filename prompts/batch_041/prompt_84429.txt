I am a retired experimental physicist. My working life was spent on precision measurement — inter-laboratory comparisons, blinded analyses, the long argument over whether two instruments were really measuring the same quantity — and for the past several years I have been reading philosophy in earnest. In three weeks I am to give a forty-minute talk to our university's machine learning group, and I would like a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a summary of my own talk.

My thesis is a narrow one. Debate-style oversight — the family of proposals in which two systems are trained to argue for opposing answers and a human or a weaker model judges the winner, on the assumption that a false case is easier to expose than a true one is to sustain — is best understood as an attempt to import the epistemology of adversarial institutions into machine learning, and I claim the import fails at one identifiable joint. Courtrooms, adversarial collaborations, and blinded multi-laboratory analyses work not because two parties contend but because the contention is eventually settled by something neither party controls: physical evidence, an independent laboratory, a measurement a stranger can repeat. Debate installs a judge in the place of that arbiter, and the judge is defined by not having independent access to the disputed fact. What debate can deliver, then, is the victory of whichever claim is cheaper to verify and dearer to counterfeit — a real and useful property, but a property of the question rather than of the method. The scalability claims made for debate, I will argue, quietly convert that property into a claim about the judge: that a weaker arbiter can be trusted to adjudicate stronger disputants. That is the one thing the setup cannot supply.

My argument has three steps: (1) adversarial procedures in law and in science track the truth only relative to an external check; (2) debate replaces the external check with a judge inside the loop; (3) therefore the assurance debate provides is bounded by the judge's competence on the disputed question. I am reasonably confident of (2), less confident of (1), since I have read enough philosophy of science to know it is contested, and least confident of (3), since there is a formal literature on this that I have not mastered.

The audience is about fifteen people: mostly PhD students and postdocs in machine learning who work on evaluation and oversight, two of whom have published on debate-style methods and will know the formal results far better than I do; one philosopher of science whose work is on Bayesian confirmation; one statistician whose day job is the design of clinical trials; and one colleague from the law school who teaches evidence. It is a friendly room and I am criticizing its subject matter, so I expect the objections to be courteous and lethal. I have nothing riding on this but my own accuracy, so please do not soften anything.

Give me the objections in the order they will actually come from this room, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — I want the statistician's point to sound like a statistician's and the evidence scholar's to sound like an evidence scholar's — with an honest ranking of how much damage each does, what I should concede in my opening remarks to take the cheap ones off the table, a two-minute reply for each, and a note on where the reply is soft or where I would be bluffing. Tell me which of my three steps is doing the least work and could be dropped to save the talk. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations, no reading list, no names of papers: if a point needs a name, describe it instead.
