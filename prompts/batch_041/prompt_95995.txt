A couple of friends of mine, Dan and Rina, spent most of a dinner party going at each other about whether voting is rational, and they wanted me to referee and I dodged it. Heres roughly how it went, as close as I can reconstruct it.

Dan's line was that the chance your vote decides anything is tiny — even in a genuinely close race he reckoned it's maybe one in tens of thousands, and in most races it's basically zero — so voting is a lottery ticket you already know is a loser. He said the honest description is that people vote for the feeling of it, and calling that a civic duty is just cheating. He also said his ballot is like a lever that was never attached to anything: he's never been in a situation where his vote changed the result, and he can't point to any world he can check where it would have.

Rina said he's computing the wrong quantity. Her point was that the value of voting isn't that you personally break a tie, it's that a bigger crowd of people each slightly better than a coin flip at picking the better option lands on the better option far more often than a small crowd does — so a vote is a contribution to getting a good outcome, not a bet on being decisive. And she added that if everyone reasoned the way Dan does there'd be no crowd left to get anything right.

Dan said that proves way too much, since the same argument would tell you to vote in a poll that doesn't count. He said the if-everyone-did-it move isn't a reason for him to do anything, because everyone isn't doing it — that's the whole problem — and him staying home is one more drop out of a bucket that's already mostly empty.

Rina came back with two things. First, the chance of a tie and the chance of mattering aren't the same number: recounts, margins, how a result gets read as a mandate, and the fact that your vote shifts what the people around you do, all widen the second one well past the first. Second, she said the probability isn't a fixed fact about the world anyway — it depends on how many people like him show up, so treating it as an external constant he can look up is the mistake underneath his whole argument.

Dan said that's circular: she's using the possibility that people like him stay home to make his staying home more consequential, and then using that to tell him to vote.

One more thing neither of them said, but it's been bugging me. Rina's crowd argument seems to need the crowd to be at least a little competent, and getting competent about politics is itself expensive; by Dan's own reasoning none of us has a reason to do that homework before voting. I genuinely can't tell whether that sinks her or hands Dan a worse problem.

Go through it move by move and say who's right on each one, then say who's got the better of it overall. Take a side and defend it — I don't want a survey of what people have written about this, or a list of considerations for me to weigh up myself. And give me a straight answer on that last bit about competence. Write it as plain prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just the way you'd explain it to me in an email.
