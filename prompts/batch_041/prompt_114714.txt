I'm an undergraduate taking a metaphysics course and I work part-time in my university museum's collections office. We repeatedly have to decide whether a conserved or reconstructed artifact is still the same object for cataloguing, loans, and insurance: a ceramic vessel with a replaced handle, a painting that has been relined and retouched, a fossil skeleton reassembled from fragments, a plaster cast taken from an original mold, a digital model substituted for a fragile original. We keep three things separate in principle—the accession identity (the catalogue number), the physical-object identity (the material thing), and the display identity (what visitors are shown)—but in practice they collapse, and our current rule of thumb, 'if most of the original material is gone, it's a new object,' produces fights because the archaeologist, the conservator, and the insurer are answering different questions. Please turn the material-constitution literature (the statue and the clay, sortal relativity, constitution without identity, four-dimensionalism, mereological essentialism) into a decision procedure my office can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with 'same object' or 'different object', and where it must hand the call to a person because the issue is policy or values rather than metaphysics. I have about ten minutes per item, and the registrar and curator have to sign off. Include two or three worked cases, at least one where the procedure yields 'different object' even though the museum would prefer 'same'. Then say what the procedure cannot do, and where the theory is too contested to settle the case. I'd rather have an honest, self-critical procedure that flags its limits than a tidy one that pretends metaphysics decides everything; if my framing is confused—for instance, if 'same object' is doing several different jobs at once—say so and suggest how to separate them.
