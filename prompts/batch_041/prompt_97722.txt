I'm in a graduate seminar on modal epistemology, and we have spent a couple of weeks on Fitch's paradox of knowability. I understand the standard derivation well enough: take an unknown truth, conjoin it with the proposition that it is unknown, apply the knowability principle, use the fact that knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, and land in contradiction. What I have not been able to get clear on is a variant that one of my classmates raised and that the seminar could not settle. Suppose we do not interpret the possibility in the knowability principle as metaphysical possibility for a single knower, but as something like epistemic possibility for a temporally extended community of inquirers: a truth is knowable if there is some feasible course of inquiry, spread across time and across many agents, at the end of which the community is in a position to know it. On that reading, the proposition that p and no one has yet known p can be known in stages: one investigator establishes p, another later establishes that p was not known before the first did so. Please explain whether the paradox survives this temporal and social indexing, and exactly where the standard derivation would have to be changed if it does. I am especially interested in whether the conjunction step, the factivity of knowledge, or the interpretation of the possibility operator is doing the real work, and whether there is a version of the knowability principle that is both independently plausible and immune to the paradox under this reading.

I would like you to take a definite position rather than surveying options: say whether you think the community-indexed version escapes the paradox, give your reasons, and identify the strongest objection to your own view and why it fails. If you think the paradox reappears in a different form, state precisely what the new contradiction is and what it costs to avoid it. Assume no access to the literature; I care about the conceptual mechanics, not about who said what.
