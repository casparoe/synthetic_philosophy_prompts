Two of my colleagues in the department — I'll call them A and B, since the point is the argument and not the personalities — spent most of our last reading-group session locked in a dispute about vagueness that neither could bring to a close, and since I was hosting and had to keep the coffee going, I never got to referee it properly. I'd like you to do that now, move by move. The occasion was a discussion of higher-order vagueness, and the illustration someone put on the whiteboard was a run of paint chips shading from an unmistakable red at one end to an unmistakable orange at the other, with a long middle stretch that nobody present was willing to call either way. A defends an epistemic account of vagueness, B a supervaluationist one; both know the textbook sorites cold, so the exchange got past the standard throat-clearing quickly and into the parts that actually matter. What I want is not a survey of the two positions but an adjudication of this particular exchange as it actually went.

Here is the sequence. (1) A said that there is a fact of the matter about the last red chip in the run — some chip is the final red one and the next is not — and that our inability to say which is not because the boundary is fuzzy but because our judgments are inexact: for any chip we fix on, a neighbouring chip would have drawn the opposite verdict from us, so a belief about the exact cutoff would be unsafe and therefore not knowledge. (2) B replied that this is the wrong diagnosis. On B's view there is no fact about the last red chip: a chip is red when it comes out red on every admissible way of sharpening the predicate, not-red when it comes out not-red on every such sharpening, and neither when the sharpenings disagree. The sorites is unsound, B said, but not through ignorance — its inductive premise simply fails to be true on the sharpenings where the boundary falls, without being false either. (3) A answered that this only relocates the problem: B says 'either the chip is red or it isn't' is true, true on every admissible sharpening, so B too has a sharp line — just drawn in the metalanguage instead of in the world — and B now owes an account of what makes a sharpening 'admissible.' If 'admissible' is itself vague, higher-order vagueness bites B exactly as it bites A; if it means 'one that a competent speaker wouldn't object to,' then B is leaning on an epistemic notion and is an epistemicist who won't say so. (4) B rejoined that the sharpness A is pointing at is a feature of the semantics, not of the world, and that the world can be thoroughly boundary-free while the semantics still assigns supertruth; that the higher-order problem is not asymmetric, since A needs a determinate notion of knowledge and of 'the last red chip' to state the margin-of-error principle at all, and 'knows' is vague in just the way 'red' is; and that A's posit of unknowable sharp boundaries is unconfirmable and does no explanatory work, whereas B's account explains the felt absence of a boundary as the genuine absence of a fact. (5) A conceded that 'knows' can be vague — A said this costs nothing — but insisted that what cannot be surrendered is bivalence and the disquotational schema, and charged that B keeps classical logic only by distinguishing truth from supertruth, so that the operator expressing 'definitely' no longer behaves classically (both 'not definitely red' and 'not definitely not red' can hold of the same chip); B has thus purchased object-language classicality with a non-classical logic of determinacy and a technical notion of truth that no longer tracks how things are.

Please work through the five moves and rate each one on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say what each party is missing — the consideration that, if either of them had raised it, would have moved the exchange forward. Finally, give me a committed overall verdict: which of the two, if either, has the better of the dispute, and on which specific points, and whether the disagreement at bottom is substantive or partly verbal. I would much rather you take a definite position and defend it than lay the options out and leave me to pick.
