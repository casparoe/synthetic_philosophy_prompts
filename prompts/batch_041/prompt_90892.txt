I have drafted a short essay on aesthetic testimony and I would like your comments on it. The draft follows.

Testimony and the Construction of Aesthetic Experience

Most discussions of aesthetic testimony take for granted a picture I want to question. The picture is this: aesthetic experience is a datum that is either present to you or not, and testimony is a proposition that either does or does not give you reason to believe something about that datum. The pessimist says the datum is private, so testimony about it cannot transmit knowledge; the optimist says testimony is just evidence like any other, so of course it can. Both sides agree that the experience is the thing and the testimony is a report about the thing.

I want to argue that this picture mislocates the work testimony does. Aesthetic experience is not, for the most part, a passive reception of a datum; it is an achievement, something you bring off by knowing how to look, listen, or read. Two people can stand before the same painting, one of whom sees a flat arrangement of shapes and the other of whom sees the tension between two adjacent blues, and the difference is not in their retinas but in the concepts and habits of attention they bring. If that is right, then the primary epistemic function of testimony is not to report a verdict but to supply the concepts and the habits of attention. Testimony does not hand you the experience; it hands you the equipment with which the experience is made.

I therefore want to distinguish two kinds of aesthetic testimony. Verdictive testimony says that something is beautiful, moving, or kitsch. Directive testimony says where to look or listen: notice that the figure's gaze never meets ours; hear the way the bass line refuses to resolve until the final bar. The first is the kind the literature fights about, and I think the pessimists are largely right about it, for a boring reason: a verdict you cannot cash in experience is a promissory note, and aesthetic verdicts are cashed in experience or not at all. The second kind is where the action is, and it is not really testimony in the epistemologists' sense at all. It is closer to a recipe. A recipe does not give you the meal, and no one thinks it should; what it gives you is a procedure whose execution yields the meal, and the knowledge it conveys is knowledge-how rather than knowledge-that.

This suggests that the optimist and the pessimist are both answering the wrong question. The optimist asks whether aesthetic testimony can be evidence for an aesthetic proposition. The pessimist asks whether it can produce the relevant experience. The interesting question is whether it can produce the competence that makes the experience possible. On that question the optimist is too thin, because a verdict is not enough, and the pessimist is too pessimistic, because a directive is not nothing.

There is an objection I can feel forming: this makes aesthetic testimony sound like instruction, and instruction is exactly what the acquaintance constraint was meant to rule out, since a critic's directives are only as good as the critic's experience, which you still cannot borrow. I think the objection lands against a strong version of my view but not against a weak one. The weak version says only that directive testimony can orient attention, not replace it; you still have to look. What the directive does is make you look in the right place, which is often most of the battle.

Two consequences I am unsure about. First, if the value of criticism lies mostly in directives rather than verdicts, then the familiar anxiety about whether criticism can be objective is misplaced, since a directive can be useful without being true in any robust sense. Second, the distinction may not be as clean as I have made it: most real criticism mixes verdict and directive, and a directive often presupposes a verdict. Telling someone to notice how the blues fight presupposes that they do fight, which is already a description with a verdict folded into it. I would like to know whether the distinction can be made to do real work or whether it collapses under that pressure.

Please comment on this draft. I am less interested in praise than in the following, and I would like you to address each point directly rather than in general terms.

First, where is the central argument weakest? In particular, is the premise that aesthetic experience is an achievement rather than a datum doing more work than it can bear, and what would a serious defender of the datum picture say in reply?

Second, is the verdictive/directive distinction a real distinction or a distinction without a difference? Given that most actual criticism mixes the two, and that a directive seems to presuppose some verdict, can the distinction survive, and if it does, what is the strongest form it can take?

Third, am I reinventing a position that already has a standard name and a standard set of objections? If so, name the position and tell me which of its standard objections I have failed to anticipate.

Fourth, what is the strongest reply available to someone who holds the acquaintance constraint, and does my concession to a weak version of my view give away too much?

Assume I know what I wrote, so do not summarize the essay back to me. Where you think I am simply wrong, say so plainly and give the reason.
