I am the head of analysis at a small risk-advisory firm. We publish short written assessments — three hundred words, roughly five a week, on a two-day clock — that clients act on directly; junior analysts with no philosophical training draft them, and I sign off, usually in ten minutes. Some years ago I put Nozick's tracking theory into our style guide as the house standard for assertion: we may state that P only if P is true, we believe P, we would not have believed P had P been false, and we would have believed P had P been true. It has never been operationalized. In practice the analysts read it as a licence to write whatever their sources suggest, and the real decision gets made by feel at sign-off. Nozick also denied that knowledge is closed under known entailment, which matters to us, because most of our assessments are chains of inference ending in a conclusion no source ever states.

Please turn the standard into a procedure the desk can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns "no basis to assess," and the points where it hands the decision to me because the question is about our risk appetite rather than about the evidence. Three difficulties should be handled rather than waved away. (i) The last two conditions are counterfactuals about our own process, and they are vacuous or unsatisfiable depending on how the process is described — "this analyst with this source this week" makes everything sensitive, "our desk's usual method" makes almost nothing sensitive. The procedure needs a default rule for fixing the reference class, a way to record a contested reference class, and a statement of what turns on the choice. (ii) Closure fails, so we cannot assert the conjunction of two claims we can each assert, and a derived conclusion does not inherit the status of its premises; a procedure that takes this seriously will forbid some assessments I would very much like to publish. (iii) Claims about concealed conduct and claims of absence behave oppositely under the two counterfactual tests, and I want to know which test bites in each case, and which error each test is protecting us against.

Run the procedure on three cases: (a) a source plus a documentary trail indicating that a named shipping firm moves sanctioned cargo through a third-country port, where the firm would conceal the practice whether or not it were engaged in it, and the client is paying for the sentence "we assess that the firm is doing this"; (b) the client's question whether a named port is used for transshipment, where we have looked and found nothing, and the analysts want to publish "there is no evidence that the port is used for transshipment" — I want the procedure to distinguish not having heard from having looked and failed to find; (c) twelve separately documented procurement irregularities, each solid on its own, from which a colleague wants to conclude that a ministry is systematically corrupt.

Deliver a numbered procedure, a short ladder of verdict labels that every stopping point maps onto, a one-paragraph version an analyst can hold in their head, and a sign-off checklist for me. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and tell me what the procedure cannot do — I would rather have one with a known failure mode than one that appears to settle everything.
