I host a small interview series on applied ethics, and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent several years arguing that moral trade is among the great unexploited opportunities in ethics. Her position, as I understand it, is this. When two parties disagree about what matters and each can affect outcomes the other cares about, they can often strike a deal in which each does something the other wants and each comes out ahead by their own lights: not a split-the-difference compromise, but a Pareto improvement in each party's own value system. She thinks such trades are far more common and far more valuable than people recognize, and — this is the part I want to press hardest — that there are very few principled limits on what may be traded, the real obstacles being coordination, information, and enforcement rather than morality. I am the interviewer, so what I need from you is questions, not answers.

Draft eight to ten questions for the interview, each with a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should turn on the content of the position rather than on her biography or on the general appeal of cooperation, and they should include at least one question on each of the following:

- why the trade is supposed to be a win for both parties rather than a compromise, and what must be true of their values for that to work — in particular, whether each party's willingness to trade a loss on one issue for a gain on another commits her to a single exchange rate across incommensurable goods, and what that does to her pluralism;
- what happens when the counterparty's preferred outcome is something appalling: whether the deal is still permissible, how the counterfactual baseline against which the trade is measured gets fixed, and whether a concession made to someone who is threatening harm is an offer or a ransom;
- the incentive worry I keep returning to: if concessions can be bought, does a market in moral trades reward people for holding, and credibly threatening to act on, ever more extreme positions, so that the practice corrodes the values it is meant to serve?
- whether everything really is tradeable, or whether some goods — rights, dignities, and above all the treatment of third parties who are not party to the deal — are off the table, and what her answer implies in cases where the gain on one side of the ledger is enormous;
- what moral uncertainty does to the picture: whether an agent who is unsure of her own values can identify a win at all, and whether uncertainty about the good makes trade easier or harder to justify.

For at least three of the questions, the follow-up should anticipate the specific move she is likely to make — distinguishing a threat from an offer, invoking the counterfactual baseline, retreating to the claim that trade is voluntary — and press on that move rather than merely asking her to say more. Close with a question about what evidence or argument would change her mind about trade being so widely available, so that the interview does not end on a summary.
