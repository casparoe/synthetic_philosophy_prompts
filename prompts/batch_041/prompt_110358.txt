For the past several months I have been one of four people who decide how to spend a modest annual fund — a few hundred thousand dollars — on the charities we judge to do the most good. I joined assuming that "which grant does the most good?" was difficult but well-posed, and I am leaving each meeting less sure that the question means anything at all. I can trace a grant's first-order effects and then the trail goes cold: the second-order effects on families, on institutions, on the distant future are beyond anyone's power to predict, and I no longer think this is merely a matter of missing data. I suspect this is the central problem for any consequentialism rather than a practical nuisance, but I want a map of the terrain before I decide whether to keep doing the work. Please build me a classification instrument rather than an essay.

The deliverable is a decision tree. It should take a reader from the starting commitment "I should try to bring about the best consequences" and route them, by way of the questions that actually divide people, to a named position about what our inability to foresee the consequences of our actions does to that commitment. The reader is a philosophically literate non-specialist — my co-grantmakers — so the prose should be plain, but the distinctions should be the real ones and not smoothed over for comfort.

Every internal node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to; no node should be "do you accept the criterion/decision-procedure distinction?" or anything that names its own destination. Aim for eight to twelve nodes and six to ten leaves. Please include, at minimum, leaves for: (a) the view that cluelessness dissolves once we evaluate actions by expected value under our best available credences; (b) the view that it is a pseudo-problem arising from confusing the standard of rightness with a method of deciding; (c) the view that we should keep maximizing but confine ourselves to interventions that are robustly good across a wide range of scenarios; (d) the view that we should switch from assessing acts to cultivating rules, dispositions, or institutions; (e) the view that the standard of rightness tracks actual consequences while our decision procedure may only track foreseeable ones; (f) the exit to non-consequentialist ethics; and (g) the view that cluelessness is no special problem for consequentialism because it afflicts every first-order moral theory equally. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or "unnamed" if no one defends it, plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the named positions in it second. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so explicitly and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Where two leaves cannot really be separated by any question in the tree, flag that rather than papering over it.

Finally, throughout the response, mark clearly what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
