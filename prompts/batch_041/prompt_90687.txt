I am in a philosophy of biology seminar and have to write a paper taking a side in a dispute that I can state but not yet settle. The dispute concerns what it is for a biological trait to have a function. One family of views locates a trait's function in its history: very roughly, a trait's function is the effect for which it was favored, so that an organ which has stopped doing its old job still has that job as its function, and a structure that arose only as an incidental by-product of selection acting on something else has no function of its own. The other family locates a trait's function in its present contribution: very roughly, a trait's function is the part it plays in some larger capacity or activity of the system that contains it, so that a structure which has just appeared and helps the organism survive has a function from its first day even if nothing ever selected for it, while a vestige that contributes nothing has no function at all. Each view seems clearly right about some cases and clearly strained by others. The historical view makes good sense of malfunction (an organ can fail to do what it is for), of vestiges, and of the difference between what a trait is for and what it merely happens to do, but it appears to deny functions to evolutionary novelties and to make every function claim hostage to phylogenetic facts nobody knows. The contribution view handles novelty and present usefulness easily, but then has trouble saying what a malfunctioning organ is doing, and it threatens to assign a function to any effect that keeps some larger process running, including effects no biologist would call functions. I also cannot tell whether the two sides are describing one phenomenon or talking past each other about two different ones, and I would like the paper to say something definite about that.

Here is the specific thing I want to work through. A friend in the seminar, impatient with both camps, proposes that we define a trait's function as the effect whose absence would explain the trait's disappearance — that is, the effect such that if the trait were to stop producing it, the trait would be lost from the population. He insists this is neither of the two views above: it looks forward rather than backward, it needs no information about the trait's ancestry, and it makes malfunction central rather than awkward, since an organ that cannot produce the effect on which the trait's persistence depends is thereby malfunctioning. He also thinks it escapes the contribution view's tendency to overgenerate, since a mere side effect that does not hold the trait in place does not count. I am not sure whether this is a genuine third option, a restatement of the historical view in the present tense, or a muddle, and that is what I want the paper to decide.

So: write the essay. Reconstruct both standard positions fairly and pin down exactly which cases each handles well and which it fudges. Then put my friend's criterion through the hardest cases you can find — a vestigial structure that no longer does anything; an organ that has taken on a new role while still bearing the marks of its old one; a structure that persists only because it is physically fused to something else that is under selection; a trait in an organism that was engineered rather than evolved; and a creature assembled by accident, with no evolutionary history at all. Say what each account delivers in each case, then commit. Does my friend's criterion improve on both rivals, collapse into one of them, or fail on its own terms? Do not end on the note that each account captures part of the truth; tell me which account I should adopt, what I must give up in adopting it, and which single case would do most to overturn your verdict. If you think the whole dispute is verbal — that the word is doing two or three different jobs and the disagreement dissolves once they are separated — then argue for that instead, but only if you are prepared to defend it against the objection that it is a way of dodging the question.
