I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I am six months into a feature about a woman who spent a decade inside a violent extremist movement, left it in her late thirties, publicly renounced it, and now works for a foundation that tries to talk others out. My editor's brief is a redemption story: she chose to become someone else, and that choice is the point of the piece. I have many hours of interviews in which she refuses the brief, and her refusal is not modesty. It is an argument, and I cannot find the flaw in it.

Her version runs roughly as follows. She was recruited at seventeen, in a house where the ideas were already ambient, by a man who happened to sit beside her on a bus. Her twin brother, raised in the same house, joined the same movement the same year; he is in prison now and has never wavered. She did not choose her temperament, she says, or her susceptibility to that particular pitch, or the fact that a stranger's offhand remark landed the way it did. The only thing she will claim is that she was willing to listen — and she will not claim credit for being willing, since being willing was simply the kind of person she turned out to be, and she did not make that either. Her brother, on her account, is no worse than she is. He was made differently, and neither of them did the making. Asked what she thinks made the difference between them, she says she has no idea, and that this is precisely her point. She then extends the reasoning to the men who recruited her: not to blame, because they too were made. That last step is where I begin to suspect the argument has swallowed more than she intends, though I cannot locate the overreach.

The foundation's donors give because they believe the choice story, my editor wants the choice story, and I would like to publish something true. Help me think this through. Is her reasoning sound? If it is, what may I honestly say about her in print — is there a way to describe her that neither credits her with an authorship she denies nor treats her as a wind-up toy? If it is not sound, where exactly does it break, and does the break happen at the same place for the recruiters? I would rather not have the usual hedge about holding people accountable while honoring complexity; I want the actual structure of the problem and where the load-bearing joint is.
