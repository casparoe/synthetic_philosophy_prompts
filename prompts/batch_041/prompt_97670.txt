I spent 40 years as a flavour chemist before I retired, and now I'm trying to teach myself philosophy in the evenings. I just read a piece in a science magazine about Daniel Dennett's essay Quining Qualia, and I honestly can't tell how much of it to believe.

The gist of the piece: Dennett says anybody who believes in qualia is committed to four things about them — that they're ineffable, that they're intrinsic, that they're private, and that you know them just by having them. Then he runs alot of thought experiments to show the whole idea falls apart. The one the writer dwells on is two veteran coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who both go off their usual blend; one says the coffee has changed, the other says his palate has. Since neither can produce any evidence for his version, Dennett concludes that qualia are a fiction, and that there is really nothing it is like to taste coffee at all — what actually happens is just that you're disposed to say mmm or yuck. The writer says the upshot is that any claim about your own qualia is meaningless, because no experiment could ever settle a disagreement about them, and that Dennett is basically a behaviourist. He ends by saying that since I can be perfectly certain of my own toothache right now, and Dennett has to deny that, the whole argument is refuted.

Here's my problem. I'm sympathetic to the debunking — forty years of measuring things will do that to a person — but the "nothing it is like" part seems like it must be an overreach, and the toothache move at the end looks too cheap to be a real refutation. Where does the writer have Dennett right, where is he sliding, and is there anything in the summary that Dennett himself would wince at? Does the toothache point actually refute anything, or is it just refusing to engage?

And I have a practical question left over from my old line of work. In my lab we would never have left the taster puzzle alone: same batch of coffee, same brewing, and if the taster's receptors for the bitter compounds had drifted, or he'd lost his sense of smell after a virus, then we'd say the coffee didn't change, he did. We could even have put the two of them in a scanner and predicted their ratings before they gave them. Does that sort of evidence rescue the taster's story and show that the two of them really are having different experiences? Or does Dennett's point survive all of it — i.e., even with the receptors and the scanner in hand, is he still claiming there's no fact of the matter about what the coffee tastes like to each of them? I can't tell wether my instinct here is doing philosophy or just nostalgia for my old lab.
