Write a short story, of roughly 1,500 words, that dramatizes one of Camus's arguments against the death penalty. In bare form: capital punishment was once intelligible as a religious act — an absolute judgment that deprived the condemned of life and handed him over to a God who might yet grant him atonement — but in a society that no longer believes in that framework, the rite survives with its form intact and its meaning gone, leaving the state to dispose of a person it has declared irremediable. Set the story in such a society, where executions are lawful, private, and routine, and make the protagonist a functionary who keeps the procedure running smoothly — the person who services the apparatus, prepares the room, or walks the condemned through the last day — not a judge, a defendant, or a priest. Let the official justification be deterrence and public safety, while what the community actually takes from the death shows only in behavior and in what no one will say aloud. Build the story around the condemned's wait: he has known the date for months, a cruelty no private murderer inflicts on his victim, and the sentence forecloses any possibility that he might change. No character should state the argument; let the reader assemble it. The condemned should be neither innocent nor a monster, and the story should not resolve whether the execution was just.
