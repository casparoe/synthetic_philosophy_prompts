I'm putting together a session for a small philosophy-of-mind reading group and I want to pair Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon with Wittgenstein's private language argument. Not Memento — the novel, the one with the progress reports and the mouse. I want a reading of the book through the argument and of the argument through the book, and I want you to say plainly where they don't fit. No citations or page refs please, I'm not marking it.

Charlie's reports are the obvious hook: a first-person written record of his own sensations and thinking, kept in a notation that's his own, and by the end the prose has slid back to the plainer style of the opening, so the early entries are in the voice of someone he no longer is. My instinct is that this isn't a private language in Wittgenstein's sense at all, because the reports were read by the researchers, so there's a public check; the interesting question is what that check is actually doing. But I'm not sure the novel knows the difference between a language thats logically private and one that's just contingently unshared, and I want you to press on that.

Two wierd wrinkles I can't settle and want you to take a position on. First: suppose the early reports had been written in a notebook nobody ever read and Charlie never reread. Does that change whether they meant anything? If yes, tell me what public thing was doing the work in the read case; if no, tell me what the public check is doing at all. Second: a friend insists the book is secretly a Kripkean horror story — that once Charlie outgrows his community of readers he's a community of one, so his words stop meaning anything and the last entries are literally meaningless. I think that's too strong, but I want you to say where it's right.

Please don't give me the standard beetle-in-the-box summary, assume I know it. What I want to know is whether the box is an argument or just a picture, and what the novel does with that. The inkblots are, I think, the best image in the book for the argument's own problem — a public object you're asked to project a private meaning onto with no way to check — so use them. And someone in the group will say the whole argument is just verificationism in disguise; deal with that honestly rather than waving it away.
