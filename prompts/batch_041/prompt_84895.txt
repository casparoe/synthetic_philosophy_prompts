In my reading group, several people defend diachronic requirements of rationality by analogy with Dutch books. The argument runs: just as an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is committed to a set of bets that loses her money no matter what happens, an agent with cyclic preferences is committed to a sequence of trades that extracts money from her; and since the Dutch book is supposed to show that the first agent's credences are irrational, the money pump is supposed to show that the second agent's choices are irrational, so consistency across time is a genuine requirement of practical rationality on a par with probabilistic coherence. I want an audit of the comparison rather than a verdict on money pumps themselves. Lay out the correspondence: what plays the role of the axioms, the bookie, the bet, the guaranteed loss. Then say where the mapping breaks, attending especially to the fact that the Dutch book targets a state the agent is in at a time while the pump targets a pattern of choices spread over time; to whether credence is voluntary in a way that preference and choice are not; and to the escape routes available to the pumped agent, such as resolving in advance, refusing to trade, or foreseeing the trap. Which of these disanalogies bear on the conclusion the analogy is being used to license, and which are idle? Does the conclusion survive them, and if it does not, is there a better comparison for making the point?
