So I need a ruling on an argument and it isn't even my argument, which is probably the worst kind of favour to ask, but here we are.

Last night at the pub two people from my reading group, Sasha and Ravi, got into it about the free energy principle (Friston's thing — predictive processing, active inference, all that). It definately went on for more than an hour, and then me and my friend Nina argued about it the whole walk home and got absolutely nowhere, so what I actually want is someone to adjudicate what Sasha and Ravi said, not what Nina and I said. Roughly how it went:

(1) Sasha: the free energy principle can't be false, and that means it isn't saying anything. Variational free energy is just an upper bound on surprise — negative log evidence plus a KL term. So "every system that persists minimizes free energy" unpacks into: systems tend to end up in states their model assigns high probability to. That's a description of what a model is, not a discovery about biology. And surprise is defined relative to the system's own generative model, so the system gets to define what counts as surprising it. You can't lose.

(2) Ravi: being a principle isn't the same as being empty. Least action, natural selection, the second law — none of those are falsifiable in isolation either, and they're not nothing: they constrain which models you're allowed to build and they tell you where to look. And the FEP isn't just relabelled Bayes — it claims perception, action and learning are all the same operation on the same quantity. That's a substantive bet, because it predicts you'll find the same formal structure in systems that otherwise have nothing in common. It also sticks its neck out: if agents just minimize prediction error, the optimal policy is to find a dark silent room and stay there, since nothing is more predictable than nothing. Real organisms don't do that, so the theory has to explain the difference, and it does — organisms have prior beliefs about the states they should be in, so for them a dark room is a huge surprise.

(3) Sasha: that explanation is the disease, not the cure. If any behaviour can be rescued by positing a prior that makes it expected, nothing is excluded. Worse, the priors are themselves supposed to be learned by the same free-energy minimization, so the preferences aren't an independent anchor either — you've just moved the circularity from "surprise" to "what it wants". Least action earns its keep because it forbids things. So name me one experiment that would kill the FEP. Not one that would embarrass a particular model built inside it — one that would kill the principle.

(4) Ravi: "name the experiment that kills it" is the wrong test, though, because you can always save the second law by positing a hidden entropy sink, and nobody concludes thermodynamics is vacuous. And there is something the framework rules out: it rules out an agent that only minimizes prediction error, because such an agent never seeks information, and real agents do. That's exactly why the interesting quantity isn't variational free energy but expected free energy — expected free energy has a term for expected information gain, so curiosity falls out of the maths instead of being assumed. If you only say "the brain minimizes prediction error", you have to bolt curiosity on by hand. So the framework is doing work: it derives the exploratory behaviour that the flat version has to stipulate.

(5) Sasha: then it predicts everything, which was my point. And the machine-learning version is the tell: this objective turns up in variational autoencoders as the ELBO. A VAE isn't alive, doesn't care about anything, doesn't act in the world and has no stake in its own existence. If your law of life is the same equation a random encoder-decoder is optimizing, what you've found is a general piece of maths, not a fact about organisms. And "persists" is where the smuggled premise lives. A flame persists, a whirlpool persists, a sand dune is in equilibrium with the wind. Do those have generative models? If yes, "model" has gone soft and means nothing; if no, you owe me a principled line between the things that get a Markov blanket and the things that don't, and I don't think the line is anything except "it looks like an animal".

(6) Ravi: I'd rather say the FEP is a framework for generating candidate mechanisms than a theory competing with other theories, and that its value gets settled downstream, when the mechanisms it suggests beat the alternatives on data. But I'll admit I can't give you the line you're asking for, and I don't think anyone can right now — so maybe the honest version is that it's a claim about the shape of the answer rather than the answer.

I honestly don't know who came out of that better, and I'm deliberately not telling you where I landed, because I kept switching sides. What I want is a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the six, what's right in it, what's wrong or overstated, and who has the better of that particular exchange. Then who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing.

Two things I'd especially like from you. First, if "surprise", "free energy", "model", "persists" or "agent" are being used in two different senses by the two of them, say so — I suspect a decent chunk of the fight was that and neither of them noticed. Second, and this matters more to me: separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested view as if it were the consensus. If a piece of this is an open fight in the field rather than a known answer, I want that labelled as such, including which parts you're confident about and which you're hedging on.
