A popular philosophy book I have been reading summarises the debate over whether conceivability is a guide to possibility roughly as follows. It says the principle goes back to Hume, who held that whatever we can conceive is possible, and that Descartes relied on it when he argued that mind and body are really distinct: because he could clearly and distinctly conceive of himself existing without a body, such a thing must be possible. The modern version, the book says, runs through Kripke's work on the necessary a posteriori and is best seen in Chalmers's zombie thought experiment — the idea that we can conceive of a being physically identical to us but with no inner life, which shows that consciousness is not physical. It adds that critics have replied that we can equally conceive of water not being H2O, which is impossible, so the principle is simply false, and that this refutation has largely settled the matter.

How fair is this as a summary? Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a philosopher working on this would accept, which are loose but defensible, and which are wrong — paying particular attention to whether the book is using 'conceive' in the sense the arguments actually require, and to whether the objection it cites refutes the principle or only a stronger version of it. My own hunch is that the zombie case is doing more work than the book admits, but I do not trust my grip on the difference between conceiving and imagining. I would like a committed answer: do not merely lay out the positions, tell me which of the book's claims I should keep and which I should drop, and give your reasons.
