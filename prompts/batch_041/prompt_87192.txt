I spent thirty-five years as a molecular biologist before retiring, and I have since been reading my way into the philosophy of biology and the literature on self-reference. I would like you to write a dialogue between two competent and serious people who disagree about what the typogenetics toy, and its much-remarked isomorphism with Gödel numbering, actually establish about self-replication. I have read too many versions of this exchange that trade in loose analogy, so I want the argument to turn on a specific system, which I set out below. Neither speaker should be a foil for the other.

The shared background is this. In typogenetics, a strand of letters drawn from a four-letter alphabet is both a piece of data and a program: a fixed table, supplied from outside, translates any strand into a short sequence of typographical operations, so that a strand can bind to another strand and rewrite it by cutting, inserting, deleting, or duplicating material. A configuration counts as self-replicating when the operations performed by the strands in it eventually produce a further copy of the original strand. What people find striking is that one and the same object plays two roles, as the thing described and as the thing doing the describing — the situation in a Gödel numbering, where numbers are also formulas and the rules of inference become arithmetic.

Now the system I want them to argue about. Keep the alphabet and keep the idea that a strand is translated into operations, but change two things. First, nothing is designated in advance as an operator or as a substrate: any strand may act on any strand, including itself. Second, and this is the part I care about, the translating table is not supplied from outside. It is carried by one strand in the system — call it the manual — and the manual is itself subject to the operations, so it can be cut, rewritten, and duplicated, and it can be used as a substrate by the very operators it specifies.

One of the two speakers holds that a system of this kind vindicates the isomorphism: wherever genuine self-replication occurs, there is a structure that stands to the rest of the system as a description stands to what it describes, and the manual is simply that structure made explicit; the self-referential loop is not an accident of the toy but the signature of replication as such. The other holds that the isomorphism is an artifact of how we write things down: replication is a process, not a description, and the toy looks Gödelian only because we chose to express the machinery in the same alphabet as the material it acts upon; redraw the same process as a flow of matter or a network of shapes and the description evaporates, leaving a loop in our notation rather than a loop in the world.

Please have each speaker put the strongest version of their case, answer the other's actual arguments rather than trade slogans, and let the exchange rest on the concrete details of the modified system — above all on what becomes of the distinction between operator and substrate once the manual is itself a substrate, on what happens when the manual is rewritten while it is still being used, on whether the system retains a stable notion of "the same strand" for replication to be a matter of, and on whether self-replication here is a property of the configuration or of the observer's choice of encoding. Either speaker may bring in biological illustrations — the fact that the machinery which reads the genetic code is itself specified by the code, or the fact that prions and growing crystals propagate structure without anything worth calling a description — but only as illustrations, not as authorities. Neither speaker may win by definitional fiat. By the end it should be plain exactly what the two have come to agree on and exactly what they still disagree about.
