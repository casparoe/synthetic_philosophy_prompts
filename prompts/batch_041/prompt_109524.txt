I am writing a paper on the concept of national character, and I keep returning to the wartime research program in which American anthropologists tried to characterize societies they could not visit. Ruth Benedict's study of Japan is the famous product: working for the Office of War Information and similar agencies, she and others assembled portraits of whole peoples out of interviews with emigres and prisoners of war, novels, films, newspapers, and recordings — the method that came to be called culture at a distance — and produced the contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures, a Japan governed by obligation and by the emperor's place, and comparable readings of Germany. I want you to treat that program as a case study for the notion of national character itself, not as a historical curiosity. For the sake of the exercise, assume the theory the researchers were implicitly working with: that a culture is an integrated configuration, that its members share a modal personality formed by common early experience, and that the configuration can be read off cultural products even at a distance. What does that theory say the researchers were in a position to know and to predict — about morale, about the likelihood of surrender, about how a leaflet or a broadcast would land? Where does the theory do real work in the episode, and where does it merely redescribe what the researchers already assumed? Be explicit about which inferences the method supports and which it merely permits.

Then run the case back through the theory, and take the coherence question seriously. A claim that the Japanese are deferential, say, can mean three quite different things: that the distribution of a trait is shifted, that the typical member has it, or that the society's ideal or self-image contains it — and the wartime work slid among these three without noticing. Which of them could its evidence actually have supported? Does the program, read carefully, vindicate a deflated version of the concept, on which groups differ in the distribution of traits and in the norms they enforce, while leaving the inflated version, a people with a single character, unsupported? And if that is the outcome, does it settle the coherence question or only relocate it? Note, too, that the portrait did not stay outside the culture it described: the Japanese reception of Benedict's account fed a postwar genre of treatises on Japaneseness, so the characterization came to circulate inside the self-image of the people it claimed to capture. Does that looping make the original claim more nearly true, or expose it as a construction?

Finally, the acceptability question. These researchers generalized about peoples in order to fight wars against them, and their portraits fed into propaganda and policy. Does that context make the generalizations epistemically worse, or does it show that the acceptability of generalizing about peoples attaches to the use and to the position of the speaker rather than to the content — that the same sentence is a hypothesis in a seminar, an instrument in a propaganda office, and an insult on a street corner? I do not want the answer to rest on how much influence any particular report had on any particular decision; that is contested and beside the point. What I want is a disciplined verdict on whether national character is a coherent object of study, and on when, if ever, it is legitimate to generalize about a people.
