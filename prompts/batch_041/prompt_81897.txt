Ok, context first: I'm a retired chemist — spent about forty years on protein crystallography, mostly staring at diffraction patterns and arguing with the beamline staff — and since I hung up the lab coat I've been reading my way through philosophy of mind. I've gotten weirdly stuck on the language-of-thought stuff, the idea that thinking happens in some inner symbol system that's prior to and independent of English or French or whatever you happen to speak. Half of me finds it obvious and the other half thinks it's a category error, and I can't tell which half is being dumb.

Heres the thing I keep circling. Fodor's crowd says thought has to be compositional, has to have a syntax, otherwise you couldn't explain how you think a novel thought you've never thought before. Fine. But then I read about people who claim they have no inner monologue at all, and I think about my own case — when I'm doing actual science it's mostly spatial, rotating a molecule in my head, no words anywhere — and I start to wonder if the wordy part of thought is just the tip showing above water.

So I want a short story that puts this to the test, and I want you to build it around a made-up device rather than a real study, so you're not just summarizing stuff you've read. Here's the gadget: a lab has a machine that reads out whatever is going on in a person's head and renders it as text on a screen, but it can only do this for mental content that already has a sentence-like structure — subjects see their inner speech scroll past. For everything else, anything imagistic or spatial or just a felt sense of a problem being about to resolve, the screen shows static. Nothing. And the machine is otherwise reliable; the scientists have checked it against people deliberately rehearsing sentences in their heads and it nails them every time.

The story is about one subject who produces almost nothing but static. He's not impaired, he does crossword puzzles, he's a decent carpenter, he can tell you the plot of a movie afterward. But when he's actually solving something, the readout is blank, and only after he's arrived at an answer does a clean sentence appear — the conclusion, with none of the work. The researchers can't agree on what they're looking at. One says the machine is missing his thought because his thought isn't in a language at all, which would be a big deal. Another says no, the thought is in mentalese and the machine only translates the mentalese that happens to be shaped like a sentence, so the static is just untranslated structure — still thought, still symbolic, just not word-shaped. A third says the static is nothing, he's doing it all in a language the machine can't index, some private idiolect.

I'd like the story to follow the subject and the two or three researchers over a few weeks as they try to settle it, and I don't want a tidy answer at the end — I want the story to make me feel why the disagreement is hard, not to hand me a verdict. Let the characters make actual arguments, in dialogue, and let the arguments have real force; a couple of them should be sharp enough that I can't dismiss them. The subject himself should have a view about his own mind and it should be a bit naive but not stupid.

Aim for somewhere around 1200 to 1800 words. Don't name real philosophers or cite real papers — I don't want it leaning on things I'd have to go look up, and anyway the machine is fake. Just make it a good story that earns its ending. And please don't do the thing where the ending is a scientist giving a lecture about the nature of thought; I've read enough of those.
