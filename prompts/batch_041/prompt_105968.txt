I teach a mixed bioethics seminar (upper-year phil and med students) and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on the definiton of death that a good student can't answer just by reciting "brain death vs. circulatory death" or by naming the statute — they've all got that memorized and it tells me nothing about whether they actually get the problem. It's a 90-minute essay, out of 20.

I want to build it around a case I've made up, so it's not sitting in any textbook. Two patients, same hospital. (1) A patient on ECMO whose heart has stopped and who shows no brainstem reflexes is declared dead by circulatory criteria, and organ procurement begins while the pump is still going. (2) Another patient meets the whole-brain criterion — no brain function left at all — but is on a ventilator, warm, still healing wounds and digesting, and sits in the ICU for months because the family won't consent to stopping. The same ethics committee signs off on both, and nobody there thinks that's a problem.

The question should make them confront: what concept of death each arm of the protocol is actually using — the biological/organismal one, the neurological one, or something about consciousness and the person — and whether the two arms really share a concept or quietly use different ones. I especially want them to dig into irreversibility: is it doing the real work in both cases, and irreversibility relative to what — the technology we happen to have now, or what's possible in principle? And whether the dead-donor rule is a constraint the definition has to meet, or just a policy that grabs whatever definition is handy.

Please write me the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B — name the specific moves each one has to make, and the specific mistakes that should cost marks (e.g. treating brain death as obviously the same as the death of the organism, or treating irreversibility as a fact about the body rather than about the state of medicine).

No citations or reading list — I'll handle sources. I want to see them reason about the concept, not whether they've read the critics.
