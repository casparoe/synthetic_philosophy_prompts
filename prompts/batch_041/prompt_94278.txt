We've been calling an exchange between two agents a *moral trade* when each agent changes what she does and each change leaves the other agent better off by her own lights. To keep it small, suppose there are two agents, A and B, and two things to do: volunteer at the shelter (S) or canvas for the campaign (C). Each agent does exactly one of them, so there are four possible outcomes, written (what A does, what B does).

Each agent scores an outcome as follows: she assigns a number to each activity, meaning how much good she thinks it does when *anyone* does it, and subtracts the personal cost to herself of the activity she does. So A's score for (x, y) = vA(x) + vA(y) − cA(x), where x is A's activity, y is B's activity, vA is how much A values each activity, and cA is A's personal cost of doing it. B's score is defined the same way using B's numbers.

The numbers:

Activity values: A values S at 10 and C at 2. B values S at 2 and C at 10.

Personal costs: for A, doing S costs 6 and doing C costs 0. For B, doing C costs 6 and doing S costs 0.

As things stand, A volunteers at the shelter and B canvasses.

(a) Is there a swap of activities that both agents score higher than the status quo? If so, which one, and by how much does each gain?

(b) What does the answer to (a) depend on? State the general condition for a one-for-one swap of this kind to be mutually beneficial, and explain why the numbers come out the way they do.

(c) Suppose a third person, C, holds a third view. What, if anything, does the fact that the swap is better on A's score and better on B's score show about whether the swap is better overall?
---

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) Right now A is at the shelter and B is canvassing, so the outcome is (S, C). A's score is 10 + 2 − 6 = 6, and B's is 10 + 2 − 6 = 6. If they swap we get (C, S): A's score is 2 + 10 − 0 = 12 and B's is 2 + 10 − 0 = 12. So yes, there's a swap they both score higher, and they each gain 6. It has to be a swap rather than one of them acting alone — if A switched by herself the outcome would be (C, C), which she scores 4, and if B switched by himself it would be (S, S), which he scores 4. Neither of them would do their half for free.

(b) What makes it work is that the two of them don't value the two activities the same way. A thinks the shelter work is worth 10 and canvassing 2; B thinks canvassing is worth 10 and shelter 2, so their rankings of the two activities are the opposite of each other. In the status quo each of them is doing the activity the other one thinks matters more, so each can give up the thing she values less and get back the thing she values more. In general, a one-for-one swap like this is mutually beneficial whenever the two agents rank the two activities in opposite orders; if they agreed about which activity was worth more there'd be nothing for them to trade and the swap would be a wash.

(c) The trade makes things better by 12 units, 6 for A and 6 for B. That's the attractive thing about moral trade: it isn't a compromise where both sides give something up, because each side's moral view ends up better satisfied than before. Since it's an improvement on A's view and an improvement on B's view, it's an improvement that nobody has grounds to object to, whatever they happen to think about shelter work and canvassing.
