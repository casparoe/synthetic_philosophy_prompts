I'm teaching a 400-level seminar called Mathematics and the World — cross-listed, so half the room are math majors who are comfortable with epsilons and half are philosophy majors who havent seen a proof since high school, and by week ten they're all equally at sea, which is how I know the syllabus is doing something. The take-home final is a single essay, 90 minutes, 30 marks, and this year I want to hang the whole thing on Wigner's unreasonable-effectiveness argument. Trouble is, every year I get the same essay back: Wigner says math is unreasonably effective; either the world is made of math (Platonism) or it's a happy accident and we only notice the hits (selection effect); the end. I want to break that reflex, and I want a question they can't answer by reciting the Platonism/nominalism debate.

Here's the move I want to build the question around. A few weeks back I floated a distinction I've been chewing on and I'd like the exam to test it: between what I've been calling borrowed effectiveness — you build a tool to solve one problem and it turns out to do real work on a completely unrelated one — and virgin effectiveness — you build a structure for no empirical reason at all, purely because it's elegant or because it falls out of something already on the shelf, and only decades later does the world turn out to need exactly that structure. I want students to say which of these Wigner is actually puzzled by, and whether borrowed effectiveness is even puzzling or just a fact about how we generalize and analogize; then to face the deflationary reply that we simply don't keep a ledger of the failures — every formalism that never found a use, every model that had to be patched past recognition — and decide whether that reply dissolves the puzzle or merely relocates it. And I want a made-up case inside the question: a civilization develops some formal apparatus for purely aesthetic reasons, and three centuries later discovers it is the exact language of a physical phenomenon. Does that case sharpen the puzzle or flatten it, and what would have to be true for us to call it unreasonable rather than lucky?

So please give me: (1) the exam question itself — a single prompt, answerable in 90 minutes, not a book report; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words that I can use as the benchmark, showing the moves a first-class answer makes and the order it makes them in; and (3) a marking scheme out of 30 that names the specific must-hit moves and what each is worth, plus a one-line note per move on the mistake that costs marks. I'm especially interested in the failure modes: students who treat the unreasonableness as a straight metaphysical thesis when Wigner's real point may be about the psychology of surprise; students who think the selection-effect reply is a knock-down when it arguably just moves the bump under the carpet; students who collapse my borrowed/virgin distinction by insisting that all math is ultimately motivated by something. No reading list please — I have the Wigner essay and that's plenty.
