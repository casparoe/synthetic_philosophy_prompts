A city council has adopted a rule for deciding planning applications. It votes separately on three propositions: whether the development meets the affordable-housing requirement, whether it meets the environmental standard, and whether the application should be approved. If a majority supports the first two propositions, the application is approved automatically, whatever the vote on the third. The council has on several occasions thereby approved applications that a majority voted against. A further clause says that if the majority-approved propositions are inconsistent, the proposition with the weakest majority support is dropped until consistency is restored, and the application is then decided on the remaining propositions. What theory of collective decision-making does this rule presuppose? Does it treat the council as a single rational agent whose judgments must be consistent, or merely as a site of aggregating individual preferences? Is it committed to a premise-based rather than a conclusion-based procedure, and can that commitment be justified on epistemic or on procedural grounds? Finally, what would have to change in the rule if the council were to require that its final set of judgments be consistent with the vote on the conclusion as well as on the premises?
