At a departmental reading group on paradoxes of the infinite, two colleagues—call them A and B—fell into a dispute over a variant of Thomson's lamp. I was the bystander and could not decide who had the better of it. The variant is this: a lamp has a dial whose position x is a real number in [0,1]. The color rule is: red for x < 1/2, blue for x > 1/2, and green for x = 1/2. At stage n = 1, 2, 3, ..., at time t_n = 1 - 2^{-n}, the dial is set to x_n = 1/2 + (-1)^n/(n+1). The stages thus occur at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ..., and the positions x_n converge to 1/2, while the colors at the stages alternate red, blue, red, blue, ... without converging. At t = 1, after all the stages, what color is the lamp?

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

1. A: The process is fully specified. The dial's positions converge to 1/2, so at t = 1 the dial is at 1/2. The rule assigns green to 1/2. Therefore the lamp is green. The alternating red and blue are simply the colors at earlier stages; they do not prevent the final state from being green.

2. B: That assumes the color must converge to the color of the limit position. But the color rule is discontinuous at 1/2. The sequence of colors at the stages alternates and has no limit. A supertask's outcome is the limit of the state sequence, not the value of the state function at the limit of the position sequence. The positions converge, but the colors do not.

3. A: The color is not an independent state variable; it is defined by the position. If the dial is at 1/2, then by the rule it is green. You are treating a discontinuity as if it made the function undefined at the point. It does not; the rule is perfectly defined at 1/2. So if the position at t = 1 is 1/2, the color is green.

4. B: I agree the rule is defined at 1/2. My claim is that the process as described does not determine that the dial is at 1/2 at t = 1. The positions at the stages converge to 1/2, but the position at the limit time is not automatically the limit of the positions unless we assume continuity of motion or add a limiting rule. The supertask specifies positions at the t_n, not at t = 1. So the final position is underdetermined as well. If you stipulate that the dial is at 1/2 at t = 1, then green follows; but that stipulation is doing the work, not the supertask.

5. A: That seems too skeptical. If the dial is a physical body moving continuously, its position at t = 1 is determined by its trajectory. Even if the motion is interrupted at the stages, the trajectory has a limit. Physical quantities do not simply fail to have values at limit times. If the dial is at 1/2 at t = 1, the color rule applies. You cannot say the color is undetermined merely because the color sequence oscillates; the color is a function of position, and the position has a limit.

6. B: For a continuous physical process, the position at t = 1 is determined by continuity—but the color as a function of time is then the composition of a discontinuous function with a continuous one, and that composition need not be continuous. The limit of the colors need not equal the color of the limit. The physical process may determine the position, but the color at the limit is whatever the rule says at that position. So if the position is 1/2, the color is green. My point is that the idealization as stated does not by itself determine the position at t = 1; it only determines the positions at the stages. Once you add a physical continuity assumption, you get green, but that assumption is an extra premise. The original Thomson lamp is underdetermined for the same reason: the on/off sequence has no limit, and the idealization does not specify the state at the limit.

7. A: But the stipulation is not arbitrary. If the dial is a physical object, the only physically sensible answer is that it has the color corresponding to its actual position at t = 1. If its position converges to 1/2, then its position at t = 1 is 1/2, and the rule says green. You are treating a mathematical underdetermination in the idealization as a real indeterminacy in the world. That seems backwards.

8. B: And you are treating an idealization as if it automatically inherited the determinacy of the physical world. The idealization is not a physical process; it is a mathematical description with a limit that is not specified. If we want a physical answer, we need to say what physical mechanism implements the infinite sequence and what happens at t = 1. Different mechanisms consistent with the stage-by-stage description can yield different limit states. So the specification alone does not determine the color.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication of this exchange, not a general essay on supertasks. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly explain why. Then address the following: (i) Is the supertask as stated underdetermined with respect to the final color, or does the rule plus the convergence of positions settle it? (ii) What exactly is the relevance of the discontinuity of the color rule? (iii) Is the physical realizability of the supertask relevant to the mathematical question, and if so, how? (iv) Does the original Thomson lamp face the same issue, or is there a relevant difference? (v) What, if anything, are both A and B missing? Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the task is to assess the reasoning as stated. Conclude with a single line of exactly the form \answer{<A|B|neither|both> — <one-sentence verdict naming which party is closer to right overall and the single most important correction>}.
