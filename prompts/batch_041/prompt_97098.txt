I am presenting a paper at a workshop next month and I need to prepare for the question period. The paper defends a resolution of Sen's liberal paradox, and the audience is mostly social choice theorists and formal political philosophers, with a couple of game theorists and one or two people who work on deontological rights; they know Arrow's theorem and Sen's theorem inside out, they are allergic to axiomatic patches that look like they were reverse-engineered to save a conclusion, and they will press hard on whether my proposal is really a weakening of minimal liberalism in disguise. The thesis is this. In the standard setup, minimal liberalism gives each of at least two individuals a pair of alternatives over which their strict preference is socially decisive, and weak Pareto says that if everyone strictly prefers one alternative to another then society must too; the two conditions generate a cycle once a third alternative is introduced. I argue that the right response is not to drop weak Pareto or to restrict the domain, but to reformulate what a liberal right is in the formal apparatus: a right is a default decisiveness over a named pair that is suspended when that pair is embedded in a larger feasible set and some third alternative is Pareto-superior to both members of the pair for every individual, unless the right-holder herself prefers the protected alternative to that third alternative. I call this right-local Pareto restriction. On this view the classic cycle is not a genuine impossibility for liberalism, because the liberal right was never meant to range over the entire social ordering; it is a local constraint on the ranking of the alternatives it names, and it can be defeated only in the narrow case where the right-holder's own ranking does not stand against the Pareto improvement.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a social choice theorist would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. I especially want the objections that target the reformulation itself—whether it collapses into welfarism, whether it respects the distinction between rights and preferences, whether it is invariant to irrelevant alternatives, how it handles meddlesome preferences and more than two right-holders, and whether it simply renames the problem. Tell me what I should concede up front so that the rest of my replies are not read as evasions, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and flag the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Skip the literature review and the citations; I know the literature and I need the live questions and the words to answer them.
