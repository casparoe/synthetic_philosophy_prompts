ok so in philosophy club we've been doing this unit on the kind of sentences that do stuff just by being said — "I promise," "I bet you five dollars," a judge going "I sentence you to two years" — and our advisor explains the whole thing with chess. His line is that a promise is a move in a game the same way pushing a pawn is a move in chess. The rules of chess don't say you may push your pawn but shouldn't push it backwards; they're what make a backwards pawn push not even a move, just a piece you shoved. Promising is like that, he says: there's no natural thing called a promise floating around that the rule of promising then regulates, the rule is what makes the thing exist in the first place. Fine, I get that part. But then he takes the extra step, which is the part I actually care about, and says this is why nobody should get so worked up about broken promises: it's a game, and if we rewrote the rules a promise would just stop existing, the way a move stops existing when you change the rules. My friend thinks he's obviously wrong and that this proves the analogy is garbage, and I can't tell who's right.

So can you audit the analogy for me. I want the mapping layed out first — what plays the role of the board, the pieces, the players, the referee, the rulebook, the clock, and whatever the chess equivelant of winning is — and then where it breaks down. A couple of things I already noticed and want you to either use or kill: in chess somebody can look up whether a move was legal and there's a ref to appeal to, but nobody can actually write down the full rules of promising; in chess my opponent doesn't have to accept my move for it to count, but if I promise something and the person I'm promising to doesn't take it in, has anything happened at all; and chess moves don't owe anybody anything, while a broken promise seems to leave a real debt rather than just an illegal move. There's also the difference between saying "I promise" when you don't mean it and saying it when you're in no position to promise at all, like promising to sell someone a house that isn't yours — does the chess picture handle both of those the same way, or does it need two different stories? Last thing: does his "so don't take it seriously" conclusion survive the disanalogies once they're on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time? And if it's a bad analogy, what would be a better one — my sister keeps saying a promise is really more like a contract (offer, acceptance, breach, damages, all that) and I don't know if that one is better or just a different mess.
