My two friends have been arguing for about a year now — one of them runs a risk-analysis shop and thinks the precautionary principle is mostly an excuse to make bad decisions with a straight face, the other works in environmental policy and thinks that line is industry apologetics. I want to force them to sit down and write the document together: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on the precautionary principle that both of them could actually sign their names to.

Here's the shape I want.

1) A numbered agreed-claims section. What can a precautionist and an expected-utility person both accept? About why the status quo isn't a neutral baseline, about what we can and can't know in advance, about which of the standard objections to precaution are fair hits and which are strawmen. And I want two specific things in there. First, a trigger clause: the conditions under which they both agree restricting an activity is warranted. Second, a burden-of-proof ledger saying who has to show what at each stage — before deployment, after deployment, and once a warning signal shows up. Also make them pin down how they're carving up the epistemic space: do they want to seperate risk (probabilities known), uncertainty (probabilities unknown), and plain ignorance (you can't even list the possible outcomes)? Get them to commit to a split like that and say what each thinks should happen in each tier, because I'm fairly sure half their fight is really about which tier we're in.

2) A disagreements table. Every remaining disagreement stated in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of the other's view, then what they take the crux to be, then for each crux a case or consideration they both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I want at least these three: whether there's a non-arbitrary threshold for triggering precaution or whether every threshold is a value judgement wearing an epistemic costume; whether irreversible harms get priority or merely a bigger weight; and whether precaution applies to the precautionary measure itself.

3) Then run their agreed criteria on a case I think is nasty for both of them: an intervention with a small chance of a large, irreversible, hard-to-attribute harm that would also avert a large harm that's already happening. The precautionist can't just say restrict and the expected-utility person can't just say do the math, because here the uncertainty is about the possibility space rather than the odds. Tell me where they land, whether they land in the same place, and if they split, whether the split is a real disagreement or just a fight over words. If part of this whole debate turns out to be verbal, name that part.

One last thing — both of them keep saying the precautionary principle as if it were one thing, so make them state which version each is definately defending, and whether the weak/strong distinction is doing any actual work or just relabelling the same disagreement.
