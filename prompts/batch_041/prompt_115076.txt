For years I have taken Black's two spheres to be the cleanest toy model in metaphysics — a world containing nothing else, two spheres alike in every intrinsic respect at some fixed distance from one another, and no fact of the matter about which is which — and I want a poem that performs the thought experiment rather than alluding to it.

The form I am asking for is a mirror poem: an odd number of lines, thirteen or fifteen, in which the last line is the first, the second-to-last the second, and so on, so that the poem reads identically from either end and a single line sits at the centre as its own reflection. Three constraints on the content. First, no line may attribute an intrinsic difference between the spheres; the only differences the poem may register are relational or indexical, and it should be plain that these are the contested ones — the reply that each stands at a certain distance from the other, or that one of them is this one — rather than real differences smuggled in under other names. Second, the poem must be invariant under the exchange of the two: substituting one sphere's name for the other's throughout must leave the text unchanged, so that no reader can recover from the poem which half is about which. Third, the central line, the one that is its own reflection, is where the argument should sit: it must be readable as committing to there being two and as committing to there being one, and the poem must not settle which.

I do not want the turn resolved. The poem should leave the reader unable to say whether the perfect mirroring tells in favour of Black — two things, whose indiscernibility is precisely the fact in need of explanation — or in favour of the principle that indiscernibles are one thing, and the mirroring merely a description arrived at twice. I want the reader to feel the choice between calling the symmetry a feature of the world and calling it a feature of our descriptions, and to see that this is the crux rather than a failure of nerve. If you want a voice, let one of the spheres speak; its only names for the pair are then I and you, and the form has to make it undecidable whether the poem has one speaker or two. Follow the poem with a short paragraph saying which way the form leans, if either, and why the lean will not hold.

Keep it to the spheres: I don't need the hole argument or identical particles dragged in, and no citations, since I know the literature. Someone who has lived with this problem should find the poem accurate; someone who hasn't should still find it a good poem about two things that cannot be told apart.
