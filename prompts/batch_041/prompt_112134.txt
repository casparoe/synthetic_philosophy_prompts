Im finishing up a chapter on sphexishness and I want to open it with a poem, so: poem request. The wasp bit, in case it's been a while — she paralyses a cricket, drags it to the burrow, leaves it at the door, goes inside to check the nest; nudge the cricket a few inches while she's in there and she comes back out, finds it, drags it to the door, and inspects the nest again from scratch, and you can keep doing this until you get bored. I want a pantoum: four quatrains, the standard interlocking rule, and a last stanza that brings the opening line back round so the poem shuts like a loop. The form is the routine — every line returns because the rule says so, not because anything decided it should. But put exactly one line in the poem that breaks the scheme, sitting where the rule says it can't be, and don't tell me whether that line is the one free act in the whole thing or just the loop's built-in hiccup. The nudged cricket is a hiccup too.

Two things to work in, because the classic version is too clean on its own. One: she isn't a wind-up toy, she does improvise — wrecked burrow gets replaced, blocked route gets rerouted — so the rigidity is local, and nobody can say in advance where it stops. Two: there are people now arguing the whole re-inspection business is actually adaptive and that the example survives on rhetorical usefulness rather than fact, so the poem should be able to hold that doubt without tipping into so she's free after all. Maybe the robot vacuum image helps, the one that starts the entire flat over from the top if you lift it mid-cycle.

Please no cute wasp. No tiny architect, not brave, not patient, not a mother. And don't mangle the sense for the rhyme, near-rhyme is fine. Someone who knows the literature should be able to see the rule being kept and the exact place it isn't.
