I'm a PhD student in decision theory, drafting a chapter that argues the word superrationality has been carrying two incompatible rules since Hofstadter's Platonia Dilemma — the scenario in the essay that leads up to the Luring Lottery, where twenty chosen reasoners may each cable in their name, the entire billion goes to a single claimant, nobody gets anything if two or more claim or if none does, and all contact among the participants is forbidden. On the first reading, superrationality constrains acts: reasoners in the same situation who reason alike must act alike, so only the pure symmetric profiles are on the table. On the second, it constrains strategies: everyone adopts the same rule, possibly a randomized one, and we take the profile that maximizes the payoff to each, even though the realizations will differ. The two readings agree on the prisoner's dilemma and on the coordination games that fill the literature. Where they visibly come apart — the Platonia Dilemma itself — the first reading simply goes indeterminate, tying between universal claim and universal abstention, which lets a critic dismiss the divergence as an artifact of degeneracy rather than a genuine disagreement between rules. I want a case where that excuse is unavailable.

So: design me one case that pulls the two readings apart, and show your work.

Specifications. An anonymous count game — N ≥ 3 identical players, one simultaneous move each from a finite action set, every player's payoff a function of the counts alone, the payoff function stated explicitly and compactly. The best pure symmetric profile and the best symmetric profile in which each player independently randomizes from the same distribution must come apart, with a strictly positive gap that can be widened by turning a parameter. No escape hatches: no communication, no pre-play commitment, no shared or correlated randomizer, no repetition, no punishment, no predictor, no common cause connecting the players' choices, and no asymmetry in information or preferences. Their shared reasoning is the only thing that makes them alike. There should be no dominant strategy; the game must not be a two-player prisoner's dilemma; and the pure reading's verdict must be determinate — a unique best pure symmetric profile, not the degenerate tie between universal claim and universal abstention that the Platonia Dilemma itself produces.

Then, in under 900 words: state the game; derive each verdict from its own reading, arithmetic included, so that I can check both against the payoff function myself; name the assumption in the derivation that does the real work and say what becomes of the case when you drop it, including the version in which the players put a small positive probability on a counterpart reasoning differently; report how the two verdicts move as N grows; and give the strongest objection a defender of the pure reading could raise against the mixed verdict — whether a private randomizer can count as the product of reasoning at all, given that the realizations differ across players. Finish by saying whether the mixed verdict can be recovered as evidential decision theory applied to one's own deliberation, and what it means for my chapter if it cannot. Do not stipulate the divergence: the payoff function alone should force it.
