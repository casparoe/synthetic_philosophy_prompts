I'm running a session for a local philosophy reading group next month. Mostly smart non-philosophers, a couple of grad students, everyone's okay with abstraction but nobody's read the literature. Topic: is sentience a matter of degree, and does moral status come in degrees too? Insects, fish, maybe thermostats. Don't explain the topic to me — I want you to plan the session. The order the ideas have to go in and why (what actually depends on what), the one example that should carry the whole thing, the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form along the way and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, and one question I can ask at the end to see whether it landed.

Here's my hunch about the spine of it, and I want you to tell me if it's wrong. I think the session only works if we keep two different "degrees" seperate — (a) how much of a subject there is at all, thermostat vs. fish, and (b) how bad or good a given experience is for a subject that already counts, papercut vs. burn. People slide between the two and then everything gets muddy. And I want to throw in a twist: even if sentience is continuous, our obligations might not be — maybe moral status is a step function sitting on top of a continuous sentience scale, so a tiny increase in sentience near some threshold flips a lot of moral weight, while a big increase far below the threshold flips nothing. Is that a good spine for the session, or does it just make the whole thing harder than it needs to be?

Carrying example: I was going to use the thermostat → fish → dog ladder, but I'm worried it's a cliché and that people just nod along. Suggest what should carry it instead, and what that example needs to have and to lack.

Also tell me what to leave out at this level, and say so out loud.

One more thing: at the end, tell me where your plan is most likely to fail — the ordering choice or the example choice you'd bet against — and what you'd see in the room that would make you change your mind about it.
