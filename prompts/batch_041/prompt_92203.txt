Since I hung up my lab coat I've been reading philosophy and a bit of game theory on my own, and I keep tripping over arguments that seem to hinge not on what a person knows but on what they know about what other people know, and what those people know about that, and so on up the ladder. I have a feeling I've just stumbled into a real-life version of one of these, and I can't work out whether the theory has anything practical to say about it, so I'd like your advice.

Here's the situation. I sit on the committee of my local astronomical society — eight of us, we meet on Tuesdays, mostly to bicker about the annual star party and the budget. For about a year now I'm fairly sure that every single member has privately concluded that our chair's grand five-year plan (a new dome, a big fundraising drive, the whole thing) is a slow-motion disaster. I know this because I've had essentially the same hushed one-on-one conversation with six of them; each swore me to secrecy, and each seemed to assume they were the only one — or at most one of two — who felt that way. So each of us, individually, thinks the plan is terrible. Yet every Tuesday we ratify it again by unanimous silence, and afterward I at least go home and fume. The bylaws say the plan passes unless somebody objects in the meeting, and objections have to be spoken aloud — no anonymous notes, no proxies, no emailing the secretary. Last month I got as far as opening my mouth and then didn't, because I honestly couldn't tell whether the room's silence meant everyone approved or meant everyone was stuck in exactly the hole I was in.

What I want is practical advice, not just "be brave." Should I simply say it at the next meeting, and if so, is there a way to do it that actually breaks the logjam rather than just making me the difficult one? And is it really true that a public statement does something a quiet round of phone calls beforehand couldn't? I have a strong hunch there's a precise way to say why the private route keeps failing and the public route might not, and I'd like you to lay out that structure for me, because I can feel the shape of it but I can't articulate it. One more thing: tell me what would change your mind — if you tell me to go public, what would you have to find out about our little committee to decide that actually, no, I should keep my mouth shut?
