I chair the collections committee at a small university art museum, and I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, because the director and the conservator are about to do something I think we will regret and I want the argument on paper where they can attack it. The facts are these. In 1974 the museum accepted, as a gift from the artist herself, a life-size seated figure modelled in unfired clay: one continuous mass of the stuff, never cast, never fired, displayed in a humidity-controlled case. The deed of gift includes what our lawyer calls the reversion clause — if the museum destroys or permanently alters the Work, title reverts to the artist. Our fine-art policy insures the Work as a single object at a stated value, pays only on destruction, and takes whatever remains as salvage. Two weeks ago a seal failed, the internal armature gave way, and the figure came down. Every gram of clay is now in a crate, chemically unchanged; the form is gone. We do still have a plaster piece-mould taken in the nineties for study, so the shape itself is technically recoverable, which the conservator says is exactly the point: we can have the shape back without having the work back. The artist, who is eighty-nine and considerably sharper than I am, says the work was the clay and is therefore still sitting in that crate, and that re-forming it would be conservation rather than creation. The conservator says the reverse: the work was the form she imposed on the clay, and the form is dead. Our registrar still has one accession number with a 'medium: unfired clay' field, which I suspect is doing more metaphysical work than anyone intended. What actually keeps me up is that we appear to be telling the insurer one story — the object is destroyed, pay the full value — and the artist another: the work persists, so the reversion clause never fires and the clay stays ours. Both can't be true, and I don't want to sign a document whose force depends on which description we happen to be using.

So: draft the opinion I'll circulate. Give me a concrete recommendation — authorize the re-forming, refuse it, or approve something narrower with conditions and a deadline — with the reasoning set out so that three curators, a conservator and a lawyer can push back on specific steps rather than on vibes. Take seriously the question of whether the figure and the clay were ever one thing or always two, and whether the answer is relative to the sort of thing we're counting (an artwork, a physical object, a quantity of material); if you think the question has no determinate answer, then tell me what a committee should do under that indeterminacy rather than leaving us with a shrug. State the strongest case for whichever side you rule against, since that is the part I'll be asked about in the meeting. I don't need a literature review; I need a bottom line, a time frame, and reasons.
