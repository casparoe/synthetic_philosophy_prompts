I host a little film club out of my apartment — eight or nine regulars, we watch something heavy every other Thursday, and lately every screening ends with the same fight. Two weeks ago we watched a movie where basically everything goes wrong for the family and the last twenty minutes are just grief, and afterwards one friend said she loved it and couldn't explain why that wasn't just masochism; another said she loved it in spite of the sadness, the sadness was the price of the good parts; a third said we only like it because none of it is real and nobody in the room actually lost anyone. I want to settle this properly instead of relitigating it every time. So please build me a decision tree, or a taxonomy with labeled branches if the tree gets unwieldy — something I can hand out at the next meeting and have people trace through to a named position on why we enjoy sad art. Nine to eleven leaves would be about right. The thing I care most about is that the branching questions are ones my friends could actually answer about their own experience, phrased as concrete cases or concrete claims, not as yes/no questions about theories they'd have to already know the names of. If someone can only answer a node after I tell them which side of the debate it leads to, it's the wrong node.

Here's the angle I want, since I know there are a dozen ways to slice this. First split should be about what the sadness actually is in the experience: a real emotion directed at something fictional, a pretend or quasi-emotion, or not really an emotion at all — more like a perception or a judgement wearing an emotion's clothes. Then, for the branches where it's real, I want the next question to be whether the sadness itself is part of what we're enjoying or merely a cost the rest of the work outweighs, because that's exactly the split between my first two friends and I don't think either of them has noticed it. Then, wherever the sadness is contributing, the next level should be the mechanism: being pleased at being moved / pleased with one's own responsiveness; a kind of release or clarification; the safety and control of feeling something with nothing at stake; the sense of insight or meaning the emotion is a vehicle for; or the structural build-and-release of tension rather than the sadness as such. And I want one branch that just denies the premise — that what we get from tragedy isn't pleasure at all but value, that the puzzle only exists because we used the word enjoy too loosely. Those five mechanisms plus the quasi-emotion and the not-an-emotion option and the value-not-pleasure option are the leaves I need to see. Beyond that, tell me which combinations of answers are live but unoccupied — is anyone actually holding the view that the sadness contributes and is also intrinsically bad to undergo, or does that combination just collapse — and say for each empty spot whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. And flag any two leaves your tree can't seperate, where different positions predict the same answers to everything I can ask my friends, because I'd rather know that than have the quiz pretend to be finer-grained than it is.

A few cases I want to make sure show up as nodes, because these are the ones that actually divide people in my experience: the film that wrecks you and you immediately say it was great; the film that wrecks you and you resent it for ruining your evening, even though you'd call it good; real bad news that makes you cry with no enjoyment anywhere in it, which is supposed to be the control case; the manipulative tearjerker you cry at while feeling like you've been played, so there's no pleasure in your own responsiveness at all; a horror or disgust case, to see whether the tree generalizes beyond sadness or is only about sadness; and rewatching the ending you know by heart, where the suspense is gone but the feeling isn't. Put those in wherever they do the most work, and if one of them doesn't distinguish anything, say so. My guess is half of our arguments are really about what we each mean by enjoying something, so wherever the tree can force that into the open, do it.
