I am a first-year graduate student in economics who has been sitting in on a decision-theory reading group run by two philosophers — we meet in the back room of a pub on Thursdays — and we have just finished the essay in which Douglas Hofstadter introduces superrationality, 'Dilemmas for Superrational Thinkers, Leading Up to a Luring Lottery', together with the puzzle he builds in it: an eccentric trillionaire assembles twenty people and promises a billion dollars to whichever one of them, and only one of them, sends him a telegram by noon of the following day; if he receives more than one telegram, or none at all, nobody receives anything, and the twenty are forbidden to coordinate. The conclusion I carried away from the discussion is that superrationality is simply the categorical imperative applied to games. In an ordinary prisoner's dilemma the two accounts plainly coincide — the superrational player cooperates, and 'everyone cooperates' is also the arrangement one could will as a universal law — so I took Hofstadter's instruction to include in one's calculations the fact that one is reasoning among people who reason as one does to be Kant's test in a different vocabulary, and I assumed that a case like the telegram puzzle must therefore be settled by asking which of the available choices can be universalized.

Yet the test either declines to discriminate or returns an answer I cannot accept. Universalized, 'send a telegram' yields twenty telegrams and no winner, while 'refrain' yields no telegrams and no winner, so the two look indistinguishable in outcome; and if one presses the stronger, contradiction-in-will version of the test, the verdict is that all twenty should stay home, which leaves the billion unclaimed and seems to me a reductio of the procedure rather than a solution to the problem. Two members of the group drew the conclusion that the puzzle has no rational solution at all. A third insisted that the answer is to randomize, which strikes me as either a dodge or a confession that no maxim can be stated, since I cannot see how 'send a telegram with such-and-such a probability' could be the content of a law that anyone wills universally; and this third member added, as though it settled matters, that the contest the magazine itself ran on the strength of the idea, in which a reader might enter as often as he liked and the million was divided among all the entries, came with advice to simulate a many-sided die and enter only on one designated outcome. I doubt that Hofstadter went to this trouble in order to construct a puzzle with no answer, and I suspect the fault lies in my identification of superrationality with the categorical imperative — but I cannot see what else superrationality could be, if it is not a test of universalizable maxims. If that identification is indeed where I went wrong, what is the reasoning that actually operates in the telegram case, why does it require no universalizability test at all, and what am I to make of the fact that the group's dissenting answer, randomization, is precisely the sort of thing such a test cannot even state?
