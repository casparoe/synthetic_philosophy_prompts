I write about science and ideas for a weekly, and I have spent the past month trailing a group of researchers who insist that history has finally become a predictive science. They fit systems of differential equations to centuries of grain prices, elite overproduction, state fiscal stress and popular immiseration, and claim that the resulting models retrodict the timing of major upheavals — revolutions, civil wars, the collapse of dynasties — with uncomfortable accuracy. A neighbouring outfit runs something similar as a commercial product: asset managers subscribe to a political-risk index that rises and falls on the same machinery. At the last conference I attended, a presenter put up a slide showing that his model had flagged the present decade as a period of elevated instability in several democracies, and said, only half-jokingly, that we no longer need to argue about whether there are laws of history, because we can now estimate their parameters. When I pressed him on what happens when governments read his forecasts and adjust their behaviour, he said the model was robust to that, and I did not know enough to know whether he was right.

Popper is the ghost at this feast, obviously — he denied that there can be a theoretical history of the kind that would license prophecy, while allowing that social science can yield conditional predictions of the form: if such-and-such conditions obtain, then such-and-such will follow. What I want from you is a careful reconstruction of what he would actually have said about this research programme, not a slogan. Where exactly would he draw the line between the conditional, testable hypotheses these people are producing and the historicism he spent his career attacking? Would he regard the fitting of long-run cycles to historical data as a legitimate extension of his own view, or as a relapse? Does his argument that we cannot predict the future growth of our own knowledge still bite when the predictor is a statistical model rather than a theorist, and does it bite differently when the model's outputs are published and acted upon — that is, when the prediction itself becomes part of the causal system it describes? I am also interested in what he would say about the ambition to replace theory with data: a claim I keep hearing is that with enough variables and enough centuries, the search for mechanisms is a distraction, because the correlations will do the predicting for us.

Please give me his likely position in some detail, organised around the distinctions he would want to insist on, and tell me where he would have to concede ground — including any place where his own framework has been overtaken by how this work is actually done. If you think he would have been more sympathetic than his reputation suggests, say so and say why; if you think the whole enterprise would have struck him as the same old historicism in new clothes, make that case and show me what in the programme earns the verdict. I would rather have the argument than a verdict, and I would rather have it precise about which Popperian objections survive contact with modern computational practice and which do not.
