Question: "Set out Molina's distinction between God's natural, middle, and free knowledge. Explain what middle knowledge is supposed to add beyond natural knowledge, and how it is meant to reconcile divine providence with creaturely freedom in a way that neither the Calvinist nor the open theist can. Then state the grounding objection and say whether Molina has a good reply to it."

Answer: "Molina distinguishes three stages in God's knowledge, ordered by how each depends on God's will. Natural knowledge is God's knowledge of everything true independently of any decision of his: necessary truths, and the full range of possible worlds he might create. Free knowledge is God's knowledge of what will in fact happen, which depends on his decree to actualize this world; it is postvolitional. Between them stands middle knowledge, which is prevolitional but not necessary: God's knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, that is, truths about what any possible free creature would do in any circumstances in which God might place it. For instance, God knows that if Peter were in the courtyard, he would freely deny Christ.

Middle knowledge is what lets God be certain that his plan will succeed without himself determining the creature's choice. Natural knowledge alone tells God only what is possible, so a God with only natural and free knowledge would have to settle what to create before knowing how his creatures would respond; he would be gambling on them. The Calvinist avoids the gamble by making God the cause of the creature's act; the open theist avoids it by denying that God has definite knowledge of future free acts at all. Molina wants God to be certain of the outcome while the act remains the creature's own, and this is why the counterfactuals of freedom must be true before God decides anything.

Now, since these counterfactuals are true, it follows that Peter, once placed in the courtyard, cannot do otherwise: had he acted differently the counterfactual would have been false, and what is true cannot become false. That is what secures God's certainty, and it is why God can arrange circumstances such that his creatures freely do what he intends.

The grounding objection holds that these counterfactuals have nothing to make them true. They are not true in virtue of God's will, since that would make the creature's act determined, and not in virtue of the creature's essence, since that would make the act necessary rather than free. The Molinist can reply that they are made true by God's decision to actualize the world in which they hold, so no independent truthmaker is required."
