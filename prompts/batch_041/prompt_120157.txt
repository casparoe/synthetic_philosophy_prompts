Foucault's great insight in Discipline and Punish is that power in the modern world does not work by repression but by production: instead of crushing subjects, it manufactures them. The prison, the school, the factory, the barracks and the hospital all deploy the same handful of techniques — timetables, drills, the examination, constant hierarchical observation — to turn people into docile bodies, useful and obedient at once. The Panopticon, Bentham's design for a prison with a central inspection tower, is the purest expression of this logic, and Foucault treats it as the blueprint that modern institutions actually followed: when the inmate cannot know whether he is being watched, he becomes his own guard, and surveillance is therefore the cheapest and most effective form of power ever devised. From this Foucault draws a political conclusion I have always found bracing: the great emancipatory struggles of the nineteenth century, the widening of the franchise and the reform of the criminal law, were largely cosmetic, because they left the disciplinary machinery intact. Power on this view is not a possession that a ruling class holds and wields but a diffuse force that circulates through every social relation, which is why Foucault insists that knowledge and power are the same thing — the human sciences, from criminology to statistics, are simply instruments of domination — and why he has so little to say about resistance, beyond the thought that it is everywhere and therefore nowhere in particular.
