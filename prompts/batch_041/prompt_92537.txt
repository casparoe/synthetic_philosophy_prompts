For most of my working life I have been a quantitative researcher, and my working vocabulary is Bayesian inference: priors and likelihoods, measurement error, selection effects and non-ignorable missingness, model misspecification, base rates, calibration, causal graphs. I am fluent in it and do not need the analogies unpacked for me. A year ago I became a patient in a long diagnostic process, and for eight months a series of clinicians were confident that what I was describing was anxiety rather than anything they needed to investigate. Epistemic injustice has stopped being an abstraction for me. I have read enough of the philosophical literature to follow it casually, but I am not a philosopher, and I am not asking for a literature review, a reading list, or citations; I am also not asking for advice about my own case.

What I want is a systematic translation. Take the central claims of that literature as you understand them — that a speaker's credibility can be deflated because of who the speaker is rather than what is said; that there can be gaps in the collective interpretive resources such that an experience cannot be made intelligible to the people who would have to act on it; and the less-discussed variants, in which a speaker pre-emptively trims what is said because the speaker has learned what will not be believed, or a hearer declines to acquire concepts that are available and would render the speaker intelligible — and restate each of them, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use. Be as faithful as you can. If a credibility deficit is best modeled as a shifted prior, or as a deflated likelihood ratio, or as something else again, say which and be precise about it; if a hermeneutical gap is a missing variable or a misspecified model class, say which and why the difference matters. State the target vocabulary explicitly before you begin translating into it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about. Which claims survive translation without loss? Which survive only if you quietly alter the phenomenon to fit my vocabulary — and at exactly which step does the fudging occur? Which have no counterpart at all? For each piece of residue, tell me whether the fault lies with my vocabulary, which may simply lack the resources to say certain things, or with the original claim, which may be less clear than it appeared. I am aware that a translation of this kind can be made to look complete while silently dropping the normativity; do not do that, and where you feel the pull to do it, mark the spot and say so.

Two further questions, which matter more to me than the rest. First, does the translation carry anything the philosophical discussion under-describes — a mechanism, a feedback loop, a selection effect — that becomes visible only once the phenomenon is written as an estimation problem? Second, is there any way, inside my vocabulary, to represent the difference between a hearer who is merely inaccurate and a hearer who has wronged someone? My suspicion is that whatever refuses to translate is where the substance of the matter lies, and I would like to be told, plainly, whether that suspicion is sentimental.
