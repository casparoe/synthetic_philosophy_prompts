For some years my city's council has capped the rent that a landlord may charge for an occupied flat. The cap falls away when a tenancy ends, so a long-standing tenant pays well below what the flat would fetch on the open market, while a new tenant pays the going rate. I own one such flat. It has been let to the same man for eleven years; he is now moving into sheltered accommodation, and for the first time I am free to set the rent at whatever the market will bear.

I had assumed I would advertise at that figure and take the first suitable applicant. Now that I have three of them, I find I cannot decide, and I would rather have help thinking it through than reassurance.

The first is a professional couple who can comfortably afford the market rent and have offered to pay a year in advance. The second is a single mother who works part-time at the hospital; she has asked whether I would consider letting at the old, capped rent, which is all she can manage — letting at that rent is perfectly lawful, it simply means forgoing the difference. The third is my nephew, a newly qualified teacher, who can pay the market rent but only erratically, and whose case my sister presses on me daily.

Three people have advised me, and I can see that each of them is partly right without knowing how to weigh them. My brother, who is an accountant, says the flat is worth what it is worth, that I should let at the market rent, and that if I want to help the hospital worker I should do it openly: charge the full rent and give her the difference each month as a gift, for as long as I choose. He adds that a subsidised rent to one applicant is a decision to give nothing to the other two, and that I have no basis for preferring her. My partner says the opposite: that a flat let at a rent someone can actually afford becomes a home, whereas the same flat at the market rent is merely an asset; that the couple can live anywhere and the hospital worker cannot; and that I would be pocketing a windfall created by a rule I have never supported and by eleven years of the departing tenant's rent, which paid off the mortgage. The departing tenant's daughter puts it a third way: her father maintained the flat at his own expense, his rent over eleven years bought the place twice over, and the decent thing is to let it to someone in the position he was in.

What I want from you is not a general essay on housing. I want to know what is actually being decided. Is the choice among the three a problem the cap has created, or one it has merely exposed? My brother's proposal — charge what the flat is worth, then give the difference away — does that dissolve the difficulty or only relocate it? Does the economic case against caps of this kind tell me anything at all about what I should do under one I did not make? And how much weight, if any, should the claims of the people not in the room carry — the applicants I turn away, and whoever in this city will next need somewhere to live? One further thing confuses me: because what I charge determines whom I can take, and whom I want to take determines what I am willing to charge, I cannot separate the two questions, and I suspect that is not an accident.

Having said all that, tell me what you would do, and why. Name the ideas you are using if they have names, but I am not asking for a reading list or for citations. Please write your answer in British English.
