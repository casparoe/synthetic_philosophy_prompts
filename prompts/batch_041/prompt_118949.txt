My thesis, roughly, and please tell me if it's mush:

The standard story about the Kelly criterion — bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth, don't overbet, growth beats greed — gets the order of explanation backwards. The real reason to size bets that way isn't "log is my utility function and I happen to be risk-averse." It's that anybody whose money compounds lives exactly one path through time. What happens to them is governed by the growth rate along that path, not by the average of their wealth across all the hypothetical parallel versions of them. That average is carried almost entirely by astronomically unlikely branches where they win forty coin flips in a row and end up owning the galaxy, and nobody lives in those branches. So: maximize the growth rate of the quantity that compounds for you, and it falls out as a theorem rather than a premise that you're maximizing expected log wealth. I've been calling this "geometric rationality" in my notes (I think I pinched the phrase from somewhere, dont hold me to it). The bit I actually want to defend is that this is a genuinely different and better-motivated criterion than expected-utility maximization — not just log utility wearing a hat and pretending to be a theory of rationality.
