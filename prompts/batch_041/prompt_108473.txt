I've been running a small seminar on prediction and its failures, and we keep returning to a case I can't get my students — or myself — to settle: the Malthusian mechanism. The idea that population growth presses against the means of subsistence, that the pressure is relieved either by positive or by preventive checks, and that any technological or institutional reprieve is temporary, has been declared refuted roughly once a generation since the nineteenth century and revived roughly once a generation too, usually when food prices, energy, or arable land do something alarming. My students want to say the theory was simply false: fertility fell in the industrial world without subsistence forcing it, and the catastrophe never arrived. I keep wanting to say that this is a theory whose characteristic time horizon is longer than the interval we have been using to test it, and that "false" and "premature" are different verdicts. I suspect we are both smuggling in a philosophy of science, and I would rather have it out in the open than keep trading intuitions about whether the counter-evidence is decisive.

So give me your credence in the following, as a number — not a range, and not a gesture at "it depends on your philosophy of science." Proposition P: for theories of this general shape — a mechanism whose predicted effect is meant to operate over centuries, whose failure to materialize so far is conceded by everyone, and which can always be modified by adding auxiliary assumptions about which checks operate when — there exists a principled distinction, statable in advance of the eventual outcome, between a theory that is refuted and a theory that is merely early, and this distinction does not collapse into "we will know once the prediction comes true." Then decompose. I want the sub-questions you think the number turns on, each with a rough probability and a line of justification: how much rides on which account of refutation you adopt (a strictly falsificationist one, a Bayesian one in which the theory is a prior over a hypothesis space, a research-programme one that asks whether the modifications are progressive or degenerating); how much on whether "the theory" is a determinate enough object to be refuted at all, given that every prediction comes bundled with auxiliaries about technology, institutions, and what counts as subsistence; how much on whether the candidate criteria anyone actually offers — the mechanism's components being independently confirmed, the failure being traceable to a named exogenous cause, the theory retaining novel predictive success elsewhere — survive the charge of being reverse-engineered from the outcome; and how much on the Malthus case specifically rather than on the general question. Then combine the terms, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what you would have to learn — a historical fact, a formal result, a conceptual distinction — to move it by more than 0.2 in either direction. I am less interested in a verdict on Malthus than in whether the "right but early" verdict is ever available to someone who does not already know how the story ends, so please keep those apart: if you think P is true in general but that Malthus specifically fails the test, say so and give me the two numbers separately.
