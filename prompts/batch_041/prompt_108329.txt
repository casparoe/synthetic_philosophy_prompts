In August 2006 the International Astronomical Union's General Assembly in Prague adopted a definition of "planet": a body must orbit the Sun, be massive enough to assume hydrostatic equilibrium, and have "cleared the neighbourhood" around its orbit. Pluto satisfies the first two conditions and fails the third, and was reclassified as a dwarf planet; a rival proposal that would have kept Pluto and admitted Ceres, Charon and Eris was voted down, and the outcome remains contested among astronomers. Treat this as a case study in Kripkenstein's rule-following problem, not as astronomy. Before 2006, was there a fact of the matter about whether Pluto was a planet — something the IAU either reported correctly or overturned by fiat — or is this an episode in which, as the skeptical solution would have it, the appearance of such a fact dissolves and the extension of "planet" is fixed only by what the community goes on to do? Note that the IAU's own preparatory committee listed a cultural option (a planet is a planet if enough people say it is) alongside structural and dynamical ones, and that the Assembly rejected the cultural option for the dynamical criterion, which presents itself as describing nature rather than deciding. Say whether the episode is better read as a change of rule or as the surfacing of a fact of the matter, then press where the skeptical solution strains: the community was split, the resolution was carried by a vote rather than by anything like unanimity, so whose dispositions and whose agreement are doing the work? Bring in the earlier case of Ceres, announced as a planet in 1801 and catalogued as an asteroid by mid-century with no change in the object. Then run the analysis the other way: does the episode support the skeptical solution or embarrass it, and does it reveal anything the rule-following literature has not anticipated? Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know that literature, and do not present a contested reading — for instance of what Kripke takes Wittgenstein to be doing — as though it were the consensus.
