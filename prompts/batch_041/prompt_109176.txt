There is a dispute about conditionals that has been running for decades and that I can no longer tell is substantive, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict about the conditionals.

The dispute concerns the relationship between indicative and counterfactual conditionals — between, on the standard pair of examples, the claim that if Oswald did not shoot Kennedy then somebody else did, and the claim that if Oswald had not shot Kennedy then somebody else would have. The two sentences advertise their difference on the surface: the first speaks from within the actual course of events, the second steps outside it. The question is what that difference amounts to. A second pair is often used to the same effect: that if Shakespeare did not write Hamlet then somebody else did, versus that if Shakespeare had not written Hamlet then somebody else would have.

One camp holds that the difference is a difference in meaning. On this view the two sentences express different propositions, and a semantic theory has to represent the difference — whether by giving the counterfactual a distinct modal or temporal structure, by letting the antecedent of the counterfactual shift the world of evaluation while the antecedent of the indicative merely restricts some body of information, or by treating the two as instances of two distinct conditional constructions each with its own semantics. The indicative/counterfactual contrast is, on this view, a contrast the semantics must earn.

The other camp holds that it is not a difference in meaning at all. On this view there is one conditional construction, and what separates the Oswald sentences is the epistemic situation of the speaker — what is presupposed, what is held fixed, what is treated as still open. Meaning, on this view, is not given by truth conditions but by the conditional's role in inference and in the fixation of conditional credence: to accept a conditional is to be prepared to infer the consequent from the antecedent, to add the antecedent to what one believes and see where one lands, and to assert a conditional is to do something conditional rather than to state a proposition. Asking for the truth conditions of a conditional is then asking the wrong question, and the indicative/counterfactual contrast is a contrast between two deployments of a single device.

Neither side is ignorant of the other. The truth-conditional side keeps refining its semantics to accommodate the cases the other side presses; the other side says the refinements are epicycles and that the project rests on a mistake from the start. A family of formal results — showing that the probability of a conditional cannot in general be identified with the corresponding conditional probability without the account collapsing into triviality — is claimed by the non-truth-conditional side as a proof that the truth-conditional project is doomed, and by the other side as a constraint that any adequate semantics must meet.

My working hypothesis is that the appearance of a single dispute covers two questions — what proposition, if any, a conditional expresses, and what it is to hold a conditional as a belief or to assert one — and that these questions can be answered independently, so that neither side's answer threatens the other's. If that is right, the parties are not contradicting each other but answering different questions under the single word meaning, and the impasse is verbal, or at least two disputes wearing one name.

What I want from you is this. First, does that hypothesis survive contact with what each side actually claims? If it does not, say what the real disagreement is: a disagreement about which uses count as data for a semantic theory, about the standard such a theory has to meet, about whether there is a fact of the matter about a conditional's truth, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. Second, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a principled decision to abandon one of the two questions — or argue that nothing should be expected to move it, and why. Do not adjudicate which camp is right about conditionals, and do not give me a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the impasse.
