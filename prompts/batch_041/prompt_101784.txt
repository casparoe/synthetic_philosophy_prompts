I'm a journalist who covers markets and money — I read the FT and central-bank blogs for a living, I know my way around a yield curve, and I've wrote enough about payment systems that unit of account, medium of exchange and store of value roll off the tounge, but I never did an econ degree and I've never touched a real theory paper. I keep circling back to Aristotle's stuff on exchange and money — the bit in the Ethics about justice in exchange and why there has to be some common measure of different goods, and the Politics bit where he splits household management off from money-making and goes after lending at interest. I want you to restate his position in the vocabulary I actually use: price discovery, liquidity, bid-ask spreads, the numeraire, double coincidence of wants, fungibility, fiat vs commodity money, seigniorage, legal tender, marginal utility, arbitrage, credit and settlement. As faithfully as you can. Don't turn him into a proto-Austrian or a proto-Marxist, and don't hand me back my own market assumptions dressed up as his views.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What translates cleanly, what only translates if you fudge it, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. In particular, does his claim that money exists by convention, and that it's need (not preference, not utility) that makes different goods commensurable, survive the translation or just evaporate? What happens to his natural-vs-unnatural acquisition split and the usury argument once you're talking about liquidity, credit and risk premia — is the leftover a real feature of his view that my vocabulary is blind to, or is it just him lacking the concepts, so the residue is his confusion rather than mine? And flag anywhere you had to smuggle assumptions from my side in and call it a translation.
