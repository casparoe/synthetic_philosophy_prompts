I'm teaching a night class on Shakespeare and political theory and next week it's Coriolanus. I want a reading of the play through the lens of populism and the idea of 'the will of the people', and I want it to actually earn its keep rather than just pinning a label on Coriolanus for being a snob about the plebs. What nags at me is that the play seems to run on two incompatible pictures of what the people's will is. On one picture it's just a count: the citizens file past the candidate and give him their voices, you add them up, and that's the will. On the other picture there's meant to be a single voice of the people, and the whole fight in the play is over who gets to be its mouthpiece — the tribunes claim it, Coriolanus flatly denies the plebeians are the people at all, and Menenius tries to fuse them into one body (though I don't want the body-politic fable to swallow the whole thing). Read the play against both pictures: which one does it actually dramatise, and is the gap between them the play's insight or just a muddle? I keep coming back to the scene where he has to stand in the market and solicit the voices — does that scene show the aggregate picture is the real one, and the single-voice picture is a fiction that somebody is always ventriloquising (the tribunes, mostly)? And does the ending, with the people turning and the tribunes winning, settle anything, or does it just show the crowd is fickle, which is a cheaper and different point? Lastly, does Shakespeare show up something the theory of populism tends to miss — that the demos might not have a will there waiting to be represented in the first place? British English please, no footnotes, and don't quote long passages at me, I know the play.
