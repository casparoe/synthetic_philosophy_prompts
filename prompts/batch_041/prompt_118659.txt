I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas, and I have reached the stage with a piece where I need an interlocutor rather than a copy editor. The piece concerns two ideas that the AI-safety discussion tends to invoke in the same breath: the alignment tax — the capability, speed, or reliability a developer forgoes in order to build a system that is genuinely safe — and the competitiveness constraint, the thought that any safety practice must survive rivalry, since a developer who is much slower or much costlier than the field will lose to whoever is willing to skip the practice. My editor treats these as two separate worries and takes the interesting question to be how steep the tax can get before the constraint bites. A friend at a policy institute says the whole debate is a reheated version of arguments about regulated industries and that I am wasting a fortnight. I think both of them are wrong, and the draft below is my attempt to say why. I would rather have comments that are harsh and specific than ones that are encouraging, and I am not looking for citations, a reading list, or a survey of who has said what.

The draft runs as follows. The usual picture treats the alignment tax as a cost of production, like a factory's pollution controls, and the competitiveness constraint as an external ceiling on how much of that cost a firm can absorb. That picture assumes the tax is levied on a single firm's output, so that the only unknowns are its size and the firm's willingness to pay. But a tax levied on one class of producer, in a market where an untaxed substitute exists, is not borne by the producer at all; the burden settles wherever it is hardest to move. Public finance has a name for this, incidence: the side of the market with the less elastic options keeps the cost, and the side with somewhere else to go passes it on. Carbon pricing taught the same lesson in another register, as production migrated to jurisdictions that never paid the price.

If the taxed good is safe capability, the untaxed substitute is unsafe capability, produced by labs that, by hypothesis, are the ones we least want producing. The tax therefore does not merely slow the taxed labs; it reallocates talent, compute, and market share toward the untaxed ones. Whether the net effect on the world is safety or danger depends on elasticities nobody measures: how easily researchers move between labs, how substitutable a cautious training regime is for a reckless one, how much the buyers of capability care where it came from. My claim is that the competitiveness constraint is not an obstacle standing outside the alignment tax; it is that tax's incidence. The two ideas are one idea.

The practical consequence is that the base of the tax matters more than its rate. A safety practice levied on something with a low elasticity of substitution — a shared input, a chokepoint, a credential everyone needs — behaves quite differently from one levied on a firm's own throughput, which can be dropped and replaced elsewhere. And a practice that raises capability while it raises safety is not a tax at all but a free lunch, and the field's habitual talk of costs quietly concedes how rare those are.

What I want from you: first, is the incidence analogy doing real work, or is it decorative — an economic metaphor draped over a claim that stands or falls on its own? Second, is the central claim falsifiable, and what observations would count against it? Third, which of the three paragraphs is doing the least work, and what would you cut? Fourth, if the framing gives the wrong answer somewhere, give me one concrete case and explain the failure. Fifth, and most important for me: is this genuinely a new angle, or have I reinvented something the field settled years ago and my friend is right to be smug about? Tell me where the load-bearing wall is, and whether it holds.
