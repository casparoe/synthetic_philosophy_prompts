For much of the twentieth century, and in many hospitals well into the 1980s, surgeons routinely performed major operations on newborns with minimal or no anaesthesia; where a drug was given, it was often a neuromuscular blocker that abolished movement and crying without dulling sensation. The justifications offered were a mixture of physiology and inference: that the neonatal nervous system was too immature to register pain, that what looked like distress was reflex rather than experience, and that even if something were felt it would not be remembered. From the late 1970s the practice came under sustained attack — through measurements of stress-hormone and other physiological responses, through parents' accounts, and through a growing clinical literature on neonatal pain — and it was progressively abandoned, with professional bodies conceding by the end of the 1980s that newborns feel pain.
