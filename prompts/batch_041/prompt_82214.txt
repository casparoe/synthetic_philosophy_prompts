I am a high school student, and for a project on the philosophy of mind I have been reading about pain asymbolia, the condition in which injury to the brain, and in particular to the insula and its connections, leaves a patient able to register a noxious stimulus and to report that it is painful, while insisting that it does not bother them in the least; some descriptions even have the patient smiling or laughing as the limb is withdrawn. This case looks to me as though it resolves an old puzzle, and I want to check whether I have read it correctly. If the sensation of pain can survive intact while all of its unpleasantness is removed by a lesion, then surely the unpleasantness cannot be part of the sensation itself; it must be a separate reaction that the brain fastens onto an otherwise neutral feeling, the way a warning bell might be fastened onto a gauge. And if the unpleasantness is only such an attachment, it ought in principle to be detachable by other means as well, by habituation or training or a deliberate change of attitude, so that pain would be bad only because we mind it, and a person who had truly mastered the reaction would find nothing bad left in it. So my question is whether this is the correct way to read the condition. If it is, does the goal of a pain-free life come down to the goal of no longer caring about pain, and is the asymbolic patient simply the person who has already arrived there? I am especially puzzled about whether the patient who says the pain is present but does not bother them is feeling the same thing I feel when I am hurt, minus one ingredient, or whether the report signals that something else has happened to the experience altogether.
