I am a backend engineer on the platform team, and I have ended up owning the change-approval gate that every production change passes through. The gate is a majority vote: a fixed panel of reviewers looks at a change, each votes ship or hold, and the majority decides. We always staff panels with an odd number of reviewers. What makes the gate measurable is roughly three years of audited history: every change in that window carries a label for whether it caused an incident within two weeks, so for any reviewer I can estimate how often they are right, and for any panel I can estimate how often the majority was right.

Our staff engineer has been the informal authority on how to staff these panels, and her track record is what I am trying to reverse-engineer. In each of the cases below she gave her call before we ran the configuration, and the audit bore her out every time.

- Three reviewers replacing a single reviewer; each of the three is right about 70% of the time, they come from three different teams, and their mistakes are essentially uncorrelated. Her call: better than one reviewer, adopt it.
- Three reviewers, again about 70% each, but all three were trained on the same team's playbook, and on the audited changes their mistakes coincide almost exactly. Her call: no better than a single reviewer, and she said not to expect better, because the majority simply reprints the shared blind spot.
- Five reviewers, each right only about 40% of the time, mistakes uncorrelated. Her call: worse than a coin flip and worse than a single reviewer, and she added that we would do better to take one reviewer's vote and invert it.
- Twenty-one reviewers, each right about 55%, mistakes uncorrelated. Her call: better than a single reviewer, and better than a seven-reviewer panel of the same kind.
- Nine reviewers, five of them right about 80% and four about 55%, mistakes uncorrelated. Her call: better than a single reviewer, and she was untroubled by the spread in quality.
- Four reviewers, each about 80%, whose misses overlap somewhat because all four lean on the same linter. Her call: still better than a single reviewer, but less so than if their misses had been independent.

Note that none of those configurations mixes reviewers who are better than chance with reviewers who are worse than chance, which is precisely the situation I am now in.

The decision in front of us: our current gate is three reviewers, each right about 90% of the time on the audited changes, with uncorrelated mistakes. Four engineers on a neighbouring team have offered to join the panel. On this class of change they are poor — each is right about 30% of the time, worse than a coin flip, and their votes are uncorrelated both with each other's and with the three reviewers we already have; they tend to wave through the changes that fail and block the ones that do not. If we take all seven, the panel votes seven ways and four votes carry. The average member of that seven-person panel would be right about 56% of the time, which is above a coin flip.

What I want from you: her likely call on the seven-person panel, and, more than that, the decision rule that best accounts for her six earlier calls. Please set out the competing rules that fit all six of those calls equally well, say what each one predicts here, tell me which you would bet on and why, and describe the cheapest configuration we could actually run that would separate the rules. If her record genuinely does not settle the matter, tell me what we would be committing to with each choice. I would also like to know how much of this is arithmetic I can check myself and how much is a real question about which rule she is running; one of the seniors told me it is all just the old jury theorem and obvious, which is not how it looks from where I sit.
