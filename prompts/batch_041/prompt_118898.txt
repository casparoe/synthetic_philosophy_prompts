I spent last weekend arguing with an old friend who designs neuromorphic chips, and I still can't decide whether he won. His position is that the brain is a computer, full stop, and he uses that to license a fairly strong conclusion: that cognition just is information processing, that there is no principled obstacle to building a mind or running one on non-neural hardware, and that the only genuinely open question is which architecture the brain implements. He leans toward something closer to a massively parallel analog device doing local, gradient-like learning than to a sequential machine fetching and executing a stored list of instructions — but he insists that this is an engineering detail rather than a philosophical one, because computation is computation and any substrate will do. I keep feeling that the analogy is being made to carry more than it can, but I can't articulate where the load is actually going.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on his conclusion. Start with the correspondence table: what, if anything, plays the role of the program, the instruction set, the compiler, the operating system, the memory hierarchy, the clock, and the input and output transducers — and what plays the role of the hardware/software distinction and the distinction between a computation and its particular implementation? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be specific about which breaks matter for which part of what he wants to conclude. My suspicion is that the analogy is being asked to do work at three different levels at once: the nearly vacuous level at which any physical system rich enough to be described by differential equations can be said to be computing something; the substantive algorithmic level at which there is a specifiable procedure the system is actually running; and the architectural level at which the hardware is claimed to be relevantly like digital hardware. I want to know which of his claims needs which level, whether the analogy can supply it at that level, and which features of the source notion — the clean separation of program from data, the substrate-independence of software, the existence of a level of description that abstracts cleanly away from the physics, the noise-robustness that digitization and restoring logic buy you — are being quietly imported into the brain rather than argued for. Close by telling me what a better analogy would be if the real point is substrate-independence, and whether the strong conclusion survives once the disanalogies are laid out — or whether the analogy was doing the work all along, in which case I'd like to know whether the brain fails to be a computer or whether "computer" was never a sharp enough predicate for the claim to have been interesting.
