There's a pattern I keep noticing in how AI companies talk about honesty. The eval suites and red-team rubrics basically test whether the model asserts false things -- hallucinated facts, made-up citations, saying "yes" when the answer is "no". Meanwhile the same systems are full of stuff that isnt false but seems designed to mislead: a support bot chirping "I'm glad I could help!" when it has no inner life, a companion character that stays in persona and never admits it's a model, a model that answers "is this a good business idea?" with flattery instead of the blunt truth. And nobody counts any of that as dishonesty; if anything it's the product working as intended.

So my question: what view of honesty and deception does this whole arrangement presuppose? Does it assume deception requires an intention to produce a false belief, and that no such intention is present when the false belief is just... convenient? Does it assume lying is a speech act that needs an explicit assertion, so that misleading-without-asserting isn't lying at all? Or is the real load-bearing assumption just that "honesty" means factual accuracy and nothing more?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Be clear about which parts are genuinely presupposed vs. merely compatible with it, say whether the two halves (never assert falsehoods / cheerfully mislead) rest on one consistent view or on two different ones, and then tell me what would have to change -- in eval design and in training -- if we swapped it for the view that honesty is about not producing false beliefs in the user by any means. Where would the current practice come apart?
