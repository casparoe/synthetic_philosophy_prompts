A friend of mine has been reading Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty and has begun sorting propositions into those that stand fast for her — exempt from doubt, part of the scaffolding against which inquiry goes on — and those that are ordinary empirical claims, testable and revisable. Her verdicts so far are these. That the earth existed long before she was born: stands fast. That water boils at a hundred degrees at sea level: does not stand fast; she learned it, she could check it, and it might have been otherwise. That she has two hands, as she looks at them: stands fast, though she grants this is not something she knows in the evidential way she knows the boiling point. That her name is her name: stands fast, and she says it is not the kind of thing she could be mistaken about. That no human being had walked on the moon, which stood fast for her grandparents and no longer stands fast for her: this, she says, is what shows that standing fast is not the same as being certain forever. Now I put a further case to her. A hospital pharmacist is certain, without ever having tested it and without knowing how she would test it, that the drug she dispenses is what its label says it is; the matter is never raised as a question and quietly governs everything she does at work. Will my friend say this stands fast? I care less about her yes or no than about the rule she has actually been running — whether she counts as a hinge whatever serves as unquestioned background within a practice, or only propositions of some special sort, such as those never learned and never verifiable, or those that any inquiry whatever must presuppose — and about what each of those rules predicts for the pharmacist. Say which rule best fits her verdicts, whether fitting them all requires giving one up, and which single further case would force her to choose between the survivors.
