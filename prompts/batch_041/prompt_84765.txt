I am preparing a two-page research proposal for a semester-long project supported by an internal grant, and I would like you to draft it. The project is in consequentialist ethics, on the problem usually called cluelessness: the thought that because the total consequences of any act stretch indefinitely into the future, and may be dominated by vanishingly improbable but enormous effects, a consequentialist can never know whether the act before her is for the best, so the theory threatens to give no guidance at all.

My half-formed idea is that the debate conflates two distinct obstacles, and that separating them reshapes the problem. The first is ordinary ignorance: there is a determinate set of possible outcomes, and we do not know their probabilities. The second I want to call open-endedness: the set of outcomes is not fixed in advance, since the consequences of an act ramify into an open future and may include kinds of outcome that could not have been represented even as possibilities — roughly, the difference between drawing from an urn whose contents are hidden and drawing from an urn whose contents are themselves partly settled by the draw. The familiar reply to cluelessness — do your best under uncertainty, by expected value — plainly addresses the first obstacle. I suspect it does nothing for the second, and that the substantive problem is entirely about the second. If so, cluelessness is not an epistemic deficit that better information could repair but a structural feature of treating the open future as the object of moral evaluation.

Turn this into a proposal. State the central question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, and lay out the candidate answers — for instance, that the diagnosis calls for a consequentialism with a narrower object (acts assessed by their foreseeable or near-term consequences), or for a different criterion of rightness altogether (robust or satisficing rather than totalizing), or that the distinction I am drawing collapses. Explain why the existing positions on cluelessness do not already settle the question, and set out the method week by week across a semester. Say what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure — in particular, what it would take to show that open-endedness reduces to ignorance under a sufficiently rich probability space, which would sink the idea. If space allows, add whether theories that are not consequentialist but still assign weight to consequences inherit the same problem. Two pages; no citations or literature review, since I will supply those myself.
