I'm prepping the universals unit for my ancient philosophy survey, and I keep getting tangled up in the opening of Categories 5, where Aristotle writes:

'Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse. But in a secondary sense those things are called substances within which, as species, the primary substances are included; also those which, as genera, include the species.'

Here's my problem, and I can't decide whether I'm just overthinking a translation. Primary substance gets defined by NOT being predicable of a subject. Fine. But then he turns around and calls species and genera substances too, and those are exactly the things that get predicated of subjects -- 'man' is said of Socrates, 'animal' is said of man. So the chapter seems to say, in one breath, that what makes something a substance is that it can't be predicated, and in the next breath, that the things which can be predicated are substances as well. A colleague of mine waves this away by saying the secondary substances are only substances 'in the order of predication' -- on her reading they earn the label because they're what answers the question 'what is it?' about a primary substance, which is a fact about how we say what things are, not about what there is. I suspect she's smuggling in the later Metaphysics stuff, but I can't quite put my finger on why that's wrong.

So: could you walk me through whats actually doing the work in the passage -- what the 'predicable of a subject' vs. 'present in a subject' contrast is for, and how a species can be both said-of-many and a substance -- and then tell me whether my colleague's 'order of predication' reading is defensible, or whether I should just be telling the students that species and genera really are substances for Aristotle, only in a derivative sense? One of my students also asked whether 'included in' is transitive, so that if animal is included in man and man is included in Socrates then Socrates somehow contains every genus above him, and I'd love an answer to that which doesn't make me look like I've never read the thing. A couple of sentences on how all this squares with the later claim that no universal is a substance would also help, since someone always asks. No need for reading lists -- I just want the passage straight.
