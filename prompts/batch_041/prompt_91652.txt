I am trying to work through the argument in Poincaré's Science and Hypothesis that is usually discussed under the heading of the non-Euclidean world or the sphere world. I no longer have the book in front of me and I keep losing the thread of the argument, so what follows is my own plain-prose rendering of the stretch I am stuck on. It is a paraphrase, not a quotation, and it may distort the original in small ways; if you know the original and I have something wrong, correct it, but please do not let the correction stand in for the interpretive verdict I ask for at the end.

The setup, as I understand it, is this. Imagine a world enclosed inside a large sphere. The temperature is greatest at the centre and falls steadily toward the surface, and in this world bodies expand and contract with temperature, so that a measuring rod is longer when warm and shorter when cold. The inhabitants move about and measure distances with such rods; they also use rays of light, which in this world do not travel in what we would call straight lines. A rod carried from the centre toward the surface therefore shrinks, and a rod carried back toward the centre lengthens. Poincaré asks us to work out what geometry these beings would arrive at if they did the ordinary things geometers do — measured triangles, compared angles, and so on. His answer is that they would find the angles of a triangle to sum to less than two right angles, and would be led to describe their space in the manner of Lobachevsky. But — and this is the part I lose hold of — he also insists that this does not show that their space really is non-Euclidean, nor that ours really is Euclidean. The inhabitants would have no reason to regard their geometry as stranger than we regard ours, and no experiment available to them within their world could force them to abandon it. He goes on to say something to the effect that we do not ask whether Euclidean geometry is true but whether it is convenient, and that our choice of geometry is a matter of convention rather than of discovery.

Here is what I am stuck on. I can follow the mechanical part: rods shrink, light bends, so measurements come out hyperbolic. What I cannot pin down is what exactly Poincaré takes this to establish about geometry. Three readings seem to come apart, and I cannot tell which one the sphere world is supposed to support:

1. There is a fact of the matter about which geometry is true, but experience can never settle it, so we are simply ignorant of something real.
2. There is no fact of the matter at all; the question of the true geometry is a pseudo-question, and truth-talk applies only within a geometry once it has been chosen as a language.
3. Experience underdetermines the choice, so we settle it by convenience; Poincaré is neutral on whether there is a fact of the matter, and the argument establishes only the underdetermination.

I would also like to know what work the point about the inhabitants noticing nothing odd is doing. Is it meant to show that their geometry is as justified as ours, or something stronger — that there is no difference between the two situations to be justified in the first place?
