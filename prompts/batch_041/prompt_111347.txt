I have been going in circles with two friends about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I have decided that trading intuitions about cases is pointless until I know what a theory of the matter would even have to accomplish. One friend takes the Epicurean line: harm requires a subject who can be on the receiving end of it, the dead are not subjects of anything, and therefore nothing can be bad for the dead — our grief at a death is grief over a loss to us, or a confusion about grammar. The other friend says this conflates not being able to suffer with not being able to be wronged, and that death is bad because of everything it takes away. Before I take a side, I want the specification: the desiderata any theory of the badness of death must satisfy before its verdict on the Epicurean argument is worth anything. I do not want a reading list or citations — I cannot check them, and would rather have the argument carried by the cases.

Here is the material the list has to cover, and you should treat each item as a demand on the theory rather than a debating point. A woman dies in her sleep, painlessly and without warning, at forty, at a moment when her life is going about as well as a life can go and nothing is left unfinished that she wanted finished; almost everyone says something has been taken from her, and if that is right the theory must locate the badness somewhere other than in her experience, since by hypothesis she experiences nothing. A man dies at forty-five who would, had he lived, have spent his remaining years in unremitting pain and dementia, and here death looks less like a loss than a relief, so the badness of dying cannot be intrinsic to dying but must be read off the value of the life that would otherwise have gone on. If that is the shape of the account, the question of when death is bad becomes pressing: not before, since she is alive and well; not during, since she does not exist; not after, for the same reason — and if you abandon the requirement that harms be datable and have a subject, I want to know what work the word harm is still doing. We mourn the years we will not have after death but not the years before our birth, though the two stretches are alike in everything but direction; the theory must either explain that asymmetry or explain it away as a quirk of our temporal perspective, and if it does the latter I want to know whether the same debunking move drains the force of the deprivation intuition too. A man's reputation is destroyed after his death, he never learns of it, and no one who cared about him survives; decide whether he is harmed, and you have decided whether the harm of death must be a state of a person or may be a fact about a life. If dying young is bad because of the good years it deprives you of, then never conceiving a child deprives that child of the same good years, and the theory must either accept the consequence or supply a restriction that the death case does not obviously provide. And since we take some deaths to be worse than others, the theory needs a measure of how much was lost and not merely a verdict that something was. The purpose I am fixing is narrow: I want an account good enough to tell me whether it makes sense to want more life, and whether a doctor may count added years as a benefit to a patient who is content and competent — that is, whether longevity is a good in itself or only ever instrumental.

Write this as follows. First, a short section titled The target (two or three sentences) fixing what the theory is a theory of, in particular what bad for you is supposed to mean here. Second, The requirements, the bulk of the answer — as many numbered items as the material above genuinely supports, each stating the requirement in one sentence, naming the case or consideration behind it, and tagging it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a commitment of one camp only; a table is fine if it keeps each item to the point. Third, Tensions, roughly two hundred words, on which subsets of the requirements you believe cannot be satisfied together, with the reason — this is the part I care about most, and if the list itself turns out to be a result, say so. Fourth, The sharpest requirement, about a hundred words on which single item excludes the most otherwise attractive theories. Fifth, The contenders, a hundred and fifty words scoring the main families of answer against the list — the view that harm needs a living subject, the view that death is bad because of what it deprives you of, and any third you think earns its place. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will do the arguing myself.
