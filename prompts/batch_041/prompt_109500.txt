I have been trying to rebuild Integrated Information Theory from its own starting points, and I have arrived at an argument that appears to show the theory cannot say who or what is conscious. The trouble is that the argument looks as though it would do the same to any theory that assigns experiences to physical systems, so I suspect it proves too much. Here is the theory as I reconstruct it, then the argument.

On my reconstruction, the theory begins with a small set of properties that are supposed to be self-evident about experience. Experience exists. It is intrinsic, existing for itself. It is specific: it is this experience and not another. It is unified: irreducible to its parts taken separately. It is structured: composed of distinguishable distinctions that can be combined. And it is definite, or exclusive: it is a single experience with a boundary, so that there are not several overlapping experiences at the same time. Each property is then converted into a requirement on a physical substrate, phrased in terms of the substrate's cause-effect power, its capacity to make a difference to itself. A substrate is conscious to the degree that it specifies a cause-effect structure that is irreducible to its parts, irreducibility being measured by integrated information (Φ); and by the requirement corresponding to definiteness, the conscious system is the set of units that is maximally irreducible, which the theory calls the maximal substrate, or complex.

The argument:

(1) By the definiteness requirement, the subject of experience at a given time is the set of units whose cause-effect structure is maximally irreducible.
(2) Maximal irreducibility is not a property of the world in the raw; it is defined only relative to a specification, namely which units are counted, at what grain (a neuron, a cortical column, a coarse state variable), and over what spatial and temporal boundary.
(3) For any physical system, several specifications are defensible: the brain; a thalamocortical loop; the brain together with the body; the brain together with its environment; the entire causally connected network. Different specifications yield different maximally irreducible structures.
(4) Different maximally irreducible structures are different subjects, by (1).
(5) Nothing in the axioms or postulates privileges one specification. The axioms are claims about experience, and experience does not announce whether its substrate is the whole brain, a sheet of cortex, or something larger.
(6) So, on this theory, which system a given experience belongs to, and even whether there is one experience or several, depends on a specification the theory leaves open.
(7) That makes the identity of the subject of experience indeterminate, which contradicts the axiom that experience is definite and has a boundary.
(8) Therefore the theory is internally incoherent.

Why I think this proves too much: replace the phrase "maximally irreducible cause-effect structure" with whatever any other theory takes to individuate a subject, whether a functional organization, a computational role, or a biological boundary, and the same reasoning runs. Any theory that assigns experiences to systems must say which system, and any such choice can be made to look like a modeling convention rather than a fact in the world. If the argument sinks all of them, the fault is probably not where I aimed it.

So where does it go wrong, if it does? The claims I would least like to surrender are (1) and the definiteness of experience. If something else has to go, I want to know which step it is, why the argument made it look surrenderable, and what the repair costs, including whether the theory can still say that some structures support consciousness and others do not.
