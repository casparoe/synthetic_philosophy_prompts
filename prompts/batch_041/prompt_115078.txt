I have been trying for a while to find out whether something has a name, and I keep failing, so I am going to describe it as carefully as I can and ask you either to identify the established concept or to build me one. Here is the pattern. A single word is in good standing in two communities that speak the same language, and in each community it has a settled, internally coherent meaning, but the two meanings have drifted apart along an axis that people actually care about. My American friends use "liberal" for someone on the left: in favour of redistribution, socially permissive, broadly aligned with the Democratic Party. My European friends, some of whom call themselves liberal with real pride, use it for something like the opposite end of the economic spectrum — pro-market, suspicious of state intervention, by no means necessarily on the left; the sort of position a German Free Democrat or a Dutch VVD voter would recognise as their own. What strikes me is not that the senses differ but how each side handles the other's: neither experiences its own usage as loose, metaphorical, or partisan. Each treats its sense as the plain one and the other's as a distortion, a euphemism, or a sign that the other lot have hijacked a good word. So when the two communities argue — over whether some policy is liberal, whether some regime is liberal, what a liberal international order would even consist in — part of the disagreement is about the world and part of it is about which concept the word has been hitched to, and the participants cannot easily separate the two, because from inside either usage the other side simply looks like it is misusing a word whose meaning is obvious.

Once I started noticing this I found more cases, and they do not all behave alike, which is part of why I cannot place it. Some are cross-linguistic in the ordinary way — the French libertaire and the American libertarian look like the same word and pull in nearly opposite political directions — and for those I know there is a familiar term from language teaching, though I suspect it does not quite fit the cases that trouble me. What puzzles me is the within-one-language versions, and also the cases where a third sense is circulating at the same time. In "liberal democracy," for instance, liberal seems to mean something closer to constitutionalist and rights-respecting, which is neither the American nor the market sense, so within one conversation you can have three live meanings and nobody flags the switches. I have wondered about "federalist" too: it named the friends of central authority at the American founding, it now often names a preference for state-level power, and in European discussion it names a preference for more authority at the supranational centre, so the same word has pointed at both ends of one axis depending on the decade and the continent. I have been calling these two-way false friends in my own notes, but I do not think that is right, because the ordinary false-friend idea is about a learner's mistake across two languages and does not require either party to be a fluent speaker with a coherent home usage.

So what I am asking for is the carving, not the label. Is there an established concept — in lexical semantics, in the history of political thought, in the literature on contested concepts, wherever it lives — that captures this specific configuration: one word, two or more settled usages, each internally coherent, the divergence systematic rather than idiosyncratic, and the mismatch generating disputes that are neither purely verbal nor straightforwardly substantive? If there is a term, tell me what it is, what it covers and what it leaves out, and how it differs from the nearest neighbours: from false friends, from ordinary polysemy, from the idea of an essentially contested concept, and from plain semantic drift, where the older sense simply dies rather than coexisting and sniping at its successor. And if nothing fits, coin something and define it properly, including the boundary cases I am unsure about. Does it apply when the two usages are merely unfamiliar to each other, or only when they are systematically opposed? What about a case where one community is fully aware of the other's sense and quietly trades on it, while the other remains oblivious? I would rather have the phenomenon's edges drawn correctly than have a tidy name for it.
