A chapter in a philosophy of mind textbook I've been reading summarises disjunctivism, and I want to know how much of the summary to trust. Could you go through it and tell me what a disjunctivist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong?

The gist of the chapter is this. Naive realism — the view that in perception we're directly aware of mind-independent objects — was killed off by the argument from illusion: because an illusory experience can be subjectively exactly like a veridical one, what we're immediately aware of in both cases has to be something mental, a sense-datum, rather than the tomato itself. Disjunctivism is the modern attempt to bring naive realism back from the dead. Its move is to insist that the good case and the bad case are two different kinds of experience, so the argument from illusion never gets going: in the good case you're aware of the tomato, in the bad case you're not aware of any tomato at all. The price is that the disjunctivist has to deny that the hallucinating subject and the perceiving subject are in the same mental state, which seems to fly in the face of the obvious fact that the two situations can be indistinguishable from the inside; the chapter says they soften this by conceding that the two states share their content and differ only in their cause. It also says the view is on firmest ground with hallucination, where the disjunctivist can simply deny that there's any experience at all, and gets shaky with illusion, where there's a real object in play. It adds that all this is a matter for the metaphysics of experience and has no bearing on whether perception gives us knowledge. And it ends by hinting that the position is a bit of a verbal rescue: the disjunctivist just defines 'experience' in a way that keeps the sceptic's argument out.

I have a feeling the last jibe is unfair, and that the stuff about same content but different cause is doing something sneaky, but I dont know the literature well enough to say. I know McDowell's name is attached to this view, thats about all. So: which sentences survive, which need a big qualification, and does the 'it's just a redefinition' verdict hold up once the mistakes are fixed?
