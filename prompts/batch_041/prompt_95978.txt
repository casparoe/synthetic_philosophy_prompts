ok, I've been rereading Aristotle's stuff on exchange — the bit in Nicomachean Ethics book 5 about justice in exchange, and the Politics book 1 stuff on household management vs. trade — and I keep getting stuck on the commensurabilty thing. He says that for exchange to happen there has to be some one common measure, then basically concedes that things as unlike as a house and a shoe can't really be commensurable in truth, and that money is what makes them commensurable, money being something that exists by convention and not by nature. So what exactly is money doing here? I've been calling it the two-measures problem: need (chreia) is supposed to be the real measure that holds the community together, and money is the conventional stand-in for need, so you end up with two candidates for the measure. Which one is actually doing the measuring, and what does Aristotle's answer commit him to?

Please explain this as a conceptual issue, not a summary of the passages. In particular: is money revealing a commensurability that was already there in the goods, or manufacturing one that isn't there in nature? Does the real measure live in the things, or in the exchangers (their needs, their demand)? And what hangs on it — (a) can there be an objective just price in exchange at all, and (b) how does it bear on his claim that usury is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting, given that money is itself a creature of convention? Also say how this fits or clashes with the Politics distinction between natural household acquisition and trade for profit — is the problem the aim (wealth for its own sake) or the exchange itself? I want the anatomy of the puzzle, not a gloss, so if the three readings I'm gesturing at are the wrong way to carve it up, say so and carve it differently.
