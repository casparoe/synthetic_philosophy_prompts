I work at a jobcentre-style welfare office and several times a day my job is to decide whether to start a sanction — cut someone's benefit for a few weeks — because they didn't do what the conditions say (missed a work-search appointment, turned down a job, didn't apply to enough places). I've got maybe fifteen minutes per case and no philosophy training, my supervisor has to sign off on anything unusual, and there's an appeals process, so I have to be able to write down why. Right now we basically go by the rulebook, which is "did they fail to comply, yes/no", and I've started to feel sick about it.

I want you to turn the philosophical work on the 'undeserving poor' into a decision procedure I can actually run on a case, question by question, in order. Build it around three things I keep tripping over. First, the difference between desert in the strict sense — where someone only deserves blame if the failure was under their control and they had a real alternative — and reciprocity, where the claim is that support is owed to people who work and can be pulled from people who won't, whether or not they could. Second, the difference between me assessing what this person deserves and me assessing what the policy is justified in doing to them; I want those kept seperate even where they end up giving the same answer. Third, the way a category like 'undeserving' gets built administratively and quietly lumps together people who defrauded us, people who are just idle, people who are unlucky, and people who are ill or illiterate and can't navigate the forms — I want the procedure to force the case into one of those boxes or to say it can't.

For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, which ones are factual and which are value judgments I'm not the right person to make, and exactly where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a human instead of deciding. Then run it on three cases: a guy who missed two appointments because the letters went to an old adress, a woman who turned down a shift because it clashed with caring for her mother and didn't tell us in time, and someone who did apply for jobs but only ones he knew he'd never get — and be honest if the procedure says to sanction any of them. Tell me what the procedure can't do, where it'll misfire, and which of my questions it turns out never had a factual answer.

And don't hedge. Take a definite position on whether the control condition should be built into the sanction rule and defend it, even if that makes the procedure harder to run and gives me answers I don't want.
