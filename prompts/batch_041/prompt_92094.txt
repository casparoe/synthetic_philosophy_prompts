Ok so me and two friends in my reading group have been arguing for like a year about cluelessness — the worry that because of butterfly effects and super long causal chains we basically can't know the expected consequences of anything we do, so consequentialism can't actually tell us what to do. One of them is what I'll call the pessimist: thinks this more or less sinks consequentialism as a practical guide. The other is the optimist: thinks it's way overblown, that we can still reason about consequences at a coarse grain, and anyway deontologists and virtue ethicists are just as clueless about whether they've done the right thing.

I want to write the joint statement that both of them could actually put their names to. Not a summary of the debate — an actual document. So:

Give those three a name each, and make the pessimist and the optimist each say which one their arguement actually needs.

Second, a disagreements table. One row per genuine disagreement. Columns: the pessimist's formulation (worded so the pessimist would sign it), the optimist's formulation (worded so the optimist would sign it), the crux, and what each side predicts about the crux before anyone checks. I want at least these two cruxes, each with a concrete case or arguement we all agree actually bears on it:
1. whether the expected value of a typical action is even well-defined, or whether it gets swamped / dominated by tiny-probability huge-value far-future effects (the Pascal's-mugging-flavored worry, plus the nastier version where the sum over the far future doesn't converge at all);

Keep it in a form we can literally sign. Don't water either side down into mush to make them agree — the agreed claims have to be things both would actually defend, not things nobody would object to.
