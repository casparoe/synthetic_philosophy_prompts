I'm putting together a little fiction-adjacent philosophy thing and I need a short story from you, roughly 900-1300 words. The theme is the gap between a theory being falsifiable in principle and anyone actually treating it as falsified. Demarcation/falsification territory, basically, but I don't want a single sentence of it explained to the reader.

Set it somewhere specific and stay there — a provincial teaching hospital in the seventies, a Soviet soil-science lab, a nineties nutrition institute, whatever grabs you, just keep it in the past and keep it concrete. Make the research programme up yourself rather than borrowing a real one: a middling scientific claim with a mechanism, two or three bold predictions that would be properly embarrassing if they failed, and a small group of people whose careers, grants and friendships are tangled up in it.

Then I want four beats in there:

1. One of the bold predictions has flopped, and the failure gets absorbed by adding something to the theory. That addition has to be the sort of move a serious, honest researcher could genuinely make — not a strawman patching things up out of stubbornness — and at least one character should be able to say, fairly, that the patch is definately the kind of thing that could also be true. Extra credit if you show whether the patch ever generates a new prediction that somebody actually goes and tests, or whether everyone just likes the sound of it and moves on.

2. Someone — probably the most junior person in the room — proposes the experiment that both sides would agree would settle it. And the reason it can't be run properly, or can't be trusted once it is, is mundane: a broken thermostat, a batch of reagent that sat in a hot van, a technician who cut a corner and owned up to it. Both sides need a genuinely good argument about whether the run counts.

3. Somewhere in there, a character clocks out loud that whether a result counts as a refutation is a decision people make, not something the data does to you — theory, instrument and background assumptions all get tested at once, so somebody has to choose which bit to give up. Don't let the story resolve that. Let it sit there being uncomfortable.

4. A character who is a ruthless falsificationist about somebody else's theory is quietly not one about their own. Not a gotcha reveal, just how people are.

Constraints: no fraud, no villains, no heroic lone genius versus a dogmatic establishment (I've read that one a hundred times), and no character ever says the words falsifiable, falsification, pseudoscience, paradigm or anomaly. Third person. Don't let the narrator tell us who's right, and don't end on a verdict — end with a small decision being made by someone with a little bit of power over the group (a funding panel, a journal editor, a departmental vote) and just watch them make it.

Oh, and british english please — realise, programme, sceptic, colour, that sort of thing.
