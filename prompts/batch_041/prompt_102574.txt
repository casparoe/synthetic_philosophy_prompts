In an ethics seminar I want to use Million Dollar Baby as a hard test case for the Stoic doctrine of what is "up to us" (eph' hēmin) and for that doctrine's current revival. The familiar Stoic line -- that only our judgments and choices are ours, while health, livelihood, and life itself are "indifferents" -- appears to make Maggie's paralysis after the fight irrelevant to how she should live, yet the film treats it as the end of a life worth living and presents Frankie's final act as the honoring of her will rather than a failure of endurance. Read the film against the doctrine, not as an illustration of it: bring in the Stoic allowance for a reasoned exit from life when continuing is no longer "according to nature," the requirement that even right action be undertaken with reservation ("if nothing prevents"), and the modern critics' complaint that "control" is really a trichotomy, since much of life is influenceable without being controlled. Where does the Stoic framework genuinely illuminate the priest's warning, Maggie's family's visit, and the final act, and where does the film expose a gap the doctrine cannot close -- does it show that the Stoics were wrong to call life an indifferent, or only that Maggie is no sage? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
