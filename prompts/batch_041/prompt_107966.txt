I'm preparing a session for a reading group on housing, land and the city, and I have been left stranded by the passage below. I can follow the polemical surface but I cannot reconstruct the argument underneath it, and I would like a careful, unhurried explanation rather than a summary. The passage is from Engels's articles on the housing question, which appeared in the German workers' press in 1872–73.

"The so-called housing shortage, which plays such a great role in the press nowadays, does not consist in the fact that the working class generally lives in bad, overcrowded and unhealthy dwellings. This shortage is not something peculiar to the present; it is not even one of the sufferings peculiar to the modern proletariat in contradistinction to all earlier oppressed classes. On the contrary, all oppressed classes in all periods suffered more or less uniformly from it. In order to make an end of this housing shortage there is only one means: to abolish altogether the exploitation and oppression of the working class by the ruling class. — What is meant today by housing shortage is the peculiar intensification of the bad housing conditions of the workers as the result of the sudden rush of population to, the big towns; a colossal increase in rents, a still further aggravation of overcrowding in the individual houses, and, for some, the impossibility of finding a place to live in at all. And this housing shortage gets talked of so much only because it does not limit itself to the working class but has affected the petty bourgeoisie also.

The housing shortage from which the workers and part of the petty bourgeoisie suffer in our modern big cities is one of the numerous smaller, secondary evils which result from the present-day capitalist mode of production. It is not at all a direct result of the exploitation of the worker as a worker by the capitalists.

And it is just the same with the housing shortage. The growth of the big modern cities gives the land in certain areas, particularly in those which are centrally situated, an artificial and often colossally increasing value; the buildings erected on these areas depress this value, instead of increasing it, because they no longer correspond to the changed circumstances. They are pulled down and replaced by others. This takes place above all with workers' houses which are situated centrally and whose rents, even with the greatest overcrowding, can never, or only very slowly, increase above a certain maximum. They are pulled down and in their stead shops, warehouses and public buildings are erected. … The result is that the workers are forced out of the centre of the towns towards the outskirts; that workers' dwellings, and small dwellings in general, become rare and expensive and often altogether unobtainable, for under these circumstances the building industry, which is offered a much better field for speculation by more expensive houses, builds workers' dwellings only by way of exception."

Three things are blocking me. First, what is being withheld by the "so-called"? Is the claim that the shortage is not a real phenomenon, or that the ordinary description of it misidentifies what is going on? And why does it matter to him to distinguish the bad housing that oppressed classes have always endured from the shortage as he defines it for the present?

Second, and this is where I am properly stuck: he calls the shortage "one of the numerous smaller, secondary evils" and says it is "not at all a direct result of the exploitation of the worker as a worker", tracing it instead to land values, redevelopment and the expulsion of working-class housing to the outskirts — a mechanism that sounds as though it could be interfered with by policy. Yet he also says that to make an end of the shortage there is only one means, abolishing the exploitation and oppression of the working class. Set out the strongest version of the argument on which both claims hold together, and tell me what kind of claim the second one is — causal, definitional, or political-strategic. If you think they cannot be reconciled, say which you would give up.

Third, what does the passage commit him to about measures that fall short of abolition — rent control, public housing, security of tenure, restrictions on redevelopment? Is he saying such measures must fail, or only that they cannot be the solution to the shortage? And does the remark that the shortage "gets talked of so much" only because it has reached the petty bourgeoisie tell us something about what turns a hardship into a political question, or merely about who is suffering? I would also like to know what the passage implies for the contemporary idea of a right to the city: whether it shows that idea to be incoherent in a city whose land is a commodity, or identifies the right thing to demand.

No citations or reading list, please — I only want help with the passage. Please write in British English.
