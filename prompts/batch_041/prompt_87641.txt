Ok so next month I'm giving a paper at the history deparment's work-in-progress colloquium, and I need to be ready for the Q&A. I'm the philosopher in an HPS program, so I'm the outsider in that room and I'd like to not get eaten alive.

My line: the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution aren't discoveries of real joints in the past — they're administrative fictions that got institutionalized, and the reason we can't drop them is that careers, journals, course catalogs and funding lines are built on them. I'm not just saying 'labels are conventional' (everyone nods at that and moves on). My claim is stronger: for the Scientific Revolution at least, there's no fact of the matter about where it starts or stops, because the relevant changes — in astronomy, anatomy, natural history, mechanics, chemistry, instrument-making — run on different clocks and only line up if you already assume the period and then go hunting for the lining-up. I have a little test I use: strip the period label out of a narrative and see whether anything explanatory is lost. And I've got a case study where a nineteenth-century textbook sorted natural philosophers into 'medieval' and 'modern' piles mostly to justify a curriculum, and the sorting did real institutional work.

Audience: mostly early modernists who teach 'the Scientific Revolution' as their bread and butter, two medievalists who get touchy when their period is treated as a waiting room for modernity, one early modernist who's done quantitative work on citation networks and thinks I'm hand-waving, and the department's one classicist who'll insist the Renaissance is a different case because contemporaries used rebirth vocabulary about themselves.
