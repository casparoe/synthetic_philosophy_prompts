I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade now — the old Overcoming Bias archives, LessWrong, the usual corners — and the more I sit with the "repugnant markets" literature, the more convinced I am that the standard framing is a mess. The debate is almost always staged as commodification versus efficiency, dignity versus willingness-to-pay, as though "repugnance" were a single sentiment rather than a bundle of distinct worries that happen to arrive together. I've been turning this over for a while, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at my university's interdisciplinary workshop on health policy, which means the audience is not a philosophy seminar: it's mostly transplant clinicians and health economists, with a handful of bioethicists, and they will each hear a different paper than the one I'm writing.

The thesis I want to defend is narrow, and I hope a little unorthodox. I want to argue for a regulated market in kidneys — but not a competitive one. My proposal is a single-buyer public body, a "kidney board," that posts one price, buys from any willing adult seller, screens hard for coercion and consent, funds lifetime follow-up care out of the same budget, and then allocates the organs strictly by medical need rather than ability to pay. The philosophical claim underneath it is that the corruption objection to commodification — the worry that a price changes what a kidney *is*, or degrades the practice of giving — actually targets the *anarchic* form of the market rather than pricing as such, and that a monopsonistic buyer with need-based allocation neutralizes most of that worry while keeping the welfare gains; the exploitation objection, by contrast, I think is genuine but is really a complaint about background injustice, and should be answered with labor and welfare policy rather than with prohibition. A friend in the econ department told me my monopsony idea is just a price ceiling with extra steps, which stung mainly because I couldn't immediately say why she was wrong.

So please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a transplant surgeon, a health economist, or a bioethicist would phrase it, not the way a philosophy seminar would, with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Assume the clinicians care about donor outcomes and won't be moved by metaethics, that the economists will treat repugnance as a taste or an externality to be priced, and that the bioethicists are the ones who will reach for the argument that markets crowd out altruism and corrupt the gift. Tell me what I should concede up front so I don't spend the talk defending ground I don't need, and then include, separately, the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
