I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. For the past few years I have worked on an interpreter and a small bytecode virtual machine, and before that on a serialisation library and a debugger, so my working vocabulary is grammars and parsers, abstract syntax trees and intermediate representations, instruction sets and evaluation order, values and references, token versus type, concrete syntax versus abstract syntax, and the distinction between a specification and an implementation of it. A friend of mine who reads a good deal of philosophy of mind has been trying to interest me in the language of thought hypothesis: the claim that thinking is carried out in a system of representations with a compositional syntax and a compositional semantics, so that thoughts are assembled from reusable constituents, the structure of a thought mirrors the structure of what it represents, and the system is productive and systematic — finitely many constituents, unboundedly many thinkable contents, and the capacity for one thought arriving as a package with the capacity for certain related ones. She insists that this is a substantive empirical hypothesis rather than a restatement of the computational theory of mind, and that the genuine difficulty lies in saying what makes the symbols mean anything, since the hypothesis is not permitted to appeal to a designer who assigned them their meanings. I would like to know how much of this I am actually understanding.

Please restate the hypothesis and the main arguments for it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — grammars, parsing, ASTs and IRs, bytecode and machine code, interpreters versus compilers, serialisation, type systems, debugging and introspection, and the difference between a language's syntax and its semantics — and then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' position translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Two specific matters I would like you to press on. First, whether describing Mentalese as the machine code of the brain is a fair translation or whether it quietly imports assumptions the hypothesis does not make — a fixed, finite, stipulated instruction set; a hardware/software distinction; a machine that is simply given rather than one whose structure is part of the empirical claim. Second, whether the interpreter regress that bothers me in my own work — something has to read the bytecode, and if that reader is itself a program, what reads it? — has any analogue in the philosophical debate, or whether the hypothesis sidesteps it by fiat. Finally, tell me whether any residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers' claim is less clear than it looks. My own suspicion is that the language of thought is simply an intermediate representation that the brain happens to have settled on, and I would like to know whether that is a translation of the hypothesis or a replacement for it.
