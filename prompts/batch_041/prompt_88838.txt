I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs and forums — I once ran a prediction market for my dorm's March Madness bracket, I keep a private spreadsheet of my own calibration, and I have unhelpfully strong opinions about scoring rules — and I've somehow ended up teaching the upper-level seminar in formal epistemology at my department. For the final exam I want a single long essay question on accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory, and I have a specific worry about the usual version of such a question: my students can recite the machinery on demand. They can define a strictly proper scoring rule, reproduce the accuracy-dominance argument that credences must be probabilistic, and state the thesis that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good. What they cannot do is tell me what that view says about an agent who is perfectly accurate and epistemically worthless, and I think that case is worth building the whole question around.

Here is the case, which I'd like you to keep intact. A forecasting league runs for a season. Each morning every competitor receives a slate of propositions — some trivial, some about matters nobody will be able to resolve for years — and must either submit a credence or mark the item as skipped. Submitted credences are scored with a strictly proper rule, and each competitor's season score is the average of her submitted forecasts; skipped items simply drop out, with no penalty of any other kind. The season is won by a competitor who forecasts only on items whose answers she can look up on the spot, submits credences of 0 and 1 accordingly, marks everything else skipped, and finishes with a perfect average. Call her the Passer. She has no false credences; she also has, in a real sense, no opinion about anything that matters. I want the exam question to force students to say what accuracy-first epistemology is entitled to say about her, and I want the crux to lie somewhere other than the usual material — not in whether her credences are coherent, but in what the view is scoring when it scores an agent at all.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes, closed-book, with one handwritten page of notes allowed, and it should be unanswerable by reciting the dominance argument or defining strict propriety. The marking scheme should be built around a handful of must-hit points with the marks for each (total 20), a one-line note on how candidates characteristically miss each one, and an explicit statement of what separates an A from a B. In particular, the top band should require the student to work out what happens to the evaluation when the same credence function is scored over a larger algebra of propositions rather than over the items the agent chose to submit, and to commit to a view about where the defect in the Passer's season lies — in her credences, in her inquiry, or only in the league's metric — and then to say what an accuracy-firster would have to add to the view to recover a criticism of her, and what that addition costs. I don't want marks awarded for naming authors, citing papers, or any other move that depends on having read the literature; the students can't look anything up, and the answer key shouldn't reward guessing at attributions either. If you think the case as I've set it up is incoherent or proves less than I take it to prove, build that diagnosis into the model answer and the scheme rather than quietly repairing the case.
