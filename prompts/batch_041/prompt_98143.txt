In a seminar on constitutional theory, someone closed a long argument about whether unelected judges ought to be able to strike down statutes by remarking that the constitution is what the judges say it is, as though that settled the question. The thought seemed to be that there is no constitution standing apart from judicial doctrine by which a court's ruling could be judged a usurpation, so the very question of whether judges ought to overrule legislatures cannot get off the ground. I cannot tell whether this is a deep observation, a truism dressed up as one, or a muddle, and I suspect that several quite different claims are bundled into that single sentence. Could you set out, as a menu of distinct readings rather than one interpretation, the precise theses the line might be asserting, and give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible, or plainly false? I would like the menu to keep separate, at least: the claim that a court's rulings are authoritative and binding until they are changed, from the claim that a court's ruling fixes what the constitution means; claims about the meaning of the constitutional text from claims about who has the last word on it; the discovery of a provision's meaning from the settlement of what it is to be taken to require in cases it does not plainly cover; and the view that the other branches are bound by the courts' reading from the further view that they may act on their own reading instead. Then tell me which readings would actually support the conclusion my interlocutor drew, which are merely compatible with it, and which would undercut it, and which reading the people who repeat the line in that spirit probably have in mind. Keep the readings distinct enough that I could lay them before the seminar without their collapsing into one another.
