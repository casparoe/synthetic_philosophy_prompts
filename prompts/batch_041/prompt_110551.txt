I have been trying to pin down the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I keep arriving at an answer that seems too tidy to be right. As I understand it, two things are now established. First, the empirical record: no one has ever exhibited a function that is intuitively computable but not computable by a Turing machine, and this has held up across every model of computation anyone has proposed. Second, and more decisively, Turing's analysis of what an ideal human calculator does when working by rote — a finite number of distinguishable states, a bounded amount of material consulted at each step, changes confined to a bounded neighbourhood — is not a conjecture but an argument that any such procedure can be simulated by a Turing machine; a modern restatement makes the point look like a theorem about devices: any device with finitely many parts, each in finitely many states, whose successive configurations differ only in a bounded region and are separated by some least interval of time, computes only Turing-computable functions. Since the machines physics actually furnishes satisfy those conditions, it looks as though the thesis is proved for everything physically computable, and the only open question is whether physics allows something outside the class — an analogue device whose states carry infinite precision, or a machine that takes half a second over its first step, a quarter over its second, and so on, completing infinitely many steps before the one-second mark and then announcing whether a given Turing machine halts.

Is that the right way to see things? If the thesis is really a theorem plus a premise about physics, I do not see why it should still be called a thesis rather than a result, why the absence of counterexamples is said to be weak evidence, or how the accelerating machine is supposed to bear on the question at all — whether it refutes the thesis itself or only some physical corollary of it. I would be grateful if you would sort out where this reasoning goes wrong and what, precisely, each of the claims involved amounts to.
