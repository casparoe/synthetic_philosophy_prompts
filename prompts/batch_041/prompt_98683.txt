I'm not sure if this is a real thing or just me, but I read alot of popular history and history-adjacent stuff and I keep noticing a move. Someone finds one beautifully documented little case — a single court deposition, one person's diary, a shipwreck with surviving letters — and uses it to make a big claim about "early modern Europe" or "the working class" or whatever. But the case is interesting to them precisely because it's vivid and survives in detail, and surviving in detail is exactly what makes it unusual; ordinary lives leave no archive. So the vivid case is almost by construction the odd one out, and yet it gets asked to stand in for everybody. And when you say "isn't this just one strange person?", the answer is usually something like "the odd case throws the normal into relief," which sounds clever but doesn't actually show the case represents anyone. Meanwhile the people writing about kings and parliaments seem to have the opposite problem — a mountain of documents, all from the tiny slice of people who wrote them down — but somehow that doesn't get flagged the same way.

Is there a name for this exact maneuver? I don't think it's just "overgeneralizing from an anecdote" — there's something more specific going on with the selection being driven by the very vividness that disqualifies it. Is it related to the ecological fallacy, or is that the wrong direction entirely? And is the "the odd case reveals the normal" defense a known thing with a name, or just hand-waving? I want to know what the nearest established concepts are, which one actually fits, and where the edges are — I won't be satisfied with a bare label. And please be straight about what's actually settled here versus what historians themselves argue about; I don't want some contested reading dressed up as the consensus.
