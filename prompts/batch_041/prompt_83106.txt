I'm writing a long piece on epistemic injustice -- the general idea that people can be wronged specifically in their capacity as knowers, not just as speakers -- and I've hit a fork in the road that I can't resolve on my own, so I'm handing it to you.

Here's the fork. The concept I want to build the piece around is Miranda Fricker's hermeneutical injustice: a whole society can simply lack the interpretive resources needed to make sense of some experience, and the people who actually have that experience are the ones who suffer, because they can't render it intelligible to themselves or to anyone else. Her stock example is sexual harassment before there was a term for it. What makes the idea pull its weight for me is that nobody has to be at fault -- it's a hole in the shared toolkit, a structural thing, not a crime. The harm runs through the marginalized person's inability to understand her own life.

Then there's Gaile Pohlhaus, who more or less says: hold on, alot of the time there's no hole at all. The concepts exist. Marginalized people built them -- that's half of what they're doing when they theorize their own lives -- and the dominant knower just won't pick them up. She calls this willful hermeneutical ignorance: the powerful look in the wrong places, and that's on them, because where you look is something you're answerable for. Note the inversion: here the marginalized person understands her life fine, and it's the privileged one who's failing to know.

So compare these two for me. Are they rival accounts of a single phenomenon, or two different phenomena filed under one label? If Pohlhaus is right, what survives of Fricker's claim that no one needs to be to blame? And flip it around: if there's no gap, if the words already exist and are merely being refused, is that still hermeneutical injustice, or has it quietly become something else -- ordinary prejudice against the people offering the concepts, say?

One more thing I'd want you to take seriously, since it's the objection I'd expect from the skeptics: calling the ignorance willful sounds like it requires a culprit with a motive, and I'm not convinced most of the ignorance I see around me has that shape. Does the account still work if the refusal is closer to a habit, or to being raised badly, than to a decision?

Finish by telling me which of the two I should hang the piece on, and what I'd loose by dropping the other.
