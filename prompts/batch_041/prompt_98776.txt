In a course I'm taking on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, our seminar has been defending a reading of it that I can't shake. On this reading, the propositions that hold a practice together — that the world did not spring into being five minutes ago, that our words keep their meanings, that our instruments work — are not things we know or even believe, but the unearned background against which knowing and believing become possible; and this certainty is, as the seminar puts it, more animal than rational, a way of going on that we share with creatures who have no reasons at all. I would like you to work out the most important implications of that reading for a concrete dispute I am caught in. A member of my reading group flatly denies one of these background propositions — he insists our historical sources were fabricated wholesale and that everything built on them is worthless — and I find I cannot refute him, because we do not share the hinge. What does the reading imply about whether arguing with him could ever succeed, about whether I am entitled to call him irrational rather than merely different, and about whether the only ground left for preferring my certainties to his is that more people happen to share mine? Please give me a committed answer that takes a definite position on the hardest of these questions rather than surveying the options, and keep it under 400 words.
