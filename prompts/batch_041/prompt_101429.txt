I came across a blog post trying to explain L.A. Paul's stuff on transformative experience, and I want to know how much of it I can actually trust before I repeat any of it. Here's more or less what it said:

"Paul's big idea is that some experiences are transformative -- having a child, becoming a vampire, gaining a new sense, falling in love. What makes an experience transformative is that it changes who you are: your preferences, your values, your whole outlook on life. And because it changes your preferences, you literally can't know in advance what it will be like, so you can't compare the life you'd have afterwards against the life you have now. That means the usual decision-theory advice -- pick the option with the highest expected value -- is useless here. Paul's conclusion is that when it comes to something like having a child, there's really no rational choice to be made at all; you just have to leap. She draws a distinction between experiences that are 'epistemically transformative,' meaning you can't know what they're like until you've had them, and experiences that are 'personally transformative,' meaning they change your core preferences -- but her point is that these are basically the same thing, since it's the change in your preferences that stops you from knowing what the experience is like. She does offer one escape hatch: instead of comparing outcomes, you can just decide based on whether you want to have the experience for its own sake, the way a vampire might be curious to find out what being a vampire is like. Though critics say that move just smuggles in the very knowledge Paul says you can't have."

I'm pretty sure the "no rational choice at all, you just have to leap" bit is doing Paul dirty, but I don't know the details well enough to say where it goes wrong.
