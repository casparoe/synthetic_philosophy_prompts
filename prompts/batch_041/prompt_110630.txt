Our school's debate club is spending the term on nuclear weapons, and I keep running into something I can't untangle. The way deterrence is actually built seems to rest on a strange picture of what people are like, and I'd like you to help me make that picture explicit and then tell me how much of it the practice really needs.

Here is the practice I mean, described as concretely as I can. A country fields hundreds of land-based missiles that sit in fixed silos, and everyone involved knows those silos are the easiest targets; the missiles could be destroyed on the ground, so there is constant pressure to launch them before they are hit. The whole system is wired to fire within minutes of a warning. At the same time, we deliberately place the authority to launch in human hands rather than automating it, because we do not trust machines to make that call. Then we build elaborate safeguards to keep the humans from firing by accident: two separate officers must each authenticate an order, launch codes are physically split so no single person holds them all, and a single commander is not supposed to be able to act alone. We also train the people in the chain to expect that some warnings will be false, and we keep humans in the loop precisely so someone can refuse.

Two episodes are usually cited here. During the Cuban Missile Crisis, a Soviet submarine officer named Vasily Arkhipov reportedly refused to authorize a nuclear torpedo launch when his fellow officers wanted to, at a moment when he may not have known whether a war had already started. In 1983, a Soviet early-warning officer named Stanislav Petrov judged that a reported incoming attack was probably a false alarm from the system itself and did not pass it up the chain. In both cases the thing that prevented catastrophe was a person's judgment overriding the machine or the protocol.

What confuses me is that we seem to be betting on two opposite theories of human behavior at once. On one hand, deterrence is supposed to work because the other side is rational: they will not attack if they believe we will retaliate, so we spend enormous effort making our willingness to retaliate believable. On the other hand, the entire command structure is designed around the fear that our own people will act on a false alarm, panic, or misread a situation. And the credibility of the threat seems to depend on our being willing to do something that would be flatly irrational to actually carry out.

So here is what I want. First, reconstruct the view of human decision-making, agency, and value that this practice presupposes, and separate what it strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with; I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined, and I want to know which parts are load-bearing. Second, tell me whether the parts are consistent with each other, especially the demand that the threat be credible and the fear that our own operators will act on bad information. Third, say which rival theories of rationality or agency the practice quietly rules out, and what would have to change in the way the system is built and staffed if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly irrational or unpredictable model of the adversary. Fourth, address the Arkhipov and Petrov cases directly: does the system's reliance on individual refusal presuppose a theory of conscience or responsibility that sits uneasily with the rest of the design?

Please write with a clear sense of what is settled versus what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
