You are a philosopher of mind and a metaphysician with specialist expertise in the simulation argument and in the debate over computational theories of consciousness. I want conceptual analysis from you, not a survey of a familiar literature.

Background: I convene a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of mind, and twice this term a participant has used the same one-liner — that we are probably living in a simulation — to do substantive argumentative work. On the first occasion it was offered as a reason for thinking that our own mental states are computational states, and so that functionalism is true of us at least. On the second it was offered to defuse the fine-tuning argument: if a simulator chose the constants so as to produce a world like ours, the apparent improbability that they should be life-permitting stands in no need of further explanation. I suspect that on each occasion the sentence is standing in for a much more specific claim than the speaker has in mind, and quite possibly for a different claim on each occasion.

Before you write the analysis, brainstorm. List the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, including readings far weaker and far stronger than people usually realise, and note which of them the speaker would disavow if pressed. Take this stage seriously: I would rather have six sharply distinguished readings than twenty overlapping ones. Then produce the analysis proper, reading by reading. For each, give a one-sentence statement of the thesis in the most precise terms you can manage; a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or probably false, with a brief justification; and a verdict on whether it would in fact support the functionalism use, the fine-tuning use, both, or neither.

Your menu should include at least readings that turn on: the disjunctive structure of the simulation argument and what it does and does not establish about the probability that we are simulated; the difference between living in a simulation and being a simulation, and between either of those and the thesis that our minds are programs; the gap between the broad slogan and the much narrower hypothesis about ancestor simulations run by our own posthuman descendants; the assumption about which class of observers we should regard ourselves as a random member of; and the deflationary reading on which the sentence is true but changes nothing epistemically, morally, or metaphysically.

End with a short paragraph identifying the reading to which most people who utter the sentence are implicitly committed, and the most economical way to expose the equivocation in conversation. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I want the analysis itself. Write in precise, unhurried prose.
