You are an expert in formal epistemology and decision analysis, with practical experience designing probability elicitations for forecasters who are not probabilists. I lead a small forecasting unit in a policy institute. We routinely ask subject-matter experts for probabilities about events such as treaty ratification, currency crises, and the emergence of a new pathogen. Our problem is not that the experts are irrational; it is that their answers arrive as a mixture of point estimates, ranges, verbal probabilities, and conditional judgments, and we have no repeatable way to tell which packages are internally incoherent and which are merely imprecise, risk-averse, or ambiguous. I want to build a coherence audit based on Dutch book reasoning and turn it into a procedure an audit lead can run in about forty-five minutes without a probability background. The audit lead does not have authority to change the expert's numbers; the deliverable is a report that classifies the package as coherent, incoherent, or imprecise, with reasons and recommended next steps. A formal modeler must sign off on any escalation.

By a Dutch book I mean a collection of bets, each of which the agent regards as acceptable, such that the agent loses money no matter how the world turns out. By a conditional bet on A given B I mean a bet that is called off, with stakes returned, if B does not occur. I am interested in both the strict case, where the agent would actually accept every bet in the collection, and the wider case, where a bookmaker could offer the bets at the agent's stated odds whether or not the agent would take them. I want the procedure to be honest about what each case does and does not show.

Please think carefully before writing. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you map the main design choices: how to elicit acceptable odds rather than naked numbers; how to represent buy and sell prices; how to test finite additivity, complementation, and the ratio formula for conditional probability using called-off bets; how to search for a sure-loss certificate; how to handle infinite partitions and events whose truth conditions are unclear; and how to distinguish a genuine incoherence from risk aversion, ambiguity aversion, transaction costs, or a refusal to bet at all. Then produce the procedure itself.

The procedure should include:

1. A one-page checklist or decision tree in words, with the questions to ask in order.
2. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a red flag, and what to do next.
3. A small set of tests the audit lead can actually run, including at least one hand-checkable test and one that requires only simple arithmetic or a spreadsheet; say when to escalate to a formal modeler.
4. Clear stop rules: when the audit should conclude that the expert's package is coherent enough, when it should record imprecision rather than incoherence, and when it should hand the decision to a person because the remaining issue is one of values, not probability.
5. A section on what the procedure cannot do. Be explicit about the limits of Dutch book reasoning: what a sure-loss package does and does not establish, why a refusal to bet is not by itself incoherence, and why the procedure may fail for events that are not well-defined or for credences that are imprecise by design.

Do not cite literature, give a reading list, or name authorities. Write as if you have no access to the literature. Define any technical term you use in plain language, and keep the procedure usable by someone who has never seen a Dutch book argument.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, showing the audit lead's actual reasoning at each step:

Case A. An expert gives P(A) = 0.5, P(B given A) = 0.8, P(A and B) = 0.3, P(A or B) = 0.7, and P(B) = 0.4. The expert says these are all her considered judgments and she is willing to bet at these odds.

Case B. An expert refuses to bet on a climate tipping point at any odds, but when asked for a credence says 10 percent, and when pressed gives a range of 5 to 20 percent. She also refuses to bet against the tipping point at any odds. The asker wants a single number for a model.

Case C. An expert's credence in A today is 0.8. She also says that if B occurs she will revise her credence in A to 0.6, and if B does not occur she will revise it to 0.9. She accepts a bet today on A at odds reflecting 0.8, and she accepts a bet tomorrow on A at whatever odds she then states. The audit lead wants to know whether this package is diachronically coherent.

For each case, end with the audit lead's verdict, the severity of any problem, the cheapest repair if there is one, and the part of the case that the procedure cannot settle and must be handed to a person. At least one case should produce a verdict the asker will not like, and at least one should show the procedure declining to force a single number where the expert's imprecision is rational. Finish with a short paragraph on the most likely ways this procedure will misfire in practice and what the audit lead should do when it does.
