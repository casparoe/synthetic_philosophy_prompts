I've been chewing on the hearsay rule and I can't tell what it's actually committed to. Here's the practice as I understand it: in court you generally can't get anywhere with "someone told me X, so X." A witness says the defendant confessed to a friend — no good, unless the friend shows up and says it himself, and the reason judges give is that the other side never got to cross-examine the person who actually said the words. OK, fine. But then the same system has a whole pile of exceptions where you absolutely can put the words in front of the jury with no cross-examination of the speaker at all: business records, public records, someone blurting something out seconds after an accident, someone saying something that cuts against their own interest, someone who thought they were about to die naming who did it (and for a couple of those the speaker has to be unavailable, which means the exception kicks in exactly when cross-examining them is impossible). Meanwhile a witness has to have seen or heard the thing with their own eyes, but an expert can give you a conclusion built on lab reports and records from technicians who never show up, and in some places can state the conclusion without the underlying reports ever coming in. And then there's the odd bit where if the statement isn't being used to prove it's true — say, to show that the listener heard it and got scared — it's not hearsay in the first place and nobody objects.

So what view of testimony is this whole apparatus built on? I've seen people argue about whether you need independent evidence backing up every individual person's say-so, or whether you're entitled to trust people by default until you get some reason not to — reductionism vs anti-reductionism, I think that's the terminology? I genuinely can't tell which one the law is. The exclusionary rule smells like the first one, and the categorical exceptions smell like the second, unless the exceptions are just a reliability guess dressed up. And I wonder whether the rule even needs an epistemic story at all, or whether it's really about juries being bad at weighing things and defendants having a right to confront their accusers — in which case the exceptions are about trial fairness, not about knowledge.

What I want out of you: reconstruct the implicit theory of testimonial knowledge that the doctrine only makes sense under. Then separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit — I'd bet a lot of the doctrine is compatible with several views, and I want to know which. Tell me if the pieces are actually consistent with each other or if the thing is just a historical pile-up. Then tell me what would have to change in an actual courtroom if you rebuilt it on the opposite view: what gets excluded that's admitted now, what comes in that doesn't now. And if you think the question is underdetermined, say which rival views would each license roughly the same set of rules. Don't bother telling me what some treatise says the rationale is — I want the commitments, not the self-description.
