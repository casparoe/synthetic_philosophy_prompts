A foundation I advise has about $3M to give out over three years on the reliability of introspection -- call it eight to ten researchers, philosophers and psychologists both welcome -- and they've asked me for a shortlist of what to fund, plus the reasoning, since they'll be arguing about it in a board meeting. I'd like you're help building that. Not a literature survey, and please no reading list or citations; I can't check them and alot of them would be you guessing.

Some constraints that should shape your answer. They don't want another round of "is introspection unreliable" -- everybody knows the classic confabulation stuff and nobody serious thinks introspection is a magic window anymore. What they want to know is what "reliable" is even supposed to mean here, and for what. Second, they care about three downstream users and want questions ranked partly by which of those three actually gets unstuck: (a) consciousness science, where verbal report is still treated as the gold standard for whether a state is conscious; (b) clinical and psychometric self-report, where questionnaires stand in for depression, pain, and emotion; and (c) the AI welfare crowd, who need to know whether asking a system how it feels tells you anything about how it is. Third, they fund people, not projects -- a question that needs a ten-year longitudinal study or a giant consortium is less useful to them than one that two or three good people could close in three years.

A colleague in my reading group keeps pushing an analogy I can't decide about. She says introspection should be treated like a measuring instrument, and that nobody in metrology asks whether a scale is "accurate" -- they ask what it was calibrated against, what the chain of traceability is, and over what range it holds. Her challenge is that the introspection literature has never had a calibration standard and has mostly been using one unvalidated measure to check another. Either that's a category mistake or it's the whole ballgame, and I'd like you to tell me which in the answer, not as an aside.

Candidate questions I've heard floated, to react to or replace: what introspection is even a faculty of -- the "why did I do that" case and the "what am I feeling" case may not be the same capacity; whether "is introspection reliable?" is a category error because reliability is domain-specific, fine for sensations and hopeless for motives; whether there's any non-circular ground truth at all, given that behavior, physiology, third-party judgment, and neural decoding each come with their own validity problems; whether reliability can be trained (meditators, therapists), and what it means for a skill to be the object of study; and whether machine self-reports, which in principle can be checked against a system's internals, give us a new lever on the human question or are just a different question wearing the same word.

What I want back, in this order, in American English:

1. "Where it actually stands" -- 200-300 words. What's genuinely open vs. what's settled, and where you think the field is fooling itself.
2. "The ranked agenda" -- 800-1000 words. Five to seven questions max, in order. For each: what the question is in plain terms, why it's upstream of the others, who can work on it, and what a good three-year result would look like.
3. "Why this order" -- 400-500 words. The criteria you used, where they conflict, and how you weighted the three audiences above. Put the calibration question here and give me a verdict on it.
4. "What would change the ranking" -- 200-300 words. Which findings in the next two years should make them reshuffle, and which results should make them drop something entirely.
5. "What I'd cut" -- about 200 words. Two or three things that look fundable but that you'd deprioritize, and why.
6. "Bottom line" -- three or four sentences I can say out loud in the meeting.

Don't hedge by telling me it's all connected; give me an order and defend it. And if there's a question upstream of everything I listed that I've missed, put it at the top and tell me what I was missing.
