I retired last year after forty years in experimental condensed-matter physics, and I have been spending my new freedom in a reading group on moral and political philosophy. We have been circling the cluster of ideas that travel under the name Moloch: multipolar traps, races to the bottom, and the suspicion that competitive dynamics can grind away values that no one intended to sacrifice. I built the following argument to make the diagnosis precise, and now I cannot decide whether it is a profound result or a trick of abstraction. (1) A multipolar trap is a situation in which each actor, choosing the action that is best given the expected actions of the others, produces an outcome that is worse for every actor than some feasible alternative. (2) In such a situation each actor's action is rational, since it is the best response available. (3) No actor can unilaterally choose the better alternative without being exploited or outcompeted. (4) Therefore the worse outcome is the rational outcome. (5) Escaping such a trap requires either an external enforcer who can alter payoffs or a binding agreement among the actors. (6) An external enforcer is itself an actor in a larger competitive environment; if it can be outcompeted, captured, or replaced, it faces its own multipolar trap. (7) A binding agreement is either enforced by such an enforcer, returning us to (6), or sustained by the actors' willingness to punish defectors. (8) Punishment is costly to the punisher and benefits the group, so the decision to punish is itself a multipolar trap. (9) Therefore every escape from a multipolar trap generates a higher-order multipolar trap. (10) Therefore no escape is stable, and competitive dynamics must eventually erode any value that cannot be defended by a self-interested best response. (11) But we observe stable cooperation in families, firms, scientific communities, and constitutional orders, where people routinely incur costs to punish defectors and uphold norms. (12) Therefore something in the argument is wrong.

I would like to keep (1) and (10), because the idea that competition can have a ratchet-like tendency to erode values seems to me one of the few genuinely illuminating ideas in this area, and I am willing to give up almost anything else. But I do not want to keep them by fiat. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. If the flaw is an equivocation, name the term that shifts meaning; if the argument is valid but one premise is too strong, tell me what weaker premise does the work and what it no longer proves.

In particular, I want to know whether the regress from first-order traps to second-order traps is a real feature of competitive systems or an artifact of treating every enforcer as just another player in the same game, and whether the distinction between one-shot and repeated interaction, or between public-good and club-good enforcement, is doing more work here than the argument admits. My physicist's instinct is that an argument which infers inevitability from a single mechanism is usually hiding a boundary condition; my philosophical education is not yet good enough to say where the boundary condition is.
