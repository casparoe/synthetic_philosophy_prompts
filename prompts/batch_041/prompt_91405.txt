I am preparing the commentary I have been asked to give at a small workshop on the unity of the proposition, and before I commit to a line I would like an honest audit of my own commitments — not a survey of the literature, which I know well enough, but an account of where my package comes apart. Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) Propositions are structured complexes: a proposition is made up of constituents standing in some arrangement, so that the same constituents differently arranged yield different propositions, and distinct propositions can share constituents. (2) The constituents of a proposition are the very items the proposition is about — the objects, properties and relations themselves — rather than intermediate entities such as senses, concepts or modes of presentation; a proposition about two particular people contains those people. (3) Nothing ties the constituents together over and above the constituents themselves: there is no additional copula or glue item on the list of what the proposition contains. (4) Propositions are neither mental nor linguistic: they exist, and have whatever truth values they have, whether or not anyone thinks or asserts them. (5) Propositions are the primary bearers of truth and falsity, and a proposition's truth is not one of its constituents and adds no constituent to it. (6) Bradley's regress is a real result and not a conjuring trick: any attempt to explain the unity of a complex by positing a further relation that ties its constituents together generates an infinite regress, since the new relation will itself need tying to the items it relates. I take (6) to close off one family of answers, not to show that there cannot be structured propositions at all.

What I want from you is a consistency audit of (1) through (6). Some of these may pull against one another; some may only look as though they do; and I would much rather be told which is which than be reassured. For each pair you think is in genuine tension, tell me what the conflict is, how severe it is relative to the others, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere. Where a pair merely appears to conflict, say why the appearance is misleading rather than silently omitting it. I have no idea in advance which of these I would be least willing to surrender, so I would like a recommendation on that point rather than a neutral menu of options, and I would prefer no citations — nothing here turns on what anyone else has written.

Please give me your answer as a JSON object with the following fields. "bottom_line": one sentence saying whether the package hangs together as it stands, with the single most important qualification. "findings": an array ordered from most to least severe, each element an object with "commitments" (the numbers from my list that are involved, e.g. [1,3]), "status" (either "genuine tension" or "apparent only"), "diagnosis" (two or three sentences explaining the conflict, or explaining why there is none), "severity" (an integer from 1 to 5, where 5 is the most severe), "cheapest_repair" (what I would have to add or give up), and "cost_of_repair" (what that concession costs me elsewhere in the package). "commitment_least_worth_keeping": the single numbered commitment whose abandonment would do most to restore consistency, with a sentence of justification. "single_revision": the one change that would clean up the most, together with what it leaves unresolved.
