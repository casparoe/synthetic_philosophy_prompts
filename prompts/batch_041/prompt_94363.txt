I am a retired entomologist. Forty years on the social insects, a great deal of it at a field station, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have been asked to write down my actual position on insect sentience for a seminar, and I would rather have it audited than defended. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature and not a reading list; I want you to tell me which of the commitments below pull against one another, which only appear to, and what it would cost me to fix the real ones.

1. Whatever experience is, it is a physical process. I have no patience for the view that it is something over and above the biology.
2. Experience comes in degrees. It is not that a creature either has a full inner life or none; there are richer and thinner ways of being aware, and a honeybee, if it is aware at all, is aware thinly.
3. And yet there is a sharp line between having some experience and having none. A quantity can vary continuously while the phenomenon it underlies either occurs or does not, as with temperature and melting.
4. I put my credence that a typical insect has any morally relevant experience at about one in a hundred, and I would not be astonished to learn that the true value is zero. I doubt the question will be settled in my lifetime, though I take it to have a determinate answer.
5. I take the numbers seriously. By the rough figures I have seen quoted, something on the order of 10^18 insects are alive at any given moment, and the estimates run higher. At a credence of one in a hundred, that is an enormous expected number of experiencing creatures, and the arithmetic does not care that each one is small.
6. My moral framework is aggregative and forward-looking: roughly, importance is the sum over individuals of the probability that the individual is sentient, times the moral weight of its experience, times its welfare.
7. The probability in (6) is not, however, a separate lottery for each insect. Whether insects are sentient is one unresolved question about whether a nervous system of that kind — a brain with mushroom bodies and a central complex, and nothing resembling a vertebrate cortex — can support experience at all. Either that architecture suffices or it does not.
8. Nor, on my view in (2), is the weight in (6) a free parameter I get to set independently. If insects are sentient, they are sentient in the thin register that a nervous system that size can manage, and that is the whole of what there is to weigh.
9. I do not dismiss the scale argument as numerology. I think small probabilities obligate when the stakes scale with them, and I would say the same about a one-in-a-million risk of a large harm to a human being.
10. I am not, however, a sucker for the version of this that comes with an adversary. I would not hand over my savings to a stranger who claims a one-in-ten-to-the-hundredth chance of torturing me forever, and I do not think I am being irrational.
11. In practice I have changed almost nothing. I still keep bees, I still garden with the attendant casualties, and I give nothing meaningful to insect welfare work.

Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look like they are and why, and order the real tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give and what that concession costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the practical stalemate in (11) is a failure of nerve on my part or is licensed by the position once it has been tidied up. Please write your answer in American English.
