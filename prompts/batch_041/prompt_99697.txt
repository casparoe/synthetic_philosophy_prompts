I would like you to act as a philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology and the philosophy of action — someone at home in normative ethics, in the empirical study of judgment, and in the conceptual question of what a bias even is. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming the full space of considerations that could bear on the question before committing to any verdict. I want a considered map of the terrain, not a first pass.

Last night a friend and I argued for hours about a claim I had stated too confidently, and I want to know whether I should retreat from it. Here is the position I defended.

We treat preference and bias as though they carve morality at a joint. We say that some moral disagreements are disagreements about preferences — the other side simply wants a different arrangement of values, and there is nothing there to correct — while others are disagreements with bias — the other side is distorted, and there is something there to correct. I claimed the distinction is not a real feature of the mental states at all. On my view, a bias is just a moral preference we happen to disapprove of, and a preference is a moral preference we happen to tolerate. The label tracks our attitude toward the state, not any property of it. To call something a bias is to smuggle in a norm and then present it as a discovery about the other person's cognition. On this reading, debiasing someone is just changing their preferences under a more flattering description, and the widespread confidence that biases are errors while preferences are not is unwarranted.

I want you to play this straight and tell me what would count against it. Consider, for instance, the person whose aversion to some practice is stable, articulate, reason-responsive, and endorsed on reflection — and the person whose aversion evaporates when the description is reframed and who disavows it on reflection. On my view these differ only in our attitude toward them. Is that really all? Consider also the implicit association that a person explicitly disavows, and the choice pattern that reverses under an irrelevant change in framing: are these preferences on my account, or does my account have to redescribe them in a way that loses something? And consider whether my view can distinguish itself from the trivial claim that we sometimes disapprove of people's preferences.

So: which observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical patterns would count against the position? Sort them by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to dismissing as question-begging. Say what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all — or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If you conclude that it is well insulated, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that insulation is a defect or a virtue for a view of this kind. I care most about whether there is a distinction here that survives first-personal cases, where a person reports a bias against their own preference; if that is where the position is most exposed, say why, and if it is where it is safest, say that too.
