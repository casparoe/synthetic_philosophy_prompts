Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about the frame problem, and I want to turn the argument into something sharper than either of us managed. His position is that the frame problem was a bookkeeping nuisance specific to a certain style of logic-based artificial intelligence — that once you stop representing the world as axioms plus an update rule, the difficulty evaporates, and philosophers who keep invoking it are simply nostalgic for old-fashioned symbolic AI. Mine is that the nuisance was always the visible symptom of something deeper: any finite system acting in a world must decide, without being told, which of the indefinitely many things that remain true are the ones worth noticing, and no amount of engineering removes that decision so much as relocates it.

Write a deadpan customer-service FAQ for a fictional insurance company, Persistence Mutual, which sells coverage against exactly this difficulty. The premise: its underwriters have "solved" the frame problem by contract, requiring every policyholder to file a Declared Persistence Schedule listing all the facts about their household that will remain true after any action they take. The comedy should come from the real structure of the problem rather than from generic absurdity. The frame problem proper (what a system may leave unstated), the qualification problem (the open-ended list of things that could prevent an action from working as intended), and the ramification problem (the indirect consequences of an action) should each appear as a distinct coverage tier, and the fine print should make clear that each tier's remedy is to hand the burden back to the customer. A claims scenario or two would help — a policyholder who moved a chair and thereby voided a rider, say. Quoting premiums per fluent per month is the kind of detail I'm after. If you want a clause with the right flavor, a sleeping-dog clause or a closed-world rider would do, but do not build anything on those names. If you draw on the standard literature's toy examples or terminology, use only what you are confident of: the piece must not depend on getting a name, a date, or a provenance right, and any quoted material must be an invented spokesperson or an internal memo, never attributed to a real person or work.

Voice: a competent, faintly sinister corporate writer who regards all of this as a matter of good household management. About 700 words, formatted as numbered FAQ entries with headings, and no winking — a reader should be able to tell what is being satirized from the content alone. The target is the habit of declaring a conceptual difficulty solved by pushing its cost onto the user's specification, not any particular company or person.
