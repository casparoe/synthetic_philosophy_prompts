I'm in my final year and taking a seminar on philosophy of psychiatry; we've just finished the unit on classification, where we read Hacking on looping effects, and I'm in a poetry workshop on Thursdays that I've been using as an excuse to write about the same material. What I've ended up writing my seminar paper on is prolonged grief disorder — the diagnosis the manuals added a few years ago, which requires that the grief persist past a set duration and, more strangely, that it exceed what the person's culture expects mourning to be. The part I keep circling is how the two ends of that criterion feed each other: the manual measures the disorder against a cultural norm, and the norm is partly set by the manual, so the thing being measured keeps shifting as the measuring goes on. Hacking's contrast between kinds that are indifferent to being classified and the ones he calls interactive, which answer back, seems exactly right here, though I want to be careful not to write anything that sounds like a debunking — the longing is real, and the category isn't arbitrary. The trouble is that the target moves while you aim at it.

So I'd rather write a poem than an essay, and I'd like your help with it. Could you write me a pantoum: four quatrains, sixteen lines, with the standard interlocking repeats, where the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza brings back the opening stanza's lines so that the poem's last line is word for word its first. The repeats should carry the argument rather than decorate it. I want two of the recurring lines to do most of the work — one that sounds like a criterion, third person, the voice of whoever writes the manual, having to do with duration or with exceeding what mourning is supposed to be, and one in the first person from someone who looked the criteria up and found herself described in them. As the poem goes on the two should trade places often enough that a reader stops being able to say which voice owns which line, and the line that closes the poem, being the same words as the opening, should by then read as the mourner's own. Let an anniversary be somewhere in it, since the form is already a kind of return on a fixed date.

Please don't use the words "loop," "label," "construct," or "narrative" anywhere — if the poem needs the idea it should be doing it, not naming it. Don't put anything in quotation marks as though it came from a manual; paraphrase any criterion in ordinary words, since I can't check quotations against the manuals and would rather the poem not pretend to. It should be accurate enough that someone in my seminar recognizes the structure rather than just the topic, and readable enough that I can give it on Thursday without having to explain it first. No epigraph, no notes, no citations. Then, on a final line of its own, write one sentence beginning "Summary:" stating what the poem claims about how the classification and the person change each other.
