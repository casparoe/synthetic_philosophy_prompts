I am preparing a session for an undergraduate seminar on paradoxes of rationality, and I want to use the film Minority Report as a way into the Death in Damascus paradox. The film is familiar enough that students will remember the precrime system, the three precogs, Anderton's flight after he is predicted to murder a man he has never met, the minority report that supposedly records a dissenting vision, and the way the prediction appears to close around him even when he tries to avoid it. What I want from you is a philosophical reading of the film through the lens of Death in Damascus, not a summary and not a verdict on whether free will exists. Treat the precogs as a predictor whose forecasts are common knowledge and whose accuracy is bound up with the fact that everyone knows the forecast; then ask what Anderton's choices look like under an evidential reading, where the prediction is evidence about what he will do, and under a causal reading, where his action is supposed to be independent of the prediction. The interesting question for me is whether the film dramatizes the paradox or quietly dissolves it: does Anderton's attempt to avoid the predicted murder have the structure of the traveler who reasons that Death will be where he is not, only to find that the reasoning is already priced into the prediction? And does the minority report introduce a genuinely new decision-theoretic possibility, a forecast that is accurate only if it is not acted upon, or is it just a device for keeping the plot moving?

I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions. On the film's side, attend to specific scenes and choices—the moment Anderton learns of the prediction, his flight, his confrontation with Leo Crow, the revelation about the minority report, and the final dismantling of the precrime system—and say where the film's own logic supports the Death in Damascus analogy and where it cheats, for example by making the predictor fallible, by introducing a manipulator behind the prediction, or by allowing a resolution that depends on exposing a conspiracy rather than on solving the paradox. On the philosophy's side, do not reduce Death in Damascus to a generic warning about self-fulfilling prophecy; keep the distinction between evidential and causal decision theory in view, and say what the film would have to show for either to be vindicated. I am especially interested in whether the film's ending—where the system is shut down and the precogs are no longer used—is best read as a political resolution to a metaphysical problem: the paradox is not solved, but the oracle is refused. Conclude by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the philosophical debate that the bare paradox does not, and where the honest critic has to admit that the film resists the reading.
