For a few years now I have been a fairly devoted reader of the rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and one of the recurring pleasures and irritations of that milieu is the way the word true gets used as a term of praise for beliefs that happen to be useful — a habit I associate, at least in spirit, with the American pragmatists, though I have never been sure whether it is their doctrine or a parody of it. I am taking a seminar on classical American philosophy this term, and a friend from the seminar — a much more confident pragmatist than I am — has been pressing on me an argument that I cannot shake. It runs like this. (1) On the pragmatic account, to call a belief true is simply to say that it works, that it is good, or expedient, to hold it. (2) A belief works when holding it improves the believer's practice: it makes him more effective, better oriented to his circumstances, more capable of the actions he already has reason to perform. (3) Take a man who becomes convinced, on what most of us would call no evidence, that he is destined for some great success; the conviction steels him, he attempts what timider men avoid, and the attempt succeeds. (4) His belief worked, so by (1) it was true. (5) But there was no antecedent fact about his destiny for the belief to fit; the belief made itself true by producing the conduct that then confirmed it. (6) Generalize, and the pragmatic criterion certifies as true any sufficiently motivating belief that manages to bring about its own confirmation — the lucky charm, the doctor's unfounded confidence in her patient's recovery, the entrepreneur's certainty that the market will reward him. (7) A theory of truth that cannot distinguish a well-founded belief from a lucky self-confirming one, and that converts successful delusion into truth, has proved too much and ought to be given up.

I am fairly sure something is wrong here — I do not believe James was quite the fool the argument makes him — but I cannot locate the fault, and every repair I attempt seems to cost more than I bargained for. So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Which step should I reject: the identification of working with being true in (1) and (4); the reading of working as present experiential payoff in (2); or the slide in (5) and (6) from a belief's being verified to its being made true by the verifying? And once I reject one, what does the pragmatist have to give up in order to keep the rest — does he abandon the claim that this is a theory of truth at all and retreat to a theory of the right to believe, or does he keep the theory of truth and simply deny that the self-confirming cases fall under it?

I am especially interested in how Peirce's version and James's version come apart at this point. My instinct is that Peirce's appeal to the opinion that inquiry is fated to settle on, at the ideal limit of an indefinite community, blocks the reductio in a way that James's appeal to expediency cannot, because the former makes the present payoff of a belief irrelevant to its truth; but I suspect this merely relocates the difficulty, since whether the community converges, and on what, now depends on contingent facts about the community's survival, its membership, and who is permitted to keep doubting. So I would like to know where the argument actually fails, why the offending step looked so compelling in the first place, and what each pragmatist must pay to escape it.
