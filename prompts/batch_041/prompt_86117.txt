Last night I argued with a friend about al-Ghazālī on causation for considerably longer than either of us intended, and this morning I'm marking the short-essay question from that unit. I'd like a second opinion on one script before I hand it back.

The question, for a third-year course on medieval philosophy, was:

A study-group handout circulating in the course makes the following claim: 'Al-Ghazālī is the Islamic Hume. Both deny that we ever perceive a necessary connection between cause and effect; both relocate causal necessity in the mind; and both leave us with nothing more than constant conjunction.' Assess this claim in no more than 400 words. Say what al-Ghazālī is arguing in the causality discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, what he takes the connection between a cause and its effect to be, what the argument is meant to accomplish in its own setting, and how far the comparison with Hume holds up. Use at least one example from the text.

The answer:

Al-Ghazālī's target is the philosophers' thesis that the course of nature is necessary. In the causality discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa he attacks Avicenna and al-Fārābī, who held that fire burns cotton by a power inherent in it and that the order of the world therefore could not have been otherwise. Against this, al-Ghazālī argues that the connection between what is believed to be a cause and what is believed to be an effect is not necessary: the two are merely conjoined, and the conjunction is due to God's decree, which preceded them. Fire, being inanimate, has no action; God creates the burning, either directly or through the intermediacy of angels. The argument is observational: contact with fire is accompanied by burning, but accompaniment does not establish production, and so nothing in our experience rules out cotton's being turned to ashes without fire, or surviving contact with it. That is the point of the whole discussion — were the connection necessary, the departures from the natural course that the Qurʾān reports would be impossible, and the philosophers could not allow them. Ibn Rushd saw the danger and replied in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut that a world without real causal powers cannot be understood at all.

The handout is right that al-Ghazālī denies any perceived necessity, and this is where the comparison with Hume has force: both hold that the necessity we attribute to causes is not found in the objects but is supplied by something on our side of the relation — custom in Hume's case, the will of God in al-Ghazālī's. Both therefore leave the working scientist with regular succession and nothing more. Avicenna, by contrast, had made the whole of nature a system of necessary emanation from the First Cause, so that not even God could make fire fail to burn. Al-Ghazālī's own alternative is that the accidents occurring on contact between bodies emanate from the Giver of Forms, an angel or angels, while the sun, the sound eye and the coloured object are only preparatory and contributory factors.

The consequence for knowledge is severe. If no connection is necessary, no inference from past to future is warranted, and the sciences of medicine and astronomy, which the philosophers had built on the powers of things, lose their foundation. Al-Ghazālī is untroubled by this, since what matters to him is not the order of nature but the power of God.
