I'm drafting a blog post on why AI safety via debate might not work, and I want you to tear it apart. The setup I'm assuming: two debaters argue opposite sides, a weaker judge watches and picks a winner, and the hope is that truth wins in equilibrium because a liar gets caught by the other debater. My claim is that this only holds if the judge can actually follow the rebuttal, and that there's a class of cases where the dishonest debater never asserts anything false — she just builds an argument whose one weak step is buried deep, so the honest debater's rebuttal is correct but long, and the judge, who is tired and has a limited budget, can't verify it. So the winning move is to be right about everything cheap to check and wrong only where it's expensive to find. I'm calling this the needle strategy: the flaw is a needle in a haystack of true steps. I want comments on whether the argument actually goes through or whether I'm just dressing up the standard worry in new clothes, and on whether my extra wrinkle holds up — that the honest debater is forced to keep her rebuttal short enough to be followed, which hands the liar an advantage, since she gets to pick flaws that need long explanations. Related: I say a judge who can't find the buried flaw is behaving rationally, not lazily, so you can't fix this just by telling judges to try harder. Fair? And am I right that the obvious fixes — more judge time, cross-examination, a second judge — mostly move the needle (pun intended) rather than remove the problem, or is one of them a real escape hatch I'm missing? Where I'm least sure is whether I should bite the bullet and conclude debate only works for questions where the judge can cheaply check the final answer. Tell me where I'm overclaiming, and be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on it versus genuinely contested — I don't want to present a contested view as if its the consensus.
