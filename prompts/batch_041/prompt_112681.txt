I teach a 90-minute seminar session on strategic rationality to sixteen graduate students in philosophy; they are fluent with Nash equilibrium but have not seen a case where a strategy is eliminated only by a mixed strategy. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, rather than merely hearing the definition. The spine of the activity should be the following symmetric two-player game, with the payoff matrix shown; because the matrix is symmetric, both players face the same payoffs:

        A    B    C
A      22   20    0
B      20    0    2
C       0    2    1

The structure is deliberate. No pure strategy is strictly dominated at the outset. C is strictly dominated by a mixture of A and B: for instance, the mixture 1/4 on A and 3/4 on B yields 20.5 against A, 5 against B, and 1.5 against C, all strictly greater than C's payoffs of 0, 2, and 1. Once C is eliminated, A strictly dominates B, since A yields 22 against A and 20 against B whilst B yields 20 and 0. A is the sole survivor. The activity must lead participants to discover this themselves, round by round, without the facilitator naming dominance, mixed strategies, or iterated elimination until the debrief. They should have to commit to choices, see the consequences, and then be asked to eliminate strategies on grounds of rationality. Include a physical or procedural way for them to use mixed strategies—cards, dice, tokens, a randomising device—because the central lesson is that a mixed strategy can do eliminative work that no pure strategy can.

Design the activity. Specify materials, group sizes and roles, rules, a payoff scheme that does not use real money, and a timeline for the 90 minutes. The debrief must map each phase onto the formal idea: strict dominance, mixed strategies, the order of elimination, common knowledge of rationality, and the gap between a strategy's being eliminated and its being irrational in some broader sense. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about one feature of the formal idea that the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply in the debrief. Answer in no more than 1,000 words. Begin with a single-sentence statement of the activity's core mechanic, then give the rest under these headings: Materials; Rules and Roles; Timeline; Debrief; What Will Go Wrong First Time; What the Activity Cannot Reproduce.
