I run the sensory panel at a mid-size coffee roaster -- six trained tasters, forty samples a week, and the cupping notes go straight into what we buy, so an argument over a score is an argument over money. The recurring argument: a taster writes 'this one tastes flatter' or 'more bitter' and someone says they only wrote that because they saw the label, the price, or whose lot it was. Half the time it's clearly loose talk -- they mean they're judging it harshly, not that it tastes different -- and half the time I'd swear the taste itself changed. Give me a procedure a panel lead can actually run on a contested sample in ten minutes: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a real answer to each, which test or control to run when the answer is unclear, and where you have to stop and admit the tasting data can't settle it. Ground it in what philosophy of perception says about cognitive penetration and its rivals, including the dull explanations -- attention, adaptation, perceptual learning, memory, plain response bias -- that get mistaken for it, and make the lead think in terms of whether sensitivity actually moved or only the criterion did. You're a philosopher of perception who has had to make this usable for people who dont care about the literature, so think it through before you commit, and don't hand me the textbook list of confounds, only the ones that bite in an actual cupping room. I want the verdict I won't like. Exactly five numbered steps, one or two sentences each, with any test or example folded into the step it belongs to, no intro and no wrap-up.
