I'm preparing to lead a session in a graduate epistemology seminar, and the topic I've been handed is a puzzle I can state cleanly but can't seem to adjudicate. The puzzle: suppose you know some mundane thing — that your car is where you parked it. Knowing that, you also know that if it is there, then any evidence to the contrary must be misleading evidence. Since knowledge seems to transmit across entailments you can follow, you now know that any contrary evidence is misleading. So when a witness tells you the car has been towed, you may discount the testimony in advance, as misleading by your own lights, and go on believing as before. Every step looks like something we want to keep — knowledge that spreads across obvious entailments, knowledge that may serve as evidence, the thought that a known proposition settles what to make of evidence against it — yet the conclusion is dogmatism of the worst kind. The published replies I've found each seem to buy their escape at a cost I can't quite locate, and I'd rather see the criteria laid out before I read any more of them.

So give me the specification: the desiderata any adequate treatment of this puzzle has to satisfy. I want the intuitive cases it must get right (the ordinary knower who would be unreasonable to wave off a police report, the knower who does seem entitled to trust her memory against a stray doubt, the case where the counterevidence is overwhelming or comes from someone better placed), the principles it must either preserve or conspicuously give up, with the price of giving each one up stated, and the distinctions it cannot collapse — between having justification and being entitled to ignore evidence, between discounting counterevidence in advance and discounting it after the fact, between a belief's being true and its being reasonable to hold, between first-order and higher-order grounds. Attach the motivating case or consideration to each requirement, since a requirement without its case is just a prejudice.

Then mark the list: which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial to one camp, and — the part I most want — whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list itself is a result and I'd like to know which principle has to go. If a single requirement does most of the work of excluding candidate treatments, say which, and say what it excludes. Skip the bibliography; I have the papers, I don't have the criteria.
