I am not a biologist, but I have read enough popular evolution writing to notice that the argument about what natural selection actually selects — genes, individuals, groups, or something else — goes in circles. Everyone agrees on the genetics and on the basic arithmetic, yet the same examples are traded back and forth and nobody moves. My hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions: one asks which entity is the cause that makes a trait spread, the other asks which entity we must track to give a complete statistical accounting of the spread. I have also seen a formal identity that splits total evolutionary change into a within-group part and a between-group part, and I cannot tell whether treating those parts as two real forces is a substantive claim or a bookkeeping choice. Diagnose the impasse for me: is it a verbal dispute over the word selection, a hidden empirical dispute about how to partition variance in real populations, a disagreement about what counts as an explanation, or two questions under one name? Use the actual moves each side makes — for instance, the gene-centered and multilevel treatments of altruism in a structured population — and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a measurement, a formal result, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
