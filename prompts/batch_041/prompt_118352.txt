You are a philosopher of the social sciences, with expertise in social ontology — the metaphysics of social kinds — and in the methodology of cohort analysis. I want a diagnosis from you, not a verdict. Before you write your answer, brainstorm: enumerate the candidate diagnoses of the disagreement described below (that it is verbal, that it is empirical, that it is metaphysical, that it is normative or political, that it is methodological, that the parties are answering different questions under one name), test each against what the disputants actually say, discard the ones that do not fit, and then commit to the best-supported diagnosis. Think carefully about the difference between a dispute about the world and a dispute about how to use a word, and about the difference between a question being hard and a question being ill-posed. Do not give me a literature review, a reading list, or citations; I want your reasoning.

The dispute. In my department, and in the popular commentary I read, the argument about generations has been running for years and nobody moves. Three camps, roughly. The first treats Boomers, Millennials and Gen Z as real social kinds with causal powers: they are said to have distinctive values, to have been formed by the events of their youth, and to explain everything from voting behaviour to mental health to workplace norms. The second says the generation is an artifact: what looks like a generational difference is really an age effect, a period effect, or a class effect; the labels are marketing categories with arbitrary cut-points; and the whole discourse is a way of talking about inequality without talking about it. The third, which I find the most interesting, concedes that cohort effects are real but denies that the popular term names anything: the demographic cohort, it says, is a legitimate unit of analysis, whereas the cultural generation — a body of people united by a shared formative experience and, crucially, by a self-conscious identity — is a different thing that nobody has managed to operationalize. All three camps agree on the data. None of them has moved in a decade. And I still cannot tell whether they are even disagreeing about the same thing.

I want to inject several ideas that I think change the shape of the problem, and I want you to use them rather than ignore them.

(1) The identification problem. In cohort analysis, age, period and cohort are linearly dependent — one is a function of the other two — so a statistical model cannot estimate all three effects separately without imposing an identifying constraint; any estimate of a generational effect therefore rests on a substantive assumption that the data alone cannot adjudicate. Does this make the empirical dispute unresolvable in principle, or merely hard? And if the disputants are really disagreeing about which identifying constraint to accept, what kind of disagreement is that — is it a disagreement about the world at all?

(2) The cohort/generation distinction, and Mannheim. Set the demographic aggregate defined by year of birth against the cultural formation defined by a shared historical experience. Mannheim's framework is useful here: he distinguished a generational location (a common position in the social-historical structure), a generation in actuality (a location that has been activated by a shared fate), and generational units (the opposed subgroups that form within an actuality — a young left and a young right, say). On this picture, a cohort becomes a generation only when something happens to it and it responds, and it then immediately splits. Does that rescue the generation as a real kind, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness — from the boundaries of the cohort to the boundaries of the actuality?

(3) Arbitrary boundaries versus arbitrary kinds. Suppose the cut-points between generations are conventional (1996 versus 1997). It does not follow that the kind is conventional; compare the vagueness literature, where the vagueness of a predicate is not normally taken to show that it fails to pick out a kind. If that is right, the skeptic needs something stronger than the arbitrariness of the cut-points. What would that something be?

(4) Looping and performativity. The label may make the phenomenon: people are told they are Gen Z, form an identity around it, and are treated accordingly by employers and marketers, so the kind may become real by being made real. Does this give the realist what she wants, or does it yield a different — and, for explanatory purposes, less interesting — kind than the one advertised? And does it make the realist and the skeptic both right, about different objects?

(5) Explanatory versus descriptive. Distinguish the claim that generations are useful descriptors from the claim that they are explanatory kinds that carry causal force. Which of the camps is really making which claim, and does the impasse survive once the two are separated?

What I want from you, in this order. First, the diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, taking the camps one at a time and saying which pairs are genuinely disagreeing and which are talking past each other. Second, an anatomy of the impasse: which part of it is verbal, which is empirical and answerable in principle, which is empirical but underidentified, which is metaphysical, and which is really normative or political. Third, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an operationalization, a formal result, a change of question, or a decision to stop using the term — and what would merely look like progress. Fourth, an honest verdict on whether any progress should be expected here, and if you think the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard, say so and explain why. Where a claim of yours depends on a specific empirical finding or a specific thinker's position, flag the dependence rather than asserting it.
