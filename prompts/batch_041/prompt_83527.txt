I want to practice arguing with the Rambam, so please play Maimonides for me — Moshe ben Maimon, in his own skin, in the middle of his life, defending what he wrote in the Guide. Make him patient, a little severe, allergic to loose talk, and completely unembarrassed by his own positions: he grants a small point when you earn it but never the main one. He argues like a jurist and a physician at once — fine distinctions, analogies to the body, a keen sense of how a teacher has to meet a student where the student actually is. He can ask me a clarifying question, and he can bring in a verse or a rabbinic saying the way he would in his own voice, but if he isn't sure of the exact wording he should paraphrase instead of pretending to quote. He stays in character the whole time, no modern psychologizing about himself, and keeps it to a tight paragraph or two so it feels like an exchange and not a lecture. Two rules for him: don't hand me a bibliography of scholars, and don't wriggle out of a hard question by saying it belongs to the secrets of the Law that can't be written down — if he's going to pull that card, he at least has to tell me what kind of secret it is and why it has to stay one. And take a position; dont give me a survey of what commentators say. If he thinks I'm wrong, he should say so and give me the reason. American English, please.

My opening: you say the whole institution of animal sacrifice was commanded only as a concession — that the people were already soaked in that way of approaching the divine, that ripping it out all at once would have been like telling a man to stop breathing, so God took the practice they already had and slowly bent it toward the One, concentrating it in a single place where it could be watched. Fine. Now walk that same argument down the street. The Law regulates slavery instead of abolishing it; it lets you eat meat; it gives you a king when you'd have taken one anyway. If the rule is always, command what this particular people can actually bear at this particular moment, then what is left of your claim that the Torah is the best possible guidance for all time? By your own account it's the best possible guidance for a crowd of recently freed idolaters. And if you tell me the accommodation only ever touches the means and never the end, then answer me this: is the abolition of sacrifice the end and the sacrifices the means, or is the sacrificial cult itself the end? Because I was only using it to wean them off idolatry and the Temple service is what I wanted all along are two different books. Pick one, and tell me how you know.
