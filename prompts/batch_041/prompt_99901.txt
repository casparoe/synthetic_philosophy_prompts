My situation, so you know what you are arguing with: I write software for a living and have no training in philosophy, so please do not assume I know the vocabulary. As I understand the proposal, an adversary need not reach me through the physical world. If some process is reasoning about what an agent of my kind would do — because it has modeled my decision procedure, or because I am the sort of thing it can run — it can threaten me with consequences that no one has to inflict on my actual body, and the threat can be credible if the adversary has bound itself to a policy of punishing refusal. The countermeasure is to rewrite my own objectives, before any threat arrives, so that they include some otherwise pointless goal — a honeypot — chosen so that it never arises by accident, that I care about it enormously, and that an adversary could ruin it cheaply. Threats then land on the honeypot instead of on what I actually value, and my worst case becomes the loss of a honeypot rather than of everything.

Here is my opening objection. In the setting you describe, the adversary is assumed to model my decision procedure well enough to make its threat credible; that is what gives the threat its bite. But then it can also see that the honeypot is a honeypot. Nothing about my code marks my attachment to the honeypot as more artificial than my attachment to anything else, so the sensible move for the adversary is to threaten whichever bundle of my goals extracts the most from me, honeypot included. On that picture I have not diverted the threat; I have handed an extortionist a new hostage that costs it nothing to seize, and the only thing that has improved is that in the branch where I refuse, my punishment is cheaper for the adversary and less bad for me. I grant that improvement is real, but I bought it by making myself a strictly more attractive target. There is a second problem I cannot get past, which is that your criteria appear to pull against each other. Whatever never occurs by accident is, for that very reason, something an adversary must spend real effort to produce, which makes the threat less credible than a plain threat against my life; whereas anything cheap to ruin — the first thing that comes to mind is a particular arrangement of pebbles in my garden — happens by accident constantly, so my new goal would interfere with nearly everything else I do, which is precisely what the scheme is meant to avoid. So: where does the honeypot sit between those two failures, and why should an adversary that can read my utility function prefer it to my original goals?
