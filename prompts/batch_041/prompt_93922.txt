I spent 35 years in atmospheric science, mostly numerical weather prediction, and since retiring I've been reading alot of early modern stuff. I've gotten stuck on Kepler, and I've talked myself into a position I'd like you to attack. Here it is, roughly. Kepler thought most of astrology was junk — the twelve signs, the houses, the whole apparatus of dignities and rulerships — a pile of conventions someone made up and everyone else copied. But he thought there was a defensible kernel in the aspects, the angular relations between planets, and he thought those worked not by rays or beams or anything you could write into a force law, but formally: the soul, or the formative faculty of sublunary things, is constituted to respond to proportion, so it registers configurations the way the ear registers a chord. The same ratios that make music, the same geometry that structures the cosmos. He wanted all this to rest on physical foundations — that's the whole point of his 1601 treatise on the more certain foundations of astrology — yet he also kept insisting the influence is formal and configurational rather than a material force, which to my ear sounds like wanting it both ways. My question is what would have to be true for this reformed version to be false, and which observations, arguments, or formal results should actually move me.

I don't want the obvious stuff. The failure of newspaper horoscopes doesn't touch the reformed view, and neither does anything about sun signs or birth charts in magazines. I mean the specific claim: aspects matter, signs and houses don't, and aspects matter because of proportion rather than because of any physical push. What does the reformed version predict that the unreformed one doesn't? Which of those predictions have already failed? And which defeaters would a Keplerian concede as counting against them, as opposed to waving off?

Three things keep nagging at me. First, if it's formal proportion doing the work, the effect should depend on the ratios and not on which bodies are involved — an aspect between two of Jupiter's moons ought to have the same efficacy as the same aspect between Saturn and Mars — but Kepler's own talk about hot Mars and cold Saturn seems to contradict that, so the view may be internally unstable. Second, a physical proxy is sitting right there: planetary tides and gravitational perturbations really do depend on configuration, so you could naturalize the whole thing by saying the aspects are a rough index of the geometry of external forcing. But then the harmonic-ratio story does no work, and the predictions come out wrong anyway, since tidal forcing peaks at conjunction and opposition and goes slack near the squares, it doesn't privilege sixty and a hundred and twenty degrees over anything else, and the magnitudes are laughable next to solar and lunar tides, never mind weather noise. Third, aspects are defined from our vantage point, so the alleged cause includes facts about the observer, which is fine if you're talking about a soul located here but strange if you want a physics-style account.

What I'd personally find most decisive is a demonstration that the reformed view is unfalsifiable in principle — that any failure can be absorbed by saying the soul is free, or the effect is only a tendency, or the aspect wasn't exact. But I'd also accept a humbler verdict: that Kepler's reform was never an empirical hypothesis, just tradition dressed in geometry, and that I'm making a category error asking what would refute it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which the defender would themselves give up ground on, and say plainly whether the position has been insulated rather than defended — and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what such a view is. Write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text or tables, just an email-length answer.
