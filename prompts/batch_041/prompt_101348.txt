I have been trying to work out, on my own, whether a society's culture—its shared beliefs and values—can really be a cause of its economic life, rather than a decoration laid over material interests. The case I keep returning to is Weber's argument that the ascetic Protestant outlook, especially its Calvinist form, with the anxiety about predestination and the demand that one prove one's election through disciplined labor in a worldly calling, helped produce a new attitude toward profit and work: accumulation as an obligation, consumption as a temptation. The argument attracts me and also makes me suspicious, because it is just as easy to tell a story in which the doctrine was downstream of the merchants, cities, and institutions that were already in place.

I would like one number and the reasoning that produces it. The proposition is this: that the ascetic-Protestant outlook was a genuine difference-maker in the emergence of the capitalist spirit in early modern Western Europe—meaning that, holding the economic and institutional conditions roughly fixed, the outlook shifted the outcome, rather than merely accompanying or rationalizing changes that would have happened anyway. Please do not answer that it depends on what one means by cause; tell me what you take the proposition to mean at its most defensible, and then commit to a figure.

Decompose your credence into the sub-questions it actually rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each. I would expect the terms to include at least the following. First, whether cultural meanings can be difference-makers at all, or whether any apparent cultural cause is a stand-in for interests and institutions. Second, whether the psychological mechanism is real: whether a doctrine of predestination could plausibly generate the specific discipline and anxiety Weber attributes to it, and whether the evidence for that is anything more than introspection or a few vivid quotations. Third, whether the apparent association between Protestant regions and early capitalist dynamism survives the obvious confounds—literacy and the push to read scripture in the vernacular, urbanization, weaker guilds, a different stance toward usury and lending, the presence of religious minorities. Fourth, whether the spirit of capitalism is a determinate enough object to have a cause, or whether it is a label we apply after the fact to a bundle of unrelated changes. Fifth, whether Weber's method—building ideal types and speaking of elective affinities rather than of laws—can carry causal weight at all, or is doing something else entirely.

Then combine the terms into a final number, tell me which one the number is most sensitive to, and say what would move that term most: a piece of evidence, a thought experiment, or an argument. I am especially interested in whether you think the whole question is well posed, and if not, which sub-question is doing the real work. As a test of your own answer, suppose it turned out that the doctrinal content did nothing and that the requirement to read the Bible in the vernacular did all the work by making Protestants literate: would you then say culture was not a cause, or that a different piece of culture was the cause?

One further thing troubles me. Weber sometimes seems to treat culture as a switchman—ideas choosing among tracks that interests had already laid down—which sounds modest, but I cannot tell whether that modest version is trivially true or just as hard to establish as the strong one. If you think the switchman picture is the right one, say so, and give me your credence in the strong claim and in the weaker one separately, so I can see how much of your final number is really about the strong thesis and how much is about the modest one.
