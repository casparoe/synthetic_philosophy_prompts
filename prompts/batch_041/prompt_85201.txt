I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of logic, and I've just finished a draft of a short paper on Carroll's tortoise ("What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", Mind, 1895). I'd be grateful for comments before I send it out. Here is the paper in compressed form.

The paper's target is the standard diagnosis of the regress: that the tortoise illicitly treats a rule of inference as an extra premise. I grant that the diagnosis is correct as far as it goes, but argue that it is uninformative — telling us that the tortoise confuses a rule with a premise tells us only that the tortoise is confused, not what makes the confusion available in the first place.

My central claim is that the regress becomes possible because three different justification questions get run together: (1) Is modus ponens truth-preserving? (2) Is our belief that it is truth-preserving itself justified? (3) Does its being truth-preserving give anyone a reason to reason in accordance with it? The tortoise's demand is a demand for an answer to (3), but the only currency available to Achilles is (1) and (2) — another truth. That is why the regress never terminates: no accumulation of truths could discharge a demand for authority, because authority is not a further truth but a standing that truths have over an agent's reasoning. The tortoise is not someone who has failed to be convinced by a proof; it is someone who has not yet adopted a practice.

I then concede the obvious objection: I give no non-circular argument that we ought to reason deductively. My line is that the question has no answer from outside, and that this is not a defect — evaluating reasoning is something one can only do from within the practice, so the request for an external vindication is not a hard question but an ill-formed one. I add, tentatively, that the tortoise's position is not a rival practice but a refusal to have one, and that this is a normative result rather than a psychological one about what we cannot help doing.

I'd like comments of three kinds. First, on accuracy: is the three-way split clean, or have I carved at the wrong joint — in particular, does (2) really come apart from (1), and does (3) come apart from (2), or does the third question simply restate the second with an "ought" attached? Second, is the charge that the tortoise's demand is ill-formed — demanding a truth where what is wanted is authority — a real argument, or just a redescription of the problem in flattering terms? A hostile referee would say I have renamed the regress rather than stopped it, and I want to know whether that referee is right. Third, and most important: is the concession fatal? If I cannot say why we ought to reason deductively, in what sense have I shown deduction to be justified rather than merely unavoidable — and if the answer is "in no sense," does the paper have any thesis left? I would rather hear where the draft is weakest than have it endorsed.
