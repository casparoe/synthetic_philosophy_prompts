Ok here's a thing that bugs me and I can't tell if it's a real inconsistency or just me.

Animal welfare rules — the ones that govern research labs, farms, vet training — basically cover vertebrates. Rats, dogs, zebrafish, sure. And then a couple of countries recently widened the net so octopuses and crabs are in too. Everything else is outside the fence: insects, worms, snails, no matter what it does.

But the same people who write those rules will tell you, if you press them, that nociception isn't pain, that a reflex withdrawal doesn't mean anything is being felt, that you need the right kind of central integration before there's anyone home to care about. Okay, fine. But then a rat passes a conditioned-place-preference test and that's treated as solid evidence of pain, while a bee does something structurally similar and it gets waved off as just nociception. Fish get pain-relief protocols in some labs and not others. Octopuses get protected now, but a cuttlefish or a lobster that's roughly as complicated might not, depending on where you are.

So what I want is this: what theory of the morally relevant computation is that whole setup presupposing? Is there an implicit view that some specific computation has to be present — a certain kind of integration, something thalamocortical-ish, a self-model, the capacity to represent your own state as bad — and that only systems running it count? Or is the real line just "has a backbone," with the computational talk bolted on afterward as justification? And notice the two halves don't obviously come from the same place: the nociception/pain distinction sounds like it's demanding a specific mechanism, while the species list looks like it's going by lineage or by how much we like the animal.

I also want the attribution side of it. You can't get inside the thing, so how does the practice decide which computation a system is running? It seems to treat behavior as sufficient for vertebrates and insufficient for invertebrates, which looks like it presupposes something about when a computation can be attributed at all — maybe that similarity to us licenses the inference and dissimilarity doesn't. Unpack that.
