I am a third-year philosophy and cognitive science student, and I am currently taking a seminar on decision theory and existential risk. For my final project, I have been assigned to design a diagnostic instrument for a debate that keeps splitting our seminar in ways that do not map onto the usual labels: expected value fanaticism. In class we have discussed Pascal's mugging, in which a stranger offers an enormous payoff for an arbitrarily tiny probability, and the devil's offer, in which a tiny probability of infinite suffering is pitted against a certain finite good. What I have noticed is that classmates who all call themselves consequentialists disagree about which of these cases they will accept, and their disagreements seem to track a few underlying commitments rather than any named school. I would like you to build me a decision tree, not a prose summary, that sorts a student from a small set of initial commitments to a named position on fanaticism. The tree should have roughly eight to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves, and it should be built from the logical space of commitments first, with the actual positions located in that space afterward. Each branch node should be a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should carry the name of the position, or unnamed if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please also note any combinations of answers that no position occupies and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The branch nodes should be concrete cases or claims, not abstract labels. I want the tree to separate at least the following commitments: whether the value of outcomes is bounded, whether probabilities below some threshold can still be decision-relevant, whether infinite values are admissible at all, whether risk aversion is rational when the stakes are infinite or astronomically large, and whether expected value maximization is the correct decision rule or whether something else, such as a lexical threshold, satisficing, or a non-standard probability weighting, should replace it. The leaves should include unrestricted expected value fanaticism, bounded-value fanaticism, probability-discounting or non-standard decision theory, risk-averse or maximin consequentialism, lexical threshold or satisficing views, and a quietist or error-theoretic position that denies the decision problem is well-posed. I also want a leaf for a hybrid I have been calling two-dimensional fanaticism, which holds that fanaticism about value size and fanaticism about probability size can come apart: someone might accept a 1/10^100 chance of 10^100 happy lives while rejecting a 1/10^100 chance of an infinite good. I am not sure whether that hybrid is a stable position; if it is not, I would rather the tree show that by leading to an unoccupied combination than by forcing it into a named view. Please use cases such as Pascal's mugging and the devil's offer, and add a case I have been calling the Epsilon Offer: a being offers you a 1/10^100 chance of creating 10^100 happy lives, but also a 1/10^100 chance of creating 10^100 lives of unbearable suffering, while the alternative is a certain modest improvement in the lives of a small number of people. Another case I would like included is a 1/10^100 chance of saving all sentient beings from a lifetime of torture versus a certain cure for a moderate disease affecting ten people. Phrase every question so that it forces a choice, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, saying briefly whether they are extensionally equivalent or whether the instrument is simply too coarse. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references; I need the instrument itself, pitched at a seminar of about fifteen advanced undergraduates.
