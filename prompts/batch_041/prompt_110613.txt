I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about scientific realism, and I keep noticing that my verdicts on particular cases don't obviously add up to any single principle — which bothers me more than it probably should, given how many evenings I've spent in comment sections chewing over the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction. So I'd like you to treat my gut verdicts as data and tell me what theory of when we're entitled to believe a theoretical entity's posits actually captures them, or whether I have to surrender one of the verdicts to get a principle at all.

Here's where my gut lands. Phlogiston: not real; combustion is real, the phlogiston isn't. Caloric: not real, even though calorimetry and latent heat were genuine predictive successes built on it. The luminiferous ether: not real, despite being central to the best optics of its day and making novel predictions that came true. Miasma: not real — and I don't count the fact that miasma theory motivated sanitation measures that actually saved lives as evidence for miasma, since germ theory motivated the same measures. Atoms, back when they were posited and nobody could see them: real; the atomists were onto something, and the later discovery that atoms have parts and come apart doesn't retroactively make them wrong. Genes, in the sense of the early factors of heredity: real, even though the modern notion has since fragmented into half a dozen different things. Electrons: real, even though the electron of 1900 — a little charged sphere — is not the electron of the standard model, which is more like an excitation of a field; the entity survived the change in its nature. Quarks: real, though they can never be seen in isolation and by the theory's own lights are unobservable in isolation. Neutrinos: real, though they were invented purely to stop energy conservation from failing in beta decay and were, at the time, about as speculative as anything gets. The Higgs: real. The gravitational field or the curvature of spacetime: real, even though it replaced a force that I'd now say was never real — so a successor's displacing something unreal doesn't make me doubt the successor. Newtonian gravity and Newtonian mass: not strictly true, but I'd call them approximately true and retained as limits rather than discarded fictions. Continental drift: was right all along and was rejected for decades, so I don't treat the current consensus as evidence one way or the other.

What I can't square is this: the ether and caloric did real predictive work and sat at the center of the best theory of the day, and I'm confident they're not real; quarks and neutrinos were unobservable posits doing theoretical work, and I'm confident they are. My tentative stab is something like: believe in whatever the successor theory still needs and cannot re-describe away, and discount whatever it can account for without the posit — but I can't tell whether that's a principle or just hindsight dressed up as one, since nobody at the time of the ether could have known which parts would later turn out to be re-describable. So: give me the principle, or the smallest set of competing principles, that best fits the verdicts above, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new case, and note which verdict is doing the most work in forcing each clause. Then tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which I should give up. And apply it to at least one case I haven't mentioned — I'm especially curious what it says about dark matter, where I keep flip-flopping between 'obviously believe in it, look how much it explains' and 'this is our century's ether,' and about the wavefunction, which I suspect is a different kind of case entirely.
