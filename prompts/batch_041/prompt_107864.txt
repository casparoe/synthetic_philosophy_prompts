A colleague of mine in the epistemology reading group has constructed an argument she describes as the lottery-style case and the preface-style case rolled into one, and I cannot decide whether to accept her conclusion or to find the fault in it.

Her framework has three parts. Fix a threshold t strictly between 0.5 and 1, and say that an agent believes a proposition exactly when her credence in it exceeds t. Say that credences are coherent, obeying the probability calculus. And say that belief agglomerates: believing p and believing q together amount to believing their conjunction.

The lottery case. A fair lottery has 100 tickets and you know exactly one will win. Your threshold is t = 0.9, and for each ticket your credence that it loses is 0.99. Then:

(1) For each ticket i, you believe that ticket i loses.
(2) By agglomeration, you believe the conjunction of all hundred claims that the tickets lose.
(3) By the threshold rule, your credence in that conjunction exceeds 0.9.
(4) But you know some ticket wins, so your credence that all hundred lose is 0.
(5) Steps (3) and (4) cannot both hold, so at least one of the three parts of the framework is false.

The general result. She says the lottery is nothing special. If you believe p1 through pn, agglomeration forces your credence in their conjunction above t; where those claims are independent, that conjunction's credence is the product of the individual credences, so the product must exceed t. Concretely, with t = 0.9, an agent who believes a thousand independent claims must hold each of them at roughly 99.99 percent confidence, and a thousand claims at 99 percent each is already impossible. So rational belief acquires a ceiling: how many independent things you may believe depends on your threshold and on how confident you happen to feel. A view introduced so that belief could fall short of certainty ends up requiring that anything like a large body of opinion be held with near-certainty.

The preface. She claims the same engine drives the author who believes every claim in her book and also believes that at least one claim in it is false: by agglomeration she believes the conjunction of her claims, by the threshold rule she must have a high credence in it, and by her own fallibility belief she must have a low one.

I cannot accept the ceiling. I believe a great many independent things and I am nowhere near 99.99 percent confident of each, and it seems wrong that how much I may believe should turn on an arbitrary constant and on how confident I happen to feel. I am most attached to coherence and to the plain fact that I believe a great many things, and I would rather give up something else. So which line do I drop: the threshold rule, the coherence of credence, agglomeration, or the claim that I rationally hold many independent beliefs at once? For whichever line you reject, say why it looked obvious before and what rejecting it costs me elsewhere. And tell me whether your repair also disposes of the preface case or whether the two need different treatment.
