I have recently been appointed to a municipal facilities board that must decide whether a religious congregation may hold weekly services in a public school auditorium, and one of my fellow members keeps invoking Locke for the proposition that coercion in religion is illegitimate because the church's business is persuasion rather than force. Wanting to understand the claim rather than merely repeat it, I have been reading A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) in William Popple's translation, and I have stalled on the following passage:

"The business of true religion is quite another thing. It is not instituted in order to the erecting of an external pomp, nor to the obtaining of ecclesiastical dominion, nor to the exercising of compulsive force, but to the regulating of men's lives, according to the rules of virtue and piety. Whosoever will list himself under the banner of Christ, must, in the first place and above all things, make war upon his own lusts and vices. It is in vain for any man to unsurp the name of Christian, without holiness of life, purity of manners, benignity and meekness of spirit. ... It would, indeed, be very hard for one that appears careless about his own salvation to persuade me that he were extremely concerned for mine. For it is impossible that those should sincerely and heartily apply themselves to make other people Christians, who have not really embraced the Christian religion in their own hearts. If the Gospel and the apostles may be credited, no man can be a Christian without charity and without that faith which works, not by force, but by love."

What I cannot get straight is the work being done by the phrase "the regulating of men's lives, according to the rules of virtue and piety." It sounds as though Locke is handing the church a mandate over conduct, which is precisely the territory the magistrate's laws also cover, while denying it the one instrument by which conduct is ordinarily regulated. I would be grateful for a close reading, organized roughly as follows.

First, settle what "It" refers to and what "instituted" implies: instituted by whom, at what point, and to what end; and say whether the four clauses that follow name rival purposes or instead set display, dominion, and force on one side against moral formation on the other.

Second, determine what "regulating of men's lives" commits Locke to. Does it give the church a jurisdiction coextensive with the magistrate's, distinguished only by the means employed? Or do "virtue and piety" pick out an inward and voluntary domain that civil law, by its nature, cannot reach? I suspect I am sliding between those two readings, and I want to know which one the sentence itself supports and which one the argument as a whole requires.

Third, explain how the sentences are meant to connect. The first states a thesis about religion's purpose; the middle ones turn to the character of the would-be reformer, that one careless of his own salvation cannot sincerely care for another's; the last draws a conclusion about the means of conversion, "not by force, but by love." Is the argument that coercion is ineffective, that it is insincere, that it is self-defeating, or that it misconceives what Christianity is? I would like to know which of these the passage actually establishes and which I am importing.

Fourth, and most important for my purposes: does this passage by itself establish anything about what a civil magistrate may or may not do, or does it only describe what the church is for? If the latter, what further premise must the wider argument supply, and is that premise doing all the work, so that the passage I am drawn to is ornamental rather than load-bearing?

Finally, tell me where a reader is likely to over-read: whether "faith which works, not by force, but by love" is a claim about the psychology of belief, about the ethics of conversion, or about the proper instruments of an institution; and whether the phrase "innocence of life," which appears later in this opening discussion in a sentence comparing moral vice with conscientious dissent, should change how I read the sentences above.
