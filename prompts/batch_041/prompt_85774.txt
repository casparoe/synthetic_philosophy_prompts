Two colleagues of mine got into an argument last week about something a third colleague had said, and because I was present for the whole exchange they have asked me to referee it. I would rather hand the problem to someone with no stake in it. The case is this. Our colleague, who trained as an epidemiologist but now works on science communication, told a journalist flatly that a particular supplement does not reduce the risk of a named condition. She had read the one large trial bearing on the question, judged its methods sound, and put her confidence at roughly 0.95. What she said was true. She has since mislaid the paper and let the details go, and could no longer reconstruct her reasons if pressed.

The exchange went roughly as follows. Anna said she should never have asserted it, because assertion requires knowledge, and a high degree of confidence drawn from a single study is not knowledge — this, she said, is the lottery case wearing a lab coat, a case in which the odds make a claim overwhelmingly likely and yet we do not say we know it. Ben said the knowledge requirement is far too strong, since it would forbid us to assert anything about the unobserved, which we manifestly do and must; what assertion requires, he said, is truth, or at any rate justified belief. Anna replied that this misdescribes knowledge, which is fallible and does not demand certainty, and that the standard is knowledge because an assertion invites the hearer to take the speaker's word for something, which one can properly do only where the speaker knows. Ben countered with the case of someone who reports the expert consensus on a question she cannot herself assess: she does not know, in any sense she could defend, yet she is entitled to say it and we do not blame her. Anna said that is testimony doing its ordinary work rather than a counterexample, and added that the teacher obliged to teach a theory she thinks false is plainly doing something wrong even though her assertion is justified and useful — a discomfort that points to knowledge rather than justification. Ben then observed that our colleague must now withdraw the claim when asked, even though it remains true and her opinion of it has not changed; on a truth norm nothing would require withdrawal, so what we respond to in retracting is the loss of grounds rather than the discovery of error. Anna granted the observation but said that losing one's grounds is normally just losing one's knowledge, which is precisely what the knowledge norm predicts. Ben closed by saying that if the standard is grounds strong enough to amount to knowledge, then Anna owes us a threshold, and any threshold she names will shift with what is at stake and with what the conversation needs; once stakes enter, the norm of assertion has become the norm of practical decision under uncertainty, and the epistemic standard has been quietly dropped.

Please adjudicate this point by point. Say which of the claims each of them makes are right, which are mistaken, and which are merely unhelpful, and note where one of them concedes something that costs more than they realise. I am particularly interested in whether they are genuinely disagreeing about the norm of assertion or about what knowledge is: if Anna's knowledge is itself sensitive to stakes and context, it is not obvious that her view and Ben's deliver different verdicts about our colleague at all, and I should like to know whether the dispute survives that observation. Tell me also what both of them have overlooked, and finish by saying who has the better of it overall. Please write it as continuous prose, as you would in a letter — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italic, and no tables. Argue the substance rather than telling me who has said what in print; I want the arguments assessed on their merits.
