A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a thought experiment neither of us could settle: the philosopher who claims that the mere fact of his existence is evidence that the universe is vastly larger than cosmologists suppose, on the grounds that a larger universe offers more observer-slots for him to have landed in. I insisted that finding myself here tells me nothing about how many other observers there are; he insisted that only holds if you treat yourself as randomly drawn from the observers who actually exist, rather than as a random slot in the space of possible observers — and that under the second reading the inference to a huge universe is forced. I want my students to feel that fork in their own reasoning instead of just hearing me describe it.

I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar: eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them run into the self-sampling versus self-indication distinction before either term is named — ideally something in which they must bet on how large their "world" is using nothing but the fact that they find themselves in it. Give me the setup and materials, how the world-size gets secretly fixed, the betting or scoring scheme (no real money), a timeline that fits the hour and a quarter, and the rules governing who learns what and when, since everything hinges on who knows whether they are an observer. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the two assumptions and onto the presumptuous philosopher's leap from "I exist" to "the universe must be enormous," and that says honestly which piece of the anthropic reasoning the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Also tell me how this is most likely to go wrong the first time I run it, and how to recover.
