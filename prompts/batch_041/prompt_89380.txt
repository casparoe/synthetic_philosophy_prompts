I've become convinced that the premise-based fix to the discursive dilemma is the right one: when a group has to judge a compound question — three judges deciding whether a contract is valid, where validity needs both a signature and no duress — they should vote on the two premises and let the verdict fall out, not vote on validity directly. Otherwise you can get a majority for each premise and a majority against the verdict, and the group has contradicted itself. So premise-voting is correct, bottom-line voting is defective.

What should move me off that? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they really are: cases where premise-voting hands down something we'd plainly call unjust, results showing its just as gameable by strategic voters as the bottom-line rule, agendas where the premise/conclusion line is drawn by whoever sets the agenda so the "fix" is arbitrary. Which of those would a defender of the premise-based rule themselves concede counts against them, and which would they shrug off? And here's a half-formed idea I'd like you to either take seriously or kill: instead of voting on propositions, the group votes on reasons — each member states the considerations they think support their verdict, the reasons get tallied, and the verdict is whatever the winning considerations support. If that could be made to work, does it count against the premise-based view, or is it just the same thing in a costume? And don't be polite about whether my position is genuinely exposed, or has just been fenced in by how the premise/conclusion line gets redrawn case by case.
