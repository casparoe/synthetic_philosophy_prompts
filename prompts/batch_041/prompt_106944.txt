I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been turning over A. J. Ayer's own strange case for years, and what I want from you is an analysis rather than a retelling. The episode is this: in his late seventies, not long before his death, Ayer choked on a piece of salmon, went into cardiac arrest, and was clinically dead for roughly four minutes before being revived. He afterward reported that during that interval he saw a bright red light, was in the presence of two beings he somehow understood to be responsible for space and time, and wanted to cross a river, and he said publicly that the episode had slightly weakened his atheism and his conviction that death ends a person, while also calling the experience very weak evidence and asking whether it might simply be the product of a dying brain. Please take those facts as given and do not spend space worrying about whether they can be checked.

The theory I want the episode read through is Ayer's own: he was the great popularizer of the verificationist idea that a statement is meaningful only if some possible observation could count for or against it, and he applied that idea to religion, holding that claims about God and about survival of death are not the kind of thing that can be established either way. What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions, so that the theory illuminates the episode and the episode tests the theory.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, in American English. In whatever order makes the argument clearest, the five items should cover: what Ayer's own view actually implies about the claim that a person survives death, and whether on his terms that claim is meaningless, merely unverifiable, or a genuine empirical hypothesis that happens to lack evidence; what he was entitled to treat his experience as, given that the experience itself is undeniably a fact about him; whether his reported reaction, calling it weak evidence while admitting it moved him, is consistent with his verificationism or a lapse from it, and what the strongest defense of consistency would look like; where the theory simply has nothing to say here, especially about the fact that the pull of the experience seems to come from how it felt rather than from any checkable prediction it makes; and finally what the episode shows about the theory itself, whether confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated, given that the theorist is also the apparent witness.
