I spent forty years as a working natural scientist and have spent the last few reading philosophy on my own, which has left me with a specialist's confidence about facts and an amateur's uncertainty about what to do with them. I am stuck on the Anthropocene, and I would rather have help with one argument than a tour of the debate.

The argument below was reconstructed from a seminar and then tightened. Every step looks acceptable to me on its own; the conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) A scientific classification is objective: which class a thing falls into is a fact about the world, not a decision by whoever draws the class.
(2) Where a classification must cut a continuum, the cut is objective only if the facts determine where it falls. If the facts leave several cuts equally defensible, then choosing among them is not a factual matter.
(3) The Anthropocene must cut a continuum: the history of human transformation of the Earth.
(4) The facts leave several cuts defensible. The spread of farming, large-scale deforestation, the Columbian exchange, industrialization, and the global fallout of mid-century nuclear testing have each been proposed as the onset, and they lie centuries or millennia apart. Nothing in the record by itself privileges one.
(5) So the choice among them is not factual; it is a decision.
(6) The decision is made by asking which human change is the significant one — the beginning of agriculture, or of industry, or of the global signal.
(7) Significance is evaluative: to call one of these the significant change is to make a value judgment about what matters.
(8) So the lower boundary of the Anthropocene is fixed by a value judgment rather than by the facts.
(9) So the Anthropocene is not an objective scientific classification but a conventional and evaluative label, and it should not be formalized as a geological unit.

I cannot accept (9). The phenomenon the word names is as real to me as anything I measured in a laboratory, and the reason the question of the boundary was put to stratigraphers is that they are the people who know how such boundaries are actually drawn. But if I keep (1) and (2), (9) appears to follow; and I am not prepared to buy my way out of (9) by conceding that the classifications I relied on for forty years were decreed rather than discovered.

So tell me which step to give up. I want a position, not a menu: name the step, explain why it looked acceptable, and tell me precisely what I lose by rejecting it. If the honest answer is that (2) has to go, say so, and tell me what becomes of the older boundaries — whether I can still say that they were discovered, or whether I have been mistaken about that all along. If some later step is the weak one, I would like to know why the argument looked so seamless. I would rather keep the Anthropocene than keep any premise that cannot accommodate it, but I want the price before I pay it.
