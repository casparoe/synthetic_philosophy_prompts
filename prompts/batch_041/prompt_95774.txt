I retired from a career in ecology and evolutionary biology about five years ago and have spent most of that time trying to teach myself philosophy, which has been humbling. Right now I'm stuck on the nationalism-vs-cosmopolitanism thing, and specifically on an analogy that everybody I argue with leans on and that I can't get comfortable with.

Here's the analogy: the nation is like a family. We all accept that you owe your own family more than you owe strangers — you feed your kid before you feed the neighbor's kid, and nobody thinks you're a monster for that. So, the argument goes, since the country is basically one big family, we owe our compatriots more than we owe strangers abroad. And that's supposed to settle the whole debate: partiality toward your own citizens is just family loyalty written large, and anyone who says a stranger in another country counts the same as your own citizens is asking you to be the kind of person who'd let their own child go hungry.

My nephew says this is obviously right and that cosmopolitanism is just people refusing to admit they love their families. A retired philosopher I have coffee with says the analogy is doing all the work and none of it is earned. I don't know who's right and I don't trust my instincts here, so I want you to actually audit the thing.

First, what maps onto what. Be explicit about it — a table of correspondences would help me. What's the family, who's the parent, who's the child, what's the household, what's the shared history, what plays the role of the love that's supposed to ground the partiality.

Then where it breaks. I'll tell you the one that bugs me most, coming from my old field: in biology, "family" means something precise — shared genes, Hamilton's rule, r times benefit has to beat cost, and so on. By that measure a random fellow citizen is a stranger, genetically speaking; the partiality you find toward kin just doesn't extend there. But maybe the analogy was never meant in that technical sense — maybe it just means "the people you happen to be stuck with, who raised you." Fine, but then I want to know whether the version that survives is still strong enough to do the work. Push on it from every direction: families are lopsided (parents owe kids things kids don't owe parents), you can't really resign from a nation the way you can move out of a house, real families can be abusive and we don't think that makes the abuse fine, nations are huge and anonymous so whatever it is that makes you love your sister may just not scale to 80 million people, and so on. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the argument being made from the ones that are just scenery.

And then the part I care about most: what does the analogy quietly smuggle in? Because it occurs to me it might cut the other way. A family that cared only about itself and gave nothing to anyone outside it would be a bad family — you're supposed to teach your kid to share, to care about the world beyond the front door. If that's right, the family picture might end up supporting cosmopolitanism rather than the nationalism it was dragged in to defend. So tell me whether the conclusion it was used to support — compatriots have special claims that outweigh the claims of strangers — actually survives once all the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time and collapses when you lean on it.

Don't send me to a reading list, I'm not going to chase down papers, I just want the argument itself. And if there's a better analogy for the thing — one that gets you the partiality without the baggage — tell me what it is and what it costs.
