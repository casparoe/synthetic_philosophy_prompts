I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of mind and language — we meet in a pub on Thursdays, which probly explains the temperature of what happened — and last week two of the others got into it over Moore's paradox and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Roughly how it went.

Sam said the whole thing is just a contradiction with extra steps. A sentence like 'the kettle's boiling, but I don't believe the kettle's boiling' has to be impossible, he said, because saying the first half commits you to the kettle's boiling and the second half takes the commitment back; believing just is taking something to be true, so you can't have p and not-believe-p without contradicting yourself somewhere. Alex said that's obviously wrong, because both halves can be true at once — the kettle might really be boiling and I might be badly out of touch with my own opinions — so the sentence can be true, and what's wrong with it is that it's absurd to say, not that it's false, which puts it in the same family as any other sentence that's fine in itself but weird to utter. Sam came back that this isn't ordinary pragmatic weirdness: you can't even *think* it, he said, try thinking it silently and your mind jams, whereas 'can you pass the salt' being a request rather than a question is just a convention; and he added that the Moorean sentence is just as bad whichever order you put the halves in, which is not how implicatures usually behave. Alex said fine, but the past tense is perfectly thinkable ('the kettle was boiling but I didn't believe it was'), and so is the third person, and there's a clear difference between the version where the second half is 'I don't believe it' and the version where it's 'I believe the opposite' — one sounds like confusion, the other like a flat-out clash — and Sam's single contradiction story can't distinguish those, so the trouble must be with the act of asserting something while believing otherwise, not with the content of what's asserted. Sam said that's exactly his point in different words: if asserting is the outward face of believing, then the sentence makes you perform two opposed commitments in one act, which is a contradiction in the practical order rather than the truth-value order, and it's still a contradiction, just not the boring kind; he also said the guy the paradox is named after apparently called it absurd rather than contradictory and never said where the absurdity was supposed to live, which Sam thinks was a fudge. Alex said maybe there's no single place for it to live — maybe the case just shows that truth and assertability come apart, and that a sentence can be impeccable and still one that nobody who believed it could get out.

I was third pint in and said nothing, and I'd rather not just pick a side, so: go through their exchange move by move, tell me who's right about which specific point, and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short justification. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. I'd like you to tell me how confident you are in the verdict overall and to single out the one point in the whole thing you're least sure about.
