I am presenting at a departmental colloquium next month a paper arguing that the Pasadena game is not merely a curiosity about conditionally convergent series but a genuine counterexample to the claim that every prospect in a decision problem has a well-defined expected value. The game, as I present it, is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if it lands heads on toss n, the player receives 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and pays 2^n/n dollars when n is even. The probability-weighted payoffs form a series that converges, but only conditionally, so rearranging the terms can make it sum to any real number or diverge; there is therefore no fact of the matter about the game's expected value, even though every outcome has a definite probability and a definite payoff. My positive proposal is that rational choice does not require expected values: an agent facing the Pasadena game can still be guided by dominance reasoning and by comparisons that do not presuppose a numerical value for the prospect, and the game is rationally evaluable even though its expected value is undefined.

The audience will be about twenty people. The majority are decision theorists and formal epistemologists who work on expected utility, Dutch book arguments, and representation theorems; several are committed to the view that rational preference is representable by a real-valued utility function, and they will treat the Pasadena game as a technical edge case rather than a philosophical problem. Two are economists who use expected utility in mechanism design and have little patience for what they regard as philosophical hand-wringing about infinite series. One is a mathematician who will care about the convergence and rearrangement details and will want to know exactly what is being claimed to be undefined. There are also several graduate students, at least one of whom will ask how this differs from the St. Petersburg paradox and whether non-standard decision rules, such as those that compare prospects by their distribution functions or that allow incomplete preferences, already solve the problem.

Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to my position. Phrase each objection the way a member of this audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary, not in mine. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest or where I am most likely to be pressed further. I also want to know which objections I should concede rather than fight, and which one I should hold firm on because the paper does not survive without it.
