I have been drawn by lot to sit on a citizens' assembly that our council convened over a contested local question, and the council has now put a second question to us, on which we vote next month: should our recommendations bind them, or remain advisory? Before I cast that vote I want the specification rather than the argument. Write the desiderata that a theory of lottocratic authority — of when a decision made by a randomly selected body has a claim on the rest of us — has to satisfy.

I am after requirements, each with the case or consideration that generates it: the situations the theory must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results it must not contradict, and the things it must not simply assume. It needs to answer for at least these:

- a draw I regard as scrupulously fair returns a body that then decides something I consider a serious injustice; is the theory allowed to say I am nonetheless bound, and what does it have to give up if it does?
- a body whose membership mirrors the population almost exactly, but whose deliberation is steered by whoever wrote the briefing document and chose the witnesses;
- a second lottery whose pool is drawn from the alumni of the first, so that service breeds a class of repeat members;
- a body that votes to entrench its own decisions against reversal by any later assembly;
- a service that a large fraction of those drawn decline, so that the pool becomes whoever is willing.

Tell me which of the following are genuine requirements and which are merely the commitments of one camp rather than demands on any theory: the distinction between selecting a body and authorising it; between a body that advises and one that decides; between a body that is a statistical sample of the public and one that stands for it; between the fairness of the draw and the quality of the outcome; between a right to a justification and a right to consent. Then flag any pair of requirements that cannot be jointly met — that is the part I most want, since if descriptive mirroring, genuine deliberation, voluntary service, equal ex ante power, and accountability cannot all hold at once, I need to know which one I am being asked to trade away and why. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the positions people actually hold on this, and close by saying what a theory that survives the list would commit me to when I vote.

I would rather this not rest on any one country's practice or on the history of any particular assembly; I want the requirements argued from the cases above, so that I can defend them to colleagues who have never read any of this.
