I am a junior at a public high school of about 1,400 students, and this year I am leading a group of a dozen classmates who want to end the hazing rituals that our sports teams and marching band still run every fall: the overnight "initiations" that the administration has banned in writing three times and that no one has managed to stop in twenty years. Our faculty adviser's theory of change is simple. Win the argument. Bring in a speaker who was hospitalized by an initiation, present the disciplinary data, get the student council to pass a resolution, and the practice will die of embarrassment. What I have been reading this summer suggests a different picture of how moral change actually happens. On that account, practices such as dueling and footbinding were not abolished because the arguments against them were finally won — plenty of people had accepted those arguments long before the practices ended — but because what counted as honorable shifted, so that continuing the practice became contemptible rather than merely wrong, and the shift was driven by the people whose good opinion the practitioners actually cared about. There is also the older economic explanation of abolition, that slavery ended once it had stopped paying, and I cannot tell whether that competes with the honor story or operates at a different level.

Please describe the most important implications of the honor-based account of moral revolutions for how my group should run its campaign this year. I want consequences rather than a summary of the theory: which of our adviser's assumptions the account says are probably wrong; whom we should be trying to recruit first, and why; what it implies about using shame as a tactic and about the ways that tactic can backfire; and what it suggests about how a practice like this actually dies — whether it stops or merely goes underground. Where the honor account and the economic explanation give different advice, say so, and where the account cannot tell me what to do, say that as well.
