I am a knowledge lawyer at a mid-sized firm with a large employment practice, and I have been asked to produce the internal screening note that our paralegals run on the did-they-actually-resign files — we get one or two a month, usually well after the event, and the stakes are a tribunal claim or a settlement, so a twenty-minute screen that separates the clear cases from the ones needing a partner is worth having. Our present screen asks one question: what would a reasonable reader take these words to mean? That is the right question for construction, but it keeps misfiring at the edges. One file had a message that plainly meant I am resigning and yet did not, so far as I can see, effect a resignation at all; another had a remark everyone in the room understood as a joke which, on our own reasoning, was a valid resignation the moment it was made. My suspicion is that we are running two questions together — what the words meant, and what was done in the uttering of them — and that the Austin and Searle material on performatives and speech acts has a far finer set of instruments for the second question than anything in our template. I was arguing about this with a colleague over lunch after a seminar we had both been to, and we could not get the cases to come out the same way twice, which is precisely the sort of disagreement I would like the note to make impossible.

Please write the procedure itself: a checklist in words, with the steps in the order they are to be run, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points at which it stops with a definite yes or a definite no, and the points at which it must hand the file to a person. The reader is a paralegal with no philosophy and twenty minutes; the supervising partner signs off on anything that reaches the client; and we can ask the employee nothing, so everything must be worked off the paper in the file. Keep the vocabulary plain and define any technical term in a line. At each step I would like the note to be explicit about which of three layers it is operating on — what was said, what was done in the saying of it, and what was brought about as a result — because I think most of our bad screens come from sliding from one layer to the next without noticing. Four things in particular I want the procedure to prise apart, since our lawyers keep collapsing them. First, an utterance that fails to bring the act off at all, where the machinery is absent, the wrong person is speaking, or the thing is only half-done, as against an utterance that does bring the act off but is hollow, being said without the intention or the feeling that the act presupposes: this is the distinction that decides our files, because a resignation nobody meant is, as I understand it, still a resignation, whereas an act that never came off is not a defective act but no act at all. I want the paralegal made to commit to one verdict or the other, with the reason recorded. Second, the role of the addressee: whether the act is complete only once it has been taken up or recognised, whether that holds of all such acts or only some, and which side the resignation case falls on. Third, the awkward class of joking, ironic, theatrical and play-acted utterances, which the tradition seems to want to set aside as parasitic on ordinary use and which we plainly cannot set aside, the joke resignation being exactly what lands on my desk; I would like the note to say whether that exclusion can be justified and, if not, what the procedure does with those cases instead. Fourth, the small grammatical diagnostics we already half-use — the explicit first-person present form, the hereby test — together with an honest account of where they mislead. I would also like a short set of test pairs, two sentences to a pair, one of which effects the act and one of which does not, that a paralegal can run against a live file to calibrate; and a list of the steps that are really questions of value, or of law, dressed up as questions of language, so that the reader knows where to stop and fetch someone.

Then run the procedure on three files, giving in each case the verdict and the step that produced it. (i) An employee, in a heated corridor exchange with her team leader and in front of two colleagues, says she has had enough and is done with the place; she goes home and turns up on Monday as though nothing had happened. (ii) A manager emails a subordinate to say that this constitutes a formal written warning and that any repetition will be treated as gross misconduct, when nothing resembling our disciplinary procedure has been started. (iii) At a colleague's leaving drinks, an employee who is several drinks in announces, loudly and to general laughter, that he resigns, shakes hands all round, and is at his desk on Monday; the employer now wishes to say that he resigned, and we are acting for the employer. I expect the third to come out against our client, and I would far rather hear that from you now than from the other side later; if it does not, I want to know exactly which step is carrying the weight. Finally, be blunt about what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which of its steps will look like tests while actually being judgments. British English throughout, and no need to send me to the literature — I want the instrument, not the bibliography.
