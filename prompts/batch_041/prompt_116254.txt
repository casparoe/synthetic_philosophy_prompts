I would like a single credence, with its derivation, for the following proposition: that there is no case in which an agent's having no preference among its available options makes it irrational, or rationally impossible, for that agent to choose one of them — that when two options are tied, neither better than the other in any respect that bears on the decision, it is rationally permissible to take either by any arbitrary means (a coin, a fixed rule such as always taking the leftmost, whichever one happens to be noticed first), and that the ass which starves between two identical bales fails through a defect of nerve rather than a defect of reason.

I am a recently retired experimental physicist who has spent the last two years reading philosophy as an amateur, so I want your reasoning rather than your reading list. Please give me the number in your opening sentence, then decompose the proposition into the sub-questions it actually rests on, assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each, and show me how they combine into the figure you gave. The terms I would expect to see, though you should add or drop them as you judge fit, are these:

1. Realizability. Can complete indifference ever obtain for an actual agent, or do any two concrete options always differ in some respect — order of presentation, distance, salience, the agent's history with each — that hands the agent something to go on, so that the tie is a frictionless plane of the mind rather than a state any real system occupies?

2. Parity. A distinction I encountered recently and do not trust myself to assess: some hold that besides better, worse, and exactly equal, there is a further comparative relation, parity, in which two items are comparable, neither better nor worse, and yet not equal, so that a small improvement in one need not make it the better of the two. If that relation is genuine, does it (or does exact equality) really block action, or does it only block the claim that the agent has most reason on one side?

3. Permissibility. Given a tie, is the arbitrary tie-breaker a reason for the option it selects, or a substitute for a reason that merely keeps the choice from being irrational? Is the resulting choice rationally permissible, or merely excusable?

4. Inaction. Is there a rational requirement to act on one's ends when the means are to hand, or is stalling simply one more permissible option under a tie?

5. Representation. If calling a choice rational amounts to the agent being interpretable as having consistent preferences, then an agent that stalls under a tie is not irrational but simply not yet an agent — there being no preferences for it to be consistent with. Does that reframing dissolve the question, answer it, or beg it?

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what observation or argument would move it furthest. My own credence in the proposition is about 0.8; a colleague in the philosophy department puts it near 0.3, and I would like to know which sub-question that gap most likely lives in.

One further matter, since I am a physicist and cannot help myself: I am tempted to say that the tie resembles a perfectly symmetric unstable equilibrium, which no physical system ever actually occupies, and that the arbitrary tie-break resembles spontaneous symmetry breaking, where the outcome is fixed by a fluctuation that carries no reason. Tell me whether that analogy illuminates the philosophical question or merely dresses it up.
