I am revising a section of a paper on the paradox of deontology and would like detailed comments on the draft below before I show it to anyone else. The draft is deliberately compressed and I have stripped out the literature, which I will restore myself — so please do not supply citations, reading lists, or attributions. If you find yourself wanting to attribute a view to a named philosopher, describe the view instead; I would rather have a description than a possibly wrong name.

Here is what I mean by the paradox. A deontological constraint forbids me to do a certain kind of thing — kill, maim, use someone — even when doing it would prevent more instances of that same kind of thing. The constraint therefore forbids the act that would minimize the very violations it exists to prevent. If the reason for the constraint is that each person's life and body matter equally and are not to be traded away, then the constraint appears to defeat its own rationale. Either the theory is incoherent, or the constraint must be justified by something other than the value it protects, and it is then unclear what that something is.

Three standard replies, and why I find each unsatisfactory. First, the reply that the reason not to kill is a reason for me, addressed to me, and so does not aggregate into an agent-neutral reason to minimize killings; the paradox is a scope fallacy. True as far as it goes, but it explains the form of the constraint rather than its stringency: it tells us that constraints need not aggregate, not why this one holds even here. Second, the reply that constraints are justified by their effects on a practice — if everyone were permitted to make the trade, trust and security would collapse. This makes the constraint a rule of thumb and turns the theory into disguised consequentialism, and it cannot explain why the constraint holds in a one-off case where no practice is at stake. Third, the influential suggestion that constraints must be paired with a prerogative permitting me to give my own life and projects disproportionate weight. That is a claim about what I may do for myself; the paradox is about what I may do to others, so the prerogative does not touch it.

My positive proposal. The constraint is not a reason at all, or not only a reason; it is a disability. It does not tell the agent that the trade is worse, or that the agent has a weightier reason against it; it removes from everyone the standing to make the trade. On this reading the paradox is not resolved by comparing reasons but dissolved, because there is nothing to compare: the aggregate of more violations is not a consideration that competes with the constraint, but a consideration that no one is in a position to act on. The constraint is best understood on the model of a normative incapacity rather than a cost. Call this the no-authorization reading.

Where the reading earns its keep. In the transplant case, the surgeon's wrong is not that he got the arithmetic wrong; it is that he took it upon himself to determine whose death would be acceptable. The reading also explains why consent changes the case: the patient may authorize the sacrifice of himself, because the disability is a disability to authorize on behalf of others. And it explains why legitimate institutions — triage protocols, conscription, courts — may do what individuals may not, since they are authorized.

Where it strains, and where I want help. The omission problem: if no one is authorized to decide who dies, then my standing by while five die is also, in effect, deciding, and the disability reading seems to have nothing to say about omissions; if I extend it to omissions, it starts to look like a general duty to save. Second, self-defense and the button case, where no institution exists to authorize anything and yet we think the agent may act. Third, a slide I suspect I have made, from the descriptive claim that no one is authorized to the normative claim that no one may. Fourth, whether the reading is really distinct from the effect-based reply once we ask what justifies installing the disability in the first place; if the answer is that authorization is dangerous, I have not escaped the reply I criticized.

What I want from you, in prose, with a heading for each point:
