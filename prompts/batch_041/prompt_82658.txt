I'm running a small grad seminar on idealised deliberation and I have to write the final this week. The last third of the course is on HCH and factored cognition — the idea that you take one human, let them break a hard question into easier sub-questions and hand those off to other humans, who can each do the same thing themselves: humans consulting HCH, all the way down, no depth limit, no budget limit, no time limit. (I think the construction is Paul Christiano's, from the AI alignment literature, but I don't want the students citing anything — they can't look stuff up and I don't want a literature review.)

I want one essay question, and I want it built around a variant I've been calling committed HCH, so nobody can just recite the standard story back at me. In committed HCH, a node has to fix its decomposition up front — which sub-questions it will ask, and how it will combine whatever comes back — before it sees a single answer coming up from below. Plain HCH lets the node look at what its children returned and re-decompose, or re-ask, or throw a branch away. So: is committed HCH just a strictly worse idealisation, or is plain HCH's extra freedom a way of smuggling in extra computation — a way of letting the node quietly do the work itself instead of factoring it? And is either one well-defined as an idealisation of rational deliberation at all, given that the recursion bottoms out in unaided humans, that the choice of decomposition is itself a judgment the idealisation is supposed to be idealising, and that combining the children's answers can be as hard as the original question, in which case the factoring bought you nothing?

Two more things I'd like the question to force out, pick whichever fits better: what would have to be true for HCH to count as an amplification of human judgment rather than a rearrangement of it; and whether HCH's competence is bounded by how decomposable the question is rather than by how good the humans are. Make them commit to a position on at least one of these, not just survey the options.

What I need back: the exam question as it would appear on the paper, with the marks and a suggested time (it's a 3-hour in-person exam and this is the 25-mark question); then a model answer of about 700 words; then the marking scheme — five must-hit points, marks out of 25, and for each one a line on what a top answer does and a line on how students typically flub it. Finish with a sentence on what separates a strong B from an A.
