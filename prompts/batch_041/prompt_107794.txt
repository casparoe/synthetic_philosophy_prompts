I have been trying, with little success, to work out the rule my college's political theory tutor, Rosalind, is actually running whenever she pronounces on whether someone is free. She is the sort of tutor who will not let a case go until everyone in the room has committed to an answer, and over the term she has given verdicts on a string of them, which I have been quietly keeping in a notebook. A man held at knifepoint and made to hand over his wallet: unfree. A villager seized by a press gang and marched off to fight: unfree. A man detained indefinitely by a government that answers to no one and has charged him with nothing: unfree. A worker whose manager may reassign her, dock her wages, or summon her at will, and who exercises that power whenever it suits him: unfree. A recluse on an ungoverned island, troubled by nobody: free. A citizen of a stable democracy whose government is fully accountable to him and which has never in fact obstructed or coerced him: free. A mountaineer who, uncoerced and fully informed, chooses a route he knows may kill him: free. A woman who gives her savings to a cause she believes in and later regrets it: free. I cannot tell whether she is applying a plain non-interference test, something closer to the republican idea of non-domination, or some third thing, because no case she has set so far has come apart under the different tests.

Next week she has given us a case I cannot predict. A woman's late father left her fortune in a trust administered by a single trustee with absolute discretion over the capital. He has never refused her anything and has consistently acted in what he takes to be her interest, but he is under no obligation to consult her or to account to anyone for his decisions, and could lawfully strip her of the whole estate on a whim. She is comfortably provided for and does as she pleases. Rosalind asks only whether the woman is free. Tell me what you expect Rosalind to say, and, more importantly, why: set out the candidate rules that fit everything she has said so far, show what each predicts about the woman in the trust, and give me your best guess at the rule she is running, with your reasons for preferring it over the others that fit.

I am especially keen to know which single further case would most cleanly separate the surviving candidates, and why that case does the separating work better than the one she has just set. I have also been wondering whether her list could be squared with a positive conception of liberty, freedom as self-mastery rather than as the mere absence of anything external; if it can, say what that reading would predict about the woman in the trust, and if it cannot, tell me which of her verdicts rules it out.
