For the past several months I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on inequality, and one recurring move in our discussions has begun to bother me. We keep returning to a pattern that shows up across a number of cross-national studies, so I do not doubt that the pattern itself is real: in wealthier and more gender-equal countries, men and women often turn out to differ more, not less, in the fields they choose and in certain measured preferences and traits, compared with less equal and poorer countries. One of the other students has a favorite way of framing this. She says the pattern is like draining a reservoir: while the water is high you cannot see the riverbed, and as the water recedes the true shape of the channel is finally revealed. On her telling, egalitarian policy is the draining — it strips away the social pressure that was masking everything — and what surfaces once the water drops is the underlying natural difference between the sexes. She then takes a further step and concludes that we should not be surprised or troubled when men and women sort into different fields, because on this picture the sorting is nature's rather than society's.

I would like to audit that analogy properly instead of trading intuitions about it. Please set out the mapping explicitly — what in the reservoir picture is meant to correspond to gender equality, to social pressure, to the water, and to the riverbed — and then say where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the picture quietly imports: it seems to assume that the water is a uniform external layer resting on top of a fixed channel, and that whatever appears when the water drops was already there underneath, waiting. Is that a fair model of how preferences and choices are actually formed under different opportunity structures, and does the picture survive once we notice that the surrounding culture might be part of the riverbed rather than the water? Please separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for her conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and tell me whether the conclusion — the differences are natural, therefore unobjectionable — still stands once the breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If a better analogy for the pattern is available, give me that too, and be explicit about whether a cross-national pattern of this kind can license any claim about what men and women are naturally like in the first place.
