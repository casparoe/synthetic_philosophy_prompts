I'm taking a third-year course on rational choice and decision theory, and the professor runs a calibration exercise each term: before he hands out his marking scheme, each of us grades an anonymised answer from a previous year's midterm. Mine is an answer to this question. "Consider the one-urn version of the Ellsberg example. An urn contains 90 balls, 30 of them red; the remaining 60 are either black or yellow, in unknown proportion. Four bets are offered, each paying $100 if the drawn ball is of the designated colour and nothing otherwise: on red, on black, on red or yellow, on black or yellow. (a) State the pattern of preferences most subjects display. (b) Explain why that pattern cannot be reconciled with expected utility maximisation, identifying the specific condition it violates. (c) Say what you take the pattern to show about the rationality of those subjects, and what a theory that accommodates the pattern would have to give up."

The answer I have to grade reads as follows. "The urn holds 90 balls, 30 of them red, with the other 60 black or yellow in an unstated proportion. The four bets pay $100 on red, on black, on red or yellow, and on black or yellow respectively. What subjects typically do is prefer the bet on red to the bet on black, and prefer the bet on black or yellow to the bet on red or yellow. Two of the bets have probabilities fixed by the description of the urn: red wins with probability 1/3, and black or yellow wins with probability 2/3. Black and red or yellow have probabilities that depend on the unknown composition of the remaining 60 balls. No assignment of probabilities to red, black and yellow can make both of the typical choices expected-utility maximising, since preferring red to black requires the probability of red to exceed that of black, while preferring black or yellow to red or yellow requires the probability of black to exceed that of red. So the pattern cannot be represented by any probability function, and in that sense the agent's degrees of belief about the colours are not well defined. The condition violated is the sure-thing principle: the bets on red and on black agree on the event of a yellow draw, and the bets on red or yellow and black or yellow also agree on that event, so the preference between the first pair should have matched the preference between the second. What the example really shows is that people are averse to second-order uncertainty: they dislike the risk that comes from not knowing the composition of the urn. Once we let the agent hold a subjective prior over that composition and maximise expected utility in the ordinary way, her choices are perfectly rational and the conflict with expected utility theory disappears. The example therefore does not refute expected utility theory; it shows only that people treat unknown odds differently from known ones."
