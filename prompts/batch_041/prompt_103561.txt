My friend Devi teaches medical ethics and I've been trying to reverse-engineer what she actually thinks about advance directives from her off-the-cuff verdicts. So far: a man with motor neurone disease who'd written a directive refusing ventilation gestures for help once he's in respiratory failure — Devi says we follow the directive anyway. A man whose refusal of dialysis was written while he was in untreated depression, and who now says the depression was doing the writing and wants the dialysis — Devi says the directive doesn't bind. A woman who wrote a refusal, then years later revoked it in writing and wrote a request instead, now unconscious with a chest infection — Devi follows the later document. And a man who never wrote anything down but had told everyone for decades he'd hate to be kept going — Devi treats that as binding on the team even though his son is pushing for everything to be done.

Now the new case. A woman in the early stages of dementia writes a directive refusing hospital treatment for infections, and adds a clause: if she later asks for treatment, that asking is to be taken as evidence that she's lost the capacity to decide, not as a change of mind. Two years on she asks for the antibiotics; the psychiatrist who sees her reckons she can explain the choice and grasp what it means, so she'd pass the ordinary test for capacity. She's not confused about anything else, she just says she's changed her mind and wants a bit more time.

What does Devi say? I'm less interested in the verdict then in the rules that fit everything she's said so far — I suspect she's running either 'only an authentic, properly authored change of mind counts' or 'only a formally settled change of mind counts', but say if you find something else — what each rule predicts here, which you'd back and why, and what further case would actually tell them apart. British English please.
