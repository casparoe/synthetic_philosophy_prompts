My former PhD advisor is retiring after thirty years running a computational condensed-matter group, and I'm giving the toast at her dinner. I did my doctorate with her and then left physics for software engineering, so I'm the one at the table with no philosophy training and strong opinions about type systems. The room splits cleanly: about forty former students and colleagues who know her work in detail, and about twenty family members and friends who couldn't tell you what a Hamiltonian is.

Here is the problem I want the toast to solve. Her group's best-known model is now understood as a useful fiction — the things it posited aren't the real constituents of anything, and the theory that replaced it says almost nothing about them. But the old model's equations turn up, barely altered, inside the replacement. Her standing line at group meetings was: "The story was wrong. The math was right. Notice which part keeps working." I don't want a toast that treats that as a joke about job security. I want it to make one definite claim about what exactly survived the replacement, and why that isn't a coincidence.

A philosopher friend tells me the position that does this work is called structural realism, and that it comes in two flavors: either the equations capture everything there is to know about the world, with nothing underneath them left to describe, or there is something underneath, but structure is all we can ever say about it. I don't have a view, so pick the one you can actually defend in this setting and commit to it. Her brother-in-law, who did a philosophy degree, has already told me the obvious objection: if structure is all we ever get, then any collection of things of the right size can be assigned whatever structure we like, which makes the claim sound empty. Don't spend the toast on that, but don't say anything it would flatten either.

Five minutes, warm, a little funny, and it must land as a toast rather than a seminar. It also must not require the room to believe something false about her: she knew the entities were fictions before most people did, and never pretended otherwise. End with two or three lines outside the toast itself stating where you've taken liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested reading, and why you committed to the flavor you chose.
