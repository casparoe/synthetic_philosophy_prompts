Question 2 (25 points). Kripke argued that an identity statement like water is H2O is necessary but knowable only a posteriori. Explain how two-dimensional semantics is supposed to account for this, being explicit about what each of the two dimensions is a function from and to. Then answer two questions: does the account require that natural kind terms like water have descriptive senses that speakers grasp a priori? And is water still a rigid designator on this account? Use Twin Earth in your answer.

Student's answer (the whole thing — there was no separate diagram or scratch work):

Two-dimensional semantics starts from the fact that when we evaluate a sentence like water is H2O there are two different things we can hold fixed. The first is the epistemic or a priori dimension. Here we ask what the word water would pick out in a scenario considered as actual, where a scenario is a centered world, i.e. a possible world with a marked centre. This gives us the primary intension of water, which is the watery stuff: the clear, drinkable, odourless liquid that fills the oceans and lakes and falls as rain. At a Twin Earth scenario that same role is filled by XYZ, so the primary intension picks out XYZ there. The primary intension therefore varies from scenario to scenario, and this is why we can conceive of water not being H2O — conceivability tracks the primary intension.

The second is the subjunctive or metaphysical dimension. Here we ask what water picks out at a world once the actual world is held fixed, and the answer is H2O at every world, since it is the actual watery stuff, H2O, that fixes the reference of the term. This gives us the secondary intension, which is constant across worlds, and that is what makes water is H2O necessary. So the necessity is metaphysical while the aposteriority is epistemic: you have to know what the actual world is like to know that water is H2O, but once you do, you know something that holds in all worlds. The framework also explains why Kripke's claim looked paradoxical — the apparent contingency of water is H2O is contingency in the first dimension, not the second.

The view is Fregean in spirit. The primary intension plays the role of sense and the secondary intension plays the role of reference, with sense determining reference; the only reason we are not in a position to know the identity a priori is that the sense does not determine the reference until we know which world is actual. So the account does commit us to descriptivism about natural kind terms — speakers have to grasp the descriptive content of the primary intension in order to use the term at all. This is also the framework Chalmers has in the background when he argues from the conceivability of zombies.

The upshot for rigidity is that water turns out not to be a rigid designator after all. Rigidity would mean picking out the same thing at every world, but the primary intension picks out H2O at some scenarios and XYZ at others, so water only picks out the same thing relative to a fixed scenario. What Kripke was really tracking, then, was the stability of the secondary intension, which is not rigidity but just the fact that we hold the actual world fixed when we evaluate counterfactuals.
