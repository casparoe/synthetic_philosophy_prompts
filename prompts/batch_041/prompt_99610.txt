Im putting together a parody piece and I need you to write it for me: a fictional popular-history magazine's contributor handbook, written completely straight, no winking. The magazine is invented — call it something like The Long View — and the handbook is the managing editor's numbered list of house rules for how contributors should write a history article. The joke is that every single rule is a real failure mode of retrospective explanation, stated as if it were good practice.

Rules I want in there, though add your own: open every piece with foreshadowing, an omen or a mood or a telling detail; attribute agency only to whoever lost; ban counterfactuals on the grounds that they didn't happen; require the phrase 'the seeds of' at least once; require a closing paragraph that extracts a lesson; require an Inevitability Score out of ten that contributers must report and that always rounds up; and something about how to handle sources where contemporaries clearly had no idea what was coming — reclassify their confusion as dramatic irony. Throw in a rule about seperate periods by how they ended, and one about how much foreshadowing is too much (answer: there's no such thing).

Then a worked example: take a genuinely contingent, low-stakes episode — an election settled by a coin flip, a company that folded because one supplier's warehouse flooded — give the flat factual version first, then the house version, and make the difference glaring without the editor noticing what he's just done.

Then a short sidebar in the same voice, filed under 'methodology', on how to handle contingency and counterfactual reasoning in a way that sounds rigorous but is quietly circular. End the whole thing with a one-line style rule of thumb that's funny and a little chilling.

Two things matter. First, the humor has to come from getting the epistemology right — outcome knowledge leaking into the description of the causes, causes selected because they led to the ending you already know, tidiness mistaken for explanation, hindsight dressed up as inevitability — so that a historian laughs instead of wincing, and a reader who doesn't know any of the literature still senses something is off. Second, the target is the style of discourse, not any real historian, book or journal: don't name anyone, don't cite anything, and don't lean on any actual magazine's style guide.

About 700 words, numbered rules, deadpan all the way through.
