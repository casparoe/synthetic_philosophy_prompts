I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on contemporary ethical theory, and we've spent the last three weeks on Scanlon's contractualism: roughly, the view that an action is wrong when every principle that would permit it could be reasonably rejected by someone seeking terms of cooperation that no one could object to, with the crucial qualification that the objections that count are the objections of individuals taken one at a time, rather than the sum of burdens spread across many people — what our professor calls the individualist restriction. This is where I keep getting stuck, and I'd like you to argue for a position rather than merely survey the terrain. Our professor put a case on the board that I can't shake: an occupational safety regulator can mandate exactly one of two equipment upgrades. Upgrade X would prevent, on average, one worker a year from suffering a permanently disabling spinal injury. Upgrade Y would prevent, on average, five thousand workers a year from each suffering an irreversible moderate hearing loss — serious, socially isolating, life-altering, but plainly less devastating than the injury X prevents. If complaints cannot be pooled, Y looks ruled out, since each of the five thousand has a weaker complaint than the one worker X protects; yet almost everyone in the section thought the regulator should choose Y, and I don't think we were simply being sloppy.

A classmate offered a repair I find intriguing but suspect, and I want you to argue for it: that complaints of the same kind be combined with diminishing marginal weight, so that each additional person with that complaint adds less justificatory force than the one before, with the total contribution of even a very large number of mild complaints converging on a finite ceiling. On this picture five thousand significant complaints of one kind can jointly outweigh a single graver complaint of another kind, while no avalanche of trivial complaints can ever outweigh one serious one. Your task is to argue that this is a principled extension of the justifiability criterion itself — that someone who takes seriously the demand that a principle be justifiable to each person has positive reason to accept diminishing marginal aggregation, not merely reason to tolerate it as a concession to recalcitrant intuition. Please build the argument in your own terms rather than by rehearsing what commentators have said; I'd rather see whether the thing works than who has defended it.

Because I'll be defending this in a section that mostly holds that any aggregation whatever abandons what is distinctive about contractualism, be explicit about the weakest joint in your case: which premise, if it failed, would bring the conclusion down, and what counterexample or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict — that the individualist restriction should stand unmodified and that the regulator should mandate X.
