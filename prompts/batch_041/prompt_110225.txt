I am the chair of the restoration committee for the old chapel in the fenland parish of Ashfen, and on the first Saturday in June I have to give the address at its rededication — roughly five minutes, about eight hundred words, spoken outdoors to a crowd standing on grass. I am not a historian, and that is the least of my problems.

Here is what I have to work with. The story everyone in the parish knows is that Ashfen was made by one man, Elias Rooke, who drained the fen in the 1770s and built the chapel, and whose family held the land for a century afterwards. Trace that story back and it comes to rest, as far as I can tell, on a single book: a History of the Parish printed in 1894 at the expense of a retired schoolmaster. He cites a parish book. There is no parish book. What survives is eleven leaves of it, recovered from a solicitor's office in 1961, numbered in a sequence that stops at page 57; the History cites pages 214 and 260. The extracts the schoolmaster gives are in his own rolling Victorian prose, not the prose of an eighteenth-century clerk. There was a fire in the parish chest in 1809, so anything he read in the 1890s had to have been out of the chest and in private hands long before.

Other pieces, none of them clean. A datestone over the door bears the year 1773 and two initials, but the porch was rebuilt in 1893 and the stone reset, so it dates the rebuilding and nothing else. The parish registers begin in 1781 and are the registers of the established church, which tells us nothing about who was meeting in a dissenting chapel. The drainage commissioners' accounts survive only as a clerk's fair copy made in 1851, from an original he notes was much decayed; it lists two subscribers named Rooke, an E. and an R., where the legend has only Elias, and a third name lost under a repaired tear. The family's own papers were catalogued in 1950 by a family member with a genealogy to prove.

Now the difficulty. The family paid for half the restoration, and the first thing on the programme is the unveiling of the plaque they commissioned, which credits Elias Rooke as the founder who drained the fen and built the chapel in 1773. I speak immediately afterwards. His descendants will be in the front row. The county archivist is coming and has been politely noncommittal every time I have asked her whether the Rooke story holds up. The village school has been teaching the story for a generation, and a party of children will be there. I cannot stand up and read the plaque aloud as though it were established, and I will not stand up and tell a bereaved family that their ancestor may never have existed. Between those two there is a real speech, and I need it.

What I want the address to do:

- Assert nothing as fact that rests only on the 1894 History's citations, on the reset datestone, or on the interested family catalogue.
- Where it reports the legend, make it audible to a listener that it is reporting and not vouching — without jargon, without footnotes, and without leaning on the word allegedly every third sentence.
- Still make at least one firm, definite claim about the parish's past that the evidence does support. I do not want five minutes of elegant shrugging; a speech that concludes that we cannot know anything would be a failure.
- Work at least one concrete item from the list above into the speech in a way a person standing on grass can follow hearing it once.
- Leave the family their ancestor. The plaque is going to stand there whatever I say, and the speech has to be able to live beside it without either endorsing its inscription or tearing it down.
- Speak in short sentences and plain words. No slides, no dates read out in a row, no lecture on method. The archivist should hear that I have been careful; the schoolchildren should hear a story.

One thing I keep noticing, and I do not know enough to say whether it is a real distinction or only a difference of style: some of the earliest historians we have keep handing you the chain — who told them, and how far the teller had travelled — and others hand you a finished account with the chain cut off. I would like this address to hand the chain back, in a form a crowd on grass can follow. Whether that is a fair thing to say about the ancients, or only a habit of mine, you can leave aside; it is the shape of the thing I am after.

Give me the address. After it, add no more than five short bullet points listing the claims you deliberately kept out and the reason for each, so that I can answer questions in the tea tent. Then, as the final line, exactly this: \answer{...} enclosing one sentence stating the strongest claim about Ashfen's founding that the address is prepared to assert as fact.
