I am a high school student who has been working through game theory on my own, and I have written myself into an argument that I cannot get out of. I would like you to identify the step I should reject, explain why it looked so plausible, and tell me what rejecting it costs.

The argument concerns a game I invented. Two players choose simultaneously: the row player chooses A, B, or C, and the column player chooses L, M, or R. The row player's payoffs are

        L     M     R
A       2     2     0
B       1     1     3
C       1     1     0.5

and the column player's payoffs are

        L     M     R
A       2     0     0
B       0     2     0
C       0     0     -1

These are two separate tables; the game is not zero-sum, and I have simply recorded what each player receives in each cell. Here is the argument.

(1) A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy.
(2) A strategy is strictly dominated when some other strategy of that player's -- possibly a coin flip between two of them -- pays strictly more against every strategy the opponent might use. So the test is entirely about the player's own payoffs: a dominated strategy is worse than its alternative no matter what the player expects the opponent to do.
(3) In this game the row player's C is strictly dominated. The 50-50 mix of A and B pays 1.5 against each of L, M, and R, whereas C pays 1, 1, and 0.5; and neither A alone nor B alone dominates C, since A is worse than C against R and B merely ties C against L and M. Symmetrically, the column player's R is strictly dominated by the 50-50 mix of L and M, which pays 1, 1, and -0.5 against A, B, and C, whereas R pays 0, 0, and -1; and neither L alone nor M alone dominates R.
(4) So a rational row player never plays C, and a rational column player never plays R.
(5) Cross out C and R and look at what remains: A and B against L and M. In that reduced game the row player's B is strictly dominated by A, which pays 2 against each of the two remaining columns while B pays 1.
(6) The rule I used to cross out B is the same rule as in (1): I asked only whether one of the row player's strategies pays more than another against every strategy the opponent might use. I never asked whether the column player is rational, or what the row player believes about the column player, or anything at all about the column player's payoffs. So crossing out B is licensed by the row player's own rationality, exactly as crossing out C was.
(7) Carry on. With B gone, the column player's M pays 0 against the only row strategy left, while L pays 2, so M goes as well. What survives is A against L.
(8) Therefore a rational player in this game must play A -- and must play it whatever they believe about the opponent, including the belief that the opponent is about to play R. Rationality on its own, with no assumption that rationality is common knowledge and no knowledge of the opponent's payoffs, would settle what I should do.

That conclusion cannot be right. If I genuinely expected the other player to play R, then B would pay me 3 while A would pay me 0, and playing A would be absurd; and B is not strictly dominated, so none of my premises seems to rule it out. Yet I cannot see where the argument slips. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than keep (8).

Which numbered step do you reject, and what do I have to give up by rejecting it? Please also say what would change your mind: which consideration or counter-argument would lead you to blame a different step, or to conclude that the argument is in fact sound and that my reaction to (8) is what is mistaken. Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
