I am preparing a session for a small interdisciplinary seminar on moral psychology, and our discussion has settled on a question I keep circling: whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion to build a moral culture around, and whether a culture can deliberately choose between them. I have a stake in the guilt side — it seems to me that guilt targets what an agent did and leaves their standing intact, whereas shame targets the agent's very self and tends to be parasitic on an audience — but I have been impressed both by the objection that this preference is parochial and by a sharper one: that the institutions I would reach for in order to cultivate guilt are themselves products of a culture that already valued it. What I want from you is not a verdict on the anthropology, which I can read up on, but a structured evaluation of a set of concrete devices by which a society might push its moral culture from shame toward guilt. I have written the list myself, and I have deliberately left out the familiar devices (criminal punishment, honor codes, public shaming) so that the assessment has to turn on the mechanisms rather than on their reputations.

1. Restitution ledgers: wrongs are recorded as debts owed to particular victims, discharged privately, with no public register of who owed what to whom.
2. Person-free reproach: criticism is always framed as a claim about the act and the future (that this was a betrayal, and here is what repairing it would take) and never as a claim about the wrongdoer's standing or character.
3. Self-audit pedagogy: schools and workplaces teach explicit standards together with regular written self-assessment, and the assessments remain in the possession of the person who writes them.
4. Status-neutral acknowledgment: the culture retains public rituals in which wrongdoing is acknowledged before others, but strips them of rank-loss, exclusion, and derogation.
5. Audience substitution: acknowledgment is made to the victim and to a small number of people who already know, rather than to the community at large.
6. Self-imposed sanction: the wrongdoer is expected to impose a penalty on themselves, chosen by them within bounds set by the victim.

Please rank these into tiers by how likely each is, over two or three generations, to shift a culture's modal moral emotion from shame toward guilt, assuming the device is genuinely adopted and enforced. For each, state the mechanism you take to be doing the work and the main way it could backfire or be reabsorbed by the surrounding shame culture; where two devices seem to operate through the same mechanism, say so and collapse them rather than ranking them separately. Then re-rank the same list on a second axis — the moral price the culture pays for adopting it, in terms of what it must give up and what it may lose in solidarity, deterrence, or responsiveness to victims — and flag any device whose position moves a great deal between the two rankings, since those are the cases where I suspect the dispute about which emotion is "better" is really a dispute about what a society can afford.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: give me a single number between 0 and 1 for how much of the distance between a shame-centered and a guilt-centered moral culture can be closed by institutional design of this kind alone, holding religion, economy, and family structure fixed, with a sentence or two of justification for the number. Then tell me whether your two rankings, taken together, tacitly presuppose that the culture already possesses the emotion it is trying to produce — and if so, name the specific point in the design where that circularity bites.
