For a while now I have been convinced that the underdetermination of theory by evidence is not a tidy logical curiosity but the normal condition of theoretical science. The version I care about is what I have come to call algorithmic underdetermination. Take any theory T that we actually use. There is a rival T-prime that is empirically equivalent to T for every observation we could make, but that differs from T by positing one extra unobservable entity or parameter whose only role is to make the derivation of T's predictions computationally intractable. T-prime is not a mere notational variant; it carries different ontological commitments. Evidence cannot distinguish T from T-prime. What distinguishes them is that T is usable and T-prime is not. If that is right, then theory choice is settled by computational tractability, a pragmatic virtue, rather than by truth-conducive evidence. That seems to me a serious problem for scientific realism: our best theories may be the ones we can calculate with, not the ones most likely to be true. I want to know what would count against this position.

Please give me a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the literature. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or developments would count against it? For each, say whether it would be decisive, merely awkward, or something a defender should happily concede. In particular: Is the construction legitimate, or does it cheat by individuating theories syntactically? Does empirical equivalence really extend to all future evidence, or does the extra posit make a difference somewhere? Could a Bayesian or decision-theoretic account absorb the computational difference into the agent's utility function, collapsing the distinction between epistemic and pragmatic considerations? Does the fact that T-prime is unusable make it not a genuine rival at all, and if so, what is the principled criterion? What does the position predict that its rivals do not, and what pattern in actual scientific practice, if absent, would count against it? What would the strongest defender of underdetermination concede counts against them, and what would the strongest critic call fatal? I would also like to know whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every possible scientific practice can be redescribed as algorithmic underdetermination, is that a feature or a bug?

I ask in part because I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I have a lot of sympathy for the pragmatist move that treats practical equivalence as metaphysical equivalence. But I also suspect the position may be confused: perhaps computational tractability is itself an epistemic virtue, or perhaps the construction begs the question by assuming a sharp evidence-pragmatics distinction. Personally, I would find it most persuasive if someone could show that the construction only works by individuating theories in a way no working scientist would accept, or that computational tractability is a reliable guide to truth. If the premise is muddled, or if the task as posed cannot be done well, please say so plainly rather than humoring me. Otherwise, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what I should update on.
