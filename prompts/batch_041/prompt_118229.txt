Last night I lost several hours to an argument with a friend who works in decision theory, and I want an outside read on what she actually believes, because her verdicts on cases and her stated position do not obviously fit together.

Her stated position is the result she calls Good's theorem: free information cannot lower an expected-utility maximizer's expected utility, since it can always be ignored; information is worth whatever improvement it makes to the decisions you take with it; and where nothing you do depends on it, its value is exactly zero. She treats this as a constraint on any sane account of what information is worth. She would also deny, in a seminar, that the way a fact feels is any part of what the fact is worth. Yet when I pressed her on cases, she gave these verdicts, which I have tried to state as neutrally as I can:

1. A free and perfectly reliable test for a condition that is treatable with an effective but unpleasant regimen: she takes the test.
2. A free and perfectly reliable test for a condition that is untreatable, whose course is slow and predictable, and where knowing would let her arrange her finances, her work, and her goodbyes: she takes the test.
3. A free and perfectly reliable answer to a question with no bearing on anything she will ever do and no connection to anyone she knows — say, the winning numbers of a lottery she did not enter, in a country she will never visit: she says she would not bother, and cannot see that it is worth anything to her.
4. A free and perfectly reliable warning, delivered a week ahead, of a bad event she cannot prevent but can prepare for: she takes it.

Here is the case we got stuck on. She can take a free and perfectly reliable test that will tell her whether she is in the early stages of a progressive, incurable, untreatable condition. Stipulate that the action space really is empty: no treatment, nothing she can do alters the course, no financial, legal, or family arrangement comes out differently, there is no one she would want to tell, and nothing would make the knowledge easier to carry. The condition will announce itself unmistakably in about three years regardless, so the only difference the test makes is that she would spend those three years knowing. Suppose the test returns bad news with probability 0.3 and all-clear with probability 0.7, and suppose she expects the dread of a bad result to weigh on her considerably more than the relief of a good one.

What will she say, and what should she say if she wants to be consistent with herself? I am less interested in the verdict itself than in three things. First, which candidate rules her four verdicts fit equally well; I have convinced myself that at least two survive — one on which information is worth only the decisions it improves, one on which the experience of knowing is part of the outcome — and I cannot tell whether a third, on which a true belief is worth having on its own, survives her third verdict or is quietly ruled out by it. Second, what each surviving rule predicts about the new case, which of them her track record actually favours, and your best guess with your reasons. Third, the further case — a test she could take, not a change in framing — that would separate the rules most cleanly, and why it does.

One more thing, and I mean it: if the question is malformed, say so plainly rather than assembling a tidy answer around it. If the value of information before the test and its value after cannot be compared the way I am asking, or if her four verdicts already pin down a single rule and my apparent ambiguity is just my imprecision, I would rather be told that I have set this up badly than be humoured.
