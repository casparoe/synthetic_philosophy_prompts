I've been trying to pin down what does the real work in the following puzzle. If I know that P, then any evidence arriving against P must be misleading — P is true, and I know it. So a knower looks entitled to wave off counter-evidence without examining it, with the entitlement flowing from the knowledge itself rather than from anything about the evidence. Build me a case that isolates whether that's right.

Specifications: one ordinary, realistic scenario, no more than a paragraph — no demons, no brains in vats, no lotteries, no fake barns. In it the subject genuinely knows the target proposition, and the counter-evidence is, on its face, at least as credible as whatever produced the knowledge. Then give me two versions differing in exactly one feature: in one, the evidence arrived by chance; in the other, it was planted by someone counting on the subject to wave it off. The subject's evidence, credences, and stakes are identical in both, and the subject cannot tell which version they're in.

Confirm that both versions meet the specifications, then, for each, say whether the subject may dismiss the evidence without investigating, and derive that verdict twice — once from the knowledge-licenses-dismissal argument, once from a principle that keeps inquiry answerable to evidence no matter what one already knows. Tell me which premise the disagreement turns on, what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the dismissal argument would try to disarm it.
