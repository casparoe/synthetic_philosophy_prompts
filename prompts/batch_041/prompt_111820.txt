My brother and I live in the same small town, and for about eighteen months we have been on opposite sides of a fight over a proposed gravel quarry on the ridge above us. The local paper has covered the dispute in a way we both find suspicious — the publisher's family has done business with the developer, and the paper has never once run the health studies the opponents keep citing. My brother noticed this first, and he was right to notice it. But he has since moved into an online group of a few hundred residents that has become, for him, the place where the issue lives; he reads it every morning before work. He now calls me with things I can neither verify nor refute — that the county health department is sitting on a report, that a particular council member's brother-in-law is on the developer's payroll — and when I ask how he knows, the answer is always the group. What troubles me is less the claims themselves than what has happened to the way he handles them. He used to be the most careful person I knew: he read the underlying documents, and he would say plainly that he did not know when he did not know. Now he tells me he has stopped bothering, because the group has already settled who can be trusted and he sees no point in checking behind them.

I can see two different things going on, and I cannot tell whether the difference matters for what I should do. The first is that he no longer encounters the other side at all: the people who think the quarry is fine, or who think the opponents are exaggerating, have mostly given up arguing in the group, so he simply never hears from them. The second is stranger. When he does encounter them, he does not weigh what they say, because he has already been handed a reason to discount it in advance — they are on the payroll, or they are naive, or they are just repeating the paper. I keep feeling that these are two distinct problems and that the second is the worse one, but I cannot say exactly why, particularly since in his case the distrust appears to have been earned: the paper really was compromised, and the people who told him so really were telling the truth that time. So I would like you to diagnose what has happened to him, and to say whether the well-foundedness of his distrust changes the diagnosis; whether my own position — I still read the paper, I still assume the council is muddling through in good faith — is any less of a trap than his; and, a question that nags at me, whether it would make any difference to your answer if it turned out that most of what the group believes is true. Does being right rescue him? Then, practically: what do I say to him? I have tried arguing the claims one by one and it goes nowhere, and I am not willing to be the brother who tells him to trust the institutions that let him down.
