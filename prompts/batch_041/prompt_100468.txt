In my school's philosophy club, our advisor recently posed the question of whether "better than" is transitive, and two of our sharpest members, Hana and Theo, ended up in an argument that I have been unable to referee on my own. I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than simply declare a winner, since I suspect that each of them is right about something and neither has noticed what the other is actually entitled to.

Here is how the exchange went.

Theo opened by saying that transitivity is simply part of what "better than" means. If A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C; there is no more room for A to come out worse than C than there is for a person to be taller than someone who is taller than someone taller than themselves. Any "spectrum" that appears to show otherwise, he said, must contain a mistake.

Hana said she had constructed a spectrum with no obvious mistake in it. Consider a possible life for a single person: one hundred years, every one of them intensely good. Now consider a second life: one year longer, and every year very slightly less good — less by so little that, were you to live either one, you could not tell them apart. Continue this way for a thousand steps, so that the final life is enormously long and every one of its years is only barely worth living. Each step, she argued, seems at least as good as the one before it: the added time is time worth living, and the small dip in quality is too small to register. Yet the last life in the series seems clearly worse than the first. Transitivity would force the last to be better than the first. So, she concluded, either one of the adjacent steps is mistaken, or transitivity fails.

Theo replied that this shows only that one of the steps is mistaken. Transitivity is not a substantive claim about goodness; it is a matter of logic, holding for any ordering whatsoever. The spectrum therefore cannot refute it, and must instead reveal that one of Hana's premises is false — most plausibly the premise that a drop in quality too small to notice is too small to matter. Admit that such a drop matters, he said, and the spectrum collapses.

Hana answered that this repair has a cost. If a difference no one could detect flips the verdict, then comparisons between lives turn on differences beneath the threshold of any possible experience, which she finds absurd. She added that the spectrum can be rebuilt using only "not worse than" instead of "better than," and that "not worse than" appears transitive as well, so Theo has not escaped. She also wondered whether "better than" might be vague in the way "heap" is, and whether transitivity even applies cleanly to a vague relation.

Theo said vagueness cannot be doing the work here. Unlike "heap," "better than" is a comparative that admits degrees, and if we permit it to be intransitive we license a kind of exploitation: someone can move us around the cycle, each move leaving us worse off, while defending every move as an improvement. Whatever we conclude about spectrums, that consequence must be avoided.

Hana replied that the exploitation argument concerns choices made over time and assumes our preferences remain fixed, whereas her spectrum compares whole lives at once; she thinks betterness might be intransitive without anyone being able to profit from us.

Please rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain briefly what each party is missing, and state what the spectrum actually establishes. If the correct answer depends on a premise that neither of them has stated, identify that premise. Then finish with a single final line in exactly the following form — \answer{...} — where the braces contain one short phrase chosen from: "transitivity must go," "a spectrum premise must go," or "the endpoint judgment must go."
