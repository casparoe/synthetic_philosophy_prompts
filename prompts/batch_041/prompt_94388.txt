I am a retired experimental scientist—thirty-one years at a national laboratory—who has spent the last several years reading moral and political philosophy with the habits of a metrologist: isolate the variable, distrust the unmeasurable, and ask what would count as an observation. I have been occupied lately with Pascal's treatment of justice and force: his observation that justice without force is mocked because the wicked are always with us, that force without justice is accused, and that the remedy is somehow to align the two—to give justice the means of enforcement and to give force the color of right. What holds my attention is not the epigram but the design problem inside it. I would like you to write a satirical piece in the form of an internal feasibility memorandum from a fictional body—call it the Office of Normative Engineering—addressed to a director who has asked whether the office can, in a limited pilot, couple justice and force in a mid-sized municipality. The memo should be deadpan, bureaucratic, and formally polite, roughly 800 to 1,000 words, organized in numbered sections, and should never break character or wink at the reader. The target is not Pascal. It is the recurring fantasy that legitimacy can be engineered from the outside—that one can either arm justice or justify force and thereby close the gap, without attending to the ordinary mechanisms (custom, imagination, self-love, the sheer fact of the multitude) by which power is already made to seem legitimate. The humor should come from taking the project seriously as a technical design and discovering, in its details, why it fails. To keep the piece from becoming a rehearsal of familiar arguments, build it around an invented apparatus: a proposed legitimacy transfer function with hysteresis, a tyranny-impotence Pareto frontier, and a pilot requirement that every coercive act carry a justice-provenance certificate. You may use Pascal's own materials—the thought that custom passes for equity simply because it is established, the incommensurability of the orders of body, mind, and charity, the role of imagination in securing obedience—but treat them as raw material for the invented apparatus, not as authorities to cite. Let the memo's own logic generate the jokes: an appendix on calibration, a section on failure modes, a recommendation that follows the reasoning to a conclusion the reader will find uncomfortable. A reader who knows Pascal should recognize that the piece understands the real difficulty; a reader who does not should still be able to follow what is happening. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to actual scholarship. Write only the memorandum.
