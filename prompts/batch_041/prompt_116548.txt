I am setting the take-home final for an upper-level seminar on the ethics of the far future, and I want a single essay question on whether the simulation argument dampens fanaticism about the far future — where fanaticism means the willingness to accept large present costs for a tiny probability of astronomically large future value, in the spirit of Pascal's mugging. The question must not be answerable by reciting the simulation trilemma and shrugging that we might be simulated. It should force students to prise apart two things that are usually run together: whether the argument is a claim about the size of the far future, lowering the probability that any very large future is real, or a claim about the identity of the far future, relocating where the value lies rather than shrinking it; and whether fanaticism is best understood as a decision-theoretic vice or as a substantive thesis about what matters. Give the question a determinate thesis to adjudicate rather than an open invitation to discuss. Please supply the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words that commits to a verdict and names the crux, and a 25-mark scheme that lists the moves an A answer must make — including one serious argument for the opposite verdict that a strong answer has to engage — along with the specific errors that should cost marks, such as treating the three disjuncts as having identical implications or conflating the probability of being simulated with the probability that the far future is large.
