I'm a novelist, third book due in the fall — historical, 1830s England, my protagonist kills a man over a hand of cards and then has to live in the same county as the widow — and I've been going around in circles on the moral-history stuff for about two years now, arguing with my writers' group and with a philosopher friend who keeps sending me essays I half understand. I want to lay out what I actually beleive and have someone tell me whether it hangs together, because I've started to suspect it doesn't and I can't tell which part is doing the damage. Here's where I've landed. (1) Moral progress is real — the end of slavery, the end of duelling, these were improvements and not just changes in fashion; I'm not a relativist, slavery was wrong in 1750 too, not "wrong for us." (2) But arguments did almost none of the work. The case was made in print, over and over, generations before anything happened, and people went on duelling and went on trading human beings; what actually ended these things is that they stopped being honorable — you couldn't look your friends in the eye and admit to it anymore. Money did some of the quiet work as well, I'm not naive about that. Shame and standing, not syllogisms. (3) Which is why, in the fiction, I don't argue with the reader. I try to make them frightened of being the kind of person who does X — I put the contempt in the room and let them feel where it would land. That's my entire toolkit and I'm not apologetic about it. (4) At the same time I'd never hold a moral opinion I couldn't give reasons for. If you show me my reason is bad I have to drop the view, no exceptions, and I don't think I'm entitled to a position I can't defend. (5) And I've always believed you're only to blame for what you had a fair shot at knowing was wrong — moral ignorance has to excuse, it's the only thing standing between me and hating every peasant in every novel I've ever read. (6) Except I also believe my duelist is genuinely to blame, that the reader should feel it in their gut, and that he isn't a product of his time, he's a coward with a pistol who could have walked away.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and which one has to go? Rank the conflicts by how bad they are, tell me the cheapest way out of each and what that fix would cost me, and please don't just say they're all in tension — I'm fairly sure some of them sit together fine and I want to know which ones those are. And if there's one single revision that would clean up most of the mess, tell me which one, because I've got another 300 pages to write and I'd like to not be a hypocrite for all of them.
