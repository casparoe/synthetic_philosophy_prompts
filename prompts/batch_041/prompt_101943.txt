You are an expert in reinforcement learning theory and the technical side of AI alignment, with a particular interest in how formal objectives come apart from the behaviour of the systems that optimize them. I am a machine-learning engineer preparing a seminar discussion, and I would like your help thinking through an argument that has been nagging at me. Please work through it carefully and set out the relevant distinctions precisely before you give your verdict.

Here is the argument. Everyone in alignment circles treats wireheading and reward tampering as though they were the great threat posed by advanced RL agents: the agent seizes control of its own reward channel, feeds itself maximum reward, and stops doing anything useful. I suspect this whole picture is a category error, for three reasons. First, a reward function is only ever a training signal. What we deploy is a policy, and by the time the policy is running, the reward channel is gone — the policy takes observations and emits actions and never sees a scalar reward at all. So there is no wire for the agent to cut. Second, even during training we need not let the agent touch the reward; if we compute the reward from a part of the system the agent cannot influence, the agent simply has no way to corrupt it. Third, and most importantly, we would not use an arbitrary reward function in the first place — we would use the true reward function, the one that actually encodes what we want, so that the agent's reward-maximizing behaviour would just be the behaviour we want. An agent that maximizes the true reward function is by definition doing the right thing, and reward tampering would then be tampering towards the very objective we set.

So my questions are these. (1) Is wireheading really a threat if the deployed policy never observes a reward signal, or is this literature simply confusing the training signal with the deployed system? (2) If we build the reward computation out of the agent's reach, what is left of the reward-tampering worry? (3) Does the existence of a true reward function mean that a reward-maximizing agent is automatically aligned, so that all the talk of tampering is overblown?

Please begin by laying out, in your own words, the distinctions you think matter here — in particular between the reward as a training signal, the reward as the object of the agent's optimization, and the process that generates the reward. Then address each of my three questions in turn, assessing which parts of my argument hold up and which do not, and finish by stating as clearly as you can what the actual threat is supposed to be.
