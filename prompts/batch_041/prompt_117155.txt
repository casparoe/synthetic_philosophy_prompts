I present a small interview podcast for graduate students in philosophy, and I am preparing the questions for an upcoming episode. My guest is a philosopher of mind who has spent several years defending a deliberately deflationary reading of two celebrated inversions of reasoning. Her position, in outline, is as follows. We are strongly tempted to explain any impressive competence by positing an agent who exercises it knowingly: the appearance of design is explained by a designer's intention, the reliability of arithmetic by a mathematician's grasp of number. Darwin and Turing each reverse the order of explanation. A process of blind variation with retention can yield what looks like design even though nothing in the process intends anything; a mechanical procedure for shuffling marks according to rules can yield correct sums even though nothing in the procedure grasps arithmetic. She maintains that these are not two analogies but a single argument-schema, and that the schema is far more general than either of its famous instances: wherever a competence tempts us to posit a comprehender, we may hope to exhibit a process that exercises the competence without comprehending it — competence without comprehension, as she puts it. She goes further. She holds that the schema reaches the normative case as well: that correctness, warrant and rationality can be produced by processes no one is in a position to survey, so that there can be norms without anyone who appreciates them. And she concludes that comprehension is not a natural kind at all, but merely competence of a higher order — competence exercised upon a system's own competences.

I should like eight to ten interview questions that press this position rather than invite her to restate it. Please include at least one question on each of the following, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give:

(i) whether the schema is a genuine unification or a merely verbal generalisation — what, apart from the absence of a comprehender, do variation-with-retention and rule-governed computation really have in common;

(ii) the regress — whether the schema explains normativity or merely presupposes it, given that the sub-processes must themselves be correct in some sense if the whole is to work, and that the schema appears to leave that correctness unexplained;

(iii) her extension of the schema to norms without norm-appreciators — if nothing is in a position to recognise a mistake, in what sense is there a mistake;

(iv) her deflationary conclusion — what she takes to distinguish a system that genuinely understands from one that merely behaves as though it does, in terms that do not presuppose the very distinction she denies.

I also want one question that turns the schema upon her own theorising: if the schema holds, her arriving at it was itself a competence produced without comprehension, and I should like to see her accept or resist that consequence on the record. Please close with a question about what she would accept as evidence against the schema. Order the questions so that the pressure accumulates, and so that she cannot repeatedly retreat to the thought that the schema is a heuristic rather than a thesis. Assume no special knowledge on the part of the audience: the questions should be intelligible to a bright graduate student who has not read the literature, and should not depend on any particular text. Do not ask for, or supply, reading lists or citations, and avoid questions about her career or how she came to the topic.
