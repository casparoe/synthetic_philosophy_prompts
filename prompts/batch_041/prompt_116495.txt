I am applying for a three-month seed grant from a small interdisciplinary research centre that supports work at the boundary of economics and philosophy, and I need a proposal of about 1,500 words for a working paper. My half-formed idea concerns the common prior assumption. That assumption is standardly defended in one of two registers. In the first, it is treated as a requirement of rationality: two agents who reason well cannot simply start out from divergent beliefs, so any residual disagreement must be traceable to a difference in information. In the second, it is treated as a modelling convention, valued because it buys determinacy, keeps the no-trade results in place, and stops Bayesian games from being swamped by equilibria. I suspect these are two quite different jobs, and that the second can be discharged without the first. What I want to investigate is whether there is a weakening of the assumption that still delivers the determinacy and no-trade results on the events that actually matter, while permitting agents who are fully rational, and who commonly know one another's posteriors, to go on disagreeing. My suspicion is that the results said to depend on the common prior really depend on something else — on the agents sharing a state space, that is, on a common algebra rather than a common prior — and that once the two are prised apart the epistemic argument for the assumption loses most of its force, or turns out to be an argument for a different assumption altogether. I may well be wrong. A clean no-go result, showing that every weakening either collapses into the full assumption or is vacuous, would be just as interesting, and I would rather learn that in three months than in three years.

Please turn this into a proposal. It needs a precise statement of the question — sharp enough that it has a determinate answer one way or the other — together with an account of why the existing positions leave it open rather than settling it; the approach I would take and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives; what I would actually do, week by week, across a three-month window; what a result would look like both if the separation goes through and if it does not, and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of the risks, including the point at which the formal work is likely to balloon and what I would cut if it did. Take the formal setting to be the simplest one that can carry the argument — a finite state space, two agents, partitional information, priors over states — and say explicitly what I give up by staying there.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add the references myself, and I would rather you refer to positions and arguments by description than guess at titles or dates. Write the proposal in British English, in a formal register, as a document I could send without rewriting.
