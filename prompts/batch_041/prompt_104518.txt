I've been in a long-running argument in a group chat that began with the old rationalist complaint that intuition pumps are arguments disguised as stories, and I want to turn it into a blog post. The post will be a tier list of six intuition pumps—some I've seen in seminar rooms, some I've built for this exercise—ranked by how much epistemic weight a careful philosopher should give the verdict each pump invites when that verdict is used as a premise in a larger argument. I'm not asking which pumps are famous or which intuitions are widely shared. Please sort the six into tiers (you can use S/A/B/C/D/F or a scheme of your own) and give each a 0–10 rating on that dimension. To make the ratings comparable, use a rubric with at least these axes and say how you weighted them: (i) whether the pump isolates a genuine conceptual distinction or merely relabels a conclusion; (ii) whether the invited verdict survives reversing the framing or swapping incidental features; (iii) whether the pump's target is a concept, a theory, or a psychological tendency; and (iv) whether the pump has an independent argumentative role or is doing all its work through vividness. For each pump, also label it as primarily an intuition generator, an argumentative shortcut, or a lens that changes what we notice, and say whether that label changes the rating. If a pump is better treated as a lens than as evidence for a proposition, rate it accordingly and explain the distinction.

The six are: (1) The Confession Booth: a reliable process tells you that one of your central beliefs was formed by a process it independently identifies as unreliable, but you cannot remember which belief; does the mere fact that some belief is compromised defeat all of them? (2) The Aesthetic Taster: a critic whose taste you have found reliable says a novel is cold but profound; you read it and feel only coldness; does the critic's testimony give you reason to believe the profundity is there but unavailable to you, or only reason to suspect your sensibility? (3) The Transcript: a scientist records every verbal report you make about a red patch, plus every behavioral and neural correlate, and builds a complete third-person model; she claims nothing is left out, while you insist something is; is the pump begging the question against the first-person, or making a genuine conceptual point? (4) The Sorting Hat: a political hat assigns you at random to a coalition, and you then adopt its full policy bundle; over time the bundle becomes internally coherent and socially rewarding; does that make the left/right distinction real, or only self-reinforcing? (5) The Two Doors: your brain is divided, each half is transplanted into a body, and you are told one door leads to the left-hemisphere body and one to the right; you must choose; is the choice arbitrary, or is there a fact about which is you? (6) The Pump That Pumps Itself: a thought experiment makes you intuit that thought experiments are unreliable because they trade on vivid particulars; does the pump undermine itself, or can it consistently exempt itself?

Finally, please be explicit about which parts of your assessment are relatively settled among philosophers who work on thought experiments and which are live disputes—for example, whether intuitions are evidence at all, whether framing effects show unreliability, and whether thought experiments are arguments in disguise. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. I would rather have a rating with an honest confidence interval than a confident ranking that pretends the methodology is settled.
