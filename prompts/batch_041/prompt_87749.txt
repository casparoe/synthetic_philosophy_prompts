My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about reasons. She holds that you have a reason to do something only if it is the sort of thing that could actually move you — that a "reason" which leaves you entirely cold is just a dressed-up way of scolding you. I hold that some things are reason-giving whether or not you care about them, and that saying otherwise lets people off far too easily. We got nowhere, and I suspect we are assigning the word to two different jobs: one explains and predicts what a person does, the other tells them what to do.

Write me a short story that puts that dispute under real pressure instead of illustrating either side. I want two siblings: one who, after a long illness, comes to care intensely about something she had previously found meaningless — a derelict orchard, a dying language, a local archive, whichever fits — and a brother who insists she always had reason to care, so that the caring is a discovery rather than an invention. She insists it is the reverse. Give me a third character who is invited to care about the same thing and simply cannot be moved, and give me one scene in which someone tries to persuade her and succeeds, so that the reader cannot tell whether the persuasion created a new motive or made a latent one visible. The best moment should be one where she says "I have no reason to" and he says "you do," and both sound true in their mouths. No philosophers, no lectures, and avoid the words internal and external entirely. About 1,500 words, and leave me genuinely unsure which of us was right.
