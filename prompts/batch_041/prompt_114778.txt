I am in the sixth form and have been asked to write a poem for the last meeting of our philosophy club's term, which has been spent on the two-envelope paradox, and I would like the poem to enact the argument rather than summarise it. Please write it in two halves that mirror each other: the second half should make the same case as the first with the two envelopes exchanged throughout, so that the identical reasoning — the envelopes have been filled, one holding twice what the other holds, I have taken one, and whatever sum is in my hand the other holds either twice as much as mine or half as much, each equally likely, so on average the other is worth more — tells me to swap whichever envelope I happen to be holding, in the same words each time. The closing lines should let that mirroring expose the fault: the argument settles nothing about how the two sums were chosen, and a reason that would serve equally well whatever I found in my hand cannot be trusted for any particular sum. I would like the poem to reach that point without stating it as a moral. About twenty lines of blank verse, spoken in the first person, with none of the apparatus named inside the poem — a reader who knows the puzzle should recognise the fault line, and a reader who does not should still find it worth reading.
