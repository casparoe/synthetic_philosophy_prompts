I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of technology, and this term the material became personal: I have just been appointed to my hospital's technology review committee, which is weighing a proposal to add a mandatory waiting period and a second-approver step to an automated prescribing tool. Half the committee treats this as a neutral workflow tweak with no political content; the other half says the tool was already making a political decision before anyone touched the workflow, and that the new step merely makes it visible. I need a final-exam essay question that forces students past reciting the stock debate over whether artifacts have politics and makes them do the analytic work themselves.

Please act as an experienced examiner and philosopher of technology. Start by brainstorming the distinct senses in which a design feature might be called political, then settle on the one or two that make the question sharp rather than merely surveyable.

I want a single essay question, answerable in about 60 minutes, that asks students to separate three readings of the claim that an artifact has politics: (i) an expressive reading, on which the design encodes or expresses a value; (ii) a causal reading, on which the design produces or stabilizes a distribution of power; and (iii) a constitutive reading, on which the design partly constitutes the political relation rather than merely expressing or causing it. The question should require students to say which reading, if any, the waiting-period proposal satisfies, and to say how one would tell the readings apart in practice — for instance, what evidence would show that a design is doing constitutive work rather than merely expressing a politics already settled elsewhere.

Then give me: the question; a model answer of roughly 700 words; and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific moves that should cap an answer at a lower band, noting for each must-hit point the way students typically get it wrong. Students will have studied the standard literature on technology and politics, so the marks should reward applying and interrogating the distinctions, not naming who said what; no citations are needed.
