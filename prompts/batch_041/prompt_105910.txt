I am a graduate student in philosophy who has been sitting in on a reading group about the ethics of promising, and one of the regulars has a habit of ending arguments with a proverb: a promise is a promise. The most recent occasion was my question whether a promise can ever be outweighed by the good that breaking it would do — I had in mind a small commitment whose keeping would block something urgent — and he said the four words and considered the matter settled. Within the same week, though, a second member used the same words, with different emphasis, to mean that a promisor cannot unilaterally rewrite the terms of what was promised, and a third used them to mean that once the words are out of your mouth the matter is no longer yours to decide. I suspect the sentence is doing at least three different jobs, that one of them is a tautology and another is false, and that the rhetorical force of the proverb depends on which job the hearer silently supplies.

What I would like is a menu rather than a verdict: the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, separated cleanly, each stated in a way that makes its commitments explicit, with a verdict attached — trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive and dubious, or simply false. I would find it useful if the readings were sorted along the axes that seem to generate the differences: what is supposed to ground the obligation (the act of promising, the practice or convention, the promisee's reliance, the promisor's integrity); what kind of reason the promise gives (a reason to be weighed against others, or one that displaces some of the weighing altogether); how much weight it carries; who holds the corresponding claim and who is wronged by a breach; how far the obligation reaches (does the promisee's release extinguish it, and does it survive the promisee's death or forgetting?); and whether the content of the promise is fixed at the moment of utterance or open to reinterpretation as circumstances change.

Please also say, for each reading, whether it would actually license the use my colleague made of the sentence — shutting down the question of whether a promise may be overridden — whether it would only appear to, and which reading ordinary speakers most likely intend when they say it. If any reading turns on a position in the literature, state the position in your own words rather than gesturing at a source; I will supply names and citations myself.
