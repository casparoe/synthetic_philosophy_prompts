A small foundation is about to commit its entire ten-year budget to one of two portfolios. The first funds near-term health and education programs whose beneficiaries are identifiable and whose benefits will be realized within the lifetimes of people now living. The second funds work intended to lower the risk of civilizational collapse or human extinction, on the theory that a surviving civilization would eventually contain an astronomically large number of people living lives worth living. A trustee has raised a methodological objection. She argues that the case for the second portfolio tacitly depends on an unexamined assumption about how each of us should reason about the fact that we exist at all — in particular, on whether we treat ourselves as a random sample drawn from all observers who ever exist (the self-sampling assumption) or, instead, treat our own existence as evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist (the self-indication assumption). She adds that one of these assumptions, pushed hard, licenses an inference she regards as presumptuous: that we should be nearly certain of whichever cosmological hypothesis implies the greatest number of observers, even without performing the experiment that would settle the matter. On this basis she concludes that the foundation cannot responsibly choose the second portfolio until the dispute between the two assumptions is resolved.

Please set out the most important implications of the self-sampling versus self-indication distinction for this decision. I want to know which components of the longtermist case are invariant across the two assumptions and which depend on the choice; how the presumptuous inference bears on the credibility of each assumption; how the two assumptions interact with the doomsday-style reasoning that our position in history is unremarkable; and what the trustees should actually do in the meantime if the disagreement cannot be settled on the merits. Where an alleged implication turns out not to follow, say so plainly.
