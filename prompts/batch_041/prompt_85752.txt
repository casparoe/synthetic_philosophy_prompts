I have no philosophical training at all — I run a small board-game café and dropped out of school early — but something came up at our Tuesday games night that I have not been able to put down since, and I would like you to help me think it through properly.

One of the regulars brought in a set of four dice whose faces are numbered in odd ways, arranged so that the first beats the second more than half the time, the second beats the third, the third beats the fourth, and the fourth beats the first — a circle. Nobody at the table was confused for more than a minute, because you can just write out the thirty-six pairings for each matchup and count; once you have the tables, the circle is fully explained and there is nothing left to wonder about. But the same person then said that 'better than' is not transitive either, in general, and that the cases that matter are ones where no such table exists: a life with a mild recurring headache against a life with a little more of something else, a hundred slightly diminished lives against one much better life, that sort of chain, where each neighbouring pair is plainly an improvement and yet the two ends obviously are not ranked the way the chain insists they must be. My reaction was immediate and I don't trust it: that this is just a dice cycle with extra words. I would like to know whether that reaction is right.

So: take the philosophers' case that 'better than' fails transitivity — the chains of small improvements, and whatever they take that to establish, whether it is that our rankings are incoherent, or vague, or that some options are 'on a par' rather than better or worse — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually have, as faithfully as you can. That vocabulary is dice and their face distributions, pairwise matchups, 'beats with probability greater than one half', round-robin standings, expected values, and the move of scoring a die by its average roll so that everything lines up again in one clean order. I know this world well — I can do the thirty-six-cell tables in my head — but I have no philosophy whatsoever, so do not assume I will catch a term you have not defined.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of their argument come through the translation intact? Which parts survive only if I quietly bend the dice vocabulary — for instance if I have to pretend that every comparison is settled and measurable, when the whole point may be that it is not? Is there anything in their debate with no counterpart in the dice world at all, something about what we are required to do, or about what it means for a comparison to be unclear, that dice simply cannot say? And finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: if the translation loses something, is that because my vocabulary is too thin to hold a real phenomenon, or because their claim was less substantial than the dice cycle it resembles, and what looks like a residue is only the fog burning off?
