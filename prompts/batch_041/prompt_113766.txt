Write an essay on the sense in which history is contingent, taking as your intuitive starting point the old proverb in which a kingdom is lost for want of a horseshoe nail — the thought that a trivial local event can redirect the course of large-scale affairs. The essay should begin by separating three things that are habitually run together.

The first is indeterminism: the outcome was not fixed in advance by the prior state of the world. The second is deterministic chaos: the outcome was fixed in advance, but by a dynamics so sensitive to initial conditions that any finite precision in specifying the starting state is eventually swamped, so that no practically available description of the initial conditions could have told us what would happen, even though the process is perfectly law-governed. The third is path dependence: the outcome was fixed in advance and the dynamics need not be chaotic at all, but positive feedback — increasing returns, lock-in, self-reinforcing expectations — means that an early perturbation can select between two or more stable end states that were otherwise equally reachable, so that the eventual outcome is not proportional to the size of the perturbation that selected it.

Then reach and defend a verdict on two questions. First, which of the three, if any, best captures what historians are actually claiming when they say that a large outcome turned on something small? Does the answer vary by subject matter — say, between the outcome of a battle, the entrenchment of a technology, and the survival of a religious movement — and if so, what are the variables that make the difference? Second, does any of this help or hurt the project of counterfactual history?

I am interested in a specific tension and want you to address it squarely. Chaos appears to license the proverb, since it makes the smallest difference matter; but taken strictly it makes every antecedent equally indispensable, so that singling out one nail as the decisive one looks arbitrary rather than explanatory. And while chaos gives the counterfactual historian a determinate answer to what would have happened had the nail held, it also makes that answer unavailable to anyone who cannot specify the initial conditions to infinite precision — the counterfactual is true but unknowable. Path dependence looks like the mirror image: it makes some small events genuinely decisive, but only by giving up the sensitivity that made the proverb vivid in the first place.

Do not settle for the answer that it depends. If the right model varies by case, state the criterion that sorts the cases and defend it. Assume you have no access to any literature: argue from the concepts themselves, and neither cite nor invent sources.
