I am a novelist at work on a book set in revolutionary Paris, and I have written a passage of narration in the voice of a fictional pamphleteer — a man of no particular party who has watched events outrun every account given of them. The passage is meant to be difficult on purpose, since I want the reader to feel it saying more than one thing at once, and I would like your help deciding whether it actually holds together. Here it is:

Citizens, you have made a great noise, and the noise has become a thing that walks. Consider it well. In the beginning there were grievances, which are private, and words, which are public; and the words have eaten the grievances, as fire eats the wood and remains fire. Now the thing that walks is not what we intended, and not what our enemies feared, but a third thing, the child of both, which acknowledges neither. It will take our heads for having made it, and their heads for having resisted it, and when it has taken all the heads that can be taken it will go quiet, and men will say that the idea triumphed. But I tell you no idea triumphed; hunger triumphed, and hunger is not an idea, though it is very eloquent. And when the last of us is eaten, the eating will be called justice by those who are left, who will also be eaten in their turn.

What I want is not a paraphrase but a reading. Tell me what this paragraph commits itself to. It seems to me that it advances at least three claims that sit uneasily together: that the words men used have become an autonomous power that consumes the grievances that produced them; that what resulted was a third thing, neither intended by its makers nor feared by its enemies; and that what actually triumphed was hunger, which is not an idea. If all three are true, is the paragraph coherent, or is one of them doing the real work while the others provide rhetorical cover? I am especially interested in the last movement, where the speaker says the makers will be eaten first and the eating will afterward be called justice. I have the old notion that revolutions devour their children in mind, but I do not want to lean on it as an inherited slogan; I want to know what this speaker, in this paragraph, is actually asserting about cause and about time — whether he is predicting, diagnosing, or consoling himself. And I want to know whether the paragraph leaves room for sheer accident, or whether its imagery of eating secretly commits it to a necessity it never states.

Answer in American English, in prose, and stay close to the sentences in front of you rather than surveying the historiography. I am not looking for a reading list; I am looking for someone to read this over my shoulder and tell me where it is confused.
