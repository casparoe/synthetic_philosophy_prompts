You are a philosopher of mind and language with a specialist's grasp of the Kantian and post-Kantian tradition of transcendental argument — how these arguments are supposed to work, and where they are supposed to get their force. I would like you to reason carefully and, before settling on a verdict, briefly reconstruct the argument's logical skeleton and sketch the main fault lines you see; then give a sharp diagnosis rather than a survey.

I am in a seminar on the analytic reception of Kant, and a friend and I constructed the following argument to defend a broadly Kantian point about the categories. I now find it suspicious, because it appears to prove far too much. It runs:

(1) For any thought or experience to have content, it must be possible to identify and re-identify items as the same or as different across occasions.
(2) Re-identification presupposes enduring objects set within a rule-governed spatiotemporal order.
(3) Hence any being capable of thought must operate with a framework of enduring objects in a law-governed world.
(4) A framework that one must operate with, one cannot coherently call into doubt.
(5) Hence no thinker can coherently doubt that there are enduring objects in a law-governed world.
(6) But a framework that no thinker can coherently doubt must be true — there is no gap between what thought requires and how things are.
(7) So it is a necessary truth that there are enduring objects in a law-governed world, and every possible thinker must share this basic framework.
(8) Therefore conceptual revolutions that overturn our most basic categories are impossible; any alien intelligence, however different, must share our fundamental ontology; and all of this is knowable a priori.

I cannot accept (8). We have, it seems to me, overturned basic categories — we abandoned absolute simultaneity, we stopped treating Euclidean space as a precondition of thought, and it strikes me as parochial to insist that a radically different mind must carve the world into enduring objects at all. Yet each of (1) through (7) looked fine when we wrote it. So which step should I reject, and why did that step look fine? If the culprit is (6) — the move from what thought requires to how things are — what exactly do I lose, and does anything of the anti-skeptical force survive, or does the argument collapse into the modest claim that we merely cannot help believing our framework? If instead I keep (6) and cut earlier, at (2) or (3), what must I then say about re-identification, and what does that cost me? And if the real trouble is a slide in scope rather than a false premise, lay out the competing readings and tell me which of them the premises actually support. I would rather keep the anti-skeptical upshot than the alien-ontology conclusion, but I want to know what I am paying either way.
