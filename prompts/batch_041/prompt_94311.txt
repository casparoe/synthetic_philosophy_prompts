Jeremy Bentham argued, in an essay written around 1785 and left unpublished until 1978, that consensual same-sex acts between adults harm no one and that the law's horror of them is ungrounded antipathy; sexual pleasure enters his hedonic calculus as a good like any other, and he saw no warrant for legal interference where no one is pained. Extend that framework to three controversies he never confronted: the rule affirmed by the House of Lords in R v Brown (1993) that a person's consent is no defence to a charge of assault occasioning bodily harm in a sadomasochistic encounter; the legalisation and regulation of commercial sex, including whether contracts for sexual services should be enforceable; and proposals to have disability services fund or arrange sexual assistance for people with disabilities. What verdict does his utilitarianism deliver in each case, and which of his commitments does the work? Then press on the framework: does the disgust or alarm of third parties count as pain in his calculus, and if so, does his argument against criminalising same-sex acts survive? Finally, identify where his hedonic egalitarianism would push him away from contemporary liberal orthodoxy on sex, and say whether the divergence tells against him or against the consensus.
