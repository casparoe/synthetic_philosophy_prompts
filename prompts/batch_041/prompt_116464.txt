You are a philosopher of science and of mind with working knowledge of mechanistic interpretability — probing, sparse autoencoders, activation patching, causal tracing — and of the philosophical debates over content, representation, and scientific realism. Think carefully before writing, and argue from the arguments rather than from the literature: no citations, no reading lists, no claims about who has said what.

Write an essay on the following controversy. Interpretability work routinely reports that a trained network "represents" some feature, concept, or variable at a particular site. A realist takes such reports at face value: the network really has internal states with representational content. An instrumentalist takes them as compact descriptions of behaviour that earn their keep by enabling prediction and control, with no commitment to content. Defend a verdict.

To keep the discussion concrete, evaluate a criterion that a colleague has proposed (treat it as an unpublished hypothesis to test, not an established view). Call a network state a representation of feature F only if all three conditions hold: (1) F is recoverable from that state by a simple read-out a human can specify; (2) the state is causally recruited — intervening on it, not merely on the input, changes downstream behaviour in the way the representational claim predicts; (3) the state is invariant across training seeds, architectures, and read-out methods. Decide whether the conjunction is too strong, too weak, or about right, and whether condition (3) in particular should be relaxed when the object of study is one particular trained model rather than learning in general.

Structure the essay in exactly these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths: Setting the terms (200 words); The case for realism (400 words); The case against, and what instrumentalism must explain away (400 words); Testing the criterion (450 words); Verdict (250 words). In the Verdict, commit to a position rather than splitting the difference, and state what would have to change — empirically or conceptually — to move you off it. Do not end on a survey of both sides; the essay's value lies in the argument.
