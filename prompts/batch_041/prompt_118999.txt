I am a professor of philosophy, and on the strength of many years teaching aesthetics I have been asked to give the commencement address at the graduation of a small independent art school: painters, filmmakers, composers, a few writers, about forty graduates, in a hall that also holds their families, several of whom have never set foot in a gallery, and a faculty who have spent their lives on craft and who will hear every word I say about it. I have accepted on one condition, that I be allowed to speak honestly about art rather than to congratulate anyone, and the honest position is Tolstoy's in What Is Art? — the one I have never once managed to talk my way out of in a classroom. I take it as a claim about what art is, not as a mood. On that view, art is not the making of beautiful or interesting or admired objects; it is a human activity in which one person hands on to others a feeling he has himself lived through, so that they are infected by it and experience it themselves. Whether the infection takes hold depends on three things: that the feeling be genuinely the artist's own rather than borrowed, that it be expressed clearly, and above all that the artist be sincere, transmitting something actually felt rather than performing the feeling he is supposed to have. What fails this test is not bad art but a counterfeit of art, and Tolstoy names the standard methods of counterfeiting — borrowing, imitation, and the manufacture of striking effects — with the endless occupation of the audience, diversion, as a fourth. He also held that art which cannot be felt by ordinary people with no training is not difficult, elevated art but the private amusement of a privileged class; that the most celebrated art of his century and most of his own fiction failed his own test; and that the test of whether a thing is art at all is transmission rather than edification, so that a feeling may be vicious and still be the material of real art, while the further question of which art is good is settled by whether the feeling conveyed belongs to the moral perception of the age and can be shared by everyone. I want the address built on that, not decorated with it. Everything I praise must be praisable on those grounds. Originality, technical mastery, ambition, the sophistication of a graduate's influences, the demands a work makes on its viewer, the market that will or will not reward them — these are the standard currency of such occasions, and I do not want to spend them as though they were the real thing. At least one of them has to be demoted out loud, in a way the faculty in the third row will recognize as a demotion, without the parents in the tenth row hearing an insult.

The room gives me one hard case I cannot dodge. The work everyone at the school is talking about this year is a forty-minute film with no dialogue and no story, an original score, and, at the entrance to the gallery, a wall of text in the vocabulary of critical theory. The faculty are proud of it, its maker is in the audience, and the address has to say something about it that is true to the Tolstoyan standard without being a sneer. It also must not take the easy way out by reading that standard as a taste for simple, pleasant, accessible work: Tolstoy's complaint about obscurity is not that difficult art should have been easy, but that obscurity is often the sign that nothing is being transmitted, that an air of depth is being borrowed in place of a feeling actually had. That distinction has to survive.

Two further things. First, the address must be subject to its own argument: if it cannot make the grandparents in the back feel anything, then by its own lights it is not a mediocre address but not an address at all, a counterfeit of the occasion, and I would rather it admit that risk in the middle of itself than pretend the risk is not there. Second, the school has asked me to be brief and uncontroversial, and the retiring dean in the front row built this program on precisely what Tolstoy attacks. I am not willing to give a speech that anyone could have given; I am also not willing to use a graduation as a tribunal, so the address has to be honest about the institution without turning the day into an indictment of the people who have just been handed their degrees.

Write the address. Spoken register, about eight minutes, roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, for reading aloud from a lectern, so it must work in the ear the first time through. Name Tolstoy once and then stop explaining him; no quotations from him, no exposition of the book beyond what a listener needs in order to follow me, no citations, no critical-theory vocabulary, and no ending on a note of uplift that the position does not license — if the only honest charge I can give these graduates is a demanding one, give it. Write it in continuous prose, the way one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables.
