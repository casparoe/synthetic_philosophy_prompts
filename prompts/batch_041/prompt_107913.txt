I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of history class arguing that the usual split between presentist and contextualist history is a false dichotomy, and my teacher wants me to build a case of my own rather than borrow a real one. Please construct a thought experiment in which the two approaches reverse their usual verdicts: the contextualist historian, judging a past actor by the standards of the actor's own time, condemns the act, while the presentist, judging by present-day standards, excuses or even admires it. Constraints: the setting should be realistic but invented — don't rest the case on any real event or person, since I want it to stand on its own without historical facts — and avoid the stock examples (dueling, slavery, witch trials); the two historians must share all the evidence and agree on every non-evaluative fact; and the reversal must not come from the past society holding an absurd standard or from present standards being callously permissive, but from one structural difference between the two standard-sets, such as a difference over who or what counts as a right-holder, or over which description of the act is taken as the relevant one. Then state each verdict and show how it follows from the standards it uses, name the single feature doing the work and say what happens to the case if it is removed, and explain how a defender of the approach that comes off worse would argue that the case is rigged. The purpose of the case is to show that the contextualist verdict may be unavailable to a historian who does not share the past society's beliefs, so please say whether the case actually establishes that. Keep the scenario itself under 200 words.
