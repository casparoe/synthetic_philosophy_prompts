I am a junior in high school, and I am writing an independent-study paper for my AP World History teacher on how historians ought to treat non-human causes — epidemics, climate, volcanic eruptions — and I have been stuck for weeks on a disagreement between two people I have been talking with about it. One of them, a graduate student who came to speak to our seminar, argues that a volcano, a pathogen, or a shift in climate can be a genuine historical actor: he points to the eruption of Mount Tambora in April 1815 and the cold, crop-failing summer that followed across the Northern Hemisphere in 1816, and he says that the famine in Europe and the westward migration of New England farm families ought to be described as things the volcano did, not merely as things it happened to accompany, and that treating such events as mere background conditions is a kind of anthropocentrism. My classmate, who is the sharpest person in the seminar, thinks this is a category mistake dressed up as a metaphor: a volcano has no intentions, no choices, and no reasons, and she says that every time you look closely at a natural shock the outcome depends on the society it strikes — the same cold year that ruined New England's harvests seems to have produced no famine in Japan at all, and the same pandemic that is credited in England with raising laborers' wages and dissolving serfdom is credited elsewhere with very different results — so the real causal weight must lie in institutions, choices, and luck, with nature at most pulling a trigger that some society had already loaded. I have been calling the two roles the "spark" and the "tinder" — the event that sets a change going and the pre-existing condition that decides how far it burns — and they both claim to like the distinction while drawing opposite conclusions from it, which is part of why I think they are talking past each other. My teacher's suggestion was that I stop taking notes and instead write the document the two of them should have produced together.

Please write that document. It should open with a numbered list of claims both of them could sign without either feeling that anything important has been conceded — claims about what a causal explanation in history is supposed to accomplish, about what may and may not be inferred from the fact that the same natural event has different effects in different places, and about what it would even mean for a natural event to have "done" something — and I want these agreed claims to be substantive rather than truisms like "nature and society both matter." It should then state, in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own position, exactly where they still disagree, in a table if that is clearer. It should then identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them, and for each crux a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I have one candidate test that I would like used if it is good enough: whether the severity of a volcanic winter's effects across regions tracks the severity of the local climatic anomaly, or instead tracks how vulnerable each society already was; both of them said in principle that this comparison is fair, so I would like each side to say beforehand what pattern it expects to find. Finally, if part of their quarrel turns out to be about words — I suspect much of it is about whether "actor" must imply intention, and whether "determinism" is a consequence of a claim or merely a slur — please say which part is merely verbal, and what, if anything, remains of the disagreement once that part is set aside. Do not tell me who is right; I want something both of them could put their names to, and I would rather it be honest about the parts that cannot be resolved than tidy.
