I would like help predicting a colleague's verdict on a case she has not yet considered. She works in the contractualist tradition: on her stated view, an act is wrong just in case some principle that would permit the act could be reasonably rejected by a person who would have to live under that principle. She has never set out, in general terms, the criterion by which she decides when a person's rejection is reasonable, but over the years she has ruled on a series of hypothetical policies, and I have kept a record of her verdicts. In the first four, both the burden and the benefit are measured in days of one and the same painful but temporary condition.

1. A policy under which one person would endure five days of the condition so that each of two other people would avoid ten days of it. Her verdict: not wrong.

2. A policy under which one person would endure twenty days of the condition so that each of two other people would avoid one day of it. Her verdict: wrong.

3. A policy under which one person would endure ten days of the condition so that each of a thousand other people would avoid eleven days of it. Her verdict: not wrong.

4. A policy under which one person would endure five days of the condition so that each of ten thousand other people would avoid five days of it. Her verdict: not wrong. She added that the person who bears the cost has no complaint stronger than anyone else's.

5. A policy under which one person must submit to a brief and humiliating public ritual that causes no lasting harm, so that each of a thousand people would avoid thirty days of the condition. Her verdict: wrong. She remarked that the objection in this case is not to the amount of harm but to the kind of treatment.

The new case is a policy under which one person would suffer a permanent and disabling injury, the loss of the use of both hands, so that each of a very large number of people — put it at a million — would avoid a single day of the condition.

Please do the following.

First, state the rules that are jointly consistent with her five verdicts. I expect there to be more than one; I also want to know which of her verdicts, if any, genuinely forces a choice among the surviving rules rather than merely being compatible with all of them.

Second, for each surviving rule, derive the verdict it yields on the new case and explain the step that produces it.

Third, give your best guess at her actual verdict and at the rule you think she is running, with the reasons for preferring that rule and an honest statement of how confident the record lets you be.

Fourth, name the further case — one she has not yet been asked about — that would best discriminate among the surviving rules, and say which verdict on it would favor which rule.

I care less about which verdict is morally correct than about which one she is committed to.
