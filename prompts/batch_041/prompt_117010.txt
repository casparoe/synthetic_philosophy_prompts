I keep running into a move in AI-safety arguements where someone says we don't need to solve the whole value-specification problem: just make the system low-impact, or get it to "avoid side effects", or "leave the world as it found it", and it can't do much harm even if its goals are off. A colleague made it at me last week -- his line was that an agent with a strong side-effect penalty is "safe by construction", and that this is how you get alignment without an alignment target. I'm fairly sure that's too good to be true but I can't say exactly where it breaks.

So lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making. "Low impact" relative to what baseline -- the counterfactual where the agent does nothing, some default policy, the state of the world when it was switched on? Measured how -- atoms moved, what humans happen to prefer, the agent's own future options, something else? And is the thing being penalized the deviation from that baseline, or the value of the deviation? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, whether it's a substantive and defensible design goal, whether it's actually false, and -- the part I most care about -- whether it lets you define impact without first specifying what anyone values. If a reading only looks value-free because it smuggles in a proxy, like "whatever goals the agent might later have" or "whatever people currently care about", point at exactly where the value gets in. Then tell me which reading my colleague most likely meant, and whether that reading actually gets him his conclusion.

No headings, bullets, bold, or tables please -- just write it out in prose the way you'd explain it to me over coffee.
