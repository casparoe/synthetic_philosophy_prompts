I'm a novelist, which means I spend an unreasonable amount of time trying to work out why people do things that don't obviously pay off, and the book I'm writing now has a character who drives four hours to vote in an election that everybody knows is a foregone conclusion, and I genuinely can't decide if she's noble or a fool. My friend Marcus, who took a lot of economics in college and has never emotionally recovered, says the question was settled decades ago and I'm just being sentimental. He wrote the argument on the back of a menu, and I've copied it out more or less as he had it:

(1) You have reason to do something only if its expected benefit to you outweighs its expected cost. (2) The only benefit of voting is that your vote might actually decide the outcome. (3) In an election with a few million voters, the chance that one vote decides it is on the order of one in several million, probably worse. (4) Say the difference between your side winning and losing is worth a few thousand dollars to you personally -- be generous. (5) Then the expected benefit of voting comes to a fraction of a cent, while the cost -- the queue, the afternoon, the reading up -- is definately more than that. (6) So voting is a losing bet. (7) So you shouldn't do it. (8) And nothing in 1 through 7 is about voting in particular: the same arithmetic kills dropping a tenner in the charity tin at the checkout, sorting your recycling, turning up to the protest. Marcus says he's fine with all that, he's a cold-eyed realist, and if it makes my character a fool then my character is a fool. He also pre-empts the obvious reply -- before I could say that people *enjoy* voting, he said fine, then it's a hobby like birdwatching, and nobody thinks you're obligated to birdwatch, and 'duty' is just a word people use to dress up a hobby.

What I want is a proper diagnosis rather than a debate. I don't think the arithmetic is the problem; the arithmetic looks airtight to me, which is why I'm stuck. So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, because the whole book is people making choices under some version of that logic, and I'd also like to keep the sense that my character isn't simply fooling herself. But I'm braced for the possibility that I can't have both, so tell me honestly which premise a reasonable person gives up, why that premise looked so good in the first place, and what they're buying by giving it up -- including whether the argument really does prove too much, or whether the overreach is itself the tell.
