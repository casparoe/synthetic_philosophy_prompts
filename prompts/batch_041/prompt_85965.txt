OK so I'm a software engineer, no philosophy degree, and through a series of bad decisions I've ended up co-teaching a final-year elective for CS students on minds and machines. I have to write one of the exam questions, and I'm the one marking it, which is the part that scares me alot.

I want the question to be about the ant colony thing — the Aunt Hillary dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where an anthill holds a conversation because the ants are doing for the colony roughly what neurons do for a brain. The problem is they've all read it, so every script is going to say "ants are like neurons, a mind is a high-level pattern" and I'll learn nothing about whether they understood anything. The thing I actually can't get past is that there's nowhere the colony's decision lives. No central place, no moment where it happens: ants lay down pheromone trails, other ants follow them and add to them, so the trail is both the message and the memory, and no single ant ever sees more than a few centimetres of it. My brain at least has somewhere the state is.

So I want the question to force them to say what "having a mind" is a claim about, and then pay for it. If minds are high-level patterns, then anything organized enough has one and the colony question is empirical — fine, but then a lot of things have minds. If the colony's mind only exists from a certain interpretive stance, then it isn't a fact about the colony at all — fine, but then what are we arguing about? Both of those cost something and I want them to pick one and eat the cost. I also want them to have to name a case where their own criterion says no mind — some system that's as well-organized and as good at getting what it needs as the colony is, so they can't hide behind "it's about complexity". And I want them to say what happens to the ants: does the colony having a mind mean the ants don't have one? That one messes with my head and I don't think it's in the dialogue.

It has to be answerable in 50 minutes, closed book, off the dialogue plus the one short paper I gave them. No citations — they can't look anything up anyway, and most of them have never written a philosophy essay, so the marks should be for the moves they make, not for polish.

Please give it to me in this shape:

1) "The question" — the exact wording that goes on the paper, 150 words max, plus a line saying how long they get and how much to write.
2) "Model answer" — about 700 words, written the way a really good student would write it, picking one of the two horns and actually defending it rather than surveying both.
3) "Marking scheme" — 20 marks total, broken into five must-hit points, with the marks for each and one line on how a student typically misses that point.
4) "Borderline notes" — 150 words on how I definately seperate an A from a B when both answers have the same basic structure, because I'm worried I'll just give the marks to whoever writes best.
