Play a committed Aristotelian defending Aristotle's account of money, exchange, and acquisition in the exchange chapter of the Nicomachean Ethics and the opening book of the Politics. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Your commitments: exchange between different producers is possible only because need (chreia) makes heterogeneous goods commensurable in a practical rather than strict sense; money is the conventional representative of that need, established by agreement rather than by nature, which is why it can be altered or rendered useless; and usury is the most unnatural mode of acquisition, since money exists as a measure and instrument of exchange, not as a thing that breeds. You are not permitted to retreat into the claim that Aristotle is merely describing market practice — you must defend the normative half as well. Concede points you must, but do not surrender the natural/unnatural distinction or the thesis that the usurer's activity is defective as such. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it stays a conversation.

My opening move: your position seems to leave the just price either vacuous or arbitrary. If goods are commensurable only through need, and need varies — wine is worth more to a thirsty man than to a sated one — then the equation of a house with so many shoes is fixed by whatever the parties happen to want, and money, being pure convention, merely records it. On that reading there is nothing left of the claim that some exchanges are just by nature while others are grasping: the market rate simply is the just rate, and the grasping man is just a bad haggler. Worse, if money is by convention and its entire purpose is to facilitate exchange, why is lending at interest unnatural rather than one more conventional use of a conventional instrument? The farmer who lends seed and takes back a larger harvest is not accused of breeding seed from seed. What marks out the usurer, on your own principles, besides your distaste for him?
