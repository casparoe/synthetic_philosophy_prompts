For the past few weeks I have been attending an after-school seminar on the foundations of mathematics, and I have become badly stuck on the axiom of choice. I am in the eleventh grade and am thinking about applying to study mathematics at university, so I would rather understand this properly than memorize the official line. What we were told is that the axiom of choice is independent of the other standard axioms of set theory: Gödel showed that it cannot be refuted from them, and Cohen that it cannot be proved from them. We also read about the Banach–Tarski theorem, according to which a solid ball can be cut into finitely many pieces which can then be rearranged to form two solid balls, each the same size as the original. My difficulty lies in putting these two facts together. If both the axiom of choice and its negation are consistent with the remaining axioms, then it seems to me that there is simply no fact of the matter as to whether the axiom is true — much as there is no fact of the matter as to whether the parallel postulate is true in geometry, so that the axiom is really a convention which set theorists are free to adopt or to reject. And if that is right, then the Banach–Tarski theorem is not a discovery about balls at all but an artifact of a convention, and my friend is right to say that the pieces in the decomposition are fictions, since they are non-measurable and cannot be described or drawn.

What I cannot get past, however, is a more elementary worry about the theorem itself. If the pieces have no volume — and that, as I understand it, is what being non-measurable amounts to — then each piece has volume zero, and a finite sum of zeroes is zero, so the two reassembled balls ought to have volume zero, which is absurd. So either the pieces do have some volume, in which case volume is not preserved by the operation, or the axiom of choice is false after all, in which case the independence results cannot mean what I take them to mean. My friend proposes that we should simply work in the version of set theory without the axiom, on the grounds that the pathological sets would then not exist and nothing of value would be lost, since analysis only ever deals with sets that can be described. Could you tell me where my reasoning goes wrong? And, more generally, should I conclude that the axiom of choice is neither true nor false, but merely a useful convention that mathematicians have settled upon by something like a vote?
