I am a historian of technology who spent a decade studying the mechanization of textile production, and six weeks ago I took a job as the in-house historian at a forecasting institute that works on artificial intelligence. Next Thursday I give the opening talk at an internal workshop, and my thesis will be unwelcome to nearly everyone in the room: that the three analogies this building reaches for — the printing press, the Industrial Revolution, and nuclear weapons — are not competing answers to one question but answers to three different questions, and that the debate is muddled because nobody says which question they are asking. I distinguish the transition question (how abruptly the change arrives), the distribution question (who captures the gains and who is displaced), and the containment question (whether the thing can be governed once it exists). On my account the printing press speaks only to distribution, the Industrial Revolution only to transition, and nuclear weapons only to containment; each analogy turns actively misleading when carried over to the other two. In particular, the Industrial Revolution analogy, which this institute leans on to argue that transformation will be slow and absorptive, is being asked to do work it cannot do, because the absorption story depends on the new tasks created by the transition being tasks the displacing technology cannot itself perform — and the technology in question here is a general-purpose substitute for the very input, human cognitive labor, that is supposed to be reabsorbed into those new tasks. That is my argument in miniature, and I would like you to treat it as the target.

The audience is about thirty people. Roughly a dozen are economic historians and growth economists who know the British case in detail and are temperamentally allergic to claims that this time is different. Half a dozen are machine-learning researchers who reason in terms of scaling curves, compute, and benchmark progress. A few are policy staff who need a defensible line to take to funders and will not enjoy a talk that leaves the practical upshot undecided. And my new director will be there; he hired me precisely because he wants the historians' caution represented at the table, and he will be embarrassed if I alienate the economists at his first workshop.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get, in the order I will get them, each phrased the way the faction that holds it would phrase it and attributed to that faction. For each, tell me how much damage it does, what I should concede without surrendering the thesis, and a two-minute reply. Tell me which objection I cannot answer and should therefore pre-empt inside the talk itself, and which one I should decline to be drawn into. Then tell me the question I am hoping nobody in the room asks.
