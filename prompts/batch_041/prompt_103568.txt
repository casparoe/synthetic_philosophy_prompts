I lead a small modeling group at a regional fisheries agency. Every year we produce catch advice for a few dozen stocks, one analyst per stock, and a standing review panel signs off before the numbers go to the commission; for any given stock we have roughly three weeks, one analyst, and no one on the panel who works on foundations of statistics. Our recurring fight is model selection. For data-rich stocks we can fit age-structured models with dozens of parameters; for data-poor stocks we are reduced to surplus-production models or a biomass-trend fit with two or three knobs. "Occam's razor" gets invoked on both sides — one analyst says the complex model has more parameters than we have data to pay for, another says the simple model is a deliberate ignorance of known biology — and the panel ends up settling it by seniority and rhetorical stamina. I have read enough about Solomonoff's universal prior over programs, about Kolmogorov complexity, and about the minimum description length idea that a model is a code for the data to believe there is something real here, and not enough to use any of it myself. Please turn that material into a procedure my analysts can actually run, and be explicit that the translation is the hard part: keep what the theory actually says, drop what only matters in the limit, and mark what cannot survive the trip.

I want the deliverable as an ordered set of steps: what to compute or write down at each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it stops by handing the decision to a person. It has to survive the facts that make the theory awkward in practice — that Kolmogorov complexity is not computable and is defined only up to a constant set by the choice of reference machine; that this constant can be the whole story when a stock has five years of data; that the description length of a model depends on choices made before anyone fits anything, such as units, whether the survey index is logged, and which years count as the baseline; and that we can compute for a day or two per stock but cannot search over a space of models. In particular I want the procedure to force us to keep apart three things that our arguments always run together: how long the model's own description is, how long the data are given the model, and how much prior weight the model class gets relative to its rivals — and to say which of those we can honestly quantify and which we are inventing and should admit to inventing.

Then run it on three cases. First, a data-rich stock where the age-structured model fits visibly better in-sample but carries an order of magnitude more parameters, and where the panel will ask in writing why we did not use it. Second, a data-poor stock with a short catch series and one noisy survey, where I suspect the procedure will refuse to license the model we want; I want to see it say so, follow the refusal through to the catch advice it implies, and be clear about how much of the refusal is the theory and how much is our prior over reference machines. Third, a case where one of two candidate models is "simpler" only because the analyst binned the lengths or dropped a year of data, so that the simplicity is a feature of the pipeline rather than of the biology — I want to know whether the procedure catches that, and at which step. For each case give the verdict, the step that decided it, and a note on where the answer is a judgment call dressed up as a calculation. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do: which decisions about catch levels, and which questions about the stocks themselves, will still have to be made by people on grounds the theory does not supply.
