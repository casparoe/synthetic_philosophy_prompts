Three years into retirement from soil chemistry (don't ask), I've turned reading philosophy into a second job, and last month our little group did consciousness. One of the members -- sharp guy, philosophy masters before law school -- circulated his summary of illusionism, and it's well enough written that I half believe it, which is definately why I want it audited. His words, more or less:

"Illusionism is the view that phenomenal consciousness -- qualia, the felt quality of experience, 'what it's like' -- doesn't exist. Not that we aren't conscious: illusionists grant that we're awake and taking in information and saying 'ow'. What they deny is that there's any inner glow we're directly acquainted with; introspection represents our own states as if they had these special properties, and we then misdescribe that representation as a felt quality. So the illusion is real, there just isn't anything it's an illusion of. That's why the fruitful question isn't the hard problem but what some people call the meta-problem: why do creatures like us end up convinced we have phenomenal states? Get the mechanism and the hard problem goes limp. Now, since the illusionists are the heirs of the 80s eliminativists, they think the whole vocabulary of experience -- pain, seeing red -- is a fiction a mature science would drop; on this view we're basically zombies who believe we're conscious. And the illusion is a kind of bad theory we've inherited: we absorb a folk picture of an inner theatre as children and then mistake it for something introspection is showing us, which is why it would be odd to say a dog or a baby is in on the trick. Really the fight with the qualia crowd is verbal -- both sides agree about the science and differ over whether to keep a word, like whether Pluto is a planet -- and since the objection that an illusion has to seem like something, and seeming is itself phenomenal, is self-refuting, the whole position is a non-starter. Which is why it's still a minority view, though a growing one, and mostly held by people who enjoy being provocative."

So: where's it fair and where's it off? Go claim by claim -- what a serious illusionist signs without blinking, what's a harmless simplification, what they'd call a straight misrepresentation. And three things I keep circling, so be concrete.

First, my working life was one "X is really just Y" move after another -- no vital force, no phlogiston, fire is just oxidation -- and those mostly turned out right. Is "phenomenal properties are the phlogiston of the mind" a fair analogy, or does it sneak in something the chemistry cases never had?

Second, my wife had a miserable time at the dentist last week and it's bugging me alot: if illusionism is true, was she not really suffering? Or is the question rigged before it starts?

Third, tell me whether this is the same view or a different one, because I genuinely can't tell. Suppose the brain does represent its own states as having a felt glow, but that representation isn't a mistake -- "phenomenal" just turns out to be our word for that kind of self-representation, and there's nothing further to be wrong about. Have I restated illusionism, or have I quietly reinvented the thing illusionists deny?

No citations or reading lists please, I'll never get to them; I just want to know which parts of his summary to trust and what the view actually commits you to.
