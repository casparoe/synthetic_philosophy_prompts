I'm a postdoc in philosophy of science, and in three weeks I give a work-in-progress talk to the Thursday seminar my department runs jointly with the statistics group. The argument is settled and the slides are nearly done; what I actually need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, so please don't spend effort improving the paper itself. The thesis is that the problem of verisimilitude -- what it takes for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another -- has been misdiagnosed for fifty years as a problem about truth, when it is really a problem about questions. The history as I tell it in the first ten minutes: Popper defined the truth content of a theory as the set of its true consequences and the falsity content as the set of its false ones, and proposed that A is closer to the truth than B when A's truth content exceeds B's while A's falsity content does not; in 1974 three philosophers published, in the same issue of the same journal, independent proofs that this definition entails that no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false theory, which is exactly the verdict the account existed to avoid, since we want to say that Newton is nearer the truth than Aristotle without either of them being true. The literature since has split into two repair strategies -- measures that treat truthlikeness as an average likeness between the actual world and the worlds in which the theory is true, and measures that filter the consequences for relevance so that the trivialisation cannot get a grip -- and I argue that both inherit one defect: they compare theories against the whole language instead of against a question. My alternative relativises the comparison. Fix a question, represent it as a partition of logical space into mutually exclusive answers, put a distance on the answers, and rank theories by their expected closeness to the correct cell; for probabilistic theories this reduces to evaluating a proper scoring rule at the true answer, so the Brier score and the classical truthlikeness measures come out as instances of a single construction, and the choice of partition and distance stops looking like an embarrassment and starts looking like precisely what scientists are arguing about when they argue about which model is better.

The audience is what worries me, and it splits four ways. Three or four working Bayesians from the statistics side will be there; they are impatient with closeness to truth as a primitive and will want to know why I do not simply talk about posterior probability, or expected log score, or expected accuracy, and they will hear any talk of distance between theories as a parameterisation in disguise. One emeritus colleague is a genuine Popper scholar and a falsificationist of the old school, for whom the growth of knowledge was never supposed to be a quantitative affair at all; he will read the whole line as an attempt to smuggle inductive confirmation back in through the side door. Two scientific realists will press me on whether question-relativity makes progress interest-relative, and if it does, whether the account can still explain why the move from Ptolemy to Kepler was progress for us and would have been progress for anyone. And a measure-theory person will ask about existence and uniqueness of the distance, countable additivity, infinite partitions, and whether my ordering survives refinement of the partition. At the back sits a working ecologist who attends the seminar for the fights, thinks the literature is scholasticism, and will ask in an entirely different register why any of this matters for whether her model is any good. Give me the objections I will actually get from each of these, phrased in their own vocabulary and roughly in the order they will arrive, ranked by how much damage they do to me rather than by how likely they are; for each, the two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft -- the assumption it leans on, the case it fails to cover. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so that I am not defending ground I should have abandoned, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I don't need citations or a reading list; the room will supply those, and what I need from you is arguments.
