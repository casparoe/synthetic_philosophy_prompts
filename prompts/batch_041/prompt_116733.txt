I'm a third-year philosophy student writing a term paper for a course on speech acts and pragmatics, and I want to use the paper to work out a problem I keep running into. The course has given us a framework that separates two ways an utterance can go wrong: sometimes the act never gets off the ground at all, because the speaker lacks the standing, the procedure, or the uptake the act requires, and sometimes the act does go through but is hollow, because the speaker doesn't mean what the act would require them to mean. I've been testing this against my own intuitions about particular cases, and I've found that my verdicts don't line up with any single line I can state. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, actually captures the pattern.

Here are the cases and my verdicts on each.

First, a promise I make to a friend -- "I'll pay you back on Friday" -- which he understands and accepts, but which I have no intention of honoring. I think this is a genuine promise, just a dishonest one; the act occurred and I'm simply a liar. Second, an apology I offer -- "I'm sorry for what I said" -- purely to smooth things over, with no actual remorse. Here my intuition runs the other way: I want to say no apology was made at all, only the outward form of one. These two look structurally identical to me -- a sincere-sounding act performed without the inner state -- and I treat them oppositely. That is the first thing I can't square.

Third, I say "I bet you ten dollars it rains tomorrow" to a stranger who doesn't hear me and keeps walking. I think no bet was made. Fourth, I say the same thing to a friend who answers "you're on," but I have no money and never intend to pay. I think the bet was made and I'm just a deadbeat -- so uptake, not sincerity, is doing the work here. Fifth, a man in a rented costume at a party announces "I sentence you to five years in prison." I think nothing was sentenced; he has no standing, and the words are just theater.

Sixth, I thank someone for a gift I secretly dislike. I think this is a real thanking -- a polite fiction, but the act went through. Seventh, I warn a rival "the ice is thin" while secretly hoping they'll ignore me and fall through. I still think I warned them, however cynically. Eighth, I say "careful, the ice is thin" to someone who has just finished telling me the same thing and plainly knows it already. Here my intuition flips again: I don't think I warned them at all -- I merely echoed something, and no warning was performed, even though I have the standing, the procedure, and their full uptake. So sincerity again seems irrelevant in one case and decisive in another, and redundancy seems to defeat an act that otherwise meets every condition.

The tentative principle I've been trying is this: an utterance performs an act just in case it meets the conditions that make the act available -- the right standing, the right procedure, and the hearer's uptake -- and the speaker's private state is irrelevant, except where the act is nothing over and above the expression of a state of mind, in which case the state must genuinely be present. I can see that this keeps most of my verdicts, but it looks gerrymandered to me: the sincerity exception is there only to save the apology case, and it does nothing to explain the redundant warning, which meets every condition I've listed and which I still refuse to count.

Please tell me whether there is a principle that keeps all eight verdicts, or whether some of them have to be given up, and if so which. State the principle or the small set of candidates precisely, tie each clause to the case that forces it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply your preferred principle to a case I haven't listed -- say, an "I forgive you" spoken by someone with no standing to forgive, or a "yes, I agree to the terms" recorded by a sleep-talker -- and tell me what it predicts. I'd rather have a principle that is simple and overturns one of my intuitions than a complicated one that saves them all by brute force, so if that's the trade-off, say so plainly.
