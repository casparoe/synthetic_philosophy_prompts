Ok so I'm a novelist — three books, the last one a weird historical thing that did alright — and I've somehow got a shot at a one-year fellowship at a university research centre, the kind where they keep one artist in residence and you have to hand in a substantial peice of writing at the end. Two-page proposal, due Friday, and I refuse to write the usual residency mush about exploring the boundaries of narrative. So help me write the real one.

Here's my actual idea, and I'm maybe 60% sure its a real question and not just me being pleased with myself.

There's an old doctrine in economics, Friedman I think, a 1953 essay, that a theory's assumptions don't need to be realistic — what matters is whether it predicts well. People call it the F-twist. The examples I remember are billiard players who shoot as if they'd done the geometry in their heads, and leaves arranging themselves on a tree as if each leaf were trying to get the best light. And there's a line about how the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions, which sounds like a dare.

My half-formed thought is that "unrealistic" is doing two jobs and the doctrine only works for one of them. Some false assumptions are false the way a frictionless plane is false: there's a dial, and if you turn it all the way up you get the real thing, so the assumption is the limit of something true. Others aren't like that. Perfect foresight. An agent who lives forever. No transaction costs at all, not even small ones. There's no dial position that makes those true; they aren't limits of anything. I'll call the first kind turnable and the second kind unturnable until someone hands me a better name.

And here's why I, a novelist, care, which is also why I think I might have something. I make up places. Readers forgive an impossible place almost instantly and they never forgive a wrong one. Put my London on the wrong side of the river and I'll get letters; invent a city that floats and nobody blinks. I think the difference is that an impossible premise tells you what game we're playing, and a wrong detail pretends to be a fact and isn't. If that's right, unturnable assumptions aren't really assumptions at all, they're premises — and a premise earns its keep by what it lets you see, not by being true. Which would mean the F-twist is quietly right about turnable assumptions and quietly wrong, or at least defended in the wrong currency, about unturnable ones.

So: write the proposal. State the question so it has a determinate answer — something like, does the licence to build a model on unturnable assumptions reduce to the licence to build one on false-but-turnable ones, and if not, what is the licence? Explain why the defences I'd get from the obvious places — prediction is the only test, assumptions are just instruments, models are fictions and fictions don't assert anything — don't settle it, and where my distinction bites into each of them. Give me a method I can actually run in a year with a library card: I think it has to be two or three worked cases rather than a survey, one unturnable assumption each, and you running the dial test on it, showing me what happens when you try to turn the knob and why nothing sensible does. Then say what the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide between them, and what would make me drop the whole thing — if every unturnable assumption turns out to be turnable under some redescription, or if the two kinds end up tracking the analyst's patience rather than anything in the model, then there's no essay here and I should write a different one. Also tell me the shape: one long piece or four linked ones, and what I'd have to give up to fit it into twelve months. The committee won't be philosophers, so flag the spots where I can't avoid jargon and tell me what to say instead.

No reading list and no citations please — I'll find the sources myself, and I'd rather you describe a position than invent a title or a page number to make it look scholarly. Finish with one line starting Pitch: — a single sentence I can paste into the box marked summary of project.
