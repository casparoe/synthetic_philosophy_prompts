I'm in a seminar that's half social choice theory and half political philosophy, and I have to hand in a short response paper this week. We just did Sen's liberal paradox and I cant stop thinking about it, mostly because I keep wondering what Mill would have made of the whole thing. The theorem as I've written it down (fix my statement if I've mangled it, but then work with the fixed version): you want a rule that ranks social outcomes, and you want it to satisfy three conditions. (i) Weak Pareto — if every individual strictly prefers outcome A to outcome B, the rule ranks A above B. (ii) Minimal liberalism — at least two people are each decisive over at least one pair of outcomes, meaning that if the person prefers one of that pair to the other, the rule goes with them; the natural candidates for such a pair are ones that differ only in something that's that person's own business, like whether they read a particular book or practise a particular religion. (iii) Unrestricted domain — the rule has to handle any consistent set of individual preferences, including preferences that are about other people's business. Sen's result is that no rule can have all three: you can arrange the preferences so the two protected pairs pull the ranking one way, unanimity pulls it another way, and you end up with a cycle. So something has to give, and the interesting part is always which thing.

What I want from you: suppose the Mill of On Liberty and Utilitarianism — the harm principle, the experiments in living, the higher and lower pleasures, all of it — had been shown this theorem and had to comment on it. Commit to a prediction rather than hedging. Which of his commitments would he abandon first, and why that one rather than the others? Some specific points I keep going back and forth on: (1) would he even accept that the theorem is addressed to him, given that his liberty principle is about when interference and coercion are permissible rather than about how to rank outcomes against each other — so maybe he'd say the formalisation is aimed at a target he never held? (2) if he does take it as addressed to him, is the first casualty weak Pareto (a utilitarian who thinks some preferences are uninformed, or base, or formed under conditions he'd reject might not treat preference-unanimity as decisive at all), or is it unrestricted domain (excluding the meddlesome preferences that do all the work in the proof — the ones where someone cares about what someone else reads or does with their own body), or does he keep both and insist that liberty just isn't a condition on ranking outcomes in the first place? (3) does he have anything in his own writing that supports the meddlesome-preferences move, given his view that we're free to dislike, avoid, argue with, and even despise people whose self-regarding conduct we think is degrading, but not to coerce them? And (4) would he treat the theorem as a substantive discovery about liberty, or as an artefact of one particular way of formalising it — and if the latter, what specifically did the formalism get wrong about his position?
