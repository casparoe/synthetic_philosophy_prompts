Someone told me about a result called Good's theorem — apparently it shows that a rational agent can never do worse by getting more information before deciding, because at worst they can just ignore it. But I dont buy it. Take whodunits: I love a good mystery, and if I find out who the murderer is before I've read the book, the whole thing is ruined for me. Knowing the ending makes the experience worse, and I cant just un-know it.

So please compare the theorem with this everyday idea that some information can genuinely spoil things. In what ways is the theorem a special case — what does it quietly assume (that the information is free, that the person is a perfect expected-utility maximizer who updates by the book and takes no pleasure in being surprised, that finding out the answer doesn't change how much the outcomes are worth to you)? And does my mystery example really refute the theorem, or does it just sit outside it, because the value of reading the book depends on whether I already know the ending?
