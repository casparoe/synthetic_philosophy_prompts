The question, worth twenty points, was this. Set out the kalam cosmological argument and the argument from contingency, the latter both as Leibniz framed it and as it is defended today, and compare them. In particular: (a) state the premises of each and the work each premise does; (b) explain what role the principle of sufficient reason plays in each, and whether either argument can be run without it; (c) assess one standard objection to each. Be explicit about the modal status of the conclusion in each case. The transcribed answer reads as follows. Both arguments aim at a first cause, but they get there differently. The kalam is temporal: its first premise is that whatever begins to exist has a cause, its second is that the universe began to exist, and from these it follows that the universe has a cause. The second premise is supported by the impossibility of an actual infinite, since a past containing infinitely many successive events would be a completed infinite and could not be traversed. The argument from contingency is not temporal at all. Leibniz asks why there is something rather than nothing and answers that the world is a collection of contingent things, each of which exists but might not have, so the collection needs a reason outside itself, and that reason is a necessary being; nothing turns on whether the universe had a beginning. The crucial difference is the principle of sufficient reason. In the kalam, the first premise is simply that principle restricted to events which begin to exist, and Leibniz held that the principle follows from the law of non-contradiction, so the two arguments stand or fall together: reject the principle and you have already rejected the kalam's first premise, accept it and the contingency argument goes through. The kalam's conclusion is that the cause of the universe is a necessary being, because a cause that was merely contingent would itself require a cause and we would be launched on an infinite regress; and since the cause must be immaterial and outside time, it cannot be a physical thing. As for objections: Hume's point that whatever we can conceive as existing we can equally conceive as not existing is the standard objection to the contingency argument, and it seems to me successful against any argument that ends in a necessary being. Against the kalam, the standard objection is that modern cosmology does not establish a beginning, since the standard model is consistent with an eternal past; but this only shows that the second premise is not proven by physics, not that it is false, and the argument can be run on philosophical grounds about actual infinities regardless.
