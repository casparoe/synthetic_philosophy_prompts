ok so background: I've been running a tiny interview series on my blog for like three years now — it's maybe forty readers, all of them the sort of people who hung out on LessWrong circa 2012 and never fully left — and next month the guest is this philosopher who works on induction and confirmation. I think she's underated and I want the interview to actually be good, not another "so what is the problem of induction" snooze.

Her view, roughly (paraphrasing, she'd phrase it better): the grue thing — you know, define a predicate that applies to things that are green if you check them before some future date and blue if you check them after — isn't really a problem for confirmation as such. It's a problem for any theory of confirmation that only looks at the logical form of hypotheses. So she splits it into two questions: a metaphysical one about which properties are "natural" / carve reality at the joints, and an epistemic one about which hypotheses the evidence actually supports. Then she says once naturalness is baked in, grue just dissolves, because "all emeralds are grue" isn't a live hypothesis — grue isn't a natural property and green is.

I'm maybe 60% on board and I want to actually press her. Write me 8-10 interview questions that go past "so explain grue to the listeners." In particular I want at least one each on:

- the circularity worry: don't we need to already know which inductions work in order to know which properties are natural?
- whether "green is more natural than grue" is even the kind of claim that could be false, and what evidence would bear on it
- the Bayesian reply that grue is just a prior problem — you pick your prior, done — and why she thinks that's not enough
- entrenchment and new predicates: "quark" wasn't around in 1800 and now it's obviously projectible, so what's doing the work there?
- the self-application thing: can't you grue-ify naturalness itself, so that "natural" becomes time-indexed too?

For each question give me a follow-up that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (e.g. if she says naturalness is a primitive, the follow-up should be about whether positing a primitive explains anything or just relabels the problem).

And don't just hand me a neutral list — commit. At the end, tell me straight which one of these you think she actually can't answer, and say why you'd bet that way, even if you're not sure. I'd rather you stake out a position I can argue with than hedge on all five.
