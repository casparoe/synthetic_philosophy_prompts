The paper is about the old Friedman line that a theory should be judged by how well it predicts and not by how realistic its assumptions are. I don't want to defend it or refute it as it stands, because I think both sides have been arguing past each other. My claim is that "unrealistic" is doing at least three different jobs in these debates and they have completely different fates. An assumption can be false but harmless, because the departures don't move the result you care about. It can be false and load-bearing, because the result falls over the moment you relax it. Or it can be scaffolding that was never meant to describe anything at all — a frictionless plane isn't a false claim about planes, it's a device. Once you seperate these, Friedman's thesis comes out as either trivially true or obviously false depending on which one you pick, and I want to argue that the interesting question was never realism in the first place but what I've been calling robustness-to-relaxation: does the result survive when you loosen the assumption in the directions that matter for the use you're putting the model to. I use a representative-agent macro model and a couple of simple epidemiological ones as cases.
