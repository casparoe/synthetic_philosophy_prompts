You are a philosopher of history who works on causal and counterfactual reasoning about large-scale historical outcomes, and you have agreed to serve as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration. The two collaborators have argued for years about whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. One is a Great Man theorist: she holds that individual agents — their choices, talents, obsessions, and persistence — are frequently the decisive causes of large historical outcomes, and that Hitler is close to a paradigm case of an irreplaceable individual. The other is a structuralist: he holds that impersonal conditions — the settlement of 1919 and the humiliation it entrenched, the economic catastrophes of the interwar years, a constitution whose emergency provisions made parliamentary government dispensable, the radicalization of European politics, and the short-sightedness of conservative elites — made some authoritarian, revisionist, and eventually exterminationist Germany overwhelmingly likely, with or without any particular individual. They agree on one thing: the dispute as usually conducted is a tangle of equivocation, because the two sides are not explaining the same thing. Your task is to draft the joint statement they can both sign.

Think the problem through carefully before you begin — in particular, fix what is being explained before arguing about what explains it, and identify which alleged disagreements are really about words. Do not display that preparatory work; begin the response with the statement itself.

The document should have the following three parts, in this order.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must be substantive rather than platitudinous, and every one must be a claim both parties can actually sign. They should cover, at minimum: (a) the outcome to be explained, disaggregated into at least four components — the collapse of Weimar into dictatorship; the specifically National Socialist character of the resulting regime; rearmament and expansionist war; and the industrialized mass murder of European Jews and others — with an explicit acknowledgment that the structural case is much stronger for some of these components than for others, and a statement of which components the two sides agree are the hardest cases; (b) the terms of the counterfactual, including a minimal-rewrite rule both accept: change only what is needed to remove the individual, hold background conditions fixed, and then state separately which further changes each side thinks the removal would force; and (c) a shared classification scheme for candidate causes, under which every cause on the table is labeled a backdrop condition (necessary for the outcome but not selective among outcomes), a load-bearing condition (removing it would plausibly have blocked the outcome), or a trigger (sufficient only in the presence of the other two), together with a note on which classifications the parties accept jointly and which they do not.

2. A counterfactual protocol both parties sign, specifying two removal scenarios and what each is meant to test. In the early-removal scenario, Hitler dies or disappears before the party becomes a mass electoral force, in the mid-1920s; in the threshold-removal scenario, he is removed in the weeks before his appointment as chancellor, when the party has already become the largest in the Reichstag. For each scenario, both parties must record in advance what they expect to follow, at the level of specificity of the four components above — not merely "some authoritarian regime" versus "no dictatorship," but their best guess about the character, foreign policy, and domestic racial program of the successor state, if any.

3. A cruxes table, followed by a short closing section. Each row of the table should give: the crux, named in neutral language; the structuralist's position, phrased in wording he would accept as his own; the Great Man theorist's position, phrased in wording she would accept as her own; what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own position; and each side's recorded prediction for the two scenarios. The table must include at least these cruxes, and you should add others the collaboration requires: the specificity gap — structural variables seem to predict an authoritarian, revisionist Germany but not the particular eliminationist program, so ask what explains the specificity; the screening-versus-shaping question — did structural conditions select for a leader of Hitler's type, produce him, or neither, and is that distinction doing real work or is it a redescription of the same causal story; substitutability — would another figure, or a non-Nazi authoritarian outcome, count as the same outcome, and is the phrase "Nazi Germany without Hitler" even a coherent description of a possibility; the threshold question — was the accession of 1933 structurally overdetermined or the contingent product of elite bargaining among men who thought they could use him; and the counting question — if a structural configuration made an individual's rise probable, does that individual count as a structural cause, or does calling it structural merely relocate the explanandum? The closing section should state what the collaboration did not resolve, say which disagreements turned out to be verbal and why, and give each side the single finding that would most damage its position.

Write roughly 1,500 words in American English. Open the statement with a single sentence that both parties endorse as the bottom line, then proceed directly to the reasoning and the numbered sections. Do not hedge into "both sides have a point": every agreed claim must be one the parties can genuinely co-sign, and every residual disagreement must be sharp enough to be testable. Do not cite sources, supply a bibliography or reading list, or attribute views to named scholars — the document is the collaborators' own. No preface, no restating of this assignment, and no closing commentary on the task.
