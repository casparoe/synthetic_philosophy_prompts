Right, so I'm meant to be writing up a chapter on adaptive preferences and I've got myself completly tangled. Here's an argument I put together for a seminar that I can't now shake, mainly because it seems to prove far more than I ever wanted it to.

(1) A preference has authority — it's something we should respect, or count when we're working out what's good for someone — only if it's one she'd still hold if she were fully informed and not subject to distorting conditions. That's the standard idealisation / "laundering" move, no?

(2) A preference someone's formed under oppression or deprivation is the paradigm case of one that fails that test. In the nearest world where she isn't oppressed, she wouldn't hold it. So it lacks authority and it's fair game to override.

(3) But look at what (1) actually asks for: a counterfactual — what she'd prefer if things were otherwise.

(4) And changing someone's conditions changes her. Make her fully informed and free and you haven't merely corrected a belief, you may have altered the person: her tastes, her attachments, what she even wants. If the alteration is big enough, "what would she prefer" stops having an answer, because there's no determinate she left on the far side of the antecedent with determinate preferences. (I think this is what people call the conditional fallacy? Never sure.)

(5) So the test faces two options and both are useless. Either it evaluates a world close enough that nothing much about her changes — but then a preference shaped by oppression comes out authoritative too, and the test does no work. Or it evaluates a world far enough that she's genuinely transformed — but then the counterfactual is indeterminate and the test issues no verdict.

(6) Either way the test can't do what it's supposed to: it either rubber-stamps every preference or says nothing at all.

(7) So we should abandon the idealisation test.

(8) But abandon it and we lose the only principled way of telling a preference deformed by injustice from an authentic one; "adaptive preference" then just collapses into "a preference the theorist doesn't happen to like."

Here's my worry. If (4)–(6) goes through it doesn't just sink adaptive preferences — it sinks the whole family of views that analyse someone's good as what they'd prefer under idealised conditions: full-information accounts of welfare, ideal-observer theories of ethics, the lot. I'm not prepared to give all that up.

So where's the damage? Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I want to keep some version of the test, what exactly must I surrender, and can the test be stated so it neither rubber-stamps nor falls silent? I'd rather keep (1) than (8) if I'm forced to choose, but tell me what that's buying and what it's costing.
