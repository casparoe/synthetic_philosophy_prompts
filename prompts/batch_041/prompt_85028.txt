I'm a second-year PhD student in history of science and I have to give a 40-minute paper at our early modern reading group in two weeks, followed by 20 minutes of questions. My thesis is that Kepler's astrology isn't a leftover from his youth or a paycheck thing that sits awkwardly beside the astronomy — it's the same project. What he does in the Prague-era astrology tracts (De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus, Tertius Interveniens, the reply to Roeslin) is cut the apparatus down to one mechanism: the soul, or the Earth's formative faculty, is aroused by the geometrical configuration the planets make among themselves — the aspects — and this arousal is more like being moved by a harmony than like being pushed by a force. Everything he throws out follows from that: the twelve houses, horary and electional astrology, the signs as causal agents in their own right, any picture where a planet shoves the soul from a distance. So the reform isn't proto-skepticism and it isn't a half-conversion; it's internal housecleaning by someone who thinks the harmonies in the Harmony book are the same harmonies the soul is tuned to.

The audience is the problem. It's our reading group plus whoever wanders in, so roughly: two historians of early modern science who are allergic to whig-ish rescues of a "rational" Kepler and will come at me with the court astrologer who needed the fees, plus the point that purified astrology is still astrology; one philosopher of science who works on mechanisms and demarcation and will want to know what "the soul responds to geometry" predicts, and whether I'm not just relocating the mystery one level up; a mathematician who will note that Kepler's own celestial physics has forces weakening with distance and no obvious channel for a bare configuration to reach a soul; a woman from the classics/religion side who has actually read more of the Latin than I have and will ask whether the providence framing is doing any real work or is just pious wrapping; and one person in the group who takes astrology seriously in her own life and will say that if I'm right then Kepler gutted the thing — no fates, no individual predictions worth having — so calling it astrology at all is generous. I'm not a Latinist and I'm early-career, so I need to know what to give away up front to stay credible. Write me the Q&A prep sheet: order the objections by how likely they are times how much damage they do, give me the wording each person would actually use (so the historian's version of the unfalsifiability worry and the philosopher's version are separate entries if they really are different questions), a two-minute reply for each, and a blunt note on where my reply is soft or where I should just concede. Flag when two objections are the same objection wearing different hats, tell me which single concession buys me the most credit at the start, and finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks and what I'd say if it comes anyway.
