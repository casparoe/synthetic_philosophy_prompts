I teach a first-year seminar that pairs short fiction with philosophy of mind, and I am rebuilding the unit on collective mentality. The trouble is that the standard material is too well-worn: my students have already met, in some popular-science form, the conceit of a sentient ant colony that converses with philosophers, and they can recite the usual moves — the colony as an emergent mind, the colony as a mere aggregate, the interpreter who supplies all the meaning — without ever having to think about a particular colony. So I would like to open the unit with an original story instead, one that puts a specific and unfamiliar case in front of them and lets the concepts do their work from inside the fiction. (I have a colleague in entomology who thinks the whole week is a waste of time, which is part of why I want the story to be worth arguing about.) Please write it, and keep the following case as I give it, since the point is that the students cannot place it. A university insectary is being shut down at the end of the academic year. In one sealed room, behind glass, lives a leafcutter colony: eleven years old, a fungus garden the size of a laundry basket, a queen no one has looked at directly in years. The narrator is the technician who has kept it alive, and who for four years has run what she calls the conversations — a rail-mounted gantry that lays synthetic pheromone trails on the floor, two feeders, and a protocol in which the colony's choice of trail stands for yes or no. Answers take about forty minutes. The colony has never been wrong about anything she could check, and has several times answered questions about itself. Now a decommissioning rule forces the lab to classify the colony before it is dispersed, as a research organism or as a cognitive system; the second classification triggers an expensive relocation and an ethics review nobody wants, and the technician is the one who has to sign. She has also found two things she cannot reconcile: the answers are stable when she poses the questions herself, standing at the glass, and scramble when the gantry runs unattended; and her predecessor's notebooks, kept under a cruder version of the same protocol, record answers to questions that appear nowhere in the log of questions asked.

A few constraints. No character should ever state a position in the vocabulary of a seminar — nobody says "emergent," "supervenient," or "the intentional stance"; the disagreement has to be carried by what the people do, by what the ants do, and by what the form obliges the technician to write in the box. Avoid the stock endings (the colony is secretly a god, an alien, or a ghost; the technician is mad; the colony dies and the question dies with it), and resist resolving the matter in the last paragraph — let the story end on an observation rather than a verdict. I would like the biology plausible enough that a myrmecologist would not throw the book across the room: real colonies do make collective decisions by crude rules of thumb, and real ants do respond to the bodies, breath, and shadows of the people who lean over them, so you have room to work without inventing a miracle. Aim for roughly two thousand words.

After the story, give me a brief structured summary — a JSON object, no more than a page — with these fields: "title" (string); "one_sentence_premise" (string); "which_reading_the_story_leans_toward" (string, naming the position in your own words rather than a school's label); "where_the_story_takes_liberties" (array of strings, each naming one liberty, biological or conceptual, and why you took it); "contested_reading_it_depends_on" (string, naming the interpretation of colony minds the story needs in order to work and who would deny it); "strongest_objection_to_the_storys_implicit_verdict" (string); "what_would_change_the_verdict" (string — the argument or the finding about real ants that would make you rewrite the ending, and where the story's implicit verdict is most likely to be wrong); and "what_a_myrmecologist_would_contest_first" (string).
