Write a dialogue between two philosophers of physics debating whether Kurt Gödel's rotating-universe solutions to the Einstein field equations tell against the objectivity of time's passage. One speaker argues that because such a universe is a legitimate solution in which closed timelike curves exist and no global division of events into past, present, and future can be drawn, passage cannot be an objective feature of the world; the other insists that passage survives, perhaps by treating "now" as an indexical or token-reflexive term whose reference is genuine even when no observer-independent present exists. To keep the exchange out of the standard literature, have them conduct it through a distinction you introduce at the outset: between the strong claim that the world possesses a single, observer-independent present and the weaker claim that each event has a genuine "now" relative to the events causally connected to it. The dialogue should press at least three points: whether the indexicality of "now" preserves or quietly evacuates the reality of passage; whether the mere possibility of a Gödel-like universe refutes a claim of necessity about time, or is idle if our own universe does not rotate; and what, if anything, would count as an observable difference between the two positions. Both speakers should be equally competent, respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other, and neither should be a strawman.
