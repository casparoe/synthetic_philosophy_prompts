My younger brother died in March, at forty-one, of a cancer that was found late and moved fast. I am giving the memorial address at the end of the month, and I would like you to write it.

The room will be split in a way that matters. Our father's side of the family is Hindu, and many of them will come to the service with the vocabulary of karma close at hand; several have already told me, gently and with real love, that he must have been working something out, that no one suffers without a cause, that his good karma will carry him well. Our mother's side, and most of my brother's friends and colleagues, are not religious at all; they will hear any such talk as a way of saying that he brought this on himself, and they will be right to hear it that way if it is said carelessly. I want an address both halves can sit through without being asked to disown what they know.

My brother spent his working life as an actuary, and he had a genuine horror of the sentence everything happens for a reason, which he took to be a way of congratulating the universe. So I cannot say it, and I cannot say anything that entails it. Whatever else the address does, it must not assert that he deserved what happened to him, and it must not assert that he did not deserve it in the sense of being owed something better — because, honestly, I no longer know what desert would mean in this case.

What I want the speech to do is one piece of real philosophical work rather than gesture at comfort. The work is this: to separate two things that our family's idiom runs together. The first is karma as a ledger of desert, in which the person who suffers is the person who incurred the debt and the suffering fits the deed. The second is karma as an impersonal causal regularity — on the model of heredity, or of compound interest — in which consequences propagate without anyone keeping score, and without any requirement that the one who suffers be the one who earned it. On the second reading, the fact that my brother could not remember, in this life, having done anything to warrant his illness is not a hole in the doctrine but a category mistake: the doctrine was never tracking memory, or individual desert, in the first place. I would like that distinction made in plain speech, in something like a hundred and fifty words, so that the devout hear their own word preserved and the secular hear the retributive reading refused.

There is a related knot I would like touched, if you can manage it without delivering a lecture. In our family it is customary to dedicate one's own good deeds to the dead. If karma were a strict ledger, nothing could be transferred — a debt is not discharged by someone else's payment. Yet families do it, and it seems to do something. I would like the address to suggest, lightly, that the practice makes sense only on the second reading, and to leave it there.

Formal constraints. Six minutes, roughly eight hundred words. No quotations from scripture, poetry, or any book; no names of scholars, texts, or traditions; no reading suggestions. It has to be sayable aloud by one person who is not a professional speaker. Do not console us with the idea that he is watching over us — our family has no such consolation and it would ring false in the room. Do not address the two halves as halves, or apologize for the difficulty; just speak so that both are included. End on something concrete rather than on a moral: the ledger, the tables he worked at, the smell of the mustard oil in our mother's kitchen, whatever you can make carry the weight. And do not pretend to settle the metaphysics. I do not believe he is being punished, and the speech should let that be visible, but it must not leave anyone in the room feeling that their way of grieving has just been corrected.
