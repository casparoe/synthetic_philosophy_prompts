I'm on the board of a housing co-op — about two hundred members, a couple of buildings, a shared workshop — and we've been flirting with the idea of deciding some things by prediction market instead of by show of hands at the general meeting. The pitch, as I understand it, is that you fix some measure of what you care about (member wellbeing, or long-run cost per member, or what have you), let people bet on which policy will move that measure, and then just adopt whatever policy the market favours. I like the sound of it in the abstract, but my gut is all over the place once I get down to actual decisions, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle behind the mess or whether I'm just making it up as I go. To be clear, I'm not after a history of the idea or who invented it — I want to know what the thing actually commits you to, and where it breaks.

Here's what I actually think. Spending the maintenance reserve on a new heating system rather than patching the old one: I'd happily let a market decide that — it's an empirical question about costs and comfort and I don't see why the meeting should outvote the forecasters. Raising the monthly member contribution by £15 to rebuild the reserve: no, that one I want a vote on, even though you could equally call it an empirical question about what keeps the books balanced. Admitting a third building into the co-op: market. Selling the workshop and splitting the proceeds: absolutely a vote, not a market. Renaming the co-op: vote, obviously — no metric captures taste, and I don't care what the market thinks about it. Endorsing a position on the local planning row: vote as well, and again the market's opinion is neither here nor there.

Two cases tie me in knots. First, a shared-car scheme that only works if enough members expect it to work — there the market price would be partly causing the very thing it's meant to be predicting, so I can't tell whether to trust it or not. Second, anything where the 'measure' is itself the sort of thing members would start gaming the moment they knew it counted; we nearly adopted a member happiness score last year and I can see exactly how that ends. I keep flip-flopping over whether those two are reasons to distrust markets generally or just reasons to pick a better measure. So: is there one principle that gets all of my verdicts, or do some of them have to go? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts for a case I haven't mentioned — deciding by market whether to replace our volunteer treasurer with a paid bookkeeper — and be honest about which of my verdicts it simply can't accommodate.
