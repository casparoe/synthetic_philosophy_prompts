A friend in my political theory seminar has built a gains-from-trade argument for moral compromise that I cannot shake, largely because it seems to prove far more than either of us wants. It runs as follows. (1) Whenever two parties rank possible outcomes differently, there typically exists a package of mutual concessions — each gives ground where he cares comparatively less — that both parties rank above no agreement. (2) Any outcome both parties rank above the status quo is a Pareto improvement over it. (3) A Pareto improvement is pro tanto good, so forgoing one always stands in need of a special reason. (4) Nothing in (1) is peculiar to goods and money: two people who disagree about a moral question also rank outcomes, and they too typically care comparatively less about some aspects of the dispute than others. (5) So for deep moral disagreements as well there typically exists a package of mutual concessions that both parties rank above no agreement. (6) To refuse such a package is to forgo a Pareto improvement, and so requires a special reason. (7) An appeal to the non-negotiability of the disputed value cannot supply that reason, since it merely restates the refusal. (8) So principled intransigence — the abolitionist who will not bargain with the slaveholder, the animal advocate who will not trade a ban on cosmetics testing for looser farm standards — is either irrational or rests on something (1) through (6) cannot supply. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept the conclusion, but I want to know what that costs. Which step should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what would each of the main lines of resistance have to concede?
