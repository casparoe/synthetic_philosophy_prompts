I'm putting together a handout for a seminar on religion and the liberal state — the comparative bit, England/Germany/France/US — and I want a decision tree rather than a reading list. The students keep collapsing everything into "seperation vs establishment", which is useless, so I want the tree to sort positions by what they actually say about concrete arrangements.

Start them from a commitment almost everyone in the room shares: the state must not coerce anyone into or out of a religion. Then the branch nodes should be the further questions that split that consensus, in an order where each question is answerable by someone who doesn't yet know which position it leads to. I want at least these distinctions to earn their place as nodes, though you can add others:

- whether non-coercion is the whole of the requirement, or whether the state must also not *endorse* or *fund* religion even when nobody is coerced
- whether neutrality is a constraint on the state's justifications or on the effects of its policies (an even-handed rule that happens to burden one minority looks fine on the first reading and suspect on the second)
- whether the relevant unit is the individual conscience or inherited/communal membership (this is what makes a church tax you can opt out of look either perfectly liberal or quietly illiberal)
- whether history and entrenchment matter — i.e. whether a toothless established church sitting on a pile of accumulated privileges is a different animal from a freshly legislated one
- whether religious associations get special treatment (exemptions, funding, tax status) or are handled like any other voluntary association

Use these four arrangements as the concrete cases the nodes should turn on, since they pull the positions apart in different directions: a church tax collected by the state as a surcharge on the income tax that you escape by formally leaving the church; an established national church whose legal privileges are mostly ceremonial; a rule barring state employees from wearing religious symbols; and state funding of religious schools distributed across denominations rather than favoring one.

Leaves should be named positions where there's a name people actually use, and "unnamed" where there isn't — I'd expect the usual suspects (strict separation in the laïcité style, non-preferentialism or even-handed neutrality, some form of accommodationism or pluralist establishment, religious nationalism, subsidiarity/Christian-democratic, and the "secularism is just another establishment" critique) but put them where the questions actually land them, not in the order I listed them. For every leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two extra things. First, mark the combinations of answers that nobody occupies and say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, flag any leaves the tree can't separate — I suspect a couple of these positions differ only in rhetoric, and I'd rather the handout say so then pretend the branches cut. Keep it to about six or seven nodes so it fits on one side of a page.
