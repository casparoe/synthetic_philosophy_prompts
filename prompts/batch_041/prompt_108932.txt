Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of physics and last week we did the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the one where you take the relativity of simultaneity and try to argue that if any event is real for anyone then every event is real, so you're forced into the block universe and 'the present' is basically an illusion. We spent the whole session talking past each other, and it slowly dawned on me that most of us were using 'real' and 'present' to mean completely different things and had just been shouting past the words. So for next week I've said I'd bring a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually believe about time and simultaneity, not by whatever label they'd slap on themselves.

Could you build me one? Start from a commitment a person could actually agree or disagree with without already knowing where it leads — so no opening node that's just 'are you a presentist'. I want the branch nodes to be the real hinges, roughly: whether there's an objective, frame-independent fact about which events are happening now; whether 'real' or 'present' can still be meaningful but only relative to a frame or a perspective; whether tensed facts are needed at all, versus tense being just a feature of how we talk; and whether the past and the future are on a par with each other. The leaves should be named positions, and I specifically want these ones to turn up: eternalism / the block universe, a presentism that buys a privileged frame (neo-Lorentzian-style), a presentism that tries to do without one by relativising the present to a perspective, growing block, moving spotlight, and fragmentalism. Add any further leaf you think the space honestly needs. For each leaf, one line on its most awkward commitment, especially wherever it collides with relativity.

Two things I really care about. First, flag any pairs of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate, and say why — I'd much rather have the holes acknowledged than papered over with hand-waving. Second, mark the combinations in the logical space that nobody actually occupies, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And somewhere in there, please address the thing that tripped us up: whether the Rietdijk–Putnam argument secretly depends on treating 'being real for' as a transitive relation you can chain across frames, and what happens to the whole argument if you refuse that move. Keep the tree to something like eight to twelve nodes so it fits on one side of A4, and write it in British English please — half the group are sticklers about that sort of thing.
