I want a joke about pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. Here is the material to build it from, so that it does not rest on anything you might misremember. In pain asymbolia, typically following damage to the insular cortex or a cingulotomy, patients report that a stimulus hurts: they can locate it, rate its intensity, and identify the experience as pain, but they report that it is not unpleasant and show no distress or aversive behavior. The sensory and discriminative features of pain survive while its unpleasantness has come apart from them. A case in the opposite direction is also on record: a patient with damage to the right parietal cortex reported burning pain in the hand, and the hand withdrew, when he merely saw an object approaching it, so that painfulness occurred with no noxious stimulus at all. Now the philosophical stake. On one familiar view, pain is bad because of what it is like: the unpleasantness is intrinsic to the experience, and a state that hurts but is not bad for the one who has it is close to inconceivable. Asymbolia presses on that view from several sides. Perhaps the badness is supplied by something added on top of the sensory state, such as an aversion or a motivation to make it stop, in which case the sensory core of pain is not bad in itself. Perhaps the word pain names the whole aversive package, in which case these patients are feeling something pain-like without being in pain at all, and the unpleasantness turns out to be built into the concept rather than discovered in the experience. Or perhaps pain is bad only through a contingent link between the hurting and the minding, a link that this syndrome cuts.

Write the joke. The punchline must turn on that content: on the separability of the hurting from the minding, on where the badness of pain lives, or on the definitional gatekeeping that decides who counts as being in pain. It should not turn on the mere oddity of a person who does not mind pain, and not on the generic absurdity of philosophers. The setup has to carry the facts, since a reader who has never heard of pain asymbolia should still follow it, and it must not misdescribe the syndrome: these patients are not insensate to noxious stimuli, and they do report pain and can describe it. It has to be funny to someone who knows the debate, with the humor coming from the content rather than from the topic being technical. No footnotes, no gloss on the punchline, no explanation of why it is funny, and no title. Keep it under about 200 words. Begin with the joke itself: no preface, no framing, and no commentary after it.
