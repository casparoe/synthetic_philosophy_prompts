I cover science and ideas for a mid-size daily, which in practice means I've spent alot of the last few years sitting in on committee meetings — juries, hiring panels, grant boards, a faculty disciplinary hearing, that kind of thing. I keep coming out of these rooms with a gut reaction about whether the decision was legit or whether somebody pulled a fast one, and I've definately noticed that my gut is inconsistent with itself. I was hoping you could tell me what principle is doing the work, or if there isn't one, which of my calls I should give up.

Here's the raw material.

(1) A three-person faculty panel deciding whether a student fails a course for plagiarism. Questions: did he copy? does copying warrant failure? should he fail? Panellist A: yes, yes, yes. B: yes, no, no. C: no, yes, no. Majority verdict: he copied, copying warrants failure, he doesn't fail. My gut: that's a flat contradiction, not a quirk. If they won't fail him they have to unsay one of the other two.

(2) Same three people, same opinions, but the dean tells them to vote only on "fail or not fail" and write up reasons afterwards. They don't fail him. My gut: fine. Decision made, next case.

(3) Three friends buying a house together. Questions: does the roof need replacing? if the roof needs replacing, is the asking price too high? is the asking price too high? Same 2-1 pattern as (1). They buy the house. My gut: no problem at all. Their call.

(4) A legislature where a bill passes on the strength of a different majority behind every clause, so no bloc actually supports the bill as a whole. My gut: fine, that's what a coalition is.

(5) A search committee ranking five finalists, where the majority ranking runs in a circle: a majority prefers A to B, a majority prefers B to C, and a majority prefers C to A. My gut: odd, it needs a runoff or the chair's call, but not incoherent in the way (1) is.

(6) An election I covered: the winning party took about 60% of the vote while more than half the voters were against each of its three headline policies, because voters weigh the policies differently and will back a party whose planks they mostly reject. My gut: the result stands, but every "mandate" sentence in our coverage was nonsense.

(7) A board that lands on a completely coherent, well-reasoned position — because the chair chose the order in which the questions were put to the vote, and a different order would have produced the opposite result. My gut: con, even though nothing they concluded contradicts anything else they concluded.

(8) Me, over fifteen years. My old self's views contradict my current ones on several things. My gut: no problem, that's just changing your mind.

My own stab at a rule: a group gets to have a verdict of its own only when it's functioning as a single agent — there's a procedure for it to revise its view, it can be asked to defend the verdict, and it's stuck with it — and when it isn't functioning that way we should report it as a poll, not a verdict. But that doesn't obviously separate (1) from (3), since both are just three people and a decision.

What I want: the principle, or two or three rivals, stated precisely enough that I could apply them at the next meeting, with a note on which of my cases each clause is there to save; which of my gut calls each candidate keeps and which it throws overboard; and an honest statement of which call I can't keep. Take a case I haven't listed and tell me what your favourite candidate says about it. And if the real answer is that my gut is tracking two different things and there's no single rule, say that, and tell me which two of my verdicts are the ones genuinely in conflict.
