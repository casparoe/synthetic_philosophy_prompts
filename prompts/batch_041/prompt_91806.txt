At a literary festival last spring a critic offered an account of my novel that I have not been able to shake. Literary fiction, she proposed, is the peacock's tail: the long apprenticeship, the unpaid years, the sentences no reader strictly needs are handicaps, and a handicap is worth something only because it is expensive, so that only a writer with something to spare can afford to grow one. What the book advertises, on this reading, is the author; its content is largely beside the point. I write novels rather than study birds, and I suspect she has borrowed the peacock's tail for work it cannot do. Set out the correspondence she is relying on — what plays the part of the tail, of the cost, of the quality that cost is meant to index, of the chooser meant to read the signal — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially curious whether the tail is the right model for a signal whose expense is paid mostly in time, and whose receivers are publishers, prize juries, and other writers rather than mates; and whether the honesty of a tail rests on a condition, that it costs a weak bird more than a strong one, which has no analogue here. Which features of the peacock does she quietly assume the novel shares? Does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, what analogy would carry the weight better?
