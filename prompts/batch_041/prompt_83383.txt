Plato's Laws treats wine with an unusual combination of severity and reverence: it is a drug to be kept from the young, an instrument that exposes and tests character, and a divine gift and medicine against the sourness of old age; the dialogue accordingly prescribes a graded regime of access (no wine before eighteen, moderation without intoxication until thirty, licensed conviviality for men past forty) and suggests that a well-ordered city would keep its vineyards few and its wine production modest. Suppose Plato could observe the alcohol-policy landscape of the present and revise his recommendations. Begin your response with a single-sentence verdict on whether the net effect would be to make him more restrictive or more permissive than the Laws — no preamble and no restatement of the question — and then defend that verdict by working through the three developments below, taking a determinate position on each and explaining what in his framework drives it.

1. A proposed drinking-licence regime. Adults would need to pass a supervised practical assessment of their comportment under the influence — a standardised dose at a licensed premises, a scored rubric covering speech, aggression, risk-taking, and solicitude for others — before receiving an unrestricted licence to drink in public. Provisional licensees would face a lower legal blood-alcohol limit and could lose the licence after a single serious infraction. This is a modern attempt to institutionalise the thought that wine is a test of character and a training ground for self-mastery. Would Plato support the scheme, and would he want the assessment to be pass/fail or graded?

2. The disease model of alcohol use disorder and its institutional expressions: medically supervised withdrawal, medication-assisted treatment, and managed alcohol programmes that provide scheduled doses to severely dependent people rather than requiring abstinence. Would Plato treat this as a legitimate medical response to a condition, or as a category error that dissolves the moral work he assigns to drinking?

3. The increasingly influential public-health position that there is no safe level of alcohol consumption, together with supply-side measures such as minimum unit pricing and state retail monopolies. Would Plato accept the epidemiological case against his medicine-for-old-age argument, or would he hold that a good life for the elderly can rationally include a known risk? And on that basis, would he favour or oppose the small-vineyard policy he sketched?

Close by saying which of the three he would regard as most important to get right, and what he would claim the modern debate systematically misunderstands about the relation between drinking, virtue, and the state.
