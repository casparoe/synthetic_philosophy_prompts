I've been reading about museum repatriation lately and I keep tripping over something that looks like a double standard, and I want to know what view it quietly commits you to.

Here's the pattern. In the US, under NAGPRA (the 1990 law), museums that get federal money have to hand back Native American human remains, and certain funerary objects, sacred objects and objects of cultural patrimony, to tribes that can show cultural affiliation. So bones go back, pretty routinely now. But the same museums are under no legal duty to return other Native artifacts, and internationally it's worse: the big European museums mostly refuse claims for things like the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon Marbles, and when they do give something back it's a voluntary gesture, not an obligation. Part of the excuse is legal -- the British Museum is literally barred by an act of parliament from disposing of its holdings except in narrow cases, and the UNESCO convention on illicit trafficking from 1970 only reaches objects exported after 1970, so anything taken during the colonial period falls outside it. And part of the excuse is philosophical -- the idea that certain objects are treasures of all humanity and belong where the most people can see them.

So my question: what does this whole arrangement pre-suppose? When we hand back a skeleton but not a bronze, what theory of who can be wronged, and by what, is being assumed -- that the dead, or the living acting for them, have claims strong enough to beat property, but that living peoples don't have comparable claims to the things that constitute their culture? Does it assume cultural identity doesn't really depend on having the objects, or that it does but that this gets outweighed? And does the 1970 cutoff assume moral claims decay over time, or only that law and morality come apart here?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me which parts the practice strictly needs and which parts it merely happens to fit -- I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined and several different theories would license the exact same behaviour. Then say what would have to change if you swapped in a rival view, say one on which a people's relationship to its own objects is itself a claim that doesn't expire.

One more thing I cant get straight. The cultural-affiliation requirement: to get your ancestors back you have to prove you're the right tribe. That seems to assume group identity is a continuous, provable sort of thing, which a lot of people would deny. Is that assumption doing real work in the theory, or is it just an administrative convenience bolted on -- and if it were dropped, what would the rest of the arrangement look like?
