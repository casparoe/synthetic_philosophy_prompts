I have recently moved from freelance interpreting into casework at an immigration legal-aid clinic, where nearly everything we learn about a client's claim reaches us through an interpreter, and a senior colleague has circulated a one-page memo on the theory behind our interviewing practice that I cannot stop worrying over. It runs roughly as follows. Quine's story of the field linguist set down among speakers of an unknown language, who hears "gavagai" whenever a rabbit runs past, shows that no possible evidence could settle whether the word means rabbit, a momentary rabbit-stage, an undetached rabbit-part, or a whole sentence rather than a word. Several mutually incompatible manuals of translation can each be fitted to every observation the linguist could ever make, so there is no single correct translation. And because the only thing we ever have access to is a speaker's outward behavior, while the meaning itself stays hidden inside, Quine concludes that we can never really know what another person means: translation is a matter of taste rather than truth, and no rendering of a client's words is more defensible than any other. The lesson for us, the memo says, is to treat an interpreter's phrasing as a convenience rather than as evidence, and to build our cases on documents instead.

I would be grateful for a careful audit. Please take the memo's claims one at a time and sort them into what a philosopher working on Quine would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what Quine would reject, explaining what goes wrong in the last group; then say whether the memo's practical recommendation for our interviews survives those corrections. Keep the whole reply under 450 words.
