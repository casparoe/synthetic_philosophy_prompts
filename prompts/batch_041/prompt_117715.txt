I have been trying to think about online cancellation through the lens of Braithwaite's work on reintegrative shaming, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that seems too tidy. His distinction is between shaming that reintegrates an offender — public disapproval aimed at the act, followed by rituals of forgiveness and readmission to the community — and shaming that fixes a permanent stigma on the person, and the empirical claim is that the first reduces reoffending while the second increases it. Online cancellation looks to me like a straightforward instance of the reintegrative kind: a community publicly identifies a norm-violation, the target acknowledges it and apologises, and the episode concludes with the person restored to ordinary standing. If that is right, then the familiar philosophical objections to cancellation as a form of social punishment — that it is disproportionate, that it proceeds without due process, that it is a cruel way of treating people — should lose their force, since the empirical literature tells us that this is the effective and humane form of social sanction and that the stigmatising form is the objectionable one. So my question is whether any philosophical objection survives once the criminological evidence is in, or whether the case for and against cancellation is now essentially an empirical matter that the moral philosophers should step back from.
