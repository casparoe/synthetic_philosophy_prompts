I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, working mainly on higher-order evidence. I've been trying to settle my own view about how evidence concerning the reliability of my own belief-forming processes should bear on what I actually believe, and I have a set of verdicts that I am fairly confident about one at a time but cannot get to add up. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they express, and which of them I ought to abandon.

The cases, and my verdicts on them:

1. Peer arithmetic. A colleague whose track record on this kind of calculation is indistinguishable from mine works a long division problem independently and arrives at a different quotient. I cannot find an error in my work, and I cannot find one in hers. Verdict: I should move a long way toward her answer, and it is not rational for me to keep insisting that my figure is the right one.

2. The pill, trivial inference. A study I trust says that a pill I have just swallowed impairs logical reasoning for about an hour in most people. Ten seconds later I reason from two premises I can verify by looking out the window: if it is raining, the match is off; it is raining; so the match is off. Verdict: full confidence. The pill gives me no reason to think I slipped on an inference I can check at a glance.

3. The pill, long derivation. Same pill, same hour, but now I reconstruct from memory a thirty-step derivation of a nontrivial theorem. Verdict: I should markedly lower my confidence in the result, because I may well have gone wrong somewhere I cannot spot.

4. My own calibration record. Reviewing a log of my past judgments, I find that when I felt about 90% confident I turned out right roughly 70% of the time, and this has held up across many domains. I am now at 90% on a fresh claim. Verdict: I should be at roughly 70%, and the fact that I feel 90% is neither here nor there.

5. The underconfidence finding. I am about to give a rough estimate of some quantity. I then read a well-replicated result showing that people with my exact training systematically and substantially underestimate their own accuracy on this kind of estimation task. Verdict: I should raise my confidence above what my unaided reasoning would have given. The higher-order evidence here moves me up, not down.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on. I reason as carefully as I can about a hard question and conclude that P. I then become convinced that, under the conditions I was reasoning in, my conclusion is probably a product of overconfidence, so that given my total evidence P is not more likely than not. I find that I still believe P, and that this does not feel as irrational as the textbooks say it should; but I also would not stake much on P.

Cases 2 and 4 strike me as demanding opposite treatment even though both are, on the face of it, just information about how someone with my cognitive profile processes evidence, and I suspect that any principle preserving both verdicts will need a rider clause doing real work. Case 6 is the one I cannot make up my mind about.

So: is there a clean principle that gets all six, or close? State it precisely, give it whatever clauses it needs, and say what it predicts about a case I have not described. If no such principle exists, tell me which verdict to abandon and why the others are worth more than it.

Please structure the response as follows: "The principle" (a precise statement of the best candidate, plus any rider clauses it needs, 150-250 words); "Earning its keep" (case by case, showing which clause each of my verdicts motivates, 500-700 words); "Extensions" (its verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed, around 300 words); "What I must surrender" (which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of my intuitions you would overrule, around 200 words); "Bottom line" (around 100 words).
