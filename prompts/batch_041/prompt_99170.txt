I've been going down a rabbit hole on how nuclear regulators and industrial risk people actually work, and something's bugging me. The whole business is framed in probabilities — there's a "safety goal" stated as the chance of a severe accident per reactor-year being astronomically small, and then on top of that there's this separate "defense in depth" requirement: you have to build in redundant, independent layers of protection even after the probabilistic risk assessment already says you're comfortably under the goal. So the same regulator that treats a one-in-a-million-ish chance as a real number worth driving down further — and keeps making plants spend real money to shave a risk that's already negligible — turns around in other contexts, trace contaminants in drinking water say, and declares a risk below some threshold "de minimis" and not worth regulating at all. Why the double standard? I'm not a philosopher, just someone who got nerd-sniped by a risk report, so I don't have the vocabulary for this, but it feels like the two halves of the practice are committed to incompatible things about tiny probabilities of huge harms.

What I want you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory behind the whole package. Does the safety-goal-plus-defense-in-depth way of doing things presuppose expected value reasoning — that a small enough probability times a big enough catastrophe is still a reason to act, and maybe even a reason that dominates everything else? Or does it presuppose the opposite, a threshold view on which risks below some size just don't count? And which one does the de minimis practice presuppose? I'd like you to say whether the two halves are actually in tension or whether one consistent view can underwrite both, and to separate what's strictly presupposed from what the practice merely fits well with. It's fine if the answer is "the practice is philosophically underdetermined here" — I'd rather know that than get a tidy answer that isn't true.

Then, the counterfactual: what would change if a regulator threw out the threshold/de minimis idea and ran everything through straight expected value, with a value of a statistical life and a discount rate? Would we end up spending wildly more on absurdly unlikely disasters, or does the math actually come out looking pretty similar? And a harder one — is "defense in depth" even the kind of thing an expected-value framework can justify, or does it only make sense under some other, non-consequentialist picture of what safety regulation is for? I keep suspecting that requirement is doing something the probability numbers can't, but I can't articulate what.
