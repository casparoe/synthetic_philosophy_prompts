Ok, so I have a privacy question that's been eating at me for about a month and I can't get it to settle. It started because my health insurer sent me a letter about managing my diabetes — I have never told them I have diabetes, so they obviously stitched it together from a prescription here and a loyalty-card purchase there, and it rattled me way more than I expected. Since then I keep finding I have strong opinions about privacy that I can't actually back up, which is annoying because I'm not a sentimental person about much else. And the timing got funny, because I just took a part-time job at a company that builds audience segments for advertisers, so I now spend my afternoons watching exactly this kind of inference get done to people, and honestly some of it looks totally fine to me and some of it looks like it should be a crime, and I genuinely cannot tell you where the line is from the inside. My friend, who is doing a philosophy masters and won't shut up about it, says my whole way of thinking about this is confused, so I want to lay out my actual gut verdicts and see what, if anything, unifies them.

Here they are, in no particular order. My roommate reads my diary because I left it open on the kitchen table — clear violation, worst thing on this list. Same roommate notices I've been off lately and quietly asks my sister if I'm ok — not a violation at all, she's being a good friend. My insurer does the diabetes thing and mails me a pamphlet about it — violation, and weirdly it bothers me more than the diary does, which surprised me. My GP forwards my file to a specialist without asking me first — fine, that's just her doing her job. A stranger reads an old blog post where I wrote about my divorce — fine, I put it out there. Someone gathers 400 of my public posts and writes up a profile that outs me as a member of a religion I have never once mentioned to anyone — serious violation, even though every single fact in it is a fact I posted myself. My friend uses something I told her in confidence to plan a surprise party for me — fine, she used the secret, but she used it for me. My sister's little speaker thing has started chiming at her whenever it hears me sounding upset on a call, and it emails her a weekly distress summary about me — violation, and honestly it's the one that creeps me out most even though it's the least informative of the bunch. A company reads my public posts and sells the religion inference to my employer — violation, though I can't tell if it's worse than the profile one or just the same thing again. And a stranger recognizes me on the street from my profile picture and says hi — fine.

So here's my problem. I have a principle I like, and I'm pretty sure it's wrong. I think a privacy violation happens when information about me changes how I'm treated and I don't get to push back on it — it's not about who saw what, it's about what it does to me. But that makes the doctor case and the surprise-party case come out as violations, which they definately aren't, and I can't patch it by saying those people had good intentions, because intentions feel irrelevant — my sister's speaker probably has good intentions too, it's trying to keep an eye on me. The only thing I can come up with is that the doctor and my friend would have to look me in the eye about it afterwards and the insurer and the speaker company wouldn't, but that's not a principle, that's a vibe. Meanwhile my friend keeps insisting the real distinction is between leaks and lenses: a leak is when something of mine gets past where I meant it to stop, and a lens is when nothing new gets out at all but the things I already let out get combined to show something I never disclosed. I'll admit leaks and lenses sorts my list cleanly, it just also makes the doctor and the surprise party violations, which they aren't.

So: is there a principle that actually gets all ten, or close, and if I have to give one up, which one is it and why that one? For each piece of your principle, tell me which of my cases it's really earning its keep on. And say what it predicts for three I haven't listed: a shop that quietly shows me a higher price than it shows the next person, based on data about me, and tells me nothing; my friend telling my secret to one other person who then does absolutely nothing with it; and a company that uses my data to send me recommendations I actually like, and never tells me. Last thing — is my friend's leaks-and-lenses thing doing real work, or is it just the disclosure-versus-aggregation distinction with a new haircut?
