I've been teaching an immigration ethics seminar for a few years now, and every time we reach the open-borders / right-to-exclude unit the discussion slams into the same wall. One side runs the argument that movement is a basic liberty, that a border regime is a coercive distributive institution, and that the burden of proof therefore falls on whoever wants to keep people out; the other side runs the argument that a political community has a claim to self-determination, that a community which can't control its own membership isn't really self-governing, and that this claim is prior to any particular migrant's interests. And here's what gets me: by the end everyone has conceded the same empirical facts — the wage effects, the fiscal effects, the point that the poverty-reduction potential of open movement probably dwarfs just about any aid program you could design — and nobody budges an inch. Worse, both camps happily profess the same moral premises (autonomy, equality, the badness of coercion, the value of community). I keep suspecting the real disagreement sits upstream of all of it: maybe about what a political community fundamentally is, maybe about whether we're allowed to lean on the fact that states as we find them are going to keep existing, maybe about who counts as a party to the justification in the first place. A colleague has been nagging me with a further thought — that any border regime coerces would-be entrants, and that coercion has to be justified to everyone it coerces, including the person turned away, which if true makes the whole "we just want to decide our own membership" reply look question-begging; but I can also hear someone objecting that this just smuggles in a particular all-affected-interests principle that the self-determination camp never signed up for, and that we're really back at the boundary problem — you can't ask a demos to decide who gets in without already presupposing who the demos is, so the whole exercise is circular.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a literature survey and definitely not a verdict on open borders. Is this a verbal dispute, a hidden empirical disagreement, a clash about which cases count as data, two groups answering different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Where does the all-affected-vs-all-subjected move actually bite, and does it dissolve the impasse or just relocate it? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result, or honestly nothing — and if you think it's bedrock, say so and explain why that isn't just a cop-out. Please take a definite position and defend it rather than laying out the options for me to choose from; I'd rather be told I'm wrong than handed a menu.
