In the colour phi phenomenon, two small dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions, the first in one colour and the second in another. Observers do not report two separate flashes; they report a single dot that appears to travel from the first position to the second and to change colour somewhere in the middle of its journey. The awkward part is the timing: the reported colour change is placed at a moment before the second dot was presented, so the experience appears to contain information about a stimulus that had not yet occurred.

There are two broad styles of explanation for this, and they differ over when the relevant content is fixed. On the first style, the experience itself is held back until the later stimulus has been registered and is then constructed whole, so that nothing is ever experienced with the wrong colour or in the wrong place. On the second, the observer genuinely experiences the first dot in its own colour, but the record of that experience is quietly revised afterwards, so that what is remembered is a smooth colour-changing movement that never actually occurred as such. The first revises before the fact, as it were, the second after it; the two are often labelled the Stalinesque and the Orwellian readings respectively.

A colleague of mine explains all of this by analogy and treats the analogy as doing real explanatory work. The brain, he says, is essentially a film studio. Sensory signals come in like raw footage; somewhere inside, an editor cuts, reorders, and interpolates frames; and what we are conscious of is the finished cut, projected on an internal screen for an internal audience. On this picture the timing puzzle dissolves: the editor simply waited for the second frame, spliced together a single colour-shifting movement, and screened that. From this he draws a substantive conclusion -- that in the colour phi case the first dot was never consciously experienced in its own colour at all, and that the colour of the earlier experience is an artefact of assembly rather than anything that was ever present to the subject.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a survey of the phenomenon. First, give me the correspondence table: what in the film-studio picture is supposed to map onto the stimulus, the sensory signal, the neural processing, the moment of experience, the subject, and the subsequent report. Second, say where the mapping breaks -- which of those roles the picture fills in by assumption rather than by argument, and which features of a real studio (a privileged viewing position, a screen, a time-stamped reel, an editor who is not himself in the film) have no honest counterpart in the brain. Third, separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that are merely decorative. Fourth, tell me whether that conclusion -- that the earlier colour was never experienced as such -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion all along, in particular by presupposing that there is a definite moment at which a given content counts as experienced. Finally, if the analogy cannot bear the weight he places on it, sketch a better one, or say why no single analogy is likely to do the job.
