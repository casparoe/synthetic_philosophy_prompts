I am trying to state the principle behind my own verdicts on material constitution, for a paper I am writing, and I would like you to tell me whether there is one, what it says, and what it costs me. My verdicts. (1) A bronze statue and the lump of bronze it is made of: one object, not two — the statue is not a second thing coinciding with the metal. (2) A bronze statue cast in a mould, so that the metal never existed as a free-standing lump: again one object, even though the statue and the lump begin and end together. (3) A statue carved from a block, with enough marble left that a lump is still there where the statue is: two objects, the statue and the lump, in the same place. (4) A statue melted down: the statue is gone, the bronze remains. (5) A rope whose fibres are replaced one at a time: the same rope throughout. (6) That rope unlaid into its fibres and relaid in the same order: the original rope is gone and a new one exists, though no fibre and no arrangement has changed. (7) A clay pot squashed flat: the pot is gone, the clay remains. (8) The planks of a ship replaced one by one, the removed planks later assembled elsewhere into a ship: the reassembled ship is the original, not a replica.

I take (1) and (3) to be in tension, since in both a shaped thing seems simply to be its material, and I take (6) and (8) to be in tension, since in both a thing is taken apart and put back together from the very same parts. My provisional guess is that there is one object just in case the material and the shaped thing could not have come apart; but that guess seems to fail on (2), where the bronze could have been squashed instead.

Please give me the principle, or the best small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to apply; tell me what it implies about a bronze statue whose leg is replaced with a leg of a different alloy, and about two lumps of clay pressed into a single lump and then pulled apart; and say plainly which of the eight verdicts I have to give up.
