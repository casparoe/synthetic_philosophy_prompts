I'm in the second term of a course on American foreign policy and I keep meeting a phrase that appears to do an enormous amount of argumentative work while resisting any single clear meaning. Last week, in a seminar on the post-war order, a classmate invoked 'a city upon a hill' to wave away a comparison between the United States and other great powers: America, she said, is not simply one state among others, so the usual comparisons miss the point. Within twenty minutes the same phrase was being used by someone else to argue what sounded like the opposite — that America's peculiar standing loads it with obligations other states do not carry — and then a third person used it to mean that the country is watched, and will be judged, more harshly than anyone else. My suspicion is that these are three different claims wearing one costume, and that at least one of them does not follow from the reading it was enlisted for. I don't want an essay on the phrase's history or its best-known users, and I don't need sources or reading lists; I want the underlying claims separated out.

Please give me a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from readings so thin that nobody could sensibly deny them up to the strong and contested ones, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something false, and what sort of consideration would tell for or against it. I'm especially interested in the readings on which it is a claim about the country's relation to a moral or legal order: that it is a model others ought to copy, that it is exempt from standards that bind others, that it is bound more tightly than others, that it has a mission that overrides ordinary constraints, or something else again. For every reading, tell me which of the three uses above it would actually support, and flag any reading that is being asked to license a conclusion it cannot deliver. I'd also like to know which readings are about what the country has in fact been, which are about what it ought to be, and which are better understood as a piece of collective self-description whose function is to mark who is inside the story — and whether any of those three can be held without the others.

Since I'll be taking this into an argument I care about, I'd rather have your confidence calibrated than your conclusions tidy: say which of your verdicts are firm and which are genuinely contestable, what argument or evidence would make you reverse one, and where your reasoning is most likely to be going wrong. Please write in British English.
