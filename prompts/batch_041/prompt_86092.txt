I spent thirty-four years as an experimental geophysicist and retired two years ago, since when I have been working through a reading list assembled over decades without ever having the time to use it. A small group of fellow emeriti meets twice a month, and we have just finished the chapter of Hume's Enquiry that deals with miracles, which I found far more intricate, and far less like a tidy summary of the obvious, than its usual invocation would suggest. It nags at me because of a memory from a grant review panel some fifteen years ago, where a distinguished colleague disposed of a startling claim of a novel physical effect with the remark that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The room nodded; I nodded; we moved on, without anyone asking what that claim would have had to look like in order to survive the afternoon. I have come to suspect that the sentence, as it is ordinarily used, is doing several jobs at once—the work of a Bayesian point about priors, the work of a legal point about burdens of proof, the work of a sociological point about who is permitted to stop listening—and that these are not consistent with one another.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict: the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, each spelled out as a claim one could actually agree or disagree with. Please vary the readings along every axis you can—what is supposed to be extraordinary (the proposition itself, the event it reports, the prior probability, the character of the claimant, the size of the revision demanded of us), what is supposed to count as extraordinary evidence (merely more evidence of the same kind, evidence of a different kind, a higher threshold of proof, or evidence whose absence would be more surprising than the thing claimed), and who is doing the requiring, on whose behalf, and with what authority. Then give me a verdict on each reading: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or something false or self-defeating—and, for each, whether it would actually license what my colleague did, which was to treat a claim as settled against by the mere fact that it was surprising.

I would also like your best judgment as to which reading a person is most likely to intend when saying it in a room like that one, and whether any of these readings is Hume's own argument or whether the slogan is a weaker and tidier thing that has been quietly substituted for it.
