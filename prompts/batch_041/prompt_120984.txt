I'm three chapters into a novel set in a village outside Granada and I've realised the book is really about the Spanish thing — the transition, the amnesty everyone agreed to think of as forgetting, and then the long unsilencing: the historical memory law, Franco dug out of the Valley of the Fallen in 2019, and the newer law that finally puts the state on the hook for the mass graves that families and volunteers had been digging up themselves for years, and re-describes the monument as a cemetery for both sides, which strikes me as either the whole answer or a dodge. I dont want a reading list, I want the argument. Take the view that memory is a debt we owe to the people we belong to — Margalit, if I've got him right — and set it against Rieff's: that memory politics mostly keeps the wound open, and that forgetting is sometimes the humane option. Run Spain through both. Who owes what to whom here: the dead, their grandchildren, or a state that is continuous with Franco's state and also the thing that was supposed to break with it? Does an amnesty that destroys nothing and simply refuses to look count as forgetting, or is that a different act altogether — and if it's a different act, does the case for forgetting even get off the ground in Spain? And the part I can't get past: if the duty is owed to the dead, can a law passed decades later discharge it at all, or does late remembering just prove the duty was never really about them? Then turn it around on the theory — what does Spain confirm, what does it break, and where are both positions simply silent.
