I have no background in mathematics or philosophy; I read about Hilbert's hotel in a popular book and have been turning it over ever since. What I want is a poem that takes one particular question about it seriously rather than retelling the story. Write as a poet who genuinely understands infinite cardinals and the difference between an infinity that is only ever a rule for going on and one that is a finished collection, and plan carefully before you begin — if it helps, jot the argument down in a few lines first, then set the notes aside and write the verse.

The form is a sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line coda, built on a fixed set of six end-words that reappear in every stanza in the rotating order, the last word of each stanza becoming the first of the next, with all six returning in the coda. Choose words that can bear being repeated with a changed sense each time, and let the rotation be the hotel itself: the same handful of things — the room, the guest, the number, the more — circling because nothing is ever added and nothing ever leaves. The speaker is the night clerk.

The part I care about: partway through, a guest refuses to move. She is a finitist, and not a confused one — she holds that the clerk's promise of always room for one more only ever exhibits an unending process and never a completed whole, and that the trick of renumbering quietly assumes the guests are all there at once, already finished, waiting to be relabelled. I do not want the poem to settle this. The clerk should keep working while the objection stays standing at the desk, and the coda should leave the two of them still in the room. If it comes naturally, let the closing lines cast the shadow of the diagonal argument — the sense that the same hospitality that takes in one more can, pushed further, show that some infinities outrun others — without spelling it out. No quotations, no citations, no footnotes; I want only the ideas to be right, so that someone who knows this material finds nothing to wince at and someone who does not comes away with a real grip on the disagreement.
