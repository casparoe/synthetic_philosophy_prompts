I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group and a grad student in it has an argument about whether DNA is really a code that I can't seem to get out from under. For context on where I'm coming from: I'd like to keep the idea that a gene carries information about the protein it specifies, and I'd like to keep it in a way that isn't just a compliment — but I also don't believe there's any little homunculus in the cell reading anything, so I'm not going to help myself to any spooky notion of meaning. Here's his argument the way he gives it.

(1) A gene carries information about the amino acid sequence of the protein the cell builds from it; that's the least controversial claim in molecular biology. (2) For X to carry information about Y is just for the state of X to reduce uncertainty about the state of Y — a reliable correlation and nothing more; whatever else people mean by information, they don't mean anything a cell could cash out. (3) Wherever there's a reliable correlation like that, there's a mapping from states of one thing to states of another, and a mapping of that kind is a code. (4) The mapping from codons to amino acids isn't forced by physics or chemistry — the same codon means different things in different lineages, so nothing in the chemistry pins the assignment down. It's arbitrary. (5) An arbitrary mapping of that kind is a code in the full sense, i.e. a convention, the way words or Morse are conventions. (6) So DNA is a code in the full sense. (7) But look at what else falls under (2)-(5): tree rings correlate with rainfall; a riverbed correlates with the history of the river; the teeth of my key correlate with the inside of my lock, and the teeth could have been cut another way, so that one's arbitrary too. Every one of those comes out a code in the full sense. (8) And if everything with a reliable, non-necessary correlation is a code in the full sense, then saying DNA is a code tells us nothing about DNA that isn't already true of my key. But if we tighten code so that keys and tree rings are excluded, DNA goes out with them, because there's no convention anywhere in a cell. So either DNA is a code is empty, or it's false.

I'd rather not give up (1), and I dont want to give up (2), because (2) is the only reading of information I can actually defend in front of a chemist. So I keep feeling the mistake has to be somewhere in (3) through (5), but I can't say where without sounding like I'm just insisting biology gets to use words however it likes. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine until you pressed on it, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, can I keep (1) and (2) both, or does one of them have to go?
