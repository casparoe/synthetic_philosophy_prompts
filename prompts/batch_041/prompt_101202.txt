I have just emerged from a long and rather heated argument with a friend about John Snow and the Broad Street pump. My friend insists that removing the pump handle caused the 1854 Soho cholera outbreak to end; I say the outbreak was already in decline and that the handle removal was at best a difference-maker among many. We both know the standard story—the contaminated water, the handle removal, the maps—so please do not rehearse it. What I want is a careful analysis of the episode through the lens of counterfactual versus interventionist theories of causation. Treat the handle removal as the candidate cause. For the counterfactual theory, say what the relevant counterfactual is and whether it is true or even well-defined, given that the outbreak was already waning and that other interventions were underway. For the interventionist theory, say what counts as an ideal intervention on the pump handle and whether the theory can distinguish the handle removal from the contaminated water itself, or from the sanitary conditions that made the water contaminated. Then turn the lens around: what does this episode reveal about the limits of each theory? Does it show that counterfactual theories cannot handle preemption or overdetermination, or that interventionist theories cannot capture our ordinary causal selection—why we single out the handle rather than the water company, the poor drainage, or the cholera bacterium? I am not interested in a bibliography or a history lesson; I want the mapping and the verdict. Begin with a one-sentence verdict, then give the analysis in no more than 700 words.
