I'm taking a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and last week we worked through Galen Strawson's case for the conclusion that no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything — the argument that moves, roughly, from the claim that nothing can be the cause of itself to the claim that being genuinely responsible would require being the cause of oneself, at least in certain crucial mental respects, which is impossible. I think I follow the mechanics, but I've been arguing with a friend about the scope of the argument, and I want to check my reconstruction before I write my response paper.

Here is how I've been reading it, and where I suspect I may have gone wrong. It seems to me the whole thing is a determinist's argument in disguise. The engine is a regress: for me to be responsible for what I did, I would have to be responsible for the kind of person I am; for that, responsible for how I came to be that kind of person; and so on back to my genes, my upbringing, and circumstances that were fixed before I had any say. That regress, I've been telling my friend, only gets its bite if determinism is true — if each link in the chain is necessitated by the one before it. So it seems to me that indeterminism is an escape hatch. If the world is genuinely open at some level, or if libertarian free will is real, then at some point the chain simply isn't forged in advance; there is a moment that is either self-caused or not caused at all, and the regress the argument needs never gets going. And I've been extending the same point to compatibilists: since a compatibilist denies that responsibility requires self-creation and locates freedom in something like a suitable responsiveness to reasons, it looks to me as though the argument doesn't reach her either.

So my question is whether the argument actually needs determinism as a premise at all, or whether I've smuggled something in that isn't there. If it doesn't need it, I'd like to understand why not, and in particular what it is supposed to do to the two escape hatches I've sketched — indeterminism, and the compatibilist's refusal of the self-creation demand. I'm not looking for a reading list; I want to know where the reconstruction goes wrong, what the argument actually claims about the relation between its conclusion and the truth or falsity of determinism, and whether my friend or I have the better of it.
