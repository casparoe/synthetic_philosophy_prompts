I run the grants program at a mid-size family foundation. We make around a hundred grants a year, and about a dozen of them come back asking for more money to keep going, usually somewhere between fifty and five hundred grand. I've started to notice we almost always say yes, and the reasons sound fine at the time, but I think we're basically playing the dollar auction: once you've bid, the only way to not lose your money is to bid again, so you keep bidding and the auctioneer walks off with everything. Our version is messier though. There's no guy holding up a dollar bill, and half the time I'm not even sure what the prize is worth, because the prize is just keeping the program running, so its value seems to creep up the more we've already put in.

I need a decision procedure my team can actually run on a continuation request. Two people, one two-hour meeting, no economist in the room, and I have to write a one-page recommendation for the board, who sign off on anything over 200k.

Things I want it to handle:

- The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
- Where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to the board because it's a values question and not a facts question.
- The difference between a real dollar auction (there's an actual second bidder out there, and their willingness to keep going is driven by their own sunk costs) and the seperate plain sunk-cost case (there's no second bidder, it's just us and our past spending). I think the rule you follow should be different in the two cases and I want that spelled out.
- The late entrant. Sometimes a new funder shows up after we've been at it for years and can come in cheap, because they never paid the early rounds. I never know what to do with that one.
- Tell me which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases: a grant where another funder has been matching us and keeps upping their side; a grant where the only one bidding is us; and one where your procedure says kill a program that's actually doing some real good.

And say what the procedure definately can't do.
