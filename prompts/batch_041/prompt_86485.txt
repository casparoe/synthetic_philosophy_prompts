I teach a grad seminar in decision theory — we do Savage, then the last third of the course is the heretics (rank-dependent stuff, Buchak's risk-weighted thing, a little Gilboa–Schmeidler). I'm writing the final and I need one big question on risk-weighted expected utility. The problem I keep having is that a student who's memorized 'expected utility bakes in risk neutrality and the risk function fixes that' can write two pages and get most of the marks. I want the question to be unanswerable that way.

So the question has to make them actually do something with the machinery, and I'll hand them the machinery in the question stem — they dont have internet and I'm not asking them to cite anything, so the stem should give them everything. Here's what it should give them: a gamble is a finite list of money outcomes with probabilities, written from worst to best; the risk function r takes probabilities to probabilities, is increasing, r(0) = 0, r(1) = 1; and for a gamble with outcomes x1 ≤ x2 ≤ ... ≤ xn and probabilities p1, ..., pn,

REU = x1 + Σ (from i = 2 to n) r(p_i + p_{i+1} + ... + p_n) · (x_i − x_{i−1})

i.e. each gap between consecutive outcomes gets weighted by r of the chance of landing at x_i or better.

Three parts I want in the question.

(a) Short warm-up. Have them show that r(p) = p collapses REU to ordinary EU, and compute REU for the gamble G = ($0 with prob 1/2, $100 with prob 1/2) under r(p) = p² and under r(p) = p³, and say what 'risk averse' amounts to in this setup — something like r(1/2) < 1/2, i.e. rejecting the fair coin-flip gamble.

(b) The real crux. Take G as above, and take the two-stage thing: flip a fair coin; heads you play G, tails you just get $100 for sure. Written as a single one-shot gamble over final outcomes, that's $0 with prob 1/4 and $100 with prob 3/4. Have them compute the REU of that one-shot gamble under r(p) = p², then compute the value of the two-stage version by evaluating each node with REU and working backwards — same r(p) = p² — and explain the gap. The point I want them to land on is that REU is not a function of the final distribution over money: the shape of the tree matters, because the risk function gets applied to cumulative probabilities at each stage. Then have them say which description they think the theory should treat as authoritative, and whether the gap is a bug or the whole point.

(c) Interpretive, and this is where I want them to be honest. Give the strongest case for each side of (b). In particular I want them to say what a defender has to concede about an agent who, at the outset, ranks the one-shot and the two-stage descriptions of the same final lottery differently — is that just what it is to care about risk, or does it leave the agent open to being pumped? And what would a critic say the theory has to give up to close the gap?

Deliverables: write the exam question itself (I'll paste it straight in, so it has to be self-contained and readable — it's the last question on a three-hour final, so aim it at about 45 minutes of work), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks out of 25, and a one-line note for each about the characteristic way students miss it. I want the marking scheme to seperate a real A from a B, so it should name the specific moves the A answer makes that the B answer doesn't.

One thing on the model answer: keep a clear line between the parts that are just arithmetic and the parts that people who actually work on this argue about. Don't write up the bug-or-feature bit as if the field had settled it — flag where the disagreement is genuinely live and who would say what.
