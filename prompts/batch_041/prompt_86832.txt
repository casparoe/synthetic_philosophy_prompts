Six weeks ago I promised a dying friend that I would see a certain sum of her money spent on a cause she had supported for decades. She died before anything was put in writing, her will is silent on the matter, and her brother — her executor and sole heir — has now told me that he would rather I treated the money as my own, or gave it to him, and that in any case he releases me from whatever I undertook. I find that I do not believe him, and I would like a number rather than a verdict.

So: what is your credence that, when a promisee dies before performance, the promisor's obligation survives and attaches to whoever now stands in the promisee's place — executor, heir, or designated successor — with the consequence that only that person can release or redirect it? Take it that the promise was clear and voluntary and that my circumstances have not changed in any relevant way. I am at about 0.3; a colleague I have been arguing with about this is at about 0.8.

Please give me the number and its decomposition rather than a discussion: the sub-questions the answer turns on, each with a rough probability and a line of justification, an explicit account of how the terms combine (whether they are independent, and which are conditions rather than weights), and the resulting figure. The terms I would expect to see, though you should revise the list as you think right, are: whether a promise generates a claim that the promisee can alienate at will, or an essentially personal obligation that cannot be handed on; whether the practice of promising has constitutive rules that settle the question of assignment, and what its silence on the point implies; whether the content of what I promised was fixed by her interest in the cause or by her will that I be the one to do it; whether the dead can be wronged at all, or whether the apparent obligation is really to the living, to her memory, or to myself; whether the duty is grounded in the promisee's interest, which has ceased, or in her authority, which if exercised beforehand might survive her; and whether the default that death releases a promisor is a genuine moral rule or a legal convenience imported into morality by mistake. Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what she could have said or done before she died — or what fact about the cause or about her brother that I have not mentioned — would move your number most, and in which direction. Answer in British English, please.
