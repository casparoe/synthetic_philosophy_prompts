ok so my employer just made every single one of us sit through a two hour workshop — I think it was called "Disagreeing Well" or "Better Conversations," one of those two-word names, I honestly can't remember — and it's been eating at me for a week and I need to get it out of my system.

Here's what was in it. A rubric. Four questions you're supposed to run on the person you're arguing with:

1. the paraphrase test — can they state your position back to you in a form you'd actually sign your name to?
2. the swap test — would they accept that same arguement if it cut the other way?
3. the update test — can they name one specific thing that would change their mind?
4. the object test — are they arguing about the issue, or about the people who hold the other view?

And the facilitator spent like twenty minutes on the difference between sincerity and good faith. Sincere = you actually believe what you're saying. Good faith = your view is genuinely responsive to reasons. So you can be perfectly sincere and still not be in good faith, and — this is the bit I keep chewing on — you can be playing devil's advocate, not sincere at all, and still be in good faith.

Fine. Great, even. Except the workshop told us to run those four questions on the *other person*, live, mid-conversation, and then use the score to decide whether it was worth continuing to engage. And I've been doing that. I've been doing it for about three years. My brother-in-law and I used to actually enjoy arguing about politics and last Thanksgiving I caught myself keeping a little mental scorecard on him and I realized I hadn't actually heard anything he said for ten minutes, I was just running the tests. So. Yeah.

Write me the parody. I want the participant workbook for that workshop — like a facilitator's guide / handout combo, around 800 words, formatted like the actual materials: the welcome blurb, the four tests with examples, a "spot the bad faith" case study or two, and the quiz at the end with the answer key. Deadpan the whole way through, no winking, no "obviously this is absurd" asides, nothing that breaks the fiction that this is a real corporate document. The jokes have to come out of the rubric collapsing under its own weight — the mind-reading problem, the fact that all four tests were built as self-tests and are now being pointed outward at somebody whose head you can't see into, the way each criterion has an obvious counter (name a disconfirming fact and you're "performing open-mindedness," refuse to and you're "dogmatic," so there's no move that scores well). Keep it non-partisan — the real workshop was visibly terrified of naming any actual issue, so the parody should be too. If you want a margin-note voice for the facilitator, go ahead, but it has to stay as deadpan as the rest.

And commit. I don't want a both-sides shrug at the end. Have the closing slide make one definite claim about whether "they're not arguing in good faith" is ever a legitimate thing to conclude about another person, and have the trainer stand behind it with reasons, no hedging, no "but of course it's complicated."
