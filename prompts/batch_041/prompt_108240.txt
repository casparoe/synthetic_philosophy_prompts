I sit on the findings panel of a state-level accident investigation board. We investigate fatal fires, industrial deaths, and marine casualties, and each report ends in a Findings section that names the causes of the death. Our findings are read by bereaved families, are routinely subpoenaed in civil litigation, and are picked up in later criminal proceedings; the report is signed by the panel chair only after counsel has reviewed it. I need a procedure I can hand to an investigator with no philosophy training, one they can run in an afternoon with the file in front of them.

The background you need is that our standing vocabulary has three labels — cause, contributing factor, background condition — and we have never had a principled way of assigning them. The fights are always about omissions: no sprinkler system, no working smoke alarm, no inspection carried out, a safety recommendation made years ago and never implemented, a screening programme defunded, a forecast not checked, a device not fitted, a person not rescued. Half the panel holds that an omission can never be a cause, because a cause has to be something that happened; the other half thinks that view is simply a way of letting institutions off the hook. Counsel wants every finding phrased so that it cannot be read as a statement of legal liability.

Please write the procedure. I want the questions the investigator asks, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure terminates in "no causal finding" and where it terminates in "cause"; how it sorts the surviving candidates into the three labels; and, marked explicitly, the points where the procedure cannot decide and a person must, together with what kind of question that is — a question of fact, of jurisdiction, or of policy.

Three constraints. First, every causal finding must be stated contrastively, in the form "the death occurred because A rather than B," with the contrast made explicit and defended; tell me how the investigator is supposed to supply B and what to do when no plausible B exists. Second, where the candidate is an omission, the procedure must distinguish naming the negative fact (the absence of a working alarm) from naming the agent's failure (the landlord's failure to maintain it), and must say when the report should do which, given that the two carry different consequences downstream. Third, the procedure must say how to treat a candidate omission by an actor who was under no duty — no legal duty, no role-based duty, and no established practice of doing the thing — and a candidate omission by this board itself.

Then run it on three cases: a fatal house fire with several candidate omissions of different kinds, at least one of them lawful at the time; a death from a cancer that a defunded national screening programme would probably have caught; and a death that two independent omissions would each on its own have prevented. At least one of the three should come out in a way the panel will find unwelcome; say which, and why we should accept it.

Ground the procedure in the actual state of the philosophical debate about whether and when omissions cause — counterfactual analyses and their counterexamples, accounts that make a norm or a duty essential to counting an omission as a cause, contrastive accounts — and be explicit where that debate is unresolved, so I can see which steps rest on a contested view rather than on settled analysis. I do not need a literature review or a reading list; I need the tests and the reasons for them. Finish by saying how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, and which single step you are least confident about.
