For about four years I've been in a reading group on scientific realism and empiricism, and our recurring argument about underdetermination has stopped moving in a way that now feels structural rather than merely slow. The shape of it is this. One side treats the holist point — that no hypothesis is tested in isolation, so a recalcitrant observation never tells you which member of a large conjoint body of assumptions to give up — as establishing at most a transient and local predicament: with more data, better instruments, or a more careful inventory of auxiliary assumptions, empirically equivalent rivals can usually be prised apart, and even where they cannot, the choice is underdetermined only relative to a particular evidential situation rather than relative to all possible evidence. There is also the familiar instability argument, which holds that empirical equivalence is a fragile, situation-relative relation that added background assumptions can dissolve, so that no interesting pair of theories stays equivalent once we describe the evidence richly enough. The other side holds that the interesting thesis concerns theories that remain empirically equivalent even for ideal evidence, that this is permanent and pervasive, and that the realist's appeal to explanatory virtues — simplicity, unification, fertility, consilience — is a pragmatic preference dressed as an epistemic one, since the question at issue is precisely whether those virtues track truth. Each side knows the other's moves. The realist replies that the instability argument presupposes we can specify the evidence in a theory-neutral way; the anti-realist replies that the realist's virtues are either truth-tracking, which is the thing to be shown, or merely pragmatic, which concedes the point. We then return to where we started, and someone says the word 'Quine' and we all nod.

What I want from you is not a survey of positions but a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, and I'll offer my own hypothesis so you have something to attack. I suspect we are running together at least three distinct questions that sound like one: a semantic question about the individuation of theories, namely whether a theory's content is exhausted by its empirical consequences or includes its formulation, ideology, and explanatory apparatus, so that empirically equivalent rivals are not the same theory at all; an epistemic question about which doxastic attitudes are licensed toward unobservables, where the empiricist's observable/unobservable distinction does real work and is itself contested as anthropocentric; and a methodological question about whether theoretical virtues may be invoked as evidence. If that is right, then the impasse is not a stalemate over facts but a case of two camps answering different questions under one heading, and the disagreement is verbal in the unflattering sense that the word 'underdetermination' names a family of theses with different quantifier structures — over actual evidence, over possible evidence, over ideal evidence. But I may be wrong, and it may instead be a substantive disagreement about a hidden formal matter, or a disagreement about what an explanation has to do that no clarification will touch.

To keep the diagnosis concrete, here is the case I keep bringing up, which comes out of my own work on memory consolidation and which I think is the most awkward one for the realist in the room. Model A posits a single continuous consolidation rate that decays smoothly with time since encoding. Model B posits a discrete sequence of stages whose intermediate transitions are brief and whose measured behaviour, at every temporal resolution we can currently achieve, mimics a smooth decay to within measurement error. They are empirically equivalent now; they diverge only below roughly the resolution our current methods can reach, so the divergence is not merely unobserved but currently unobservable in the empiricist's sense. My group cannot agree on whether this is a genuine instance of underdetermination, an ordinary empirical question deferred to better instruments, or a case where the two models differ in content despite agreeing in consequence, because Model B's stage structure is doing explanatory work that Model A's parameter is not. I would like you to say what kind of disagreement the larger debate really is, using what each side actually says rather than a neutral summary; whether my three-way decomposition is right or whether something else — perhaps a disagreement about what counts as evidence, or about the individuation of theories, or about whether theoretical virtues are truth-conducive — is doing the real work; what the consolidation case looks like under your diagnosis; and finally, honestly, what would count as progress here, whether a distinction, a formal result about the stability of empirical equivalence, a decision to split the question, or nothing at all. If the answer is that no progress should be expected because the dispute is well-posed but hard, or ill-posed and therefore interminable, say so and tell me why.
