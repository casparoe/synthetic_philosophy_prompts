Ok, odd request, so hear me out.

I'm a retired chemist — 40 years in labs, spectroscopy mostly, then a long stretch in industry — and since I stopped working I've been teaching myself philosophy in the evenings, slowly. Which is how I got stuck on something called Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I keep seeing it cited as the grown-up, technical justification for utilitarianism, and I'd like to actually understand what the thing does before I decide whether I believe it.

Here's my understanding, and correct me if I've mangled it. You take each person's preferences over risky options and assume they satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms — the usual rationality conditions on how you rank lotteries. You assume there's a single "social" preference ordering over those same lotteries, satisfying the same axioms. You add a Pareto condition: if everyone prefers A to B, so does society. The theorem then says society's preferences must be representable as a weighted sum of everybody's individual utilities. And then if you imagine yourself behind a veil of ignorance, equally likely to end up as any of the people involved, the weights come out equal and everyone counts for one — at which point the Rawls people push back, because behind that same veil they say you shouldn't be maximising expected utility at all, you should be maximising the worst case, and then you don't get a sum, you get a floor. Someone on a forum also told me the theorem is really just a representation theorem: it mostly says IF a social ranking of lotteries with those properties exists THEN it's a weighted sum, and all the real work is smuggled into the assumption that such a ranking exists. I have no idea whether that's fair, but it sounds like the kind of thing that matters.
