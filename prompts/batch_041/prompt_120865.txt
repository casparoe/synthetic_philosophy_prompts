I was appointed to the grants committee of the Aldervale Foundation in March, and our allocation meeting is on the 14th of next month; the awards have to be announced before the fiscal year closes. This is the first cycle in which I have a vote, and the first in which I am the member asked to circulate the written opinion that frames the decision, so I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

The background you need. Our shortlist this year has five proposals and a fixed budget of $900,000. We cannot agree on a single ranking, and we never have. The committee's standing rule in such cases is what we call the split: each trustee ranks the shortlist individually, and the money is divided among the proposals that are somebody's first choice, in proportion to how many trustees put each one first. It was adopted before my time precisely to sidestep a question we could never answer — whose scale of value governs when we compare a program that averts deaths now against one that reduces a small risk of something much worse later. Under the split no trustee has to translate her values into another's units; the first-place votes stand in for the values. I have run a cruder version of the same rule on my own giving for three years, half to one thing and half to another, and I have begun to suspect that it survives only because the amounts are small.

This year the split yields $400,000 to the immunization catch-up campaign (four firsts), $300,000 to the clinic network (three firsts), $100,000 to the bioethics training program (one first), and $100,000 to the wastewater surveillance pilot (one first, Dr. N.). A fifth proposal — a surgical-capacity program that every trustee ranks second or third and no one ranks first — receives nothing. Dr. N. has attached a two-page annex arguing that her pilot has, on her estimate, roughly a one-in-two-hundred chance of catching a novel respiratory pathogen early enough to matter, and that the catastrophe it would avert is bad enough that the pilot's expected value exceeds our entire budget several times over. She also observes, without threatening anything, that the split rewards proposals rather than views: if one ally joined her in ranking a variant of the pilot first, essentially the same project would draw $200,000.

What I need in the opinion. First, a bottom line at the top: a specific allocation of the $900,000, a position on Dr. N.'s annex, and a recommendation about the split itself — keep, amend, or replace — reasoned enough that the other trustees can respond to it at the meeting. Second, an honest account of the strongest case against whatever you end up recommending, in particular Dr. N.'s, which I take to be that a committee which declines to follow the arithmetic is simply refusing to take a small chance of a very bad thing seriously, and the case for keeping the split, which is that it is the only procedure we have that does not smuggle one trustee's scale in as the common currency. Third, a short section on whether I am confusing two different things when I call this a problem of moral uncertainty: the committee is not uncertain, we disagree, and I have a suspicion that the split is a device for managing disagreement dressed up as a device for managing uncertainty — though I may be making a fuss about nothing. Fourth, a paragraph I can read out if the vote goes against me.

Practical terms: around 1,000 words, memo format, plain prose, no citations and no reading list — I want an argument, not a bibliography.

One more thing, and I mean it: if the framing I have given you is confused, or if the opinion I am asking for cannot honestly be written — because there is no way to compare these proposals without a common scale, or because the amendment I am fishing for is incoherent, or because nine trustees with nine moral outlooks should not be deciding this way at all — say so plainly rather than producing something smooth. Tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and where it is weakest. I would rather hand the chair a memo that names its own soft spot than one that papers over it.
