I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group this term, and we have been working through the underdetermination literature and the realism debates, but the discussion keeps collapsing into an argument that a friend of mine treats as decisive and that I half believe but cannot fully swallow. His case goes like this. Take any system whose internal states we cannot directly inspect — a physical system that we only ever touch through its measurements, a mind we only ever touch through behavior, or, as he likes to put it, a model whose weights are technically visible but whose "ontology" is not. Whatever accessible profile the system presents — its input-output behavior, its responses to every intervention we can perform — that profile is compatible with indefinitely many mutually incompatible internal ontologies: a lookup table, a bundle of bare dispositions, a field, a swarm of particles, a set of computational states. So long as the accessible behavior comes out the same, he says, there is nothing in principle to choose between them. From this he draws the conclusion that the information we would need in order to fix the ontology is permanently inaccessible, and therefore that there is no fact of the matter about what such a system really contains — that "identifying the ontology" is at bottom a matter of selecting whichever description is most convenient, with nothing to be right or wrong about. He regards this as a straightforward corollary of the arguments we have been reading, and he wants me to accept that the question "what is this system actually made of?" is not a hard question but an empty one.

I am inclined to agree with the first half of what he says and to resist the last step, but I cannot articulate why the last step does not simply follow, and I do not want to defend a strawman or a merely verbal objection. So what I would like from you is a diagnosis of the argument rather than a restatement of it. Does the inference from "we cannot access the information that would distinguish these candidate ontologies" to "there is no fact of the matter about the ontology" go through, or does it quietly substitute one claim for another — for instance, an epistemic limitation for a metaphysical indeterminacy, or underdetermination by the bare behavioral profile for underdetermination by everything we could bring to bear? And if the question is not empty after all, what exactly keeps it non-empty: what kinds of evidence or constraint, over and above the input-output record, could legitimately bear on identifying a system's ontology, and why does my friend's premise about permanently inaccessible information fail to rule those out? Please be concrete about the precise point at which the reasoning goes wrong, and about what a defensible version of his conclusion would look like once the confusion is removed.
