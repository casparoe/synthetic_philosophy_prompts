In Plato's Laws, books 1 and 2, the Athenian ends up defending wine-drinking as both a test of character and a kind of training in self-control: get someone tipsy and you find out what they're really like, and repeated exposure in a supervised symposium supposedly makes you better at handling fear, desire, and shame. I want a clear explanation of the conceptual issue here, not a summary of the dialogue. Specifically: are the diagnostic and the therapeutic uses of wine really compatable, or does one under-cut the other? If the point of the drinking party is to reveal the drinker's true self, then the test seems to measure the very disposition the practice is supposed to improve, so the baseline keeps moving and it's unclear what's actually being tested. And if the practice is supposed to build self-control, it seems to presuppose the self-control needed to take part in it responsibly, plus the Laws wants sober commanders overseeing the un-sober, which just pushes the question back: who tests them? Explain how the Athenian might escape this, and say whether the escape works. Also bring in the age rules (no wine before eighteen, moderation until thirty, full symposia at forty) -- do those help resolve the tension or make it worse?
