Ok, so I'm in a seminar on Lewis's stuff about convention and we keep doing examples in class, and I've noticed my gut verdicts on what counts as a convention don't obviously come from any single rule, so what I want is the principle (or a couple of candidate principles) that actually captures them, plus an honest note on which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to keep it. Here's my running list. Driving on the right: convention, obviously, no question. A village where everyone signals a left turn with the same little wrist-flick — it started because the schoolteacher who taught everybody to drive had a tic and the first students copied her, and now the flick is just what you do, everyone expects it, and you'd get a weird look if you didn't: I still want to call that a convention, and the fact that nobody chose it or agreed to it doesn't bother me in the slightest. Now a dorm where everyone drinks way more than most of them actually want to, because each one thinks everyone else is having a great time and doesn't want to be the buzzkill — there's a regularity, everyone conforms, everyone would rather conform than stand out, and if you polled them privately they'd all rank the quiet version higher. My gut says that is not a convention, it's some other kind of thing, and I can't immediately say what fails. Those two cases feel like they should get the same verdict and they don't, and it's driving me a bit nuts. Then: everyone in a town walks on the north side of the street in summer. If they do it because it's the shady side and they'd do it no matter what anyone else did, I say not a convention, just a coincidence of interest. But if both sides are equally shady and the north side is simply where the crowd is, and I walk north partly because that's where the people are, then it starts feeling like a convention, which maybe means my rule is doing more work than I thought.

A few more that I keep going back and forth on, in case they matter. Suppose a law is passed requiring the wrist-flick, with a fine for not doing it — does the legal backing make it stop being a convention, or is it just a convention with a fine attached? And does it change anything that driving on the right is also legally required? A couple who always go to the same restaurant on Fridays, not because either prefers it but because it's become the thing they do: convention for two people, or too thin a population to count? A town where tipping used to be obligatory and is now maybe sixty percent: mid-collapse, is there a convention or just a residue of one? A bilingual village where the greeting language is switching over one generation, so right now the old people greet in one language and the young in the other and everybody adjusts to whoever they're talking to: is that one convention, two, or a transitional mess with no convention at all? And a rule everyone follows that nobody could actually state if you asked them why — does the inability to articulate it matter?

My first stab at a rule was this: it's a convention if the behaviour is arbitrary, everyone does it, and everyone would go on doing it if the opposite had gotten started first instead. That gets driving and the wrist-flick, but the dorm case satisfies it and I really don't want it to, and the equally-shady-street case fails the arbitrariness bit yet I want to let it in. So tell me: what principle or small set of principles gets the most of my verdicts, which clause of it is doing the work for which case, what it predicts about cases I haven't listed (a convention between exactly two people, a regularity nobody realises others are also following, one that's half-decayed), and which of my verdicts I should just drop. I'd much rather you tell me straight which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on conventions — if there's a real split about whether Lewis-style conventions need common knowledge, or whether pluralistic-ignorance-type norms belong in the same bin at all, say that instead of smoothing it into one confident story. My roommate says I'm overthinking it but I don't think I am.
