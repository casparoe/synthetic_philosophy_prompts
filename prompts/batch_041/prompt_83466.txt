I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day public forum in my city, and I would like you to write them. The occasion: a longtime resident has left the city an unexpected bequest, and the will names two possible uses without choosing between them. One is a professional theater, with a resident company, a subscription season, and a new building on the old market site. The other is a permanent fund for free public festivities in the squares and parks, with money for music, dancing, and amateur productions that anyone may join. The council has convened the forum to hear the public out, and I am the chair of the citizens' committee, so my opening remarks set the terms of the two days.

What makes this hard is that I am, by temperament and conviction, on Rousseau's side of this question, and everyone in the room knows it. I have read his letter against d'Alembert's proposal for a theater in Geneva, and it convinced me. But I do not want to stand up and deliver a sermon against the stage. I want a speech that takes his argument seriously enough to find out what, if anything, of it still applies here, and that leaves the theater people in the audience — some of whom have given their working lives to the craft — feeling addressed rather than scolded.

Some things I need the speech to get right, because two Rousseau specialists will be on the first panel and I do not want to be corrected from the floor.

First, his objection is not the popular one. He is not chiefly saying that plays have wicked content and will teach the audience wickedness. His worry is about the institution: what it does to a community to have a class of people who are paid to simulate feelings they do not have, and to have everyone else assembled in the dark to watch them. The audience is moved by passions it has no stake in and cannot act on, and comes away with the pleasant sense of having felt something noble without having done anything. A city that acquires a taste for this acquires a need for it. The taste is not for vice so much as for being a spectator.

Second, he has a positive alternative, and it is not austerity for its own sake. It is festivity in the open air, where the lines between performers and audience dissolve and everyone is both. That is the part I care most about, and it is why the second option in the bequest appeals to me.

Third, his argument is local, not universal. He is answering a proposal for one particular small city, and much of his case rests on facts about that city — its size, its industry, its climate, the character of its inhabitants. He is willing to grant that in a great capital, where people are already crowded and already corrupt, a theater might serve as a distraction from worse things. So the argument cannot simply be repeated here; it has to be re-made, and I need the speech to do that work out loud rather than assuming it transfers.

Fourth, one strand of his case rests on claims about women, public influence, and the proper order of the sexes that most of this room will reject, and I do not want to smuggle those claims in under a friendlier name or to pretend he never made them. If there is a usable insight underneath — something about how a theater creates a public whose real currency is being seen and judged — I want it separated out and stated in its own right, and the rest set aside honestly.

Now the complications that make this city not Geneva, which I want the speech to confront rather than glide past. This city is a weekend destination. Perhaps a third of the people who would buy theater subscriptions live elsewhere and come here in summer. The public that would fill a new auditorium is not a stable body of neighbors who will see each other again on Monday; it is partly transient, and the theater would depend on that transience to survive. Meanwhile the festival fund would almost certainly be livestreamed, because the council will insist on it for the people who cannot come out — which means that even the alternative I prefer would create an audience of watchers at home, and I need the speech to be honest about that rather than claim a purity it does not have. And the professional company, if we build it, would not be a caste of strangers: some of its members grew up here, and several of the strongest advocates in the room tonight are among them.

Please write the opening remarks. They should run about eight minutes spoken aloud, in a register suitable for a mixed public audience — plain sentences, no jargon, nothing that requires a footnote. They should not quote or cite anything; if you want to put one of Rousseau's thoughts in your own words, do it plainly and without attributing a specific phrase to him. They should not lecture the audience about Rousseau, but they should be recognizably his in their instincts, and the specialists should be able to hear that I have not caricatured him. They must land on a clear recommendation between the two uses of the bequest, and I would rather be disagreed with than vague. Do not end by asking the audience to keep an open mind; I will have said what I think by the time I sit down.
