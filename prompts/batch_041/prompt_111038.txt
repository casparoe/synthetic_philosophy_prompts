Hey, I run a scrappy little history-of-philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher with a pretty revisionist take on Hume's essay on miracles. His line is that everyone reads it wrong. The standard reading is that Hume weighs the testimony for the miracle against the standing improbability of the event and says the testimony basically never wins — a probabilistic, evidential point. He thinks that's not it at all: on his reading the argument is meant to be a priori and airtight, because to call something a miracle you already have to classify it as a breach of a law of nature, and a law of nature just is an exceptionless regularity, so a miracle report presupposes the very uniformity it denies and can never even get onto the scales. He also likes to say that "the Deity did it" is a catch-all — a hypothesis with no positive content — so it can never outcompete a specific law. The balancing talk and the Indian prince, on his view, are just rhetorical concessions Hume knew he didn't need.
