I'm a backend engineer with no real philosophy training beyond a couple of podcasts, and I've got stuck on something that started as a pub argument and has now eaten two evenings. It's about dispositions — the way we say a glass is fragile, or sugar is soluble, even when the glass is sitting there unbroken and the sugar is dry in the jar. The folk story is that "fragile" just means "would break if struck", and I can see straight away that it's too simple: there are cases where the thing would be struck, would still have the disposition, and yet wouldn't break because something is in the way, and other cases where striking it is precisely what takes the disposition away. Apparantly people call the first sort masks and the second sort finks. Fine. What I can't find is the spec. Every theory I've half-read seems built to survive one counterexample and then quietly falls over on the next, and I'd rather know what the thing has to do before I judge any of them. So no names, no reading list, I can't be arsed with any of that — just the requirements.

Give me the desiderata for a theory of dispositions: the phenomena it has to account for, the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it mustn't collapse, and the background facts it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and a call on whether it's non-negotiable or just something one camp happens to like. Three cases I'd want on the list, because I can't get them straight: (1) a pane of glass with a self-healing layer bonded into the glass itself, so that when you hit it the layer stiffens and it doesn't break — the blocker isn't something wrapped around it, it's part of the very thing we want to call fragile; (2) a pane sealed inside an indestructible shell, so it can never break, ever, in any circumstances — has it stopped being fragile, or is it still fragile and simply never able to show it, and if the latter, what is the difference supposed to consist in; (3) a model whose weights get updated the moment you query it, so that asking what it would output for a given input changes what it would have output — the test itself removes the disposition, and nothing outside the thing is doing it. I'd also like to know what makes one thing more fragile than another, since that's a claim we make constantly and it isn't obvious what grounds it.

The bit I most want is the tension: which of your requirements can't all be satisfied at once, and why. My suspicion is that "a thing's dispositions are intrinsic to it" and "we can always tell a masked disposition from a lost one" pull in opposite directions, but I don't trust my own nose here. Finish by saying which single requirement rules out the most otherwise-plausible theories, and don't bother scoring them all — that part I can do myself.
