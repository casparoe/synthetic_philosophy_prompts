Four years ago I retired from a career in invertebrate sensory physiology and, having nothing better to do with my mornings, began taking graduate courses in philosophy; next month I am presenting a paper to a joint seminar hosted by the philosophy department and the veterinary school's animal welfare group at my local university. The thesis I will defend is that sentience admits of degrees and that moral status should be graded accordingly, but that the gradations are not a single scalar: I argue they vary independently along at least three dimensions, namely the integration of noxious input into a global state rather than a bundle of local reflexes, the presence of a felt valence that functions as a goal for the animal rather than as a mere signal, and temporal reach, by which I mean the extent to which an animal's welfare stretches into past and future through memory, anticipation, and the capacity for violated expectation. These dimensions come apart, I claim: a creature may integrate richly while living almost entirely in the present, another may have thin sensory integration but a long reach forward, and I therefore refuse to collapse them into one index of how sentient a thing is. On my view a thermostat has at most a degenerate form of the first, an insect plausibly has the first and some portion of the second, a fish has all three though with a shorter temporal reach than a mammal, and a dog or a person has all three at high values. I also claim that the evidence for these dimensions is very unevenly distributed — we have tolerable evidence for integration, poor evidence for valence, and almost nothing for temporal reach in small invertebrates — and that this asymmetry should be handled by a rule of evidential humility that assigns weight according to the highest plausible value of a dimension whenever the costs of error are lopsided. I take the practical upshot to be real: anesthetizing a shrimp before killing it is not obviously absurd, while switching off a thermostat is not a moral event at all.

The audience is a mixture I am frankly not prepared for. At least two Kantians will be there, holding that moral status is a threshold matter bound up with something like rational agency, and they will hear any talk of degrees as an assault on the dignity of persons; one or two utilitarians will be sympathetic to gradation but will want a common currency in which the grades can be added and traded off; the veterinary anesthetists and welfare scientists will care chiefly about what any of this obliges them to do on Monday morning, and will be skeptical of inferences from physiology to felt experience; and a roboticist from the engineering school, who builds artificial agents with preference-like internal states, will press me on why my criteria, taken seriously, do not already include some of his creations. I have never had to answer that combination at once.

Please draft my question-period prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise — ordered by how much damage they do to my position rather than by how likely they are to be voiced — each stated in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it and attributed to the likely questioner, each followed by a two-minute spoken reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, and identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to be taken seriously at all. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks; I have a suspicion about which one it is, but I would rather you tell me. Write in American English, and do not send me to any literature — I am asking you to reason from the positions themselves, not to point me at what others have written.
