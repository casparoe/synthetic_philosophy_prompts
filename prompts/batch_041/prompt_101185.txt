I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been reading my way into epistemology on my own. A colleague who studied philosophy as an undergraduate gave me the following summary of the dispute between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony, and I wrote it down as he put it:

"Reductionism about testimony, which goes back to Hume, is the view that you should never accept anything on someone's word alone: a belief based on what you are told is justified only if you can support it with your own observation and with your own past experience of how reliable the speaker, or people in general, have turned out to be. Anti-reductionism, which goes back to Reid, holds the opposite -- that testimony is a basic source of knowledge, like perception or memory, so you are entitled to believe what you are told unless you have some specific reason to doubt it; and in fact the reductionist programme is incoherent, since you could never justify all testimony by non-testimonial evidence without already relying on testimony to learn the language in which you would conduct the check. The trouble with reductionism is that no one can personally verify everything they are told, so it demands a standard of checking that no human being could possibly meet; that is why the reductionist position is now widely considered untenable. But the anti-reductionist cure looks worse than the disease, because if belief is the default then the anti-reductionist has to accept whatever a confident stranger asserts and has no basis for criticising someone who believes everything they read. Underneath the technical vocabulary, then, the whole debate is really about how far we should trust other people, and the anti-reductionist cannot have the last word, since it is obvious that we should not trust everyone equally."

My colleague is usually reliable, but the way this is framed strikes me as suspicious in places, and I do not know enough to say where the problems are.
