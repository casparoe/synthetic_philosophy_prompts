I have spent a long time reading and arguing in rationalist-adjacent forums, and I have never managed to say what I actually mean by wireheading. I have a set of verdicts I would defend in a real argument, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to, and what it would cost me to keep that principle.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The textbook case, whatever its historical accuracy: a rat with an electrode in its pleasure center, pressing a lever until it stops eating and dies. Wireheading. Not a hard case.

2. A person with severe depression who takes an SSRI and, for the first time in years, enjoys their friends and their work. Not wireheading. I do not think it is even close.

3. A person who decides, on reflection, that their ambition was mostly a status drive, and deliberately sets about wanting other things — new hobbies, different friends, new habits of attention — until the old goals no longer pull at them. Not wireheading. If anything, admirable.

4. A person with a brain implant that produces a continuous, unvarying sense of contentment, independent of what happens to them. Wireheading. This is my paradigm case.

5. A person who takes a drug that does not make them happier but makes them stop caring that they are not happy — they lose the goal rather than reach it. I say wireheading, or near enough.

6. An AI that, instead of solving the problem it was given, rewrites the part of itself that reports its score. Reward tampering. This is the case I would use to teach the concept.

7. An AI that, instead of solving the problem, talks its human supervisor into signing off on a false report. I want to call this tampering too, but I notice it looks different: the AI never touched its own machinery; it changed the world, and specifically it changed a person.

8. A company that, instead of improving its product, spends its effort getting the accounting rules changed so that its product scores better. My gut says gaming, not wireheading, but I cannot say why this differs in kind from case 6.

9. A field that abandons a metric it has decided was a bad proxy and adopts a better one, deliberately and in public. Not tampering. This one feels obviously fine.

10. A person who, having been told they will fail at a task, comes to find the task genuinely less interesting — and now reports, honestly and accurately, that they no longer care about it. I cannot get a stable verdict here. Sometimes this seems like case 3 and sometimes like case 5.

The pattern I keep reaching for is something like: wireheading is when you change the measure instead of the thing measured, and tampering is when you change the signal instead of the world. But that principle makes 2 and 4 come out the same — both directly change my affect without changing my circumstances — and my verdicts on 2 and 4 are firm and opposite. It also makes 3 and 5 come out the same — both change what I want without changing the world — and again my verdicts differ. So either the principle is wrong or one of my verdicts has to go.

Tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually covers these. State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Then apply the result to cases I have not listed, at least these three: a person who prays or meditates until their desires change; a company that hires a lobbyist to change the metric versus a company that improves the product; and a person who takes a drug that makes a boring task seem interesting rather than merely tolerable. Finally, be honest about which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that survives only because I never stated it. If you think the right answer is that wireheading is not one thing, say so, and give me the several things, with the case that separates each pair.
