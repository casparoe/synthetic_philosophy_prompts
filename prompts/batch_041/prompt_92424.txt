After a long career in the natural sciences, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I have lately been going to what I can only call the theater of the mind. I would like you to write me a review — in the deadpan register of a newspaper arts column, roughly 500 words — of two rival productions that are, awkwardly, playing in the same house.

The first, The Cartesian Theater, stages the familiar picture: a lighted stage on which the contents of experience appear, and somewhere in the dark an audience — a little person, or a committee of them — whose watching is what makes the show mean anything. The second, Multiple Drafts, has no fixed stage and no single curtain call; scenes are being rewritten all over the building at once, and what we call the performance is only whichever revision has, for the moment, attracted enough notice to be reported in the press.

The comedy should come from getting the philosophy right rather than from inventing it: the regress that opens up the instant you ask who is seated in the audience; the puzzle of dating a scene that appears to have occurred before the process that produced it; the way a finish line gets quietly smuggled back in the moment no one is watching for it. Keep it legible to a reader who has never heard either term.

I would like the review to reach a verdict, not to hedge: say which production is the honest one and which is a comfortable illusion, and give your reasons for the judgment. Where the piece takes liberties with the ideas, or leans on a reading that is genuinely contested, add a short note afterward telling me which jokes are load-bearing and which are merely decorative.
