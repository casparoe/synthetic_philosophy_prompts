But I'm not satisfied with that, and I want to twist it in a way that's philosophically interesting rather than just spooky.

Here's the twist. Suppose the correct reference class isn't all humans, but all beings that can be surprised by their own existence, i.e. creatures with a felt sense of being an observer. And suppose the sampling isn't uniform over birth ranks. Instead, observer-moments are sampled with a probability proportional to how narratively central they feel to the life they're part of, so a moment that feels like the climax of a story counts more than a moment that feels like set dressing. I know that sounds hand-wavy, but I want you to take it seriously as a formal-ish variant. Maybe formalize it as a weighted self-sampling assumption, or as a reference class defined by reflexive surprise plus a salience bias. Start by brainstorming two or three ways to make the twist precise, then tell me the most important implications of the variant you find most defensible.

Please take the role of a philosopher who specializes in anthropics, decision theory, and the ethics of future generations. Think carefully, and don't just regurgitate the standard doomsday argument, because I already know it. What changes for how much credence we should give to humanity being in its final century? What changes for the ethics of having children or creating new observers? And what changes for how I should write a character who uses this argument to justify either hope or despair? Please be concrete, and flag where the twist makes the argument weaker or stronger. Also, if the whole thing collapses into incoherence, tell me that too.
