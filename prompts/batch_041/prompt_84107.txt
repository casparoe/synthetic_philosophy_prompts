I am a high school junior who sits in on the meetings of our school's philosophy club, mostly to listen, and last week I watched two seniors argue for the entire hour about how a superintelligent AI ought to speak to human beings. I stayed out of it then, and I am not asking you to take my side; I want the argument they actually made adjudicated, move by move.

Ana argued that because a superintelligent system's powers of argument and explanation would vastly exceed any human's, its audience could never tell whether they were persuaded by the reasons or merely by the competence of the reasoner. She said this asymmetry is structural rather than a comment on anyone's intelligence, and that it imposes a duty of restraint, which she called the deference discount: the system should deliberately hold back, understating its conclusions, presenting them as one option among several, and declining to deploy the full case it could make, in proportion to how much of the audience's agreement it predicts would be owed to its superior rhetoric rather than to the evidence. She compared it to a grandmaster playing a child, where the point is not to win.

Ben replied that holding back is itself a way of managing people, and that an agent which says less than it believes in order to produce a predetermined amount of influence is manipulating its audience rather than informing them. He said it is also paternalistic, since it treats human beings as too fragile to be told what the system actually thinks, and dangerous in the cases where the system's honest judgment is that a plan will fail. The honest alternative, he said, is transparency: state the full case, state your confidence, and warn the audience that fluency is not evidence, so that they can do the discounting themselves.

Ana answered that he was conflating what is asserted with how it is asserted, and that a discount need not involve a single false statement, since it operates on register, emphasis, pacing, and what one declines to say, none of which is a proposition that could be true or false. She added that telling someone they are probably being swayed does not undo the sway, because the warning arrives through the same channel that is doing the swaying, and that in any case an audience cannot consent to terms it cannot assess.

Ben rejoined that a warning is still more auditable than silence about it, and that the deference discount requires the system to decide, unilaterally and invisibly, how much influence is appropriate, a decision of enormous consequence that no human could check. He also said the discount assumes the system knows its own persuasive effect better than the human does, so if it is wrong about that, the discount becomes a lie about its confidence.

Ana's final point was that there is no neutral option: choosing not to discount is also an intervention, since the system still decides what to say, in what order, and when to stop, so transparency is a style too.

Please adjudicate this point by point, rating each of the moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, rather than only telling me who wins overall. I am especially interested in four things. First, whether the deference discount as Ana formulated it is coherent, or whether it collapses into ordinary honesty about uncertainty. Second, whether her structural-asymmetry argument establishes a duty of restraint or only a reason for caution, and whether the duty, if it exists, scales with the stakes. Third, whether Ben's transparency proposal escapes her final objection or is caught by it. Fourth, whether the disagreement is ultimately empirical, about how influence actually works, or conceptual, about what honesty and manipulation consist in, and if it is mixed, which parts are which.

Both of them kept returning to one case, so please say how each of their positions would handle it: a superintelligent system is asked by a seventeen-year-old whether to leave school for a job that the system believes is a serious mistake, and the system is confident it could talk the student out of it. Finally, a third member of the club, Sofia, suggested something neither of them took up, namely that the real problem is not the system's persuasiveness but a monopoly on articulate advocacy, so that the remedy is a plurality of comparably capable interlocutors rather than any restraint by the system. Please say whether that is a genuine third position that dissolves the dispute or merely a change of subject, and close by saying what both Ana and Ben are missing.
