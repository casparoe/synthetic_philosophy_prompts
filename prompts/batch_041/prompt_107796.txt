A colleague in tech policy forwarded me an explainer on futarchy — the governance scheme usually credited to Robin Hanson, though I have that attribution secondhand — and asked whether it had persuaded me. I have read it twice and cannot tell how much of it is the actual proposal and how much is a popularizer's compression. What I want is an audit, sentence by sentence: what someone who has thought seriously about the mechanism design would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply not the proposal.

The explainer runs roughly as follows. 'Futarchy replaces elections with prediction markets. Instead of voting, citizens bet on which policies will work, and whatever the market favors becomes law. The bet is a simple one — will this policy succeed? — and the market's verdict is what gets implemented. This should outperform legislatures, because speculative markets aggregate information better than polls, expert panels, or official forecasts; the record on racetrack odds, election betting, and corporate sales forecasts is the evidence. Payouts are tied to what actually happens, so the incentives are aligned and no one has to be trusted. Critics object that deep-pocketed speculators could rig the market, but the objection is self-refuting: a manipulator who distorts prices is handing money to arbitrageurs. And it is no longer purely theoretical — several blockchain organizations now use decision markets to decide which proposals to fund. So futarchy is essentially prediction markets applied to politics; given how thin, manipulable, and legally awkward real prediction markets are, it is not a serious rival to democracy.'

That last sentence is the part I actually want adjudicated, but I cannot judge it until I know which of the earlier claims survive. Three things in particular. First, is the explainer right that the market is betting on which policies will work, or is the market doing something narrower, with a different input supplied from elsewhere? Second, the slogan I keep seeing attached to the idea is "vote on values, but bet on beliefs," and I cannot square that with the explainer's picture of markets deciding everything — which of the two descriptions should I trust? Third, the claim that payout-on-what-actually-happens aligns the incentives: is that straightforward, or does it conceal a problem that people who work on these mechanisms argue about?

For what it is worth, I lean toward believing the explainer: the market-beats-pundits point seems well established, and I do not see why government would be different. Please be concrete about where the compression has cost it accuracy — which sentences are faithful, which are standard shorthand that a proponent would still sign off on, and which a proponent would call a misdescription of their own view. Then say whether the closing conclusion follows from the parts that survive correction, and whether any of the errors run in the opposite direction, that is, whether anything the explainer presents as a strength is in fact contested among people who are sympathetic to the idea. Assume I understand how a betting market works but that I have no way to look anything up, so explain any mechanism you invoke rather than pointing me to a paper.
