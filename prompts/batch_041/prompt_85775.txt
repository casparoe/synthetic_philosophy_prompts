For the past few weeks a reading group I'm in has been working through the literature on determinism in spacetime theories, and one of the members has spent the whole time trying to convince us that the standard argument in that literature is an artefact of how it is set up. He put this on the board and I copied it down nearly verbatim, because I genuinely cannot tell whether I am looking at a reductio or at a shell game. (1) A theory is deterministic just in case any two of its models that agree on the complete state on a spacelike slice agree everywhere. (2) A model of general relativity is a manifold of points equipped with a metric, and any diffeomorphism of that manifold which is the identity outside some bounded region carries solutions to solutions. (3) So take a slice lying to the past of such a region, and a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside the region and not the identity inside it. The two models agree point for point on the slice, since the same points there carry the same metrical properties; and they disagree in the interior, where the same point carries different metrical properties. (4) If the points of spacetime are genuine individuals, so that it is a fact of the matter which point is which independently of any properties the points bear, then these are two distinct futures sharing a single past, and general relativity is indeterministic. (5) A theory that fails to determine the future is defective, so the antecedent in (4) must be false: spacetime points are not genuine individuals, and substantivalism is refuted. That much I follow. I also know the standard ways out — identify diffeomorphic models, count only diffeomorphism-invariant quantities as physical, retreat to some sophisticated form of substantivalism — and I have a rough sense of what each of them costs.

What I cannot handle is the second half of his case. He says: (6) nothing in (1) through (5) turns on anything peculiar to general relativity; the argument requires only a theory whose models admit transformations that are the identity outside a region and not the identity inside it. (7) So run it on Newtonian mechanics with absolute space. Take a solution and shift every body one metre in absolute space, applying the shift at every time after some fixed moment t0 and not before. The laws are invariant under spatial translations, so the shifted history is as much a solution as the original; the two histories agree on every body's position and velocity at t0, and disagree about absolute position at every later time. If absolute space is a genuine individual, those are two different futures from a single past, and Newtonian mechanics is indeterministic — which is absurd. (8) Therefore the argument form is broken, and if it is broken for Newtonian mechanics it is broken for general relativity as well: the hole argument proves nothing and substantivalism is untouched. (9) He adds that one can run the same trick on the vector potential in electromagnetism, or on the global phase of a quantum state, and get the same absurdity.

I would like to keep substantivalism if I honestly can, and I would certainly like to keep determinism for the theories where everyone agrees it holds; I suspect I cannot have everything, but I want an itemised bill rather than a gesture. So: which step or steps do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If more than one can be rejected, tell me which is cheapest and what each rejection does to the rest — in particular to the treatment of gauge freedom in electromagnetism, to the claim that Newtonian mechanics is deterministic, and to whether a physical theory is in the business of describing possibilities at all or only equivalence classes of them. And I would like to know whether the trick really generalises the way he says it does, or whether it only works for theories of a certain kind, and what that kind is.
