My half-formed idea. Lewis's analysis of convention strikes me as two separable claims that the secondary literature tends to run together. The first is a solution claim: a convention is a regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem, where which regularity it is, is arbitrary — driving on the left would have served just as well — but is fixed for the group by salience and by precedent. The second is an epistemic claim: the regularity is sustained because the relevant expectations and preferences are common knowledge among the parties. My suspicion is that the epistemic claim is doing less explanatory work than it appears to. Once we attend to how conventions are actually acquired and transmitted — locally, by imitation, by learners conditioning on what a handful of neighbours happen to do — common knowledge starts to look like a limiting case of something weaker (mutual belief of some finite depth, plus a disposition to treat precedent as salient) rather than a precondition for a convention to exist. And if that is right, I want to know what becomes of the standard objection that an analysis like Lewis's cannot account for the ought in a norm. Perhaps the missing piece is not normativity but stability: a regularity that is arbitrary and held up only by precedent should be vulnerable to invasion by even a few agents who follow the rule for its own sake, or who sanction deviations, and that suggests that the evolution of norms might systematically convert conventions into things with a different structure — things that look more like enforced norms than like Lewis conventions.
