Write a dialogue between two philosophers walking home from the funeral of a colleague who died at forty. One is an Epicurean: death is nothing to the one who dies, because harm requires a subject who exists at the time the harm lands, and at no time are the dead around to be harmed. The other is a deprivationist: death is bad because it cuts off the goods the person would otherwise have gone on to enjoy. Both are sharp, neither is a strawman, and each must answer the other's actual last point rather than pivot to a prepared speech or a canned tour of the textbook moves.

Push the argument into the joints where it hurts. The deprivationist has to say when the harm occurs and to whom, given that before death nothing is yet missing and after death no one is there to be worse off; the Epicurean has to explain why the failure to locate the harm counts against it, rather than showing that some harms simply are not located in time. Let two test cases do real work: the asymmetry between dreading the years we will not see and shrugging at the years we never saw, and a person whose life's work is betrayed after their death — the Epicurean should press on whether that harms anyone at all, and the deprivationist should be made to say whether the dead person's thwarted wishes matter and, if so, how that differs from deprivation.

End with one of them having genuinely won the exchange on the central question, then a short paragraph in your own voice stating which side you think has the better of it, why, and the concession that most damages the side you land on.
