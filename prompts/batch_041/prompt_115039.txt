I write about science and ideas for a monthly mag, and next Thursday I'm recording a long interview — podcast first, then it runs as a Q&A — with a decision theorist who's whole thing is 'superrationality': the idea that when you and the other players all know you'd reason your way to the same answer, you should pick whatever's best for all of you at once, rather than whatever's best for you given what the others do. She thinks that should be the default way to think about one-shot games, not a party trick.

To give the interview some teeth, I've talked the magazine into running a stunt while we record, and I want the questions to keep yanking her back to it. Here's the game: we put $25,000 in escrow. Anyone can buy tickets at $2 a pop, as many as they want, and at the end one ticket gets picked at random and that person takes the whole $25,000. Ticket money doesn't go into the pot — it goes to the magazine, or to charity, whatever, just not back to the players. So if nobody buys a ticket, nobody wins anything but nobody's out anything either; if you're the only one who buys, you've basically got $25,000 for two bucks; and if everyone buys a stack, we're all shoveling money into a hole. We're still arguing about whether to put a live ticker on the page showing how many tickets have sold so far.

Give me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want: one that makes her say out loud what she'd personally do in our game and defend it; one on the standard complaint that 'we'd all reason the same way' is a fantasy when the other players are a million strangers who've never heard of the puzzle — and whether her view just collapses into ordinary expected-value reasoning with an odd prior about other people; one on the ticker, and whether her answer changes once people can see the running total, and what that does to the idea that there's a single right answer; one on whether superrationality is a claim about what's rational or about what's right, because if it's the second one I want to know what's supposed to separate it from just telling people to universalize their maxims; and one on the machine version — if every entrant were the same model running the same code, is her answer different, and if so, is the theory secretly about transparency rather than about reasoning? She's slippery, and her move whenever the idealization gets pressed is some version of 'that's an objection to the idealization, not to the theory', so build the sequence so that gets harder and harder to pull. Land on one question about what evidence or result would definately change her mind. Skip the how-did-you-get-into-this stuff, and keep them short enough to actually ask out loud.
