For the past year I have been writing a novel with two central characters, a Dutch diplomat and a Japanese translator, and I have been writing their dialogue under a rule that I now need either repaired or replaced. My editor gave me the rule, and it is this: politeness is a single dial running from plain truth at one end to the white lie at the other; the Dutch are set near the first end, the Japanese near the second; the novelist's only job is to decide, exchange by exchange, how far to turn the dial. I have taken it literally. The diplomat's lines are all true, however rude they sound; the translator's lines are all false, however gentle they sound; and whenever a scene needs softening, I reach for a lie. Two hundred pages in, the Dutchman has become a man incapable of tact and the translator a woman incapable of honesty, and I find them both tedious. So I would like the dial repaired if it can be repaired, and replaced if it cannot, and I would like the replacement to be something I can actually use at a desk.

Two things nag at me and I would like them settled rather than waved away. First, I have begun to suspect that most of what I have been calling polite evasion consists of saying nothing false at all, that my translator's careful remarks are usually true as stated and merely lead the hearer somewhere, and that I have been confusing the act of asserting a falsehood with the act of getting someone to believe one. If that distinction is real, tell me what it does to the dial, and whether a novelist can make a character tactful without ever making her lie. I care about this beyond craft. If softening a blow really does require a falsehood, then good manners are a species of dishonesty, and I would rather not have spent four years writing a book that quietly says so.

Second, I spent six years in Rotterdam, and I no longer believe the Dutch are simply parked at the honest end of a shared scale. The directness I heard there did not strike me as the absence of manners; it struck me as a different set of manners, one that signals equality and treats the hearer as someone who can take a plain answer, and it had its own indirections for the occasions when plainness would be cruel. The translator, for her part, seems to be doing something else again: many of her evasions are not improvisations at all but fixed moves whose meaning everyone in the room knows exactly, so that very little is actually hidden. Is the difference between these two habits of speech a difference in how much gets said, or in who is expected to work out what is meant, or in whether the hearer is permitted to pretend not to understand? And is there a sense in which the translator's settled indirections are more honest than the Dutchman's plainness rather than less?

I am not asking for a reading list; I would have to take the scholarship on trust in any case, so give me the argument and not the citations, and invent your own examples where you need one. Please write in American English.
