my reading group is doing social choice this term and we've got ourselves stuck on the discursive dilemna. you know the one: every single person in the group is internally consistent, but the group isn't. take a majority vote on each of the two premises and you get one verdict, take a majority vote on the conclusion and you get the opposite one, and there's no non-arbitrary way of saying which vote was the real one. the stock example is three judges on liability: two of them find there was a valid contract and a breach, so they'd hold the defendant liable, the third finds neither, so she wouldn't. each premise carries 2-1, but liability itself only carries 1-2. the crucial bit is that the conclusion genuinely follows from the premises, if the three questions were unrelated nothing would go wrong.

i want to open the session with a poem about it, 15 lines: three quatrains, one per judge, then a closing three-liner for the group. each quatrain has to be a sealed, coherent little world of its own, a valid argument, with rhymes that close inside the stanza and don't leak into the next one, so that each one reads as someone being perfectly reasonable. the last three lines are the group's position, which nobody actually holds: the first two should be the premise-by-premise verdict, the third the conclusion-only verdict, and the rhyming there should stop resolving without it turning into a joke. anyone who knows the dilemma should be able to point at the exact line where the two procedures split.

send me the poem, then a few sentences of ordinary prose saying what you were doing and where the split falls. write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and in British English please, since the group's in Leeds. we meet in a pub so don't be too long-winded about it.
