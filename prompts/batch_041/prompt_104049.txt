I run privacy review at a mid-sized consumer software company. A few dozen product features cross my desk each quarter, each on roughly a one-week clock, and my sign-off has to survive two audiences that share none of my concerns: a legal review that cares only about statutes and a public post-mortem if we ship something and it goes wrong. My team is made up of lawyers and product managers with no philosophical training, so whatever you give me has to be runnable by them without the theory behind it. The philosophy of privacy is the only body of thought I know of that takes seriously the things a compliance checklist cannot see: that a flow of information can be entirely lawful and still wrong; that privacy is not secrecy, so the nothing-to-hide objection settles nothing; that the relevant unit of concern is not always the individual whose data it is; and that the wrong of a privacy violation can survive even when no one can point to a concrete harm. I want you to turn the usable part of that literature into a procedure.

Write it as an ordered set of questions a reviewer asks of a proposed feature. The questions should surface what the feature changes about who knows what about whom, in which role, under the norms of which context, and against which expectations of the people involved; whether it substitutes one kind of relationship for another (friends into mutual monitors, customers into suspects, patients into data subjects); whether the individual's control over the flow is preserved or quietly reassigned to someone else; and whether the interest at stake is individual, relational, or collective, and who bears the cost when those diverge. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must not collapse into "obtain consent": I need to know which cases consent can rescue and which it cannot, and why the difference matters. Where the literature disagrees with itself — for instance over whether privacy is best understood as control over information, as limited access to the self, or as the fit between a flow and the norms of its context — say which reading your procedure commits to and what it therefore cannot see.

Then run it, step by step, on three features, marking as you go the points where the procedure strains:

- A closeness score computed from metadata — how often two users message, call, and are co-located — displayed beside each contact in the address book.
- An opt-out crisis-detection system that scans messages for indications of self-harm and routes flagged conversations to a human moderator and, in severe cases, to emergency services.
- A default-on setting that shares a user's precise location with a small circle of family accounts, including adult children monitoring elderly parents.

At least one of these should come out the way the product team will not want to hear, and you should not soften the verdict to avoid that. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will give a confident-sounding answer on the basis of a contested norm, the cases where two of its questions point in opposite directions and the tiebreak is a political choice rather than an analytic one, and the cases where the honest output is that the feature is not wrong but the company should not ship it anyway. Do not cite sources or supply a reading list; I care about the machinery, not the bibliography.
