I'm putting together a handout for the third week of a seminar I run jointly with the econ department on social choice and welfare, and I want to do something other than the usual here-are-the-four-objections-to-Harsanyi routine. What keeps happening is that my students (and, honestly, a couple of the faculty who sit in) can tell you which team they're on — utilitarian, prioritarian, Rawlsian, don't-believe-in-interpersonal-comparisons — but they can't tell you which of the theorem's assumptions they're actually giving up, and about half of them think the theorem itself is the argument for utilitarianism when the theorem says nothing at all about what the weights are. So here's what I want from you: a sorting quiz. Ten questions, each one a concrete case or a forced choice between two or three answers, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. The whole point is to sort people by what they say about the cases, so no question should just ask do you accept welfarism, or do you accept the sure-thing principle — the labels have to fall out of the answers. And please no reading list, no references of any kind, I'll put those on the back of the handout myself. Assume whoever takes it has seen the theorem once and remembers roughly nothing else, so the cases have to carry all the weight.

To be clear about what the quiz is sorting. The result I'm teaching is the one where, if social preferences over risky prospects satisfy the expected-utility axioms and the group is indifferent whenever everyone is indifferent, then social welfare has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities — and the weights come out free, which means "and the weights are equal" is a second step, usually got by sticking an impartial observer behind a veil and handing them equal probability of being any of the individuals. I want the quiz to seperate the people who think that second step works from the people who think the weights are arbitrary or fixed by some normalization convention, and to separate both of those from the people who think the whole thing is a representation theorem about any consistent expected-utility agent and carries no ethical content whatsoever (one of my colleagues insists it's just Savage applied to a committee, and I'd like the instrument to catch that view rather than lump it in with the utilitarians). So the questions need to get at: whether the object of evaluation is the lottery or the outcome (ex ante vs ex post); whether there's a single well-defined social preference ordering for the axioms to bite on in the first place; whether you keep the ex ante Pareto principle when it collides with fairness about who gets a good that can't be split; whether social value is a function of individual utilities alone (this is where the adaptive-preference case, the expensive-tastes case, and the person whose own preferences aren't even expected-utility-consistent belong); whether utilities are comparable in units, in levels, or not at all; and whether population is held fixed (an extra happy person — average or total). Cases I definitely want in there: one indivisible good, two claimants, and you have to choose between a fair coin and just handing it to the first one; a hazardous job that one person takes on, which raises everyone's expected utility but exposes only that person to the risk; the same policy applied to two people whose utility scales differ; someone who's been deprived so long that they report being satisfied with very little; and the committee-as-agent case, where the same axioms are applied to a firm's two divisions or to one person's future selves.

On the leaves: I'd like at least eight, no more than ten, and I want them to include (a) the view where the impartial-observer step goes through and equal weights follow, (b) the view where the theorem is the whole story and the weights are arbitrary or convention-fixed, (c) the fairness-first view that drops ex ante Pareto or independence rather than call the coin flip as good as anything, (d) the ex post view that treats a lottery as just a consequence like any other, (e) the ex ante egalitarian who wants a concave social welfare function and knows it costs them ex ante Pareto, (f) the view that social value isn't a function of individual utilities at all, (g) the maximin, veil-without-probabilities view, (h) the Arrow-ish view that there is no single social ordering to satisfy the axioms, (i) the deflationary representation-theorem view, and (j) the average-vs-total split. Where a position has a standard name, use it; where it doesn't, write unnamed — I'd rather have a couple of unnamed leaves then a name that doesn't quite fit. Then at the end, tell me which answer combinations no position occupies and diagnose each one in a sentence (is nobody there because it's incoherent, because it's coherent but repulsive, or because nobody's bothered?), and flag any pair of leaves the quiz genuinely can't separate, with a note on what extra question would do it. If you can't prise (a) apart from (b) for a respondent who never notices that the weights are free in the theorem, say so rather than fudging it. Three to five lines per question, and keep the whole thing to what fits on one two-sided handout.
