I am an undergraduate member of the programming committee for my university's continuing-education division, and this term I am taking a seminar on astronomy and astrology in early modern Europe. A local astrologer has submitted a six-week evening course for our fall catalog. As submitted, the title is "Kepler's Astrology: The Science That Got Away"; the description presents Kepler as a defender of astrology, argues that the modern birth chart is a legitimate descendant of his work, and promises students that they will learn to interpret their own charts. Our committee votes on Thursday and is split. Two members want to decline the proposal on the ground that a public university should not sponsor instruction in a pseudoscience; two want to approve it as a history-of-science offering. The director has asked me to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting.

Please draft that memo, no longer than two pages. It should close with a definite recommendation — approve, decline, or approve subject to conditions — and any conditions should be concrete enough to write into the instructor's contract: catalog category, title and description, whether a co-instructor from astronomy is required, whether the instructor may offer individual chart readings in or after class, and what the syllabus must cover. Please also state the criteria in general terms, since we will use this memo as precedent for the other borderline proposals that reach us. Assume the committee does not need to be told that astrology has no demonstrated predictive power; the question is what a university should do about that fact, not whether it is one.

Two things I particularly need. First, the historical claim at the center of the proposal. Neither side on the committee has looked into it; both treat Kepler as either a credulous defender of astrology or a merely reluctant one, and I would like the memo to be specific about which parts of astrology he actually defended and which he rejected, and about what kind of influence the heavens were supposed to have in his system — whether this course can stand as history may depend on the answer. Second, I want the strongest version of the case against my recommendation stated fairly, so that the members who lose the vote can see their position taken seriously rather than caricatured.

One further request. The committee keeps sliding between studying a practice and teaching a practice, as though the distinction were self-evident. Please make it do real work in the memo: say precisely what separates the two in a case like this, and what follows for our decision. I will handle citations myself if we publish the memo, so you can write it without them. American English, please.
