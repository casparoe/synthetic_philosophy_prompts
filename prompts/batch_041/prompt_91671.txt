I teach a seminar on the modern revival of Stoicism, and I keep encountering the same piece of pedagogical machinery. To explain the dichotomy of control, the popular version of Stoicism reaches for the archer: the archer does everything within his power — chooses the bow, draws, aims, releases — but whether the arrow reaches the mark depends on the wind, the movement of the target, and a hundred conditions outside him. So, the lesson runs, the shot is yours and the hit is not, and the whole art of living consists in transferring your concern from the hit to the shot. A colleague who teaches this material to a general audience draws a further conclusion from the image: since the hit was never yours, you should stop minding where the arrow lands; attachment to outcomes is precisely the error the analogy is meant to cure, and the Stoic life is the life of the archer who no longer cares whether he hits.

I doubt the image will bear that weight, and I want a full audit rather than a defence or a dismissal. First, the correspondences: what in the target domain plays the role of the archer, the bow, the aim, the release, the wind, the mark, the judge of the shot, and the other archers on the line? Second, where does the mapping break — I am especially interested in the features the image quietly imports that the doctrine need not contain: that a single shot at a determinate mark is the right unit of analysis; that skill is acquired before the moment of shooting rather than during it; that the mark is set by someone else; that the archer can tell how good his shot was independently of where the arrow landed; that success and failure are cleanly separable from the effort that produced them. Third, the older image from the same tradition — the actor given a part in a play he did not write, whose business is to perform it well — seems to place the emphasis elsewhere; say whether the two images are consistent and which one better captures what the doctrine is after. Fourth, and most important: does my colleague's conclusion survive the disanalogies? If the Stoics classed health, wealth, and reputation as preferred rather than good, worth selecting though not worth staking happiness on, does the archer image support indifference to the hit, or only the refusal to make one's happiness hostage to it? And does the modern dichotomy — which some writers expand into a third column of things we partly influence — correspond to what the ancient texts actually set apart as up to us, or is the image being asked to license a reading of the doctrine it never had?

Give the mapping explicitly, a table of correspondences is welcome; separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's use of the image from the ones that are merely picturesque; and close by saying what image would carry the doctrine better, if any.
