I coordinate a reading group on the foundations of AI safety that meets every other week, attended by a mix of machine-learning engineers and graduate philosophers, none of whom has worked on agent foundations. The next session is on embedded agency, and I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience that will not have read anything in advance. What I want from you is not an explanation of embedded agency but a plan for how I should explain it.

The difficulty I keep running into is that the group's reflexes are all wrong for this material. They will want to treat it as a claim about computational limits, as if the fix were simply a larger model or more time; or as a restatement of the frame problem; or as the familiar observation that an agent's sensors give it a partial and noisy view of its surroundings. The thing I actually want to get across is more structural. An agent is itself a part of the world it is trying to model, so a model complete enough to be reliable would have to contain a model of the agent's own modeling — including the very output the agent is in the middle of computing. I want them to feel that this is not a shortage of compute but a problem of shape, one that an idealized agent with unlimited resources would still face, and that it therefore cannot be discharged by scaling.

Please plan the session as follows. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit: what has to be in place before the next step even makes sense, and what I should refuse to introduce early even if someone asks for it. Second, the one example that should carry the whole ninety minutes, and what it must have and lack. I am tempted to build it around a program that is asked to output a complete description of the machine it is running on, its own output included; tell me whether that will do the work or whether it will collapse into a puzzle about self-reference rather than about agency, and if it will not carry the session, say what should replace it and why. Third, the misconceptions the group will arrive with and the new ones they will form during the session, in the order they will surface, with the exact point in the sequence at which I should head off each. Fourth, one question I can put to them near the end that will reveal whether the structural point has landed rather than whether they can recite a definition. Fifth, what I should explicitly leave out at this level and tell them I am leaving out.

Two things to keep in view throughout. The group will have heard of the frame problem, of bounded rationality, and of the halting problem, and part of your job is to make sure the session does not get absorbed into any of them. And I would rather the session end with a live tension they can feel — an agent that cannot fully model itself, and yet must act — than with a tidy definition they can write down.
