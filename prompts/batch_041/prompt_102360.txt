After thirty years as a physiologist, I have spent my retirement teaching myself philosophy of medicine, and next month I am to lead a sixty-minute session for our local reading group — intelligent non-specialists with no biology past school and no philosophy at all — on whether disease is a biological fact or a value judgment. Do not rehearse the positions for me; plan how I should teach the dispute. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, and name the single case to build the whole session around, with an account of what it must have and lack. I want that case drawn from ordinary clinical life rather than a stock philosophical example, and I want it to be one on which the two accounts visibly come apart without being settled by the first obvious objection. Set out the misconceptions this audience will bring — my own guess is that they will treat the biological account as simply the scientific one and the value-laden account as soft relativism, and that they will slide disease into illness, the patient's felt experience — and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head off each. I also want one moment where I let the biological account feel the pressure of the reference class it has to choose (which age, sex, and environment count as typical for the species), so tell me where that belongs and how to present it without a reading list. Close with one question I can put to the group to see whether the distinction has actually landed, and tell me what I should openly set aside at this level.
