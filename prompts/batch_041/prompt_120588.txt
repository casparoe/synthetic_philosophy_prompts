I'm an undergraduate in a course on vagueness and the semantics of vague predicates, and the final assignment for the unit asks for a short story rather than an essay: we are to make one of the standard positions on vague language visible in fiction. I have chosen the dispute between the two views my professor labels epistemicism and supervaluationism, and I would like you to write the story. Since the assignment is graded on whether the story genuinely embodies the disagreement rather than merely mentioning it, let me set out both positions as precisely as I can, so that you are not relying on whatever you already associate with the labels.

The epistemicist holds that every vague predicate has a sharp boundary, and that our failure to locate it is a failure of knowledge rather than a feature of the world. There is a fact of the matter about whether a given man is bald, or a given pile a heap, at every stage of the series; that fact is simply hidden from us, and hidden in principle, so that no amount of investigation could settle it. On this view, the sorites reasoning is compelling only because we cannot bring ourselves to believe in a line that we could never identify. The supervaluationist holds that there is no such hidden fact. A vague predicate admits many admissible ways of being made precise, each drawing the line somewhere permissible; a sentence is true when it comes out true on all of them, false when false on all of them, and otherwise has no truth value at all. So the pile's being a heap can be neither true nor false, not because we are ignorant but because the question has no answer until the predicate is precisified, and the precisification is something we do rather than something we discover. Where the boundary falls is a decision, though a constrained one: not every line is admissible, since the clear cases must remain clear.

Two wrinkles from our seminars are the reason I chose this topic, and I want the story to carry them. First, both views strain at the boundary between the clear and the borderline. If every vague predicate has a sharp line, then so does the predicate clearly a heap, which means there must be a pile that is a heap whose neighbour, one grain short, is not clearly a heap even though it too is a heap. If instead the line is created by precisification, then the predicate borderline case must itself be precisified, and the problem recurs one level up. I would like a scene in which the question is not whether a case falls under the predicate but whether it is a clear case or a borderline one, so that the decision is about the clarity of a classification rather than the classification itself. Second, the views disagree about verdicts reached before the line was drawn. If the line was always there, an earlier verdict was simply correct or incorrect, knowable or not; if the line comes into existence only when drawn, it is unclear what to say about cases decided while the predicate was still unsharp, whether they were retroactively vindicated, retroactively voided, or never answers at all. The story should not resolve this, but it should make the reader feel its weight.

For the story itself, I would like the following. Roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, in the third person, set in a mundane present-day institution that must issue verdicts — an office, a committee, an inspectorate, a clinic, a licensing body — with procedures and paperwork rather than seminars. A concrete predicate that is uncontroversial in clear cases and contested only in the middle, and a specific person whose situation turns on where the line falls, with ordinary but real stakes: money, eligibility, status, access, custody. At least two characters whose conduct and expectations embody the two views without either of them stating or arguing for a view, so that a reader could reconstruct each position from what the characters do, what they assume is knowable, and what they believe the drawing of a line accomplishes. A decisive scene that can be read consistently either way: a reader who thinks the boundary was always there should be able to read the outcome as the discovery of a fact, and a reader who thinks the boundary is made should be able to read the same outcome as a decision that created one. Please keep the technical vocabulary out of the story — the words vagueness, borderline, epistemicism, supervaluationism, precisification, and sorites should not appear in it — and write it as though the disagreement were an ordinary feature of institutional life. No exposition, no character delivering the theory, and no ending that explains what the story was about.
