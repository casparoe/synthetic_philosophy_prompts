I sit on the board of a small charitable trust — four volunteer trustees, a few hundred thousand dollars to give away each year, decisions made in a single afternoon once a month, no staff and no lawyer in the room — that a local Buddhist community set up and that we have spent several awkward meetings trying to run on openly consequentialist lines: we want the grants to avert as much suffering as possible, and we are deliberately unsentimental about whether a project looks Buddhist. The friction is that we also describe ourselves as Buddhist in method, and the two halves keep colliding in ways none of us can adjudicate. Last month we burned ninety minutes on whether to fund a supervised consumption site that distributes paraphernalia we are fairly sure our founding donors would have called a violation of the precept against intoxicants; the month before, a trustee proposed a public-health campaign built on a misleading framing of the risks, which our own numbers suggested would save lives. A friend who reads the same rationalist blogs I do keeps insisting that the tradition we are half-aping is itself best read as a species of universalist consequentialism — strong agent-neutrality, a willingness to let the compassionate agent break the precepts when breaking them does more good, an ethic that treats no-self as dissolving the special standing of one's own projects, and a doctrine of radical altruism in which the bodhisattva takes on others' suffering — while the familiar claim that karma turns on intention is not a competing criterion of rightness at all but a separate ledger recording what happens to the agent. I find this reading suspiciously convenient, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the precepts are absolute constraints, training rules, or rules of thumb whose breach is merely expensive for the person who breaches them, nor how the intention-centered karma story is supposed to interact with a welfare-based criterion. I have read enough virtue-ethics framings of Buddhist ethics to be unsure the consequentialist reading is even the majority view, and no one on the board has read the primary texts.

What I want from you is a procedure, not a lecture: something the four of us can actually run on a grant proposal, in the order we should ask the questions, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to keep whatever the tradition actually commits to while being usable by people who have not read it, so tell me explicitly which distinctions must survive the simplification and which I can afford to drop, and be honest about the points where the procedure will misfire or is really just a judgment call wearing the costume of a step. Then run it end to end on three proposals — the supervised consumption site, the misleading but effective campaign, and one more I leave to you, ideally something where the maximising answer and the precept answer diverge and the money is small enough that the decision feels beneath the theory. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not enjoy, and after the run-throughs I want a short section on what the procedure cannot do and which cases should be kicked to the whole board rather than settled by the checklist.
