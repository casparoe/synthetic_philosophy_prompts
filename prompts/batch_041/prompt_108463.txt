Bit of a strange request, I know. I'm a retired geophysicist — thirty-odd years in the field, never read a word of philosphy until I was 62, and now I'm hooked enough that the department here has let me run a small upper-level seminar on philosophy of the social sciences. Which means I have to write the final, and I'm out of my depth in one specific way: every question I draft can be answered by my students reciting the same Popper paragraph at me. They all know the move — history can't have laws because we can't predict the growth of knowledge — and they all write it up with the same three examples, in the same order. I want a question that can't be answered that way.

So here's what I've been doing in the seminar. To get them to take the idea of a law of history seriously, I wrote one myself, the way a natural scientist would. I even gave it a name:

The Institutional Entropy Law. Take any society. Define its organisational complexity as the number of distinct, non-redundant rules it actually enforces. Then: (a) in any period in which the society receives no new knowledge from outside itself, its organisational complexity decreases; and (b) the rate of that decrease is an increasing function of the complexity itself.

I know it's crude. That's half the point — it's crude in the way a real proposed law is crude, and it has the shape I'd recognise from thermodynamics: a conditional, a monotone quantity, and a prediction that only holds relative to a boundary condition. What I can't work out is whether a thing like this is a law of history, or a trend dressed up in formalism, or something in between that Popper didn't really have a slot for. I'd like the exam to make them sort that out instead of reciting.

So: please write me the essay question (90 minutes, upper-level, closed book, except they get the law above printed on the paper), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. For the scheme I want five must-hit points at 4 marks each, 20 total, and under each point a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it — the specific wrong move, not something vague like they don't understand Popper. And I want the question itself to force them to say what Popper's argument does and doesn't rule out, rather than just that historicism is dead: in particular whether a conditional law of this sort is even the kind of thing he was attacking, and what an unconditional law of historical development would have to look like for the attack to land on it. If it helps, I'm happy for there to be a second part asking whether my law is falsifiable at all, because I have a nagging feeling that the boundary clause is doing all the work and that a Popperian ought to be suspicious of exactly that.
