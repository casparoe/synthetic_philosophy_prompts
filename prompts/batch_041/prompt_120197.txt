I chair the grants committee at a mid-size family foundation -- five of us, quarterly meetings, and the job is picking which proposals get money. Our current method is the usual one: everybody ranks the shortlist, the most first-place votes wins, and if nobody has a majority we drop the weakest and revote. Its produced a couple of outcomes nobody will defend. Last fall we funded the safe literacy program over two riskier proposals that split the vote between them, and everyone's least favorite thing about the process is how much the winner depends on which proposals happened to be competing that quarter.

At the last meeting one of my trustees, Dana, came in with a fix. She wants us to score each proposal independently on a 0-100 "expected impact" scale, average the five scores, fund the highest average, plus a floor: anything averaging under 40 gets nothing no matter where it places. She says this isn't just her preference, it's the uniquely rational way for a group to aggregate, and she brought in Harsanyi's utilitarian theorem -- the one that says if every individual's preferences and the group's preferences are shaped like expected utilities, and the group never overrules a unanimous judgment, then the group's ranking has to be a weighted sum of the members' utilities. She says once you add that everybody's interests count the same, the weights come out equal, so a straight average is the only game in town. She wants it adopted for the spring cycle and never revisited.

What I can't articulate is why I'm uneasy. Two of the five of us think in bands, basically A through F, and the numbers on our current form were assigned to the bands after the fact, by me, in a spreadsheet -- so I'm honestly not sure 47 and 52 mean anything different. And this cycle one of the three proposals is a gamble: a pilot with maybe a 30% shot at a big regional payoff and a 70% shot at nothing. At least two members who'd say yes to a gamble like that in the abstract will score it low, because they can picture us being the ones who lit the money on fire if it flops. Dana says that's exactly the kind of noise her method cleans up. The other two proposals are boring and safe: a dental clinic expansion and a scholarship fund.

So write me the short opinion I'll circulate before the spring meeting. It should land on one side -- adopt her rule as is, reject it, or adopt a modified version with conditions attached -- and give the reasoning in a form Dana and the other three can actually argue with, not a philosophy seminar. I want a real recommendation with a date on it, an honest account of what she gets right, because I think she gets a fair amount right, and if the theorem doesn't do the work she thinks it does, say exactly which of its premises our situation fails and what that leaves us with instead. Under two pages, American English, it's going in the board packet.
