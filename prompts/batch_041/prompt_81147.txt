I run a small research group that works on risks from advanced AI, and on Friday I give the opening remarks at our annual two-day workshop — roughly eight minutes, from a lectern with no slides, in a borrowed seminar room, before the first session, with next year's research priorities still unsettled. The room is genuinely mixed, which is the hard part. About a third of the attendees are technical researchers drawn to the prospect of a system that quietly acquires real-world influence and then uses it for ends of its own: a fast, legible catastrophe with an agent at the centre of it, the kind of thing one can argue about for hours in terms of evaluations and control, and they tend to hear the softer warnings as either vague or as public relations. Another third think that story is a science-fiction distraction from what they take to be the actual mechanism of failure — many deployed systems, each profitably optimizing some narrow measurable target such as engagement, approval, conversion or ratings, slowly reshaping what people attend to, what they are rewarded for, and eventually what they want, so that human preferences are edited by the tools built to serve them and no moment of seizure ever occurs. (That is my rough compression; I would phrase it more carefully on the day.) The remainder are funders, two programme officers from a family foundation, a journalist writing a profile of the group, and my brother, who teaches high-school physics and has twice asked me what 'alignment' means. The wifi in the room is unreliable, so anything I say has to stand entirely on its own.

Please write the remarks. I want the contrast between those two pictures of failure to do structural work rather than appear as a bullet point I mention in the middle: it should determine what the speech can promise, what it has to concede, and where it finally comes down. I do want it to take a position — I think the second picture deserves considerably more of our attention and our money than it gets, and I would rather say so than split the difference — but it should be honest that the position is contestable, and should give the first camp a real argument to push back against rather than a straw man. Nobody in the room doubts that advanced AI matters; the disagreement is entirely about the shape of the failure, so please don't spend any of the eight minutes establishing stakes everyone has already accepted.

A few constraints. No quotations, no citations and no named researchers: I cannot check attributions on stage, and I would rather the argument be mine. No invented statistics — where a number would help, keep it qualitative. Avoid the phrase 'existential risk', which the journalist will lift out of context, and avoid dwelling on takeover imagery, since that is exactly the material that makes the funders' eyes glaze. My brother is the test: if he is lost by minute three the speech has failed, and if he is condescended to it has failed worse. Close with something both camps could plausibly act on by Monday morning — the point of these remarks is to set next year's priorities, not to settle a metaphysics.
