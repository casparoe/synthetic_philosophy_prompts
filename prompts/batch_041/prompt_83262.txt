I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I will state things in engineering terms. I would like a careful comparison of two pictures of how an artificial agent might be built. I suspect the two are closer than they look, and I would rather be told that plainly than be given a both-have-their-merits answer.

The first picture is the familiar goal-directed one: the system has an objective, understood as a fixed function from situations (or whole trajectories) to a score, and it chooses actions that score well. The second is approval-directed agency: rather than optimizing a fixed objective, the system chooses the action it predicts would win the approval of some overseer — a person, a panel, or an idealized version of a person. The selling point of the second is that the overseer can judge situations the designers never anticipated, so the agent is not frozen to a specification written in advance.

Here is the concrete design I have in mind, because I want the comparison to bite on something specific rather than on slogans. At each step the agent generates a set of candidate actions; an overseer — or a model trained to predict an overseer — assigns each candidate a number standing for how much it approves of that action in that situation; the agent takes the highest-scoring candidate. The overseer is only ever shown single actions in the context of the current situation; it is never asked to endorse a whole policy, and it is never consulted about consequences the agent could foresee but the overseer could not.

I would like you to address the following.

First, the collapse worry, which I take seriously. If approval can be captured by a function — feed in the situation and the proposed action, get out a degree of approval — then this looks like ordinary goal-directed agency with that function as the objective. Is that the end of the distinction? Or is there something in the approval-directed picture that genuinely resists being written down as a function? If so, I want to know what is doing the resisting, and whether that is a virtue or merely an evasion.

Second, assuming the distinction is real, where does it show up in behaviour? I am interested in four contrasts in particular: when the overseer's judgment is itself unreliable or manipulable; when the agent is more capable than the overseer and can foresee what the overseer cannot; when approval is elicited at different moments — before acting, with full information, or in hindsight; and when several such agents interact, each optimizing for a different overseer.

Third, failure modes. Each picture seems to have a signature way of going wrong: the goal-directed agent gaming its objective, the approval-directed agent gaming the overseer — landing on actions the overseer would endorse but would not endorse if it understood what was really happening. Are these two failure modes genuinely different, or is the second just the first with the objective set to whatever the overseer approves of? And which picture is more robust, and to what?

Fourth, a verdict. Which of the two do you take to be the more fundamental notion, and what would settle the question — a working design, a proof that one reduces to the other, a demonstration that they come apart in some case, or something else entirely? If the answer depends on how approval is defined, say which definition you are assuming and why.
