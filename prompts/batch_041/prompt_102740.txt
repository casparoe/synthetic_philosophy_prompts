I am an undergraduate in an animal-ethics course, and I have been assigned to lead next week's eighty-minute seminar on the scale argument for insect sentience: the thought that however small we take the probability of an insect being sentient to be, the sheer number of insects may make their expected moral weight enormous. I am not asking you to argue the case for or against insect sentience. I am asking you to plan how I should teach it to a room of my peers who have read a little Bentham and a little Singer, but who have no philosophy of mind and no decision theory.

Give me the explanatory arc. First, what has to be in place before the scale argument can even be stated as a serious position rather than a curiosity: what does the audience need to accept about moral status, about uncertainty, and about how the two combine, and in what order, so that the conclusion arrives as something that follows rather than something I have asserted. Second, the one carrying example the whole session should hang on, together with what it must and must not presuppose. I had considered building it around a single concrete choice, a food company weighing cricket flour against beef, but if that case begs a question or quietly imports a contentious assumption about how to compare animals, say so and hand me a better one. I want the arithmetic of probability times number to carry the weight of the session, not the neurobiology of nociceptors, so the example should let us vary the probability and the headcount independently and watch the verdict move.

Third, the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, and the precise point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I am already braced for a few: that the argument only bites if insects really are sentient, that having more of something by itself settles the matter, and that a conclusion this strange must be a fallacy somewhere. Tell me the others I am not seeing, including the ones my own framing is likely to plant. Fourth, one question I can put to the room at the end that would show me whether they have understood the structure of the argument rather than merely been startled by its conclusion. Fifth, what I should openly tell them we are not going to settle in an hour, and why.

Be candid about which parts of this plan you are confident in and which are your best guess, and push back if the request itself is misconceived. If I am assuming that a low probability of sentience and a vast number of insects can simply be multiplied into one expected-welfare figure when they cannot, or if the seminar as I have framed it cannot be taught well in eighty minutes to this audience, say so plainly rather than handing me a lesson plan built on a broken premise.
