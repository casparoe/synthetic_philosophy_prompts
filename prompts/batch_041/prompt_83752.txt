Last term my school's philosophy club set out to write a formal rule for what members should do when they disagree about a judgment the club has to make, and the drafting argument turned into the most substantive dispute we have had all year. I was taking minutes and stayed out of it, so I would like you to referee it for me.

The two people arguing were Ada and Ravi. Ada's position was that when two people who are roughly equally well-informed, equally careful, and equally honest end up on opposite sides of a question, each should treat the other's conclusion as evidence and shift some distance toward it. The shift might be small, but it cannot be zero, because a zero shift treats one's own opinion as having a privilege that one has just conceded it does not have. Ravi's position was that a person may reasonably stand firm: if he has thought a question through and his reasoning still seems sound to him, the bare fact that someone he respects reached a different conclusion does not by itself require him to move, since the only way he can check his reasoning is by using it, and surrendering to every equally confident opponent would leave him holding nothing but an average of other people's opinions.

Four moves followed that I would like you to rate individually.

(1) Ravi said Ada's rule destroys itself. The two of them disagree about whether disagreement obliges anyone to move, so if Ada's rule is correct, Ada must now move partway toward Ravi's view about moving. But the size of that move is fixed by the very rule she has just weakened, so she can never settle on a definite position, and she ought to end up unable to trust her own rule enough to apply it. Ada answered that this assumes a disagreement she can explain is still evidence against her view. Her view explains this disagreement perfectly well, she said: any general rule about how to respond to disagreement will itself be disagreed about, because people who think hard about it land in different places, so Ravi's rejection of her rule is exactly what her rule leads her to expect, and a disagreement that your own view predicts is not evidence against it.

(2) Ravi replied that this shows Ada's rule is not the rule she announced. If she may decide that this particular disagreement carries little weight, she has granted herself permission to discount disagreeing opponents whenever she judges them likely to be mistaken, which is precisely what she accuses him of doing. Ada answered that the two cases differ: her rule treats disagreement as genuine evidence and specifies how far to move, whereas his rule instructs him to move zero in every case, and a rule whose output is the same no matter what the input is not a rule of evidence at all.

(3) Ada then produced the club's ledger. For two years we have recorded disputes between members and later marked who turned out right once the matter could be checked; the tally reads six for Ada and four for Ravi. Ada said the ledger settles it: Ravi is disagreeing with someone who has the better record, so he should move much further toward her than she toward him. Ravi said the ledger proves nothing. It is contaminated, because Ada is the one who decides which disputes get recorded and because the mark for being right usually meant that the club voted; it is tiny; and its disputes concerned whether a scheduled speaker would arrive and how many people would attend the fair, not how to reason about disagreement, so even if the tally is accurate it does not transfer. Ada conceded the point about size and said the correct response is a small adjustment rather than a large one, but that a small adjustment is still an adjustment, which is all she needs.

(4) Finally, because the rule was to govern the club itself, Ada proposed that the club adopt a midpoint rule: when two members disagree about a judgment the club must make, the club's official position is halfway between their two positions. Ravi objected that the midpoint rule applied to itself yields a position nobody holds. We disagree about whether to adopt it, so by the rule the club's official view is halfway between adopting and not adopting, which is not a policy at all, and a rule that cannot tell the club what to do about the only question before it is useless. Ada said a halfway position is perfectly definite: it means the club records the rule as uncertain and follows it provisionally, revisiting it later. Ravi, she said, is confusing the claim that no one holds this exact view with the claim that it is not a view.

I would like a point-by-point verdict. For each of the four moves, tell me which party has the better of it and why, being specific about which part of the move is sound and which part overreaches. Then tell me what each of them is missing that neither of them said. Finally, say who is closer to right overall, and if the honest answer is that each is right about something and the disagreement turns on a choice neither has justified, say that instead.
