Write me a short story. Setup: a linguist has spent about a decade documenting a language whose last fluent speaker is an old woman in a village that's slowly emptying out. He's got thousands of recorded sentences and a rough grammar, and for any situation he's already seen he can predict what she'll say almost perfectly. What he can't do is predict the situations that haven't come up yet — and the language has constructions that, in every case on record, do one particular thing, but nothing in the record forces them to keep doing that. One day she'll say something new, and whatever she says will look like it reveals what the rule really was. But there's no speech community left to tell her she's got it wrong; she's the only one left who could. So he starts to wonder whether there was ever a fact about what the rule was, or whether following a rule is just whatever a community lets you get away with — and whether a community of one counts. That's the Kripkenstein puzzle, but I want it dramatised, not explained. Don't resolve it neatly: leave the reader uneasy about whether he should write down the rule he expected or the one she ends up using. Under 1500 words, and keep the philosophy in the furniture of the story rather than in a scene where someone spells out the paradox.
