Okay, this might be a dumb question, but I'm a second-year PhD student and its been bugging me for weeks. I've always took the Dennett line — the self as a center of narrative gravity — to mean that the self isn't a thing in your head at all, it's just the middle of the story you tell about yourself, the way the center of gravity of a rock isn't any particular lump of rock. Real but not a thing. Fine, I'm on board. But I keep pulling on it and I think I'm getting something out of it that isn't there.

Here's the thing. If the self is just the center of the story, and stories can be told differently, doesn't it follow that someone who substantially revises their life story — or a patient who confabulates a fresh autobiography every morning — is literally a different self, and not just the same self with a new story? And if that's right, then when my grandma signed an advance directive saying she didn't want to be kept alive like that, and now she's a decade into Alzheimer's and is cheerful and wants the antibiotics — those are two different selves, so the earlier one has no say over the later one? Everyone around me seems to treat the narrative view as settling that in favour of the present narrative, and I can't see where they get that. Same worry about whether there's any fact of the matter that I'm the same self as the twelve-year-old who promised something, or whether that's just a question about which story it's more useful for us to keep telling. My advisor says I'm conflating two or three things and then changes the subject, which is not helpful.

So: does the center-of-narrative-gravity picture actually entail that identity over time is just whichever story is most useful to tell? And if I'm smuggling something in, what exactly is the smuggled premise? I want to know what I'm actually committed to, not just a label for it.
