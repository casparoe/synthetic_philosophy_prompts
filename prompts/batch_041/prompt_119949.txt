In three weeks I'm giving a work-in-progress talk to a joint session of our philosophy of science reading group and the causal inference group from the biostatistics department — roughly half the room argues about the metaphysics of causation for a living, and the other half spends its days on DAGs, target-trial emulation, and identification proofs, and the two halves do not read each other. The paper defends a claim I expect to annoy both of them: that the interventionist, manipulationist account of causation is not merely a serviceable epistemology for finding out about causes but the better account of causal truth, and that the counterfactual account's fatal soft spot is causal selection — it will happily tell you that a hundred conditions were all difference-makers and then has nothing to say about why we single one of them out as the cause. My running case is one the biostatisticians will recognize from their own work: a patient's lung cancer, where a genetic predisposition predicts the outcome better than the smoking does, and yet the patient, the clinician, and the public health office all say the smoking caused it. I want to argue that this is not a pragmatic gloss laid over the real causal facts but a case the manipulationist framework gets right — I'm going to claim that manipulability is doing the work even here, because the cause is the variable whose value could have been changed by an intervention on the system, which is why the genotype quietly drops out. I've been arguing about this over coffee with a friend in the epi group, and she made the obvious objection that I am just relabeling the pragmatics; it has been nagging at me since.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the eight or so objections this specific room will actually raise — not the ones I'd collect at a metaphysics conference — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that half of the room would phrase it, and where the two halves would put the same worry differently, give me both versions. For each, a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Tell me which objections to concede immediately, which to refuse outright, and which two or three I cannot afford to lose — the ones where, if the room doesn't buy my answer, the paper is dead. Say which ones I should preempt in the talk itself rather than leave for the question period, and tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.

I don't need a reading list; I know the literature well enough. What I don't know is this room, and the room is what will decide whether the paper survives contact with people who spend their afternoons estimating effects.
