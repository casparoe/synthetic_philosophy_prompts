I run a small logic reading group — grad students who already know the standard menu (ZF, NBG, type theory, the usual suspects) — and im tired of handouts that are just a list of -isms. For next week I want to hand them a decision tree for Russell's paradox that's built out of the argument itself, not out of the list of replies. So start from the naive derivation and branch only on which premise of it you're willing to give up. Every branch node should be a question a person could answer without already knowing which answer leads where. Leaves are named positions; where a combination of answers lands on somthing nobody actually defends, say so and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves the tree can't honestly pull apart.

Two leaves I specifically want in there: one for the idea that the paradox isn't a reductio at all but shows that 'set' is an indefinitely extensible, never-completed notion, and one for the idea that the real culprit is the predicate 'is not a member of itself' being illegitimate rather than the set. Tell me whether those are actually two views or one view wearing two hats. Dont tell me who held what — I want the shape of the space, not a who's-who.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, nothing else, no intro and no wrap-up. Items 1-4 are the four premises you can drop — unrestricted comprehension, the assumption that a predicate's extension has to be a set rather than a class, the classical treatment of contradiction, and the assumption that you can quantify over the whole domain including the very thing you're defining — each a sentence or two, and each one should name the positions it leads to and note any dead branch or pair of leaves you can't seperate. Item 5 is where you tell me which of those four branches you're least confident in, and what would actually change your mind about it.
