I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background to speak of (closest I got was a gen-ed ethics class), and I've been argueing with a coworker about procrastination for like three weeks. I want you to write an essay about it and actually pick a side.

The setup: people seem to discount future rewards hyperbolically, meaning the extra value of waiting shrinks as the payoff gets closer. That's the usual story for why you plan all week to start the report Monday, and then Monday at 2pm it's somehow obviously not worth starting yet. The standard label for that is weakness of will: you judged that working was best, and then you didn't do it.

My coworker says that label is a fiction. Model a person as a stack of time-slices, each with its own preferences over what happens from that moment on. At any given moment there's only one preference set at the wheel. Monday-me isn't overriding Monday-me's judgment, he's just doing what Monday-me most wants. So there's no conflict *inside* a slice, only drift *between* slices, and calling the drift "weakness" smuggles in a unified self whose preferences are supposed to outrank whichever slice is currently driving. He thinks the unified self is a leftover from religion and that the whole akrasia literature is people moralizing about a coordination problem between strangers sharing a body.

Two things I want you to actually deal with, not skate around.

First, I blame my past self and I try to bind my future self — delete the app, set the alarm for 6, hand my credit card to a friend. Both attitudes seem to presuppose that the slices are answerable to each other, which is exactly the unified self he says is doing all the heavy lifting. Is that stance coherent without the unified self? Does it hand us back a real kind of akrasia, or is it just a fancy version of "I wish my roommate wouldn't eat my food"?

Second, some people *know* they'll cave and pay in advance to lock themselves in, and other people don't see it coming and get ambushed every time. That difference has to matter for whose story is right, and I want you to say how.

Weigh both sides properly and end with a verdict. Don't cop out with "it depends how you define it" — if the answer is that akrasia is real only under certain conditions, state the conditions. No bibliography needed, just argue.
