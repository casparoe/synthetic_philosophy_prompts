Although my own training is in the earth sciences—I spent the better part of forty years in isotope geochemistry—I have lately been attending a graduate seminar in practical philosophy, and I find that the disputes there have a texture quite unlike the disagreements I am used to. Last week two of the students got into an argument that the rest of us could not settle, and they asked me to referee because I am the only one without a stake in the seminar's internal politics. The topic was acausal trade—roughly, the proposal that two agents who cannot causally affect one another, and who may not even be certain of each other's existence, can nevertheless each do better by adopting a policy that would benefit the other if the other were to adopt a corresponding policy, on the basis of a purely logical or probabilistic correlation between their decisions rather than any causal channel. One of them, whom I will call A, advanced a principle she called the reciprocity condition: acausal trade is rationally permissible only when each party's decision procedure is a deterministic function of a common set of logical facts, so that the correlation is not merely a matter of each guessing the other's psychology but is entailed by the structure of the problem. The other, B, objected that this condition is either vacuous or too strong. A replied that the condition is satisfied in the intended cases because each party can simulate the other's reasoning, so that each choice can be a fixed point of the other's decision procedure; that, she said, is a logical fact, not a causal one. B answered that simulation is either causal reasoning about a computational process or a coordination equilibrium under common knowledge, and that common knowledge is precisely what is missing in the acausal case. A then said that B was conflating the absence of a causal channel with the absence of information, since information can be present without a channel. B replied that A was conflating a mathematical correlation with a reason for action: even if their policies were perfectly correlated, if neither could influence the other, why should either choose the cooperative policy? A's final move was that choosing a policy can make the other's choice more likely in a logical sense—if she is the kind of agent who cooperates, then B, being the same kind of agent, cooperates—so that it is a fact about the world she can influence by being a certain kind of agent. B said that this is either a triviality about reference classes or a causal claim in disguise.

I have reproduced the exchange as faithfully as I can. What I would like from you is a point-by-point adjudication, not a general verdict. Please say, for each of the following, who has the better of the argument and why, rating each point on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating: (1) whether A's reciprocity condition is too strong, too weak, or correctly stated; (2) whether B's objection that simulation is just causal reasoning (or common-knowledge coordination) is sound; (3) whether A's final reply—that adopting a policy can make another's choice more likely in a logical sense—is a genuine reason for action or a confusion; and (4) what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relation between logical correlation and practical reason. I am not interested in references, reading lists, or citations; I want the arguments assessed as they stand, with the specific steps identified where each of them goes right or wrong.
