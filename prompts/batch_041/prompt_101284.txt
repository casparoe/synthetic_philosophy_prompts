I am presenting a paper at a metaphysics and philosophy-of-mind reading group next month and I need a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper defends a claim about Parfit's teletransporter and the thesis that identity is not what matters in survival.

The audience is small but opinionated, and I know roughly who will speak. Two members are committed reductionists of a Parfitian stripe: they regard it as settled that the teletransporter shows identity is not what matters, and they will press me on whether I am quietly smuggling in a further fact of the matter about persons. One member is a non-reductionist about personal identity who will welcome my conclusion but attack the route I take to it, on the ground that I assume what I set out to prove. Two members work empirically on memory and the neuroscience of the self; they are suspicious of teletransporter intuitions altogether and will ask what work the case does that a real phenomenon — anterograde amnesia, commissurotomy, progressive dementia — could not do better. A decision theorist in the group will want to know what my view implies about how one should discount one's own future, and whether my talk of anticipation is anything more than a relabeled discount rate.

Here is my position, in outline, for your objections to target. I claim that the teletransporter case is almost always described from a neutral, third-person standpoint — a replica is produced, the original is destroyed, and we are invited to agree that nothing that matters has been lost — and that this description hides the very feature of the case that should decide the issue. To bring that feature out I use a variant of my own. The scanner records my pattern and builds the replica, but the original is not destroyed at that moment: the original survives, in good health and with uninterrupted psychological continuity, for another ten years before dying, while the replica simultaneously begins an independent life. Now ask what each party can anticipate. On Parfit's own reasoning the original has as much reason to anticipate the replica's future as its own; but the replica cannot anticipate the original's ten remaining years, and nothing in the case runs in the reverse direction either. So the relation that grounds anticipation is directed and asymmetric, not the symmetric relation of psychological continuity that reductionism needs. A symmetric relation cannot supply a direction; only a persisting subject can. My conclusion is therefore the reverse of the usual one: the teletransporter, properly described, tells against reductionism, because what carries anticipation is a subject that persists rather than a pattern that is copied — the replica is a second subject that shares my history, not a continuation of me. I am aware the argument is compressed and that the weakest joint is likely to be my move from the direction of anticipation to the existence of a subject; do not spare it.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the generic ones any audience would raise — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way the member who will raise it would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft or concedes too much. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I want you to commit: for each objection, give me a definite verdict on whether it lands, and if you think my overall position fails, say so and say where, rather than laying out the considerations on both sides and leaving the judgment to me.
