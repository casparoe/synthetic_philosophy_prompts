I'm setting the final for a final-year AI ethics option and I want one essay question on impact measures that a student can't get through by reciting the alignment problem in general. I dont want the topic to be "is AI hard to align" — I want them inside the specific family of what I'll call impact regularisers: instead of telling the agent what the world should be like, you penalise it for straying from a baseline (say, what would have happened had it done nothing) or for reducing what it can still bring about later, on the idea that this way you never need a full specification of value. The question should force them to say whether that idea survives contact with detail, and I want the crux to be three things: first, that a "side effect" seems to be defined relative to an intended effect, so if you refuse to specify what's wanted there may be nothing to count as a side effect in the first place; second, that the choices you still have to make — what baseline, what counts as still reachable, how you aggregate over possible futures, how hard you weight the penalty against the task reward — each look like they smuggle in value commitments of their own; third, that a penalty strong enough to be safe just makes the agent inert, so the remaining trade-off between initiative and caution looks like a value judgement rather than something you read off a measure. A good answer has to take a position on whether some version of this is genuinely value-free, and if not, what the least-bad fallback is. Please write the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five or six must-hit points, each with its marks and a one-line note on how students typically miss it. Finish the model answer with a last line \answer{...} holding its verdict in a short phrase. British English throughout.
