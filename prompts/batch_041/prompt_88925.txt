I'm running a small discussion group on metrics and incentives — the kind of thing where someone always says "Goodhart's law" and everyone nods and we move on, which I find kind of dissapointing. So I've been trying to break the law down into its actually distinct failure modes, and I want a worked example for the session.

Here's the rough taxonomy I've landed on, and I'd like you to take it as given rather than re-deriving it: (1) selection/regressional failure — when you pick out the cases with the best proxy score, the proxy and the goal come apart in that selected slice just because you selected on the proxy, not because anything else changed; (2) range failure — the proxy tracks the goal fine in the ordinary range it was built and checked on, but you're now operating far outside that range, where nobody ever verified the relationship holds; (3) causal failure — the proxy correlates with the goal only because some common cause drives both, so pushing on the proxy directly severs the link to the thing you care about; and (4) gaming — someone with a stake in the number figures out how to move the number without moving the thing. There may be other flavors, and I'm genuinely not sure my four are seperable — flag it if they aren't.

What I want: a single realistic scenario — a school, a firm, a hospital ward, a city department, whatever, nothing sci-fi, no omniscient or perfectly rational agents — of no more than one paragraph, that isolates exactly one of those four flavors and holds the other three off by construction. Specifically I'd like the causal one to be the live one: the failure should be there even though nobody is gaming anything, and even though the proxy is, in the ordinary range, a genuinely good indicator. And the isolation should be strict — it should be clear from the setup itself that selection isn't doing the work and that we're not just off the edge of the calibration range.

Then walk me through it: for each of the four flavors, say whether it applies and why, so I can see that the three exclusions are real and not hand-waved. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — and show me what happens if I remove it, i.e. does the failure vanish, or does it just migrate into one of the other flavors? The thing I most want to avoid is a case that only works because I stacked the deck, so finish by saying how someone who thinks the whole taxonomy is overblown — who'd say all of this is just "bad proxies, go find a better one" — would attack the example, and whether their attack actually lands. If the task as I've posed it is incoherent — like, if it turns out you can't hold the other three off in a case that's still realistic, or my four categories aren't really distinct — please just say so and explain why, rather than producing a tidy example that quietly cheats. I'd rather find out the taxonomy is muddy than get a polished illustration of a muddy taxonomy.
