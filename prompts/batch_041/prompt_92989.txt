I drafted the following argument for a reading group on decision theory, and I have come to think that it proves far too much, but I cannot locate the step that is doing the illegitimate work. Please tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

(1) If two agents share a prior over a common state space, and their posterior credences in a given event are common knowledge between them — each knows the other's credence, knows that the other knows it, and so on up the hierarchy — then those credences are equal. (This is Aumann's agreement theorem, stated informally.)

(2) A long, sincere, open-ended exchange between competent people who hold no private information puts their credences on the table in exactly that way. Suppose each party has written out a derivation and the other has checked it line by line; each then knows the other's number, knows that the other knows it, and so on, so that the disagreement is fully out in the open between them.

(3) So if two such people share a prior, their credences coincide; a disagreement that survives that much scrutiny shows that they do not share a prior.

(4) Shared evidence and shared training ought to produce a shared prior. If two rational agents start from the same data and the same background, any residual difference in their priors must be traceable to a difference in information, and by hypothesis there is no such difference; for epistemic peers, a common prior is the default.

(5) So a disagreement that survives full mutual scrutiny between two peers means that at least one of them has reasoned badly.

To fix ideas: two statisticians in one lab, both careful Bayesians, both working from exactly the same data and the same model space, argue for two years about whether a result replicates. Each has checked the other's derivation and grants that every step is valid; each regards the other's credence as one a competent person might hold; one settles at 0.85, the other at 0.45. Neither has private evidence, and neither thinks the other is careless, dishonest, or confused. The argument says this situation is impossible as described.

I am not willing to accept that. I am confident in (1), and I am confident that both statisticians can be rational and that the mere persistence of their disagreement is not by itself evidence that one of them has failed. I am prepared to lose (5). So the fault is somewhere in (2) through (4), and I cannot see where. Which step should I abandon? For the step you nominate, explain why it looked compelling in the first place, then price the alternatives: what I would be forced to accept if I kept it and gave up a different step instead, and what follows downstream for how much weight a peer's disagreement should carry.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Please write in American English.
