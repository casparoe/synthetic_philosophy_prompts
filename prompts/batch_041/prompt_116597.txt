I'm a novelist who has been writing a collection of essays about how fiction trains our evaluative attitudes, and I've drafted one that I want a philosopher's comments on before I decide whether it holds together. The essay begins from the wrong kind of reasons problem—the puzzle that a fitting-attitude analysis of value appears to count a threat as a reason to admire something, because the threat gives you a reason to have the admiring attitude even though it does not bear on the thing's merits. My draft argues that this problem is usually treated as a narrow issue about value and fittingness, but that its real interest is wider: the same structure appears whenever a novelist arranges a plot so that the reader has overwhelming prudential reason to adopt an attitude the story has not earned. The central case I invent is a novel in which a demonic narrator tells the reader that unless they admire a saucer of mud by the last page, the protagonist will be tortured; the question is whether the reader's resulting admiration is a genuine instance of the wrong kind of reasons or something else entirely, such as a reason to pretend or to bring it about that one has the attitude.

The essay proposes a threefold distinction among reasons that bear on (a) the merits of the object, (b) the costs and benefits of having the attitude, and (c) the social or narrative role of having the attitude—what I call positional reasons—and it claims that the wrong kind of reasons problem is best understood as a clash between (a) and (c) rather than between (a) and (b). I also suggest that this reframing explains why the demon case feels like a trick: the demon is not giving us a reason to admire the mud, but a reason to occupy the position of someone who admires it, and those are different things. A friend who works on metaethics told me the whole thing sounds like a category mistake, and I want to know whether that is right. I am not a philosopher by training, so I may be reinventing a wheel or missing an obvious objection.

I would like you to comment on the draft as a piece of philosophy. Please tell me whether the account of the wrong kind of reasons problem is accurate, whether the literary analogy is doing real work or merely decorating the argument, and whether the positional reasons category collapses into the usual object-given/state-given distinction. Be specific about where the argument is weakest, and suggest one or two counterexamples or cases that would put pressure on the claim that the demon case is about positional reasons. No citations or reading lists, please—I am more interested in whether the conceptual machinery holds up and whether the essay earns its conclusion.
