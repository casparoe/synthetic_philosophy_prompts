I'm putting together a seminar session on family resemblance for second-years, and I've realized the problem with the standard examples is that evreyone has already read them — say the word and half the room has the Investigations passage half-memorized, and the other half nods along without any sense of what would be at stake if the account were false. So instead of asking you to explain the view, I want you to build me a fresh case from scratch. Please take the role of someone who knows the concepts literature cold — the classical definition view, family resemblance, prototype/typicality theory, and the disjunctive or cluster fallback that people reach for when they want to save definitions — and think carefully about whether your case actually does the work, because I'm going to use it as the exam question and if it cheats, my students will find the cheat. Take your time with this one; I'd rather have a single case that works than five that almost do. Start by brainstorming a handful of candidate terms and striking out any that quietly smuggle in a definition, then build the case around whichever survives.

Here's the spec. Pick a mundane term used inside a small, real-ish practice — a trade, a sport, a hobby, a hospital ward, a family's private vocabulary — and NOT one of the burnt-toast examples (nothing about games, chairs, art, religion, species, or pornography; I want a term the room has no prior about). Build a case in which the clear cases are agreed on by everyone, so nobody can dismiss it by pointing at the easy end. Then engineer the interesting object, subject to these constraints: (i) no single feature is shared by every member of the extension, but — and this is the bit I actually care about — it is not the chain structure, where A resembles B, B resembles C, and A and C share nothing at all; I want the harder configuration, where every pair of members resembles the other in some respect and still nothing is common to all, so a clever student can't wave it away with the standard "they're linked by a chain of overlaps" move; (ii) the case stays short and realistic, no more than three short paragraphs, no sci-fi devices, no communities of immortals or aliens, no stipulating that the practitioners are confused; (iii) exactly one feature should be doing the work, and I want you to prove that by giving me a minimal variant of the case in which only that one thing changes and the verdicts move.

Then do the demonstration properly. Say what the classical definition theorist has to say about the engineered object, and whether they're permitted to say "we simply don't know yet" or are forced to say "that's a different term entirely"; what the family resemblance theorist has to say; what the prototype theorist has to say; and — the one I expect to be awkward — what the disjunctive-definition fallback says, i.e. whether writing the extension out as a big disjunction of features trivializes the family resemblance claim or leaves it untouched. Where two of these views land on the same verdict for different reasons, say so, since that's usually the interesting part. Finish with which feature of the case is load-bearing, what collapses if you remove it, and the strongest line of attack for whichever view comes out worst — I'd rather hear the objection now than from the back row. Also flag any point where the case seems to be stipulating its verdicts rather than generating them from the views, since that's the failure mode I'm worried about. No citations or reading lists, please; I just want the case and the reasoning.
