I clerk for the constitutional court of a religiously plural democracy, and I have to draft the bench memorandum on a case the court has just agreed to hear. What I need from you is not a summary of the briefs but a disciplined prediction: on the court's own record, which way should it come out, and what principle it commits itself to either way.

The record. These are the court's relevant holdings from the past two decades; I give you the verdicts, not a survey of the field.

- It upheld a compulsory public-health measure — a vaccination requirement with a genuine epidemiological basis, applying to everyone — even though it fell hardest on a small number of religious objectors who were fined for refusing it.
- It struck down a ban on a method of ritual slaughter, where the only reason ever offered for the ban was the majority's religiously grounded conviction that the practice was barbaric; no independent evidence showed the method caused more suffering than the permitted one, and the prohibition fell almost entirely on two small minorities.
- It struck down a statute authorizing an official prayer at the opening of municipal council meetings, defended as civic tradition and solemnity, to which dissenters had either to stand or conspicuously leave.
- It upheld an exemption permitting members of a minority faith to depart from a general uniform-and-safety rule — a head covering, a ceremonial item — where compliance would have cost them their livelihood and the state's interest in uniformity was slight; the reasoning was framed in terms of equal participation in public life.
- It struck down a public subsidy granted to the majority church and denied to every other congregation, defended on grounds of historical tradition.

The new case. The national legislature has enacted a statute barring the display of religious symbols by students on state-school premises. The statute is facially neutral — it names no faith and forbids the symbols of all of them — and the government's official defence is entirely secular: state neutrality, the protection of children from proselytism, and gender equality. But the coalition that passed it is on record, in documents that have since leaked, as aiming to make the children of one particular minority faith feel unwelcome and to reassure the majority's base; on the evidence, the secular rationale was assembled after the fact. In practice the ban falls almost wholly on that one minority, which is small and has few alternatives: its families can enroll in private religious schools, but these are costly and exist in only a few cities. The burden is real but not total — no one is compelled to profess or renounce a belief, and private worship is untouched. The government's position is that its reasons are the reasons it has stated publicly, and that a court has no business inspecting the motives behind a facially neutral law.

What I want.

First, the candidate rules that fit the record. I expect there is more than one, and I want them stated as rules — what each takes the court to be testing — not as slogans. The clerks' room is split: some of my colleagues think the court has been testing whether a public reason for the law can be given at all; others think it has been testing whether the reason the government actually acted on was a public one; others think it has intervened only where someone was coerced, or only where a minority was being put in its place. Tell me which of these the record actually supports, and whether some rule none of us has named fits it better.

Second, what each surviving rule predicts for the new case, and precisely where they diverge on these facts.

Third, your best guess at the rule the court is really running, with the reasons for preferring it to the others that fit.

Fourth, the closest analog in the record, and a candid statement of the principle the court adopts if it upholds the ban and the principle it adopts if it strikes the ban down — I want the cost of each choice made plain.

Fifth, the single further case I could add to the record that would most sharply force the surviving rules apart, and what each would say about it.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature on public reason, nor for what the court ought to do from first principles; I want the internal reconstruction of the record. If the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so, and tell me which way the tie breaks and why.
