I've somehow become the unofficial referee in a fight between two colleagues in our informatics department, and I'd like you to help me figure out what they're actually disagreeing about. The group is building a program that gets shown a handful of letters drawn in some invented style — say a lowercase a, e, g and m — and is supposed to generate the rest of the alphabet in that same style. The two of them have been circling the same argument since the spring and neither has moved an inch, even though they agree about every example and every result the program spits out. One of them insists that each letter has a core or a skeleton — an a-ness that is the same in every a ever drawn, from a ninth-century manuscript to a spray-painted tag to a bank logo — and that a style is just a systematic way of dressing that core up: thickening the strokes, adding serifs, slanting it, and so on. The other says there is no core whatsoever, and that what makes a shape an a is only its place in a web of contrasts with the other letters it keeps company with, so something is an a as long as it stays sufficiently unlike b, d, q, o and the rest, and "style" is just whatever you can vary without wrecking those contrasts. A third person in the room keeps saying they're both right in different ways, which somehow manages to be less useful than taking a side. I do metaethics, so I'm the wrong person for this, but they've decided I'm the referee.

The reason I think there might be a real question buried here, and not just two people talking past each other, is a specific thing that keeps happening: the generator will produce a shape that ordinary readers reliably identify as an a — it passes every legibility test they run — but which the designers in the room reject as not an a at all, just a decorated blob. So there's some disagreement about which cases even count as data, and I can't tell whether that's a symptom of the deeper fight or the whole of it. My own suspicion, and I want to be clear that it's only a suspicion, is that one of them is answering a question about how human readers actually recognize letters while the other is answering a question about what a designer is normatively trying to preserve, and that they've never noticed because both questions keep getting phrased as "what makes an a an a." But it could just as easily be a purely verbal dispute about the word essence, or a disagreement about whether letterforms are discovered or invented, or a hidden empirical question that a clever experiment could settle, or bedrock intuitions about shape versus contrast that neither of them could argue for.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right, and please skip the reading list — I don't need citations, I need to understand the shape of the thing. Is this substantive or verbal? Are they answering different questions under one heading, and if so which ones? Is there a hidden factual or formal question doing the real work? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, an experiment, a proof, or just changing the question — or whether you think this is the kind of dispute where no progress should be expected and I should stop refereeing and go back to my own work.
