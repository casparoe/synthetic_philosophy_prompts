Leibniz maintained that among the possible worlds God chose the best, and that nothing happens without a sufficient reason. I want a prediction of how he would have responded to a family of problems that postdate him, now grouped under the heading of infinite ethics, and that he never considered. Set the case before him as follows. The candidate worlds are infinitely many, and each contains infinitely many created persons, each person enjoying some finite degree of happiness; a world's value is stipulated to be the sum of the happiness of its inhabitants. On this stipulation, for any world there is another in which every person is somewhat happier, so the array of worlds has no maximum. It is further proposed that an already infinite world is improved by adding one more happy person, or by raising everyone's happiness by a finite amount, even though the sums in question do not converge and so cannot be compared in the usual way. Predict what Leibniz would say. Would he hold that the principle of sufficient reason requires God's choice to land on a maximum, and if there is no maximum, does the best-of-all-possible-worlds doctrine collapse, or does it survive in some altered form? Would he accept the additive theory of value at all, or would he reject the question -- perhaps on the ground that an infinite aggregate is not a number and cannot be increased by a finite addition? And what would he make of the proposal that finite additions improve an infinite world? Reconstruct his likely reasoning in his own conceptual vocabulary -- sufficient reason, the principle of the best, the infinite, harmony -- while making clear where you are extrapolating beyond anything he wrote. Separate what his actual commitments settle from what is genuinely contested among interpreters (for instance, whether his argument requires a greatest world or only a reason to prefer one world to the alternatives), and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Do not quote him or cite texts; paraphrase. Write the whole answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, as one would write a long letter.
