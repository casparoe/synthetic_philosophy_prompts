I program a small documentary festival: roughly 1,200 submissions a year, ninety slots, no realistic chance of my seeing more than a fraction of them. Most of my decisions rest on what other people tell me — pre-screeners' reports, two colleagues whose taste I trust, a distributor's enthusiasm, a director's track record — and then I write the program notes and do press in which I say flatly that a film is extraordinary, or devastating, or the best thing in its strand, and defend the slate to a board that signs off on it. A colleague tells me this is defensible as a programming decision and indefensible as a claim, since I don't have the judgment I'm expressing; I half agree and can't see what follows, because the notes still have to say something.

Give me a procedure I can actually run, one submission at a time, in the ten minutes I have per title, and then again on the copy: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a verdict by itself, and where it stops and hands me a judgment call rather than a fact. It should keep whatever the philosophy of aesthetic testimony genuinely establishes — the difference between acting on someone's say-so and asserting on it, the principle, sometimes called the acquaintance principle, that a genuine aesthetic judgment must rest on first-hand acquaintance with the work, and whether what licenses deference is the testifier's credentials or her access to the thing — and drop whatever can't survive a deadline. Then run it on three cases: a film I cannot see before the deadline, covered by two reports I trust; a film I programmed on a friend's word and disliked once I finally saw it; and the year-end list, where I am asked to rank a film I haven't seen above one I have. Include at least one verdict I won't like, and tell me what the procedure can't settle — including whether someone who defers this much can be said to have taste at all.
