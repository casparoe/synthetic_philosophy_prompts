An essay I read recently (some historian, name escapes me) tried to lay out the cyclical-vs-linear thing in one paragraph and I want to know how much of it I should trust. Here's the gist as best I remember:

"For most of history people thought time went in cycles — the same things coming round again — and linear time, the idea that history actually goes somewhere, is basicaly a Judeo-Christian import that only took over in the modern West. The cyclical crowd — Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee — all say civilizations move through a fixed sequence of stages and then fall, and they all say the sequence is the same everywhere because there's a single mechanism driving it. For Polybius it's constitutions decaying in a set order, monarchy sliding into tyranny and so on round the wheel. For Ibn Khaldun it's dynasties running out of asabiyya, the group solidarity that gets them going in the first place, after three or four generations. Spengler treats each culture as a living thing with a lifespan and says the West has entered its final phase, technically clever but spiritually dead. Toynbee's line is that civilizations die by their own hand rather than being killed, because they fail to answer some challenge. So the payoff is prediction: if they're right, you can look at a society and say where on the wheel it is. And the reason nobody takes it seriously now is just that modern historians think history is linear."

Something feels off about this but I can't put my finger on it. Can you go through it and tell me what's accurate, what's a simplification a specialist would wince at, and what's flatly wrong — and whether the "it's predictive" payoff even follows from the parts that survive? Also, seperate question: I had a thought that maybe the whole cyclical/linear split is a bit of a fake choice, and the real dividing line is whether you think a "civilization" is a real thing out there in the world or just a label we slap on a stretch of time — and that this is what actually decides wether you end up seeing cycles or progress. Is that a genuine distinction or am I just inventing something to sound clever? Don't worry about citations, I'm not writing a paper.
