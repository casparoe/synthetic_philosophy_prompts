I'm collecting short stories that each smuggle in one idea, and I need one for the Condorcet jury theorem. Setup I have in mind, change whatever you like: a small town settles criminal cases by majority vote of a randomly drawn panel of citizens, each panelist being, on their own, right a little more than half the time. Two things I want the reader to feel by the end, without ever hearing the theorem's name or the 18th century mentioned. First, why adding panelists makes the majority more reliable than any single panelist when the votes are independent, and why that whole reassurance flips once individual accuracy slips below one half — and let someone do the grim arithmetic of how many panelists you'd need for real confidence when each one is only a hair better than a coin. Second, and this is seperate, have a character run into Condorcet's other, unrelated headache: majority rule that goes in circles once there are three or more options and no way to order them, so the town's "will" depends on the order things get voted on. Don't let the two get mashed together — the accuracy point and the cycling point are different results and I want a reader who notices. Somewhere in there the independence assumption should visibly break (gossip before the vote, families, whatever you like), since that's the load-bearing wall. Under 1500 words, American English.
