For the past few years I have been reporting on the dispute over whether thermodynamics explains the direction of time, and I have come to suspect that the participants are not really disagreeing about physics. They work with the same statistical-mechanical formalism and the same low-entropy initial condition; the argument keeps turning on whether that condition does explanatory work or merely restates what needs explaining, on whether it deserves to be called a law, and on whether the probabilities in statistical mechanics are objective chances or epistemic ones. My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that this is not an empirical dispute and not finally a dispute about entropy, but a disagreement about what an explanation has to be — that someone who takes laws to be the regularities that best systematize the world and someone who takes laws to govern it will never be moved by a calculation. Consider a universe whose entropy is low at both ends of time and maximal in the middle: both camps can run the same equations on it, they draw opposite lessons from it, and I cannot see what observation would settle the matter. Using what each side actually argues, diagnose the disagreement for me — is it substantive or verbal, is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, are the two sides answering different questions under one name — and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress, or why none should be expected.
