I have been trying to work out what model of work, harm, and responsibility is built into the way Japan handles death by overwork, because the pieces do not seem to come from a single theory. On the compensation side, the state presumes that a heart attack or stroke was caused by the job when overtime in the month before onset exceeded 100 hours, or when the monthly overtime average over the two to six months before onset exceeded 80 hours: a purely quantitative dose threshold, applied to a physiological outcome, with no inquiry into why the hours were worked or who wanted them worked. On the regulatory side, the same legal order sets a principled ceiling of 45 hours of overtime a month and 360 a year, then allows special clauses to stretch that ceiling up toward the very same numbers the compensation regime treats as lethal; exempts managers and supervisors from the overtime provisions altogether; and has created a further category of high-paid specialists who are exempt from working-time limits, which means that when one of them dies the hour-based presumption that does most of the work elsewhere is simply unavailable and causation must be shown some other way. The suicide branch of the system runs on nothing like hours: it runs on a checklist of psychological burdens, with harassment now among the most frequently cited. And the law's baseline for ordinary workers is that genuinely voluntary extra work is not overtime at all, so the employer's duty attaches only where the work was effectively commanded. France, by contrast, treats the 35-hour week as a title the worker holds rather than a risk threshold the coroner consults.

Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of this arrangement. What conception of harm does it presuppose: that overwork is a cumulative dose with a cliff in it, or that hours are merely a convenient proxy for a causal story the state cannot litigate? What conception of the worker's agency, given that consent under a labor market with thin exit options is treated as sufficient to move an hour out of the employer's responsibility and into the worker's? What conception of the firm, given that the protected class is defined by rank rather than by vulnerability, so that the people with the least ability to refuse are protected and the people with the most formal freedom are not? What conception of leisure, given that time off appears here only as recovery from a risk rather than as something the worker has a claim to? Say which of these the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is underdetermined enough that a Protestant-work-ethic reading, a republican anti-domination reading, and a straightforward epidemiological reading would all license it. Then tell me what would have to change if the state adopted each of those rival views in earnest: what the hour thresholds would become, whether the exemptions could survive, and whether the 35-hour week would look like a labor-market policy or a different theory of what a life is for. I am especially interested in whether the 80 and 100 hour numbers are doing physiological work or administrative work, and what the practice looks like once firms learn to schedule against them.
