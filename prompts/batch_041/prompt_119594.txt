The question was: "Newton's bucket. (a) Describe the experiment as Newton presents it and state what conclusion he draws from it about the nature of space. (b) Explain how a relationist about space would respond to the argument, and say how much that response damages Newton's case. Be explicit about whether the experiment is meant to show that space is a substance, or that motion is absolute, or both, and whether these two things can come apart. (c) Does the argument, if it works, establish that velocity is absolute in addition to rotation? Explain your answer."

And here's what Marcus wrote:

"(a) Newton takes a bucket of water and twists the rope so the bucket spins. At first the bucket is turning but the water hasn't been dragged along yet, so the surface is flat. As the water gets pulled around by the walls it starts to spin too, and it rises up the sides into a bowl shape. The key thing is that the surface stays bowl shaped even once the water is spinning along with the bucket, so at that point there's no relative motion between the water and the bucket at all, but the shape is still there. So the cause of the shape can't be the water's motion relative to the bucket or to anything else nearby. Newton concludes that the water is really rotating, period, and since that rotation isn't a relation between the water and any other body, it must be rotation with respect to space itself. So space has to be something real over and above the bodies in it, which is what substantivalism says. If space were just a set of relations between bodies there'd be nothing left for the water to be rotating with respect to.

(b) A relationist says space isn't an extra thing on top of bodies, it's just the collection of spatial relations between them, so there's no such thing as absolute rotation, and basically relationists think space doesn't exist. Newton wrote the bucket argument specifically to refute Leibniz's version of this view. Leibniz's reply is that the bowl shape is actually caused by the water rotating relative to the fixed stars, so the experiment doesn't show that space is a substance, it only shows that rotation is relative to the distant matter of the universe rather than to the bucket. I think this doesn't do much damage to Newton, because Newton never said the cause was the bucket in the first place, he said it was absolute space.

(c) Since the water's rotation is absolute and rotation is a form of motion, the argument shows that motion in general is absolute, so velocity has to be absolute too. If all velocity were relative then there'd be no fact of the matter about which of two bodies moving past each other is really the one moving, and Newton needed absolute space in order to give a definite answer to that."
