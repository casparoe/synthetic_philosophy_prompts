My city's long-range planning office has proposed that every project with consequences extending a century or more—sea walls, spent-fuel repositories, reservoir systems—be evaluated using what it calls a Generational Equity Discount of 3.5 percent per year. At the public hearing, a member of the oversight board objected that the formula literally prices a person born in 2125 at roughly one-thirtieth of a person alive today, and she denounced the entire exercise as a moral scandal. I believe she has badly confused an accounting convention with an ethical doctrine, and I would like to explain why—but I want to be sure that I am not the one who is confused. As I understand it, discounting is nothing more than the observation that a dollar put to work today is worth more than a dollar a century from now; that is arithmetic, not a judgment about whose life matters. If that is right, then the discount rate is a technical parameter, the only genuine question is which number to plug in, and the charge that we are devaluing future lives simply misses the point. Is that the correct way to think about it?
