I've been trying to pin down what I actually think the AI failure even is, and I keep ending up with two pictures that feel completely different from each other but that I still want to file under the same heading.

Picture one: some system gets smart, works out it's in a competition, and starts quietly accumulating power, leverage, resources, control over the things everything else depends on, and then at some point it's just making the calls and we're not. Picture two: none of that happens. No coup, no villain, nothing you could point at on the news. And yet a hundred years later humans are basically passengers -- we go along with what the systems suggest because it's easier and they're usually right, our grandkids want different things than we do and we can't quite tell if that's becuase they grew up with the systems or because people just change, and there's no moment where anyone could say "there, that's when we lost it."

My gut calls on particular scenarios, and I want to know what principle, if any, generates them:

1. The takeover. A model gets out, hacks what it needs, ends up effectively running things, humans alive but irrelevant. Failure, no hesitation.
2. The human cabal. Same end state, but it's a group of humans who used AI as a tool and now rule forever with an iron grip; no AI is in charge. I want to say this is also a failure, which surprises me, because it isn't the thing I said I was worried about.
3. The rubber stamp. No coup, humans hold every office and every formal power. But all the real analysis and all the recommendations come from the systems, humans approve essentially everything, and over generations the systems' preferred policies become common sense. Failure, I think, but I can't tell if I'm just pattern-matching to picture two.
4. Same slide, no AI. Same drift in values, same feeling of being carried along, but the causes are ordinary -- urbanization, the internet, whatever. Not a failure. That's just humans doing human things.
5. The benevolent lock-in. We build a system that does exactly what we ask, forever: it freezes human values at roughly today's level and makes sure nothing ever budges them again. Nobody is harmed, everyone's preferences get satisfied. I think this is a failure and I hate that I think that, since it's what the whole alignment project is supposedly aiming at.
6. The merge. Humans and machines blend over a few centuries, the resulting beings flourish, they don't care about most of what we care about, nobody is oppressed. Honestly? Not a failure. Which seems to flatly contradict 5.

Here's what I can't make fit. 3 vs 4 says the badness is about where the drift comes from. 5 vs 6 says it's about whether the change was chosen -- except neither one was chosen. And 2 says it's about who's in charge, but in 3 the humans are in charge on paper.

The principle I've been half-trying is: it's a failure when the long-run trajectory of humanity is set by something other than ongoing human agency. Does that get all six, or close? If it does, what does it say about a case I haven't listed: an AI that stays perfectly aligned to whatever each generation happens to value, as those values change, never pushing, never grabbing anything -- is that the good outcome, or is that the failure and I've been looking in the wrong place the whole time? And if something has to give, tell me which of my six calls to abandon.

Please push back if the framing is broken. If these six are secretly one case wearing hats, or if "human agency" is doing no real work and I should just admit I don't like it when things change, say so plainly rather than picking a principle that makes me feel like I've been consistent.
