A friend of mine, Dan, announced at dinner that the city museum is free on Thursdays. It is true, but Dan had only his recollection of what a friend once told him, and he admitted afterward that he had half-wondered whether that friend was confusing our museum with one in another city. No one made plans, no one lost anything, and Dan believed what he said. Two friends of mine have been arguing about it ever since, and the argument has widened into a general one.

Anna says Dan did nothing wrong: you may say what you believe, provided your grounds are not crazy. If you were entitled to say only what you actually know, with every way of being wrong ruled out, almost nothing anyone says would qualify.

Ben says that is the point — knowledge is demanding, and the demand is what separates asserting from guessing. Had the museum turned out to charge admission, we would all have blamed Dan; the best explanation is that he was never entitled to say it.

Anna replies that we blame people for things that were not wrong to do, so this shows only that Dan took a risk, not that he broke a rule. Ben answers that the half-suspicion is the crux: a nagging doubt about one's source is exactly what keeps a belief from being knowledge, and asserting through such a doubt is like bluffing at cards — gambling with other people's plans. Anna says the size of the gamble matters: Dan's claim cost nobody anything, whereas a doctor saying the same sort of thing about a diagnosis would be out of line, so a rule blind to what is at stake has the wrong shape. Ben says that if she may bring stakes in, she has given up her own position for a third one.

Please work through this exchange move by move, saying who is right about what rather than who wins. I would especially like to know whether the truth of what Dan said, or the fact that he believed it, or his half-suspicion, settles whether he was entitled to say it; whether being entitled to say something and being blameworthy for saying it are one question or two; whether the matter should be judged by what Dan could have known at the time or by what we learned afterward; and whether Anna's appeal to stakes repairs her position or replaces it. End by saying which of them is closer to right overall, and what both of them are missing.
