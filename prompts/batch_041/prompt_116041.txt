I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I keep running into the same proverb whenever my colleagues argue about punishment: There but for the grace of God go I. I gather the line is traditionally traced to a sixteenth-century English martyr who is said to have used it of himself while watching condemned criminals being taken to their executions, which would make its original home a theology of sin and unearned grace rather than any thesis about outcomes; whatever its history, it now circulates in my department as an argument. A friend who works on sentencing put it to me like this. Two men drive home drunk; each runs a red light, one when the pavement is empty and one when a child is standing there; the law punishes the second far more severely, and my friend thinks this is indefensible, because the only difference between the two men is grace, and we do not punish people for what is a matter of grace. I find the conclusion tempting and the reasoning opaque, and I want to see exactly how much of that one sentence is doing the work.

Please act as a philosopher working on moral responsibility and the criminal law, and answer at length. Begin by spending a few sentences brainstorming the space of readings before committing to any of them, so that you don't simply fasten on the first one that comes to mind. Then give me a numbered menu of the distinct, precisely stated theses the proverb could be expressing, ranging from the nearly vacuous to the very strong. I would expect the menu to include at least the following candidates, though you should add any others you think belong: the bare exhortation to humility; a claim about the limits of our evidence concerning counterfactuals, namely that neither man could have known which of them would hit a child; a claim that character and dispositions are themselves a matter of luck, so that the drunk driver is no more the author of his recklessness than of the child's death; a claim that no one is ever deserving of anything, praise and blame included; and a claim about divine grace that may not survive translation into secular terms. For each entry, state the thesis precisely, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, identify the further premises it needs, and then say whether it actually supports my friend's conclusion that the two drivers should be treated alike, or whether it supports something stronger (that neither is blameworthy at all), something weaker (that blame is equal but punishment may still differ), or, surprisingly, the opposite of what he wants. I am especially interested in which readings turn on a substantive position about how far luck reaches into character, which ones collapse into something much more modest, and whether any reading lets my friend keep his conclusion while also keeping the ordinary judgment that what the unlucky driver did was seriously wrong. Close by telling me which reading the people who reach for this proverb in an argument most likely intend, and what sentence they should say instead if they want to be making a claim they can defend.
