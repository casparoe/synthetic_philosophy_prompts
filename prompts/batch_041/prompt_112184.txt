I'm putting together a session for an upper-level bioethics seminar I run this term — sixteen students, ninety minutes, a mix of pre-med and philosophy — and I want to spend the whole session on one thing: that the line between being alive and being dead is drawn by a decision rather than discovered as a fact, and specifically the uncomfortable place where the definition of death meets organ procurement. My students will cheerfully recite the standard line that brain death and circulatory death are two criteria answering to a single underlying definition, and that both are meant to require irreversible loss of function. What I want them to feel in their bones is the difference between a heart that cannot be restarted and a heart that simply will not be restarted — the gap between irreversibility and what one might call permanence — and how the whole edifice of donation after circulatory death leans on quietly sliding from one of those to the other. I don't want to lecture them into it; I want them to walk into it themselves.

So please design a runnable activity. The frame I have in mind, though you should improve on it if you can, is a hospital ethics committee that has to write its institution's protocol for controlled donation after circulatory death: after life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn, how long must the heart have been stopped before the patient counts as dead and the surgical team may proceed? I want them to have to fix a number, then apply their own rule to a set of cases I'll hand them, and then hit the discovery that the number they chose is doing the philosophical work — that they have all along been deciding when death happens, and that the organ-viability clock, not biology, is what really pins the number down. Arrange it so that the discovery arrives through the mechanics of the exercise rather than through anything I say out loud. Then give me a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the underlying problem — the permanence/irreversibility distinction, the dead-donor rule, the way a criterion smuggles in a normative choice — and that is honest about which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Include the set-up (roles, materials, any handouts, how the cases are presented), a timeline for the ninety minutes, the rules or payoffs that generate the pressure, an account of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and the debrief itself. Assume no real stakes and no real money. Write in British English throughout, and keep the whole thing to no more than 900 words.
