Consider a single regulator that treats two products posing the same hazard in opposite ways. A new medicine must be shown to be safe and effective before it may be sold: the manufacturer carries the burden, the default is exclusion, and the product stays off the market until the evidence arrives. A dietary supplement may be sold as soon as it is marketed, and the regulator must show that it is unsafe before it can be withdrawn: the regulator carries the burden, the default is access, and the product stays on the market until the evidence arrives. A substance can pass from one regime to the other by changing how it is labeled rather than by changing what it does.

Reconstruct the theory of risk and evidence that this asymmetry presupposes — the account of the precautionary principle, or of what a regulatory default is, under which the two regimes form one coherent policy rather than an accident. Distinguish three readings of precaution and say which the practice commits to: (i) precaution as a stricter evidential threshold for permitting a hazard; (ii) precaution as an allocation of the burden of proof, so that whoever would change the status quo must justify the change; (iii) precaution as a substantive priority of avoiding harms over securing benefits. Then say what the practice rules out: a single category-neutral cost-benefit test, a single category-neutral precautionary test, or a rule placing the burden on whoever would disturb the present state of affairs, whatever that state happens to be. Note that under reading (ii) precaution is a friend of incumbents — the existing supplement industry is protected by the very rule that keeps novel drugs off the shelf — and say whether that is a reductio, a feature, or evidence that the practice is not about precaution at all.

The asymmetry also appears to rest on a further premise: that the harms it guards against are irreversible, so that wrongly blocking a good product can be corrected later while wrongly permitting a bad one cannot. Test the practice against that premise. Does it presuppose irreversibility, or would it license the same asymmetry even for reversible harms? If irreversibility is doing the work, the practice is defensible only where harms are irreversible, and its extension to reversible harms is a mistake; if not, some other commitment is doing the work, and you should name it.

Finally, suppose the regulator replaced the asymmetry with a single category-neutral standard. Describe the two most defensible candidates, say which products and industries would move under each, which harms would rise and which would fall, and which standard you would adopt.

Throughout, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license it.
