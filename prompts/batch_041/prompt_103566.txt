Write me a parody of an official product-recall notice — the kind of deadpan thing the CPSC or a consumer safety agency puts out for a defective toaster — except the recalled product is a batch of intransitive preferences. Invent a plausible regulator (something like the Bureau of Practical Consistency or the Office of Preference Standards) and a lot number. Around 700 words, formatted like a real recall: affected product, hazard description, what consumers should do, remedy, and a "what this notice does not say" section. The target is the coherence argument for expected utility maximization — specifically the move from "an agent with cyclic preferences can be made to pay a fee and end up exactly where they started" to "that agent is irrational." Nobody real gets named; the whole thing should be in the voice of a bored civil servant.

The jokes have to come from getting the argument right, so a decision theorist should nod rather than wince. Things I want it to be accurate about, however it smuggles them in: that the pump only works if you accept the trades one at a time, on each occasion, as offered — so the notice has to be weirdly insistent on that point, and treat a consumer who sees the cycle coming and just stops trading as a compliance problem rather than a counterexample; that what's demonstrated is a liability to a fee-extracting adversary, not an error about anything, since at no point is the cycler wrong about any pair; that transitivity and completeness are two different requirements and the agency should get noticeably shakier when it drifts onto completeness; and that the full representation theorem wants more than transitivity — it needs completeness, continuity, and independence, and it delivers a utility function only up to positive affine transformation, which the notice should mention in a way that suggests the author doesn't fully understand why that matters. The remedy section should say the only approved fix is "preference recalibration," and the side effects should be the real costs of forcing coherence: you can no longer leave two options unranked without something being lost, and you can no longer do the Allais thing. Have an intern's footnote or a correction notice at the bottom pointing out that ordinary risk aversion is fine, that it's only the sure-thing-violating kind the bureau is after, and that the bureau regrets the confusion. Keep it readable for someone who's never heard the term "money pump" — the joke should land either way — and don't ask for citations or further reading.
