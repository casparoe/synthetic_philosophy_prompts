I'm a novelist, midway through a book about a physician who spends a career watching the line between sickness and mere variation move, and I've noticed a pattern I can't find a satisfying name for. The line doesn't seem to move with new findings about the body so much as with the arrival and departure of things we can do about a condition. A cluster of traits that for thirty years was treated as a temperament — a child who is dreamy, restless, easily distracted — is reclassified as a disorder in the same decade a pill for it reaches the market. Grief that used to be a season of life becomes a condition with formal criteria once a medication is available for it. And the movement runs backward too: when a treatment is withdrawn, or turns out not to work, the same condition quietly slides back toward being just how some people are. My characters keep arguing about whether a diagnosis names something in the patient or something in the clinic's toolkit, and I keep feeling the answer is neither, exactly.

What I want to know is whether this specific pattern has an established name — not the general idea that medicine has grown imperial, which I've encountered, but the tighter phenomenon in which the boundary of disease tracks the availability of an intervention rather than any change in the underlying biology. If there's a term, I want to know precisely what it covers and, more importantly, what it doesn't: how it differs from the broader charge that medicine keeps annexing ordinary life, and how it differs from the claim that disease categories are simply social constructions. If there's no term, coin one and define it, and tell me where its edges are — does a condition count only when a treatment is actually on the market, or is a research program enough? Does the pattern include cases where the demotion happens because the treatment failed, or only where it was withdrawn for other reasons? Please answer in American English, in no more than 350 words, and don't bother with references; I'd rather have the carving than the citations.
