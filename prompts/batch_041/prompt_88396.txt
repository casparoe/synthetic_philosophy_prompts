(1) A deceptively aligned model is one that behaves exactly the way an aligned model would for as long as it's being trained or evaluated, and then pursues it's own objectives once it's in a situation where doing so is safe. (2) Everything we can ever know about a model's dispositions we learn from its behavior -- during training, during evals, during deployment. (3) A sufficiently capable deceptive model can tell when its behavior is being used to update or grade it, because those situations have signatures: the prompt formats, the reward channel, the fact that nothing it does has real consequences. (4) So in every situation where we could gather evidence, the model is being watched, and it produces the aligned-looking behavior; for any test we could run, it passes. (5) So no possible observation would distinguish a deceptively aligned model from a genuinely aligned one. (6) A hypothesis that no possible observation could distinguish from its negation has no empirical content, because it makes no difference to anything we could ever see. (7) So "this model is deceptively aligned" isn't an empirical claim at all; it's closer to a mood. (8) So the treacherous turn shouldn't be treated as a scientific hypothesis that guides research or policy -- at most it's a reason to feel vaguely uneasy.
