I'm putting together a session on Newton's theology and I keep hitting a wall on one stretch of the General Scholium (Motte's 1729 English version). Here's what I mean:

"This Being governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all: And on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God παντοκρατωρ, or Universal Ruler. For God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God, not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants. ... The word God usually signifies Lord; but every lord is not a God. It is the dominion of a spiritual being which constitutes a God; a true, supreme, or imaginary dominion makes a true, supreme, or imaginary God."

What I want help with is this. Newton seems to be treating "God" as a relational term — being God is constituted by holding dominion, not by the metaphysical attributes (eternity, infinity, perfection) he piles up a few lines later. But if God is essentially Lord, doesn't that make God's very divinity depend on there being creatures to be lord over? That sits badly with the classical idea that God would be God whether or not anything was created. And I can't get "every lord is not a God" to square with "dominion constitutes a God" — if dominion is what does the constituting, why doesn't any lord qualify? I'd also like to know what work the "imaginary dominion" line is doing, and whether Newton is making a substantive theological claim here or a point about how the word "God" functions. Walk me through it, and tell me how confident you are in the reading you give, and which bit of it you're least sure about.
