I am assembling seminar materials on performance legitimacy — the claim that a government's record of delivering prosperity, security, and order is itself a ground of political legitimacy, sufficient to justify authoritarian rule. Before we assess any particular defense of that claim, I want its specification written out: what must a theory of political legitimacy be able to do before the performance argument counts as settled rather than assumed? Give me the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it — the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results it must remain consistent with. Keep legitimacy distinct from mere stability, from legality, and from the bare fact that people believe the regime legitimate. Three demands I especially want addressed: first, that the theory handle a regime that is broadly successful yet built on the systematic sacrifice of a small, disfavored group; second, that it say what becomes of legitimacy when the performance on which it was founded ends; and third, that it confront the fact that any performance comparison presupposes a counterfactual about how the country would otherwise have fared, which no one can observe. The cases usually cited are Singapore and China under reform, but treat them as illustrations rather than evidence. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, and identify any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most need. Leave the competing theorists to me.
