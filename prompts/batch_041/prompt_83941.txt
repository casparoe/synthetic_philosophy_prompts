I've been going back and fourth with a friend about why promises bind, and I want to pin down where our disagreement actually lives instead of just trading intuitions at each other. Her line is that a promise is essentially something you do *to* someone: you hand them a claim, and what makes it binding is that they now have standing to demand performance and to release you from it. Mine is that this gets the order of explanation backwards -- the obligation comes from the promisor's act, or from a practice she's participating in, and the promisee's expectations are downstream of that rather than the source of it. The case that started the whole fight is what I've been calling a naked promise: I promise my dying grandmother I'll keep the family land undeveloped. She dies that same night. Nobody else knows about the promise, nobody expects anything of me, there's no one around to be let down and no one with the standing to release me, and by the time I could actually break it everyone who could possibly care is dead. I say it still binds. My friend says that's just sentiment plus a habit of keeping my word, and that if it "binds" at all it's because of my own interest in being a certain kind of person -- which she says isn't promissory obligation, it's self-regard wearing a promise costume.

So give me your own credence -- an actual number -- that the correct account of promissory obligation is promisee-dependent in roughly her sense, i.e. that the obligation exists only if and because some actual promisee holds a claim (an expectation, a right to demand, a power to waive), as opposed to being generated by the promisor's act or intention alone. Take "correct account" at face value; assume for the sake of the question that there's a fact of the matter and you're estimating which way it goes.

And please don't just hand me the number -- decompose it. I want a rough probability for each sub-question it rides on, one line of justification apiece, then the combination: (a) whether the naked-promise intuition is trustworthy evidence at all or just culturally trained squeamishness about breaking your word; (b) whether a practice- or convention-based story counts as promisee-dependent or as a genuine third option; (c) whether the power to *release* is the real tell -- if the promisee can cancel the obligation, doesn't that show the obligation is theirs to hold?; (d) whether a promisor's interest in integrity and self-trust can generate a genuinely promissory obligation, or only a reason of some other kind; and (e) anything else you think belongs in the decomposition that I've missed. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, how much the final number swings if you're wrong about it, and what kind of argument or case would move you most in either direction.
