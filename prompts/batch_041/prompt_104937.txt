I have no philosophical training, but I have been keeping a notebook of cases about what a text means, and I want to know whether my verdicts on them add up to a single principle, what that principle says about cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts I should give up.

My verdicts:

- A novelist says in a late interview that the clocks in her book were just furniture and carried no symbolism. I do not think that settles it; the clocks can still be about the passing of time, and her remark is evidence rather than a verdict.
- A poet's line was misprinted at the typesetter's, and she later said she had come to prefer the misprint. I think the first printing still means what her original wording said, and that a reissue with the new wording means something else.
- A sentence in a novel can be read as sarcasm or as sincerity, and the author says she meant sarcasm. That settles it for me.
- I find a line in a friend's private diary, never meant for anyone's eyes, that reads as a confession. I think only what she meant settles what it means, and that reading it against her is a mistake rather than an interpretation.
- A will whose wording points one way while the testator plainly intended another: the intention governs.
- A contract whose wording points one way while the two parties privately intended different things: the wording governs.
- A manifesto drafted by four people, one of whom later insists the group never meant what the text plainly says: the text governs.
- A poem produced by a machine from a prompt: I am inclined to think it can mean something, and be misread, even though nobody meant anything by it.

The principle I would guess at is this: intention governs when the text is addressed to a particular reader, and the text alone governs when it is offered to whoever happens to read it. Does that actually trace my verdicts, or is some other principle doing the work? Please state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, give two or three further cases of your own with your verdicts on them, and say where the reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind.
