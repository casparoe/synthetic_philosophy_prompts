I run the collection side of a mid-sized city museum — I'm the registrar, so anything that goes on display or out on loan passes my desk. Four or five times a year I have to make a call on an object where "is this the original?" is basically the whole question, and right now I do it by gut, inconsistently, and then whoever shouts loudest wins — curators, developement, the director, sometimes a donor. Cases from the last two years: a marble head re-carved and re-nosed in the 18th century so maybe a third of the surface is later work; a 17th-century panel where the painting is fine but somebody added a signature around 1900; a bronze described to us as a Roman cast after a lost Greek original, next to a 20th-century cast from the same mould that the donor insists is "the same thing"; a manuscript gallery where we display high-quality facsimiles because the real ones can't take the light; a lender offering an "original" impression from a plate that was re-struck after the artist died.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run on these, grounded in what philosophy of art has worked out about copies, forgeries, restoration, and why originals matter at all. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clean no or a clean yes, and where it hands the decision to a person because the issue is values rather than facts. Please be honest about which of my criteria are just institutional fear dressed up as principle. I know the standard distinction between art forms where the notation fixes the work (any copy of the novel is the novel, any competent performance of the score is the piece) and forms where the particular object's history of production is part of what the work is — I want that distinction doing real work in the procedure, not just named. Same for the idea that a forgery can be perceptually perfect and still be worse as art, and that what separates a forgery from an honest reproduction is mostly whether the audience is being told.

The procedure should also survive the weird cases: a thing made in antiquity as a fake of an even older thing, so the fake is itself the genuine antique; a later copy that's our only evidence of a lost original; an object whose "original" state nobody agrees on because it's been restored three times; a living tradition where "the original" isn't how the makers think about the object at all.

Then run it on three of my cases — the 18th-century-restored marble, the added signature, and the facsimile gallery — and include at least one where the answer is something I don't want to hear, because I suspect one of our star objects comes out of this needing a label I can't put on it without the director having a fit.

Practical stuff: I've got about two hours per object, no lab, no money for a conservator's report every time, and I can't bring in outside experts before the call. If the procedure says "consult the conservator" at every step it's useless to me. I'm the one signing off, with the director's rubber stamp, and once a year the board asks why we spent money on something, so it has to end in a decision I can write down in a paragraph and defend. And dont give me citations or a reading list — I can't look anything up and I'd rather have the reasoning than the references. Don't hedge every step into "it depends"; if a step is a judgment call, mark it as one and say what kind of judgment it is.
