I have no training in philosophy, but for a few years I've been circling a thought I can't get to sit still, and I'd like your help finding the principle underneath it — and then a definite answer, not a survey.

The thought is this. For a person to deserve blame for something they did, the deed has to come out of who they are. But who they are — temperament, character, what they care about, how much self-control they have — is something they never assembled. It came from upbringing, genes, the culture they were dropped into, and a long run of accidents. And whatever effort they spent shaping themselves came out of a self that was already shaped. Following the chain back, I never reach a link where the person is the author of themselves. So it looks as though nobody ever deserves blame or praise in the deep sense — and yet nearly every verdict I actually have treats some people as deserving.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can state them.

1. A man whose personality turns cruel because of a brain tumor: not responsible, not blameworthy, full stop.
2. A man raised by parents who beat him, who grows up violent: I don't blame him the way I blame case 3, but I do blame him somewhat, and the somewhat feels like real moral judgment rather than just squeamishness.
3. A colleague who is simply selfish — no trauma, no tumor, just someone who never considers other people: fully blameworthy. I resent him without reservation.
4. A friend who was, by her own account, a bully at school and has spent a decade trying to be kinder: I credit her for the change, and I think she deserves something for it.
5. Same friend, except her change was itself the product of a temperament she didn't choose — she was born eager to please and malleable, so the effort was as involuntary as a sneeze. I still want to credit her, and I can't say why, since by the opening reasoning she had no more to do with that temperament than with her earlier cruelty.
6. Two people with identical upbringings, one turning out kind and one cruel: I don't think the kind one earned anything the cruel one lost. It feels like a coin landing heads.
7. A pianist with perfect pitch who plays beautifully without practicing, versus one with ordinary ears who practiced twenty years to play nearly as well: I admire the second more, and I'm fairly sure I think the second deserves more credit, even though the first is the better musician.

The pair I can't reconcile is 3 against 6. The pair I can't reconcile is 5 against 1. I keep wanting a line between the act being genuinely his and his deserving to suffer for it, but every time I try to draw it, the line either swallows case 3 or lets case 1 back in.

A principle I've been trying on: a person is answerable for an action only so far as the action flows from a self they had some hand in building, where the hand itself needn't be self-built — it only has to be there. I suspect that's a cheat, since it lets in exactly what the opening reasoning ruled out, but it also seems to be what I'm doing when I separate 3 from 6.

What I want: the best principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see what it commits me to; what each clause is there for and which verdict motivates it; what it predicts about cases I haven't listed — in particular someone who deliberately cultivates a worse character over years, and someone who does one decent thing while being on the whole a coward; and an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts it cannot keep. If you must choose between a complicated principle that saves all seven and a simpler one that forces me to give one up, choose, and tell me which verdict you'd sacrifice and why. I'd rather have a definite answer I can argue with than a menu of options with the choice left to me.
