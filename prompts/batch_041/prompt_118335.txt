I belong to a reading group that has spent the past term working through eighteenth-century writing about money and empire, and last week we took Berkeley's *Querist* — the string of queries he brought out in Dublin in the 1730s, in which he asks, among other things, "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" and suggests that gold, silver and paper are "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof". One of our number has built an entire position on that figure, and I should like it audited rather than applauded or waved away. On his reading, once you grant the ticket picture, the rest of the *Querist* follows of itself: real wealth is "the four elements, and man's labour therein"; money matters only in so far as it stirs up and circulates industry; the denominations can survive the disappearance of the bullion, so a paper currency and the national bank Berkeley sketches are not merely permissible but the obvious remedy for Irish distress; and the commonplace alarm about issuing too many tickets is confused, since tickets are not themselves wealth and there is nothing in the vault that could be exhausted. He also takes "counter" in the old sense of the token one reckons with on a board rather than the shop counter, which he says strengthens his case.

What I want from you is an audit of how much the analogy will actually bear. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what stands in for the ticket, the issuer or box office, the goods behind it, the attendant, the seat or performance, and the queue — and say plainly which of these Berkeley supplies and which my friend has had to invent. Then say where the mapping fails, separating the failures that bear on the four conclusions he draws (the paper currency, the national bank, the indifference to the quantity of money, and the diagnosis of Irish poverty as a failure of industry rather than of bullion) from those that are merely decorative. Pay particular attention to what the ticket case quietly imports and the monetary case may lack: that tickets are redeemed rather than held indefinitely; that they are claims on particular goods rather than on whatever the bearer happens to want; that someone stands behind the counter with the goods in hand; that the price of the seats was settled before any ticket was sold; and that the tickets circulate only because each holder expects the next to take them. Then tell me whether the conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether Berkeley would have done better with one of the other figures he canvasses in the same query — money as a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge — or with something else altogether, such as money as a language.

Please structure your answer as follows: "The analogy as Berkeley states it" (roughly 150 words); "The mapping" (a table of correspondences, one row per element, closing with a sentence on what the analogy is being used to license); "Where the mapping breaks" (about 400 words, divided into the breaks that bear on the four conclusions and those that do not); "What the ticket case smuggles in" (about 200 words); "Do the conclusions survive?" (about 300 words, taking the four in turn); and "A better figure, if there is one" (about 150 words).
