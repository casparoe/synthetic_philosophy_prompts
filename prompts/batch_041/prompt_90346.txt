I am drafting a welfare standard for a retailer that sells farmed finfish, shrimp and other crustaceans, and every clause in it will have to be defended in writing. Before I write the clauses I need the specification: what would any adequate theory of the welfare of these animals have to deliver? I want the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that forces it, rather than a survey of positions — and where a requirement turns out to be the prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine demand on any theory, I want that said plainly.

The list has to cover at least this ground. What welfare consists in for a fish or a decapod — felt experience, biological functioning, preference satisfaction — and whether an adequate theory must settle that question or may remain neutral among the answers. A criterion of subjecthood: what would make the answer yes or no for a prawn, a mussel and a salmon. The evidential requirements, with an account of why each piece of evidence is supposed to bear on the verdict: nociceptors and whether their signals reach an integrative centre; motivational trade-offs against a noxious stimulus; the reversal of a response by analgesia rather than by anaesthesia or motor blockade; avoidance learning; self-protection; lasting effects of noxious experience. Calibration cases at both ends, since a theory that gets only the easy cases right is worthless here: it must not admit the cases I am confident are non-subjects — a mussel, a plant, an immune response — and must not exclude the ones I am confident are subjects, a salmon, and both kinds of error have to be priced rather than merely avoided. Extrapolation: the standard will be applied across hundreds of species on thin evidence, so the theory must say how far a finding in one taxon licenses an inference about another, and what to do when the case for a species rests only on its relatives. Verdicts about practice: whether the theory can rank, or at least compare, the aversiveness of crowding, air exposure, handling, live chilling, electrical stunning and live boiling, and whether it owes us anything about killing as distinct from suffering. And the treatment of uncertainty: what it says when sentience is plausible but unproven, and whether a precautionary rule belongs to the theory of welfare itself or is a decision rule grafted onto it from outside.

It should also respect the distinctions that the field tends to collapse — between being sentient and having a welfare, between suffering and dying — and it should explain rather than explain away the cases that embarrass simple views, such as a noxious-evoked withdrawal reflex that outlasts the structures thought necessary for feeling, or a response abolished by a local anaesthetic while the reflex itself remains. Then the part I care about most: flag any pair of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect a graded, evidence-proportional account of sentience sits badly with the bright lines a standard needs, and that the demand for specificity is in tension with the demand to avoid false negatives; tell me whether either suspicion is well founded, which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and finish with a short assessment of how a feelings-based theory, a functioning-based theory and a preference-based theory each fare against the list.
