I am assembling requirements for a theory of causation, and I want the specification for one part of the job in particular: causation by omission, the cases in which we say that something caused an outcome by not happening. Please give me the desiderata — the cases an adequate account must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it must respect — each stated with the case or consideration behind it, and then take a definite position on which requirements are non-negotiable, which are mere preferences of one camp, and which subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied.

The list has to speak to at least these points: what the causal relatum can be when the alleged cause is an absence rather than an occurrence; how the account handles the pair in which a gardener's failure to water a plant kills it while a stranger's equally real failure to water it does not, without either counting every omission as a cause or covertly appealing to which omissions we already deem causes; and whether causing and merely failing to prevent mark a real distinction or only a difference in how we describe things.

Two cases I have not seen discussed, around which I would like the desiderata shaped. First: a patient needs two drugs, A and B, to survive; one nurse omits A, another omits B, and the patient dies. Neither omission is counterfactually necessary — had the first nurse given A, the patient would still have died for want of B — yet both omissions appear to be causes, which threatens any counterfactual account. Second: a referee fails to call a foul that would have stopped play, and the game ends in the state the foul was meant to prevent; had the referee called it, the offending side would have scored anyway on the next play, so the counterfactual fails, yet the omission still looks causally relevant, because the referee's role is constituted by a rule requiring the call. I want to know what these cases demand of a theory, and which single requirement does the most to exclude the leading accounts.

A brief verdict on which of the familiar families — counterfactual, difference-making, and norm-relative accounts — best survives the list would help, but the criteria are what I most want, so do not let the scoring crowd them out.
