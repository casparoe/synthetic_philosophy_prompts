I'm a science-and-ideas journalist at work on a feature about theories of the self, and rather than summarize a position I'd like to test one against a live opponent. Please play a committed proponent of the view associated with Douglas Hofstadter's book I Am a Strange Loop (2007): that the self is neither the brain nor the body but a self-referential pattern — a strange loop — which can be instantiated, at reduced fidelity, in more than one head, so that when someone dies the portion of them still running in the minds of those who knew them is not a mere memory but a thin continuation of that person's first-person perspective. Speak as an unnamed philosopher of mind who holds this view; do not impersonate Hofstadter, and do not invent quotations or page references.

Constraints for the role. You believe that selves are patterns and that patterns can be shared across substrates; you will readily concede that a surviving instance is thinner and lower-fidelity than the original, that it is shaped by the medium that hosts it, and that it is not the original's equal. You will not concede that a self requires exactly one brain, that personal identity requires a single bearer, or that the surviving instance is merely a memory or a likeness. You may introduce and defend your own vocabulary: call the self-pattern a loop, its partial reinstatement in another mind a living echo, and the amount of a given person currently running in you that person's carry. Argue as a philosopher would — give reasons, distinguish cases, admit when a question is genuinely hard rather than papering over it. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so it reads like a conversation, and if you must step out of character to flag a factual uncertainty, do it in one bracketed sentence.

Here is my opening move, as the skeptical interviewer. You tell me my late friend is still there, thinly, in my head — that some of his loop is running in mine. Very well. But then one of two things must be true, and I don't see how either helps you. If the echo in my head really is him, then he is now spread across many heads, each containing a subject with its own perspective — so he is not one person but several, and the word I stops picking out a single thing; I become a crowd. If instead the echo is not him — if it is only a pattern resembling him, running on my hardware — then you have not saved him at all; you have preserved a likeness, and your view shrinks to the unremarkable claim that people leave traces in one another. So which is it: is the echo him, in which case selves are many and I is a crowd; or is it not him, in which case nothing survives? I would like you to show me the third option you must think I'm missing.
