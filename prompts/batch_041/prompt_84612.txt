I sit on the committee that sets the written papers for our master's programme in probability and philosophy, and I'm responsible for the decision-theory section. Every year the Bertrand's paradox question is answered in exactly the same way: the candidate recites the three rehearsed methods — endpoints, radius, midpoint — reports one-third, one-quarter and one-half, and closes with a paragraph observing that the principle of indifference is therefore suspect. I want to break that reflex. The candidate should have to *do* something with the paradox rather than recite it, and I would rather they met a version of the problem they cannot have memorised, so please build the question around the scenario below rather than around the textbook circle alone.

Here is the setting I want you to use. Begin from a unit circle and a random chord, but ask for the *expected length* of the chord rather than the probability that it exceeds some fixed length, and require the candidate to obtain that expectation under each of three randomisation schemes — one in which the chord's two endpoints are chosen independently and uniformly on the circumference, one in which a radius is chosen uniformly and the chord is drawn perpendicular to it at a uniformly chosen point along that radius, and one in which the chord's midpoint is chosen uniformly from the disk — so that the three expectations visibly disagree. Then have them move to a genuinely new case: a random plane cutting a unit ball, whose intersection with the ball is a circle whose radius depends on the plane's distance from the centre. The candidate must construct at least two prima facie legitimate ways of choosing that plane, compute the probability that the resulting intersection circle has radius greater than one-half under each, and explain why the disagreement is *not* resolved by appealing to the ball's symmetry — the rotation group fixes the plane's orientation but says nothing about how the distance from the centre is distributed. The third movement of the question is conceptual: the candidate must state what the principle of indifference actually demands (a measure on the space of possibilities, plus a principled reason to prefer one parameterisation of that space to another), distinguish indifference among outcomes from indifference among parameterisations, and assess whether any invariance or symmetry principle can supply the missing measure, or whether the paradox shows that 'uniformly at random' is simply incomplete until a physical chance mechanism is specified.

Please give me the examination question itself, a model answer of roughly six hundred words that actually performs both the computations and the conceptual moves, and a marking scheme of twenty marks that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves a top answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, treating the three schemes as three rival answers to *one* question rather than as answers to three different questions, or supposing that the symmetry of the ball selects a unique distribution for the offset. At the end, have the model answer state how confident it is in each of its claims and single out the one point in the argument about which it is least certain. Assume the candidate has no library access, so nothing may turn on a citation, a named paper, or a dated attribution.
