I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs and forums, and I run a small, fairly advanced reading group that has spent this term working through the arguments meant to force any rational agent into expected-utility maximization. For the final session I want to set a written exercise, and I need the whole package: the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme I can hand to the two people who will grade it. The topic is the coherence argument for expected utility maximization — the loose cluster of representation theorems, Dutch-book arguments, and money pumps that gets compressed into the claim that a rational agent "must" maximize expected utility.

I don't want a question that can be answered by reciting the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and then intoning "money pump." The crux I want the question to force is the one that actually separates people who understand the argument from people who have merely memorized its vocabulary: the gap between (i) the representation-theorem claim that a preference relation meeting certain axioms can be represented by a utility function, (ii) the no-exploitation claim that a rational agent cannot be systematically pumped, and (iii) the deliberative claim that expected-utility maximization is the uniquely rational way to choose — and whether a money pump, which is usually aimed at preference cycles, reaches incomplete preferences at all.

Build the question around a concrete agent whose preferences are intransitive in a way that includes a gap: it strictly prefers A to C and C to B, but has no preference either way between A and B. Have the question examine the obvious pump against such an agent — it pays a little to move from B to C, pays a little again to move from C to A, and is then offered a no-cost swap back across the gap from A to B, ending worse off than it began. The students should have to say precisely what the pump assumes about the agent's behavior at that last step; whether having no preference between two options is supposed to license swapping them for free, and whether refusing such a swap is irrational; and what becomes of the argument once we distinguish a naive agent that evaluates each trade in isolation from a sophisticated agent that foresees the whole sequence and a resolute agent that pre-commits against it. I also want the question to ask what all this does to the completeness axiom the coherence argument leans on, and to the alignment-flavored use of the argument — the claim that a sufficiently capable agent must have a utility function, and hence a fixed thing to maximize. Please do not have the question ask for citations, reading lists, or references to particular papers or authors; whoever answers it will not be able to look anything up, so it has to stand on the ideas alone.

Write three things. First, the exam question, as a single essay prompt that a strong participant could answer in roughly ninety minutes of writing. Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words that a top participant might produce — it should take a definite line rather than survey the terrain. Third, a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total, where each point names the specific move a top answer has to make and, for each, one line on the characteristic way candidates miss it. I especially want the scheme to separate an answer that concludes that the pump shows incomplete preferences to be irrational from one that sees the pump really targets a trade-acceptance policy, and that declining to trade across a gap is not by itself irrational.
