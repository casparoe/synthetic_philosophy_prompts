For the past two terms I have been convening a small epistemology seminar for our MPhil students, and we are closing the year with a short series of recorded conversations with visiting philosophers — partly for the students' benefit, partly so that I have a teaching resource I can reuse. Our next guest works on the epistemology of peer disagreement, and for the last few years she has been defending a view she calls the two-channel account. Crudely put: she holds that when you meet a peer who disagrees with you about whether p, you receive two quite different pieces of epistemic input — a first-order channel, consisting of whatever reasoning or evidence the peer offers for not-p, which you can in principle assess on its merits, and a second-order channel, consisting of the bare fact that someone you take to be your epistemic equal has arrived at a different verdict. Her central claim is that these two channels can pull in opposite directions, and that consequently the question the conciliationists and the steadfasters are really fighting over — how much weight the disagreement, taken as a whole, ought to receive — is ill-posed. The students have found this bracing but slippery, and I suspect our guest is very practised at retreating to the thought that the two channels are simply doing different work whenever a case becomes awkward. The format is a twenty-minute conversation, so the questions need to be answerable in a few minutes each.

I should like you to draft nine or ten interview questions that press her on the substance rather than inviting her to restate the two-channel account or rehearse the familiar conciliationism/steadfastness taxonomy. Specifically: at least one question on whether the two channels are genuinely separable, or whether the second-order channel can be characterised without covert reference to first-order considerations — and if it cannot, whether her view amounts to a total-evidence account under a new name; at least one on what the account delivers when the peer's reasoning cannot be assessed on its merits, whether because it is too long or too technical or because the peer cannot articulate it at all, since that looks like the case in which the second-order channel must carry the entire load; at least one on the ubiquity of disagreement — if dissent is common, does each additional dissenting peer contribute less and less second-order evidence, and does her account then make the epistemic force of disagreement turn on sociological facts about how often one meets dissenters, which appear irrelevant to what one ought to believe; at least one on self-application, that is, on what the account says about the disagreement between her and the steadfasters, given that she counts as their peer; at least one on whether her verdicts are content-dependent in a way that leaves the account silent, or implausibly permissive, precisely in the moral, political and philosophical disputes where peer disagreement is most keenly felt; and at least one on whether the two-channel picture is meant to apply diachronically as well — to disagreement with one's own earlier self, or with a peer whose evidence one has since absorbed. For each question, please supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; I would far rather have those in hand than improvise. Close with a single question about what would count against the two-channel account, phrased so that she cannot answer it by redescribing the account. No questions about how she came to philosophy or what she is reading at present, and nothing that would oblige either of us to cite a particular paper.
