I'm a retired chemist — 40 years of kinetics — and I only got into philosophy after I retired, so go easy on me. A friend in a phil. department dropped this arguement on me and I've been chewing on it all week.

(1) My last review paper makes about 200 separate claims. I checked the data and I stand behind each one; for any given claim I'm about 99% sure of it. So its rational for me to believe each of the 200 claims.
(2) If it's rational to believe one thing, and rational to believe another, then it's rational to believe them both together.
(3) So, chaining (2) 200 times, it's rational for me to believe all 200 claims at once.
(4) But 200 claims at 99% each have only about a 13% chance of being all true, and honestly I'd bet good money there's at least one slip somewhere in a paper that long. So it's not rational to believe all 200.
(5) So (3) and (4) can't both be right. Same shape as the lottery, by the way — I'm sure each ticket loses, ticket 1, ticket 2, all of them, and equally sure that one of them wins.

Here's my worry. Whatever I drop, it looks like I have to conclude that I never rationally believed my own paper. And I can run the same move on any scientist and any body of work, since we all believe alot more than we're jointly sure of. That seems to prove way too much. So: which step do I give up, and what do I pay for giving it up? I'd really rather keep (1) — I'm not about to stop trusting my own results.
