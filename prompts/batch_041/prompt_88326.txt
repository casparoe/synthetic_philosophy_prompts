I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or archaeology, and I've spent the past few months volunteering on weekends at a local excavation and then reading my way into the interpretive literature around it, which has left me more puzzled than when I started. The gap that bothers me is between what comes out of the ground and what gets said about it: a pit of pots that were clearly smashed on purpose, a peat bog holding a few hundred deliberately bent weapons, a burial with grave goods and the body arranged in what looks like a sleeping pose, a set of near-identical weights, a midden of butchered bone, paintings deep in a cave where nobody seems to have lived. Reports and popular books move from patterns like these to claims about what people believed and feared and how their institutions worked, and they do it with a confidence that doesn't seem to match the evidence. A friend who works in the field tells me the interesting question is what kind of warrant you're using, and that different schools answer it differently; I'd like a map of that rather than a shrug.

So please build me a taxonomy of the positions on this question, laid out as a decision tree rather than as a list of names. It should start from the commitment that at least some material patterns are the residue of deliberate human action rather than of taphonomy and erosion, and each branch node should be a question whose answer separates the views below it, phrased so that someone looking at one of the cases above could answer it without already knowing which position the answer commits them to. I want the branches to cover at least these splits: whether an interpretation has to rule out plain function before it can invoke meaning or belief at all; whether analogy to living or historically documented people is the only bridge from object to belief, and whether that analogy has to be independently testable against the record; whether ritual is a legitimate category of explanation or just a label for our ignorance; whether the target of the reconstruction is individual mental states, collective or public meanings, or only practices and institutions; whether an interpretation has to be falsifiable to count as knowledge; and whether beliefs are supposed to be causes of the material pattern or byproducts of other things. Each leaf should carry the name of a position — a standard label if one exists, otherwise a descriptive one — together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Use the cases above as the test cases the tree has to sort, and add others only where a branch can't be exercised without them.

A few constraints. Don't give me citations, reading lists, or the names of individual scholars; I have no way to check those and I don't want a bibliography, so keep the labels impersonal or descriptive. Aim for eight to ten leaves, and keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout for a reading group. Where two leaves come out of the tree indistinguishable, say so instead of papering over it, and where some combination of answers has no position attached to it, tell me whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. I also suspect I'm running together two separate axes — what the inference is about, and what licenses it — so if a single tree can't carry both, tell me that and give me the second axis separately.
