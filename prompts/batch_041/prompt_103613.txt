I'm a junior in high school writing a long paper for an ethics seminar that I hope to expand into my senior thesis, and my teacher has let me focus on just war theory. I've gotten stuck on the doctrine that soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on a par — that a soldier conscripted into an unjust aggression has the same right to kill, and is no more blameworthy for killing, than a soldier defending against him. I've read enough to know this is usually called the moral equality of combatants, and that it gets defended both as a legal convention, since lawful combatants on either side enjoy immunity from prosecution for killing in accordance with the laws of war, and as a moral truth in its own right; some philosophers I've encountered even argue the doctrine is simply false, because an unjust combatant is doing something impermissible regardless of what the law says. The trouble is that when I press on it, the arguments keep splitting into several quite different questions and I can't tell which ones are actually doing the work. Some people frame it as a question about permissions — whether both sides are permitted to kill — while others frame it as a question about liability, meaning whether both sides deserve to be killed or blamed; some say the whole dispute is downstream of whether you assess a war by its cause or by how it is fought; and still others think the interesting question is what the doctrine is for — military discipline, reciprocity between armies, the protection of civilians, or keeping law and morality from collapsing into one another. I've also noticed that veterans and the writers who discuss moral injury describe guilt in ways that don't look symmetric at all, which makes me wonder whether the equality is a truth about morality or a useful fiction that the practice of war happens to require.

What I want from you is not a survey but a defended agenda. Which of the open questions in this area is most worth settling first, in the sense that the most other questions depend on its answer, and which are comparatively idle or overrated? Please rank something like four or five candidate questions, state the criteria you're using — importance, how many downstream claims rest on the answer, whether a high school student with a year and a library card could actually make progress on it, and whether the answer would change anything for international law, for how soldiers are trained, or for how veterans are treated afterward — and tell me which of my framings above is a genuine crux and which is a distraction. I'd rather have your reasoning about the ordering than a reading list, and I'd like to know what evidence or argument over the next year would move a question up or down your ranking.
