I'm a journalist who writes about science, data, and the history of ideas, and I've spent the past year on two stories I assumed had nothing to do with each other. The first is a long piece about a county archive whose holdings for a certain stretch of the nineteenth century consist almost entirely of records produced by the agencies that policed the community it supposedly documents; the second is a series on how training corpora and benchmarks actually get built — who scrapes, who labels, who drafts the taxonomy, and what falls outside the schema and therefore never gets counted at all. When I described the second story to a historian I'd interviewed for the first, she was a little impatient with me: everything I found surprising, she said, her field had worked out decades ago, and the phrase "silences in the sources" was doing real analytic work rather than being a mood. I want you to test that claim rather than accept it.

Please restate the central commitments of the archive-and-power tradition as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in. The commitments, roughly: that archives are instruments of rule rather than neutral storage; that power operates first and most effectively through the categories that decide what is recordable in the first place (the birth, the deed, the tax assessment, the arrest); that silence is structural rather than accidental, and often lives in the finding aid and the catalog rather than in the boxes; and that the historian's evidence is itself a product of the same administrative machinery it claims to document. The target vocabulary is dataset construction and audit: provenance and lineage, sampling frames, coverage and inclusion criteria, annotation schemas and guidelines, splits, missingness and its mechanisms, documentation artifacts, drift and refresh cycles. Assume I'm a competent practitioner of that vocabulary — I've built and audited datasets, I know what a sampling frame is, and I know what "missing not at random" means — but I'm not a statistician, so if a term of art from statistics is the only honest translation, use it and tell me you're doing so. Keep the translation honest: don't quietly substitute the data-side assumption for the archival one and call the result a translation.

Then the part I actually want, which is the residue report. Which of the archival commitments come through intact, which come through only in a distorted form, and which have no counterpart in the data-curation vocabulary at all? Be specific about the near-misses — the places where a term looks like the right translation but smuggles in an assumption, and where the resemblance is load-bearing in one framework and merely decorative in the other. My own hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than confirm, is that "silence" and "missingness" are the crux: I suspect the statistician's treatments of censoring, truncation, and structural zeros get closer than missingness does, and that even those presuppose a mechanism you can specify, which is exactly what the archival claim denies. Close with a judgment I can defend in print: does the residue show that the data-curation vocabulary is genuinely missing something, or that the archival claims were less determinate than my historian was willing to admit — and if the latter, which of them in particular? Three sharp mismatches would be worth more to me than a table of twenty loose analogies. No reading list or citations, please; I'm not going to chase down titles. Please write in American English.
