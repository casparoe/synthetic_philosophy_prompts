ok — I'm giving a talk on diachronic rationality next month and I want a consistency check on my own views before I embarrass myself. You're a decision theorist who's actually worked on money pumps and on time-slice rationality, so brainstorm first (which of these items even interact with each other?) and then be blunt. Don't tell me each one is "a respectable position" — I know they're respectable, I want to know if they're compatable.

Here's where I've landed:

1. Slice-ism, as I've been calling it in my notes: rationality is entirely synchronic. You're rational or not in virtue of what's in your head at a moment. Nothing is irrational merely because it fails to fit your past state, and there's no requirement to be the kind of creature that's stable across time.

2. So the diachronic Dutch book arguments — for conditionalization, for reflection, that whole family — are worthless. They show that if you don't see the trap coming, you'll lose money. Fine. But exploitabilty isn't irrationality, and an agent who anticipates the book can just decline to bet.

3. Intransitive preferences are irrational, though, and the money pump is what shows it. Someone whose preferences run A > C > B > A, starting out holding A, will hand over money to get walked around the circle and end up holding exactly A again. That's the mark of a defective preference structure.

4. Related: I'd obviously take the mirror-image version of that setup, where the broker pays me the toll at each step instead of charging me. I end up where I started, plus $3. Free money; nothing irrational about taking it.

5. Preference change over time is fine and doesn't have to answer to the earlier self. Case I invented for the talk: Nadia holds a burger booking. Right now she'd pay $20 to swap it for a ramen booking, so she does. She also knows that in six months, once she's sick of ramen, she'd pay $20 to swap back — so she does that too. A broker offers her both swaps, today and in six months. She's out $40 and ends up holding exactly the booking she started with. I say: fine. Tastes change, and nobody owes their past self consistency.

6. But I do think the young heir who wants to hand the estate over to the tenants should lock it up before he inherits — trust, deed, whatever — since he knows that when the day actually comes he'll want to keep it. He's irrational if he leaves himself a way to undo it.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest fix for each real one? Rank them by severity, tell me which one is the actual crack rather than a hairline, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. I don't want a list of five "interesting tensions" — I want to know what it costs me.
