You are a comparative constitutional scholar and political philosopher who works on church–state relations, and I would like you to treat what follows as a problem in conceptual precisification rather than an invitation to survey a field. Think carefully before writing, and open your answer with a brief paragraph of brainstorming — the candidate readings you can see, before you have sorted them — followed by the finished analysis.

The occasion was a public panel on the German church tax: the arrangement under which the state's revenue offices collect a surcharge on the income tax (and capital gains tax) of members of certain churches and religious communities, and under which a member ceases to owe it only by making a formal declaration of departure before a civil authority. One of the other panellists disposed of the entire institution in a single sentence: we are supposed to have 'separation of church and state'. Nobody in the room asked him what he meant, and I have not been able to leave it alone, because the sentence appeared to be doing work that only one or two of its possible readings could support.

What I want from you is the menu, not a ruling on which reading is correct.

1. Set out the distinct precise theses the phrase could be asserting, ordered from the least contentious to the most radical. State each as a proposition with its scope made explicit: what is to be separated from what — institutions, funds, legal authority, personnel, symbols, schooling — by whom, and to what end (protecting the state from the church, the church from the state, or the individual conscience from both).

2. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, a contested principle that reasonable people reject, or simply false; and say what it would imply for the church tax in particular — that it condemns the tax, tolerates it, or is silent about it.

3. Since the phrase is normally deployed as though it named one thing, say which reading is actually doing the work in the standard arguments: the French laïcité argument, the American non-establishment argument, the defences of the English establishment, and the terms of the German debate itself.

4. Identify the reading the panellist most likely had in mind, and say whether it really entails his conclusion — and, if it does, whether he can keep the things he presumably wants to keep: state-funded theology faculties, religious instruction as an ordinary school subject, and chaplaincies in the military and the prisons.

Two constraints. Do not give me citations or a reading list; I want the analysis, not the literature. And do not treat the phrase as having one canonical legal meaning — the point of the exercise is that it does not. Where you suspect a reading is one that nobody actually holds, say so rather than attributing it to a named camp.

Finally, be self-critical about the result. Tell me which of your verdicts you would revise, and under what argument or consideration — for instance, if the opt-out from the tax turned out to be costless in practice, or if the state's collection of the tax could be shown to be severable from any state endorsement of the churches — and identify the step in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
