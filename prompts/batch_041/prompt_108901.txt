I spent my working life on inverse problems — recovering a model of a system from data that never determined it uniquely, and learning to distinguish the parts of a solution that the data actually fixed from the parts that were artifacts of how we had chosen to parameterize the problem. Since retiring I have been reading Davidson on radical interpretation, and the resemblance has become difficult for me to set aside: the interpreter is handed a finite, noisy record of behavior and must recover a total assignment of beliefs and meanings that vastly outruns it, under a constraint — charity — that looks to me less like a discovery about the nature of meaning than like the stabilizing assumption a modeler imposes in order to make an ill-posed problem tractable.

I want you to argue for this thesis: that the indeterminacy Davidson finds in interpretation is better explained as the ill-posedness of the interpretation problem than as a distinctive feature of meaning; that charity operates as a regularizer, selecting among the many hypotheses that fit the evidence; and that once we ask the question a scientist would ask — which features of an interpretation are stably determined by the evidence, and which are fixed only by the regularizer? — the indeterminacy shrinks to something continuous with ordinary underdetermination in the empirical sciences, carrying no special consequences for the metaphysics of meaning.

Argue it as a sustained case rather than a survey; I have read the essays and do not need a literature review. In the course of the argument, do at least the following. State the criterion of well-posedness you are relying on — existence, uniqueness, and stability under perturbation of the data — and say precisely where radical interpretation fails it. Say what charity is doing in this picture, and why treating it as a constraint on admissible hypotheses is more than a relabeling of what Davidson already says. Then confront the strongest version of the opposing view: that charity is constitutive of interpreting anything as a thinker at all, and that Davidson's indeterminacy concerns what there is no fact of the matter about, not what our evidence cannot settle. Finally, say what the regularization does not remove, and whether what remains is enough to sustain his conclusions. If the analogy breaks down somewhere, I would rather have that stated as part of the argument than used as an excuse to avoid it.
