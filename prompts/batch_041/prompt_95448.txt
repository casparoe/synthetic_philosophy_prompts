Eight years ago I moved from Aarhus to a mid-sized city in the American Midwest, and I still have not adjusted to the way the two places talk about success. In Denmark a colleague who announces a promotion is quietly corrected for it — a cooling in the room, a raised eyebrow, a joke that is not quite a joke — and I absorbed that correction early enough that I still apologize for my own good news. In the Midwest the same announcement gets applause, and the applause is sincere. I have been trying to write about this for a year, and I keep circling back to Death of a Salesman, which I taught for the first time last spring and which I now suspect I had been reading entirely through American eyes.

What I want is a reading of the play through the two norms that have been dividing my life. On one side is the Law of Jante, the code that Aksel Sandemose gave its classic form in his 1933 novel about the fictional town of Jante: you are not to think you are anything special, not to think you are as good as us or smarter than us or able to teach us anything, and — the eleventh rule, which serves as the code's penal clause — you had better assume that we know something about you. Sandemose wrote it as satire, and Scandinavians still disagree about whether the law is a brake on ambition or the ground of a genuine egalitarian ethic. On the other side is the American Dream's cult of the self-made man, with its corollary that failure is a verdict on the person rather than on the arrangement, and the psychology of the tall poppy — the Australian and New Zealand name for the cutting down of the successful — where the research on deservingness suggests that what actually provokes resentment is less excellence as such than perceived arrogance or unearned success. It has not escaped me that the countries which gave the law its name sit at the high-mobility end of the standard cross-country comparisons, while the country that celebrates the self-made man sits well below them.

So: is the play a tragedy of the Dream, or does it become, read from Jante, the study of a man whose crime is thinking he is somebody? Willy Loman inflates his sales figures, insists that being well liked is the road to success, and plans his own death as a financial transaction. He is precisely the figure Jante is built to humble. But notice where the cutting down actually comes from: not from the community, which mostly indulges him, but from his own sons, and from himself. The leveling has been installed inside him, which may be a claim about American culture or may be an evasion of the social mechanism I grew up with.

Take the play and the norms equally seriously, and be honest in both directions. Where does the Jantelagen reading illuminate a scene — Willy's flashbacks to his sons' adoration, the Boston hotel room, the restaurant, the insurance policy, the Requiem, the contrast between Biff and Bernard? Where does the lens distort the play, or the play resist the lens — is Linda's insistence that Willy's life be granted weight and attention regardless of what he achieved a third position that neither the Dream nor Jante can name? Does the play quietly recruit its audience as the tall poppy, so that we do to Willy what no character in the play will do? And what does the play supply that the argument alone cannot? I have read enough essays about the American Dream; I want to know what the theatre knows that they do not.

I do not need quotations — I know the play. What I want is a reading I could put in front of a class of Danish and American students, each half of which will see something the other misses.
