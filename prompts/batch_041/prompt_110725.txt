I am a novelist at work on a book that turns on a single procedure: a woman of about fifty elects to have her neurons replaced, one at a time, with functionally identical synthetic components, over the course of roughly a year, remaining awake and in every observable respect herself throughout. The novel follows her. My problem is not the plot but the teaching. The situation carries a philosophical question — what it is for a person to persist, whether there is a fact of the matter about whether she survives the year, and what could serve as the criterion of her survival — and that question has to reach readers who have no philosophical training and no patience for a lecture. I do not need the problem explained to me; I understand it well enough to write the book. What I need is a plan for how the idea gets taught.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind who has spent years teaching this material to undergraduates and who has thought carefully about how untrained intuitions are formed, and act as my consultant. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate orderings of the ideas before you commit to one, and state briefly why you set the others aside. Then give me the following, in order of importance.

1. The sequence in which the components must be introduced, and the dependencies that fix it. What must the reader already have accepted before the question of her survival can be put to them as a genuine question rather than a riddle about machinery?

2. The one example that carries the whole thing. Should it be the novel's own case, or something external? State what the carrying example must have and what it must lack. In particular, I want a judgment on whether the familiar puzzle of the ship rebuilt plank by plank should be kept out entirely, and why.

3. The misconceptions readers will predictably bring or form, in the order they will form them — my own guess is that the first will be some version of "this is a copy, and copies are not people" — and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Note which of these misconceptions are also felt by competent philosophers, since those cannot simply be corrected.

4. The granularity problem. Does the reader's intuition track something real in the difference between replacing one neuron at a time, one synapse at a time, and large blocks at once, or is it tracking only the rate of change? Advise me whether to exploit this or to suppress it, and where in the sequence it belongs.

5. A complication specific to my book: the consent form includes a reversal clause, on the understanding that the process could in principle be run backwards. Is the notion of reversal coherent on the premise I have given, or does it presuppose an answer to the very question the novel is asking? Should I teach with it or treat it as a trap?

6. The check for understanding. I make book-club appearances, so give me a single question I can put to a room to discover whether they have grasped the question rather than merely followed the plot.

7. What I should leave out at this level, and say so.

Be concrete, rank your recommendations, and reason carefully about the dependency structure rather than offering a survey of positions.
