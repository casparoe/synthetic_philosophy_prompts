Here's the position I've ended up defending, and I want you to tell me what should count against it. Take a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two transparent agents: each player writes down a program and hands it to a referee, each program gets to read the other's source code before it outputs an action, and then the two actions are played once and that's it. I think mutual cooperation is the correct prediction there, and not a cheap trick. The equilibrium is plainly there: write a program whose rule is, cooperate if the other program is this very same program, and defect otherwise, and neither side has any incentive to deviate. I want to say that's more than a cute fixed point — I want to say it's the right account of what rational cooperation between like-minded transparent agents actually amounts to, and that it dissolves the usual hand-wringing about cooperating in a one-shot PD. I realize I'm compressing a lot into that (the referee, the code-reading, the self-reference), so fill in whatever you think the strongest version of the view is and run with that.

So, what would count against it? I want the map of its exposure, not a summary of the standard debate. Does the thing survive when the symmetry breaks — different payoffs on the two sides, noise, one player a bit more likely to misread the other's code, or a population of more than two agents who aren't all clones of each other? I have a nagging worry that the referee and the code-reading assumption are doing all the work, and that once you strip the mediator away and leave only two agents who can predict each other, the cooperation quietly evaporates — if that's right, is it a refutation of the view or just a relocation of the puzzle? I'd also like to know whether the machinery licenses cooperation where it obviously shouldn't: if the same reasoning tells me to cooperate with a blackmailer, or to pay up in some counterfactual-mugging-type case, does that count against the account, or is it a bullet I should just bite?
