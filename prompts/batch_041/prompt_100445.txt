A friend who writes popular philosophy sent me the following paragraph as a summary of the "utility monster" objection to utilitarianism, and I would like a careful audit of it rather than a defense of either side. This is what she wrote:

"The utility monster is a thought experiment that refutes utilitarianism. Imagine a being who gets vastly more pleasure out of any given unit of resource than anyone else does — a being for whom a single slice of bread produces more ecstasy than the rest of us get from a lifetime of achievement. A utilitarian, committed to maximizing total happiness, has to keep feeding this monster, sacrificing the rest of us one by one, because every sacrifice adds more to the total than it subtracts. This shows the theory is internally inconsistent: it cannot both respect each person and maximize the sum, so it has to be rejected. The monster has to be a sadist — the being who delights in others' suffering — which is why the argument is really about how utilitarianism licenses cruelty. And it is the same basic move as the experience machine: a thought experiment that exposes the theory's verdicts as absurd. Since the monster's utility is infinite, the objection can be dismissed by anyone who adopts a bounded utility function, which is why sensible utilitarians now bound their utility functions and get on with it. Anyone who bites the bullet and accepts the monster has simply abandoned morality."

I am inclined to think utilitarianism survives this, but I do not want to be comforted — I want to know where the paragraph is reliable. Please go claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a careful utilitarian, or a philosopher of ethics who has thought hard about aggregation, would accept it, wince at it, or reject it outright. Separate what is accurate from the common simplifications and from the genuine errors, and indicate which of the errors are doing the work in her conclusion. Then tell me whether her verdict — that the monster refutes the theory — follows from any of the parts that are accurate, or only from the parts you have flagged. I am especially interested in three points: whether the argument is meant as a proof that the theory is inconsistent or as something weaker; whether the sadism and the infinity are essential to it or imported from outside; and whether her closing move — that anyone who accepts the monster has "abandoned morality" — is a substantive objection or merely a restatement of the intuition the thought experiment was supposed to generate. Finally, if there is a version of the objection that a careful utilitarian should still lose sleep over, say what it is and why it is not the version she gave me.
