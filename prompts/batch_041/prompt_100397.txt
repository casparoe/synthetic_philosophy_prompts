Ok so I'm a novelist, and for the book I'm working on — it's about a small-town paper that runs a big story and then has to live with the fallout — I've been shadowing the reporters, and there's this thing about how they decide what's printable that I can't stop thinking about. Their rule: they won't put a factual claim in the paper on one person's word. Need a second independent source. Ok fine. But then the same paper will print, off a single source, that the police say a man was arrested, that the company says earnings fell, that the senator denies the allegation — one source, printed, no problem. When I asked why, the editor kind of shrugged and said thats not a claim about the world, thats a claim about what they said. Which, sure. But they also treat some single things — a court filing, an official record, a named document — as good enough for the thing itself, not just for the fact that somebody said it.

So here's what I'm actually asking. This whole bundle of habits has to be resting on some view about when you're allowed to take something as true just because a person told you, right? I know there's some fight in philosophy between people who think you need independent reasons to trust a teller and people who think you're allowed to trust by default unless something gives you a reason not to. Which one is this newsroom secretly committed to? Both? Neither? And be honest with me about the parts where the practice doesn't really pick a side — where its just a practical hedge that either theory could justify. Because if it's just a hedge, then my reporter who trusts the wrong single source isn't a story about knowledge at all, shes just got bad judgement, and I'd like to know that before I write 300 pages. And if the practice does commit them to a view, tell me which bits of it would have to change if they switched to the other one — and whether treating an official document as a different kind of thing from a person is a real philosophical distinction or just them being snobs about paperwork.
