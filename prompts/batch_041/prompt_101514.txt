I chair the technical review panel of a national infrastructure safety authority, and I need to circulate a written opinion before our next meeting. Last autumn an unseasonal storm drove a coastal storm-surge barrier past its design load and a sequence of its moveable gates failed in cascade; the low-lying district behind it flooded. The operating authority has now filed a defense that turns on a scientific claim, and the panel has to decide whether to accept it.

The authority's consultants argue that the barrier's coupled mechanical, hydraulic, and control system is a deterministic chaotic system: it has a positive largest Lyapunov exponent, so the exact ordering and timing of component failures was sensitive to initial conditions and could not have been forecast past a short predictability horizon. They go further and invoke the shadowing result from dynamical systems theory — that a numerically computed pseudo-trajectory stays uniformly close to some true trajectory of the system with a slightly altered initial condition — to argue that their own simulation runs cannot show what was foreseeable in the actual case, because the true trajectory they shadow is not the one that occurred. Their conclusion: the specific failure was unforeseeable, and a duty-of-care finding should not rest on it.

The affected residents' representatives concede none of the microscopic detail but deny its relevance: they say a duty to foresee attaches to classes of outcomes at foreseeable loads, not to individual trajectories, and that the authority's designers knew or should have known the barrier had a non-trivial probability of cascade failure above design conditions.

Please draft the panel's written opinion: a formal memo, roughly 800 to 1000 words, addressed to the authority's board, recommending a concrete disposition (whether to accept the operator's defense, and what remedial action and time frame to require if any) with the reasoning the board can act on. It must engage the consultants' argument seriously rather than dismissing it, and it should give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the side the panel rejects. In particular, work through the distinction between a system's being deterministic and its being predictable; whether a positive Lyapunov exponent eliminates all predictability or only predictability at the level of individual trajectories, leaving statistical or attractor-level structure intact; whether the shadowing result actually supports the consultants' conclusion once its caveats are taken into account; the step from unpredictable to genuinely a matter of chance; and what foreseeability should mean for a regulatory standard of care — foreseeability of a class of outcomes versus of the single event that happened. Do not cite literature or supply a reading list; argue from the concepts themselves.
