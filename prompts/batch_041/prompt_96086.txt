Adopt the role of a philosopher of mind who also works on the formal side of consciousness science, and who has both defended and attacked the theory that a system's experience is identical with its maximally integrated cause–effect structure. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming the full space of possible defeaters, including ones I don't list, and only then sort them.

I have come to accept that position, together with the exclusion postulate that each system contains exactly one such structure — the local maximum — and that this structure simply is its experience. As I understand the view, it commits me to several things. A strictly feedforward system, however behaviourally rich, has no experience at all. The cerebellum contributes nothing to experience, though it holds more neurons than the cerebral cortex. A severed corpus callosum should yield two complexes, and so two subjects, whenever each hemisphere is internally more integrated than the pair is together. And experience has a grain fixed by the timescale at which integration peaks, rather than by any functional or reportable role.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed defender would concede rather than wave away. Work out in particular where the two cases I keep circling fail or succeed: a behavioural twin of a human implemented strictly feedforward, which the view calls a zombie, and a small recurrent circuit with high integration and almost no behaviour, which the view calls conscious. Which of those predictions is more exposed, and what would count as evidence against either? Take seriously the dilemma that any behaviour can be reproduced by an architecture with no integration at all — so that either the view is untestable by behavioural or functional evidence, or it is committed to behaviourally identical zombies. Say whether that dilemma can be escaped, and at what cost. Add the practical point that the central quantity cannot be computed for any system of realistic size, so none of these predictions can currently be checked.

Then tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — through later reformulations that weaken its postulates, or by recasting a testable correlation as an identity — and whether that insulation is a defect in a theory of this kind or simply the price of the claim it makes. If a rival account of the neural basis of consciousness handled the same evidence better, how much would that count against this one? Flag anywhere I have misstated the view; I would rather be corrected than flattered.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on how exposed this position really is — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. No citations; if a result matters, name the result rather than its literature.
