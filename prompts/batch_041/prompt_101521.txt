Please act as a tutor marking an undergraduate essay in philosophy of economics. The question and the student's answer are reproduced below. Award a mark out of 20 and give itemised comments justifying it, saying clearly which claims are sound and where the reasoning breaks down. Write your response in British English.

Question. Consider an agent whose strict preferences over three acts, A, B and C, form a cycle: A is preferred to B, B to C, and C to A. Whenever two of the acts are offered to her in isolation, she will pay a small fee to exchange the less preferred one for the more preferred one. (a) Explain how a sequence of such exchanges can leave her strictly worse off even though each exchange, taken on its own, is an improvement by her own lights. (b) Does the availability of such a sequence show that her preferences are irrational? In your answer, distinguish between synchronic and diachronic money pumps, and between mere exploitability and genuine inconsistency.

Student's answer. (a) Each exchange moves the agent from one act to a strictly better one, so after each step she is better off; but after three steps she is back holding the act she began with, having paid three fees. Her preferences have not changed, so she now faces the same act with less money, and is therefore worse off in every state of the world. The cycle cannot be represented by any real-valued utility function, since such a function would have to satisfy u(A) > u(B) > u(C) > u(A), which is impossible. Because the cycle is literally self-contradictory, any agent who has it is bound to be irrational, and the pump merely makes that visible.

(b) One should not overstate the argument, however. A fully informed agent who sees the whole sequence coming can simply decline the first exchange, so the pump works only against naive agents. The coherence argument is thus a psychological point about susceptibility to manipulation rather than a genuine constraint of rationality. What expected utility maximisation really requires is that preferences be complete and transitive, and the money pump is just a vivid illustration of what goes wrong when completeness fails.
