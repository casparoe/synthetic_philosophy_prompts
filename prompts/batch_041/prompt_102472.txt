I'm convening a small graduate reading group on free will and moral responsibility, and I want us to fix our standards before we start adjudicating between compatibilism and incompatibilism — otherwise the whole term degenerates into everyone defending the position they walked in with. So before we read any positions, write me the specification: what must a theory of free will actually deliver in order to count as adequate? I mean the full list of desiderata, each one attached to the case or consideration that motivates it — the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse, and the commitments or results it has to remain consistent with. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves and on why each is a requirement; I do not want a survey of who says what, and I do not need citations or a reading list.

For the cases, I have in mind at least these: the person who signs a contract at gunpoint against the willing collaborator who signs with enthusiasm; the addict who endorses her habit and the addict who fights it; the Frankfurt-style agent who could not have done otherwise but acted exactly as she wanted; the kleptomaniac; the agent whose values were deliberately sculpted by someone else and who now endorses them wholeheartedly; the person who simply does nothing while someone drowns, which makes me wonder whether a theory of free will owes an account of omissions and not just of actions; the person acting on a false belief; and the person whose conduct is the output of a committee procedure or a model. For the distinctions, I want the theory held to the difference between freedom of action and freedom of the will, between being a source of one's action and being able to do otherwise, between being free and being responsible and being blameworthy, and between compulsion, coercion, constraint, and mere determination. For the background commitments, it should be consistent with determinism being either true or false (or explain why it cannot be neutral), with our excusing of children and the deluded and the intoxicated, with the phenomenology of deliberation, with the thought that responsibility has a point within our practice of holding one another to account, and with the idea that freedom might come in degrees.

Then do the part I most want: tell me which requirements are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp, and flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied — if some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, that is itself the most interesting result on the page, and I want it stated plainly. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidate theories out. And I would like one further requirement treated as first-class rather than an afterthought: that the theory be able to say whether the freedom worth wanting can be attributed to collective agents such as a corporation or a committee, and to artificial decision-makers. Accounts that work beautifully for a solitary human being tend to fall silent here, and I want to know whether that silence marks a defect in the theory or the proper boundary of the subject matter. Finish with a sentence or two on where compatibilism and incompatibilism each look weakest against your list; I will run the full scorecard myself in the seminar.
