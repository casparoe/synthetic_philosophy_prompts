I teach a graduate seminar on consequentialism and aggregation, and every year the discussion of the dust-speck case stalls at the same place: someone insists that no number of trivial harms can outweigh a single atrocity, and no one in the room can say whether that is a considered judgment about aggregation or merely an artifact of the example's arithmetic. I want to test the intuition with a case whose structure runs in the opposite direction from the standard one, so that the anti-aggregationist rather than the totalist is the one who has to do something uncomfortable. Please construct a decision case in which a proponent of lexical priority — someone who holds that a harm above some severity threshold outweighs any quantity of harms below it — is forced to accept an aggregative trade that a totalist would find entirely unremarkable, with the pressure coming from the structure of the severe harm itself rather than from a stipulated enormity of numbers. The constraints I care about are these. No torture, and no other harm selected for its rhetorical weight; the severe item should be an ordinary misfortune of a sort a person might actually live through. The minor harms must be of the same kind as the components of the severe harm, so that the difference between the two options is one of number and distribution rather than of kind, and the anti-aggregationist cannot escape by appeal to a difference in category. Every quantity must be small enough that a reader can check the arithmetic on the back of an envelope — nothing that needs exotic notation, and no population-scale policy affecting billions, so that the numbers stay intuitive. The case should turn on a choice between two futures for the same individual or for a small group, and it must not be resolved by a prior commitment about personal identity: no counting episodes as persons, no splitting a life into time-slices, no appeal to when someone begins or ceases to exist. Finally, the case must not be settled by who inflicts the harm or by consent, since I want the aggregation to be the only thing doing the work.

After the scenario, which I would like in a single paragraph, walk through what totalism, lexical priority, and a threshold view each prescribe, and say precisely which feature of the case forces the lexicalist's hand, whether removing that feature — by making the two harms differ in kind rather than in degree — restores the standard verdict, and what the strongest line of resistance open to a defender of lexical priority would be, including whether that resistance is an argument about the case or a refusal to play. The purpose of the exercise is to pry apart the intuition that size matters from the intuition that kind matters, so a case that stipulates its own verdict, or that leans on the reader's squeamishness about a named atrocity, will not serve my purposes.
