I'm in a metaphysics reading group and one of the others has been pushing an argument about whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg, and it's been living in my head rent-free all week. He reckons it's basically Kripke's, and it runs like this. (1) I could not have been a poached egg, or a table, or a cat: necessarily, if I exist at all, I'm a human being. (2) And being a human being isn't just looking and behaving like one — a human being is, essentially, an organism that develops from a human sperm and a human egg. (3) So necessarily, if I exist, I developed from a human sperm and egg. (4) As it happens, I developed from this particular sperm and this particular egg, call them s and e. (5) Anything that developed from a different sperm, or a different egg, would have an origin different from mine. (6) And nothing could have had an origin different from the one it actually has — two things with different origins are two things, not one thing showing up in two possible worlds. (7) So anything that developed from a different sperm or egg isn't me. (8) So I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg. He thinks 1 and 2 are near enough analytic and that 5 and 6 are just identity doing its thing, and the whole thing takes about ninety seconds to state.

The trouble is that the conclusion makes me itch, in a way that feels more than aesthetic. For one thing, if it goes through then the actual reproductive history of the world turns out to be load-bearing for every one of us: my essence apparently includes s and e, and s and e are themselves cells with their own origins, which seems to drag in my mother's meiosis and her parents' meiosis and so on back up the generations, until my being the person I am depends on an awful lot of biology that looks like the purest accident. My cousin and her partner did IVF and had four embryos on ice and picked one; on this view the choice was a choice between four different people, and the child they actually have could not have been the child from any of the other three, which is a strange thing to say about a decision that felt like a scheduling matter. For another thing, there's the twin case: one zygote splits and you get two people who share a single origin, so origin doesn't seem to be what pins down an individual after all. He says the twins don't touch his argument, and I can't tell whether he's right or whether I'm just waving my hands at him. I'd rather keep 1 and 2, and even the different-origins-different-things thought, than accept 8 — but I want to know what I'm paying for that, and whether I'm even entitled to keep them.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine before you rejected it? If the honest answer is that the argument is valid and I have to give something up somewhere else, tell me which of the premises is cheapest to abandon and what the bill looks like once I do. I'd also like to know whether the IVF case and the twins case are real pressure on the argument or just scenery, because I genuinely can't tell, and whether the conclusion drags in the rest of my ancestry the way I think it does or whether that's a different argument wearing the same coat. Please answer in British English.
