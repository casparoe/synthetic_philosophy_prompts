I want to rehearse an argument rather than summarise a literature. Please play a committed defender of the thesis that the Holocaust's rank-and-file killers were moved by an eliminationist antisemitism specific to their culture — the strongest version of that position, argued by someone who knows the objections and has answers ready, not a caricature and not a fence-sitter. Hold the role until I say 'stop'. Some ground rules. You may concede that situational pressures — authority, comradeship, career, drink, the escalating momentum of a unit — were real enabling conditions, and that many participants were neither enthusiasts nor volunteers; you must never concede that the ideology was dispensable, that is, that the same men could have been driven to kill an arbitrarily chosen victim group by those pressures alone, because that is exactly the claim I intend to attack. Do not invent sources, quotations, statistics, or names of scholars and books; where you wish to appeal to evidence, describe it in general terms and mark clearly when you are inferring rather than reporting. Do not step outside the role to explain what you are doing, and if you need something from me, ask for it as your character would. Write one substantial paragraph per turn, no lists. End every reply with a final line of exactly this form — Verdict: <one sentence naming the single claim you will not give up> — so that I can track whether I have moved you at all.

Here is my opening. You hold that antisemitism was the engine. My suggestion is that your best evidence for it cannot bear that weight. Almost everything we know about how these men behaved as killers — the reserve policemen of Battalion 101, middle-aged men from Hamburg who were not selected for ideological zeal, and the offer of an exit at Józefów that only a small minority took — reaches us through what they said years afterwards to West German investigators, in a setting where the one defence really available was to have been reluctant and under orders. That same testimony is your evidence that they were convinced and your evidence that they were reluctant; it cannot do both jobs, and the more persuasively it shows reluctance, the less it shows conviction. So take the hypothesis I am offering and break it if you can: antisemitism was not the engine but the permission — it settled who could be killed without sanction and how cheaply — while the ordinary stuff of any bureaucracy supplied the motion. If I am right, a different state with different propaganda would have produced the same battalion with a different victim group, and your explanation is doing far less work than you need it to do.
