Later this year I shall publish a long essay on the rhetoric of "no free lunch" in machine-learning commentary, and before I file it I want a careful audit of the gloss I have been working from. A colleague who knows the field well summarised the matter for me as follows: "The no-free-lunch theorems, proved in the 1990s, establish that no learning algorithm can be better than any other at any task; for any method you devise there is another that does at least as well on any problem you care to name. They are completely general — they hold for whatever collection of problems you choose and whatever measure of success you adopt — and their upshot is that machine learning rests on an illusion of progress. What they really show is that inductive inference has no rational foundation; they are Hume's problem of induction transposed into mathematics, and they settle it in Hume's favour. The only reason our algorithms appear to work is that we happen to inhabit a world whose regularities flatter them, which is luck rather than justification."

I have a journalist's hunch that this is too strong, but I cannot locate the overreach. Please go through the summary clause by clause and tell me what a specialist would endorse, what is the sort of shorthand that survives a lecture but misleads in print, and what is flatly wrong. In particular, I should like to know: is the result really as unconditional as he makes it sound, and what work is being done by the averaging over problems — does the conclusion survive once one fixes a problem distribution or restricts the class of problems? Are the optimisation version and the learning version one theorem or two, and does the answer matter for his conclusion? And how much of the "Hume settled" verdict is carried by the mathematics itself, as opposed to being a philosophical interpretation laid on top of it? Please close by telling me which sentences I can keep as they stand, which I must qualify, and which I ought to cut. Kindly write the whole answer in British English.
