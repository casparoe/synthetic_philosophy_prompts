For as long as I have been reading epistemology, the contest among foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism has struck me less as three rival answers to one question than as three parties talking past one another, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Each position is simple to state and each carries an obvious cost. Foundationalism must explain how a basic belief can justify others without standing in need of justification itself. Coherentism must explain how a circle of mutually supporting beliefs can justify rather than merely cohere, and why circularity is a defect here when it is a defect almost everywhere else. Infinitism must explain how a chain no finite believer could ever traverse can be the very thing in which a believer's justification consists.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion, is that these are not three answers to one problem but answers to at least two questions concealed by the single label, the regress of justification. One question is structural: given a body of beliefs and a support relation among them, what must be true of the resulting structure for the beliefs to be justified at all? The other is dialectical: when a believer is pressed for reasons, what must the believer be able to do? Infinitism looks defensible under the first reading and close to absurd under the second, which is itself a clue that the two are being run together. A third question, about what makes a whole system of beliefs justified as opposed to a particular belief, may be what coherentism is really addressing. I also suspect the entire trilemma inherits an assumption it never defends: that justification is transmitted from belief to belief, one link at a time, along a linear chain, so that the only available structures are a chain that ends, a chain that loops, or a chain that never ends. If justification is holistic, or if the primary bearer of justification is not the individual belief, that taxonomy may be an artifact of the picture rather than a feature of the subject.

What I want from you, argued from what each side actually claims rather than from its label: first, a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, whether verbal, substantive, or several distinct questions filed under one heading, and if several, which question each side is really answering; second, an assessment of whether the linear and transitive picture is doing the load-bearing work, and whether abandoning it dissolves the trilemma or merely relocates it; third, an honest account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether a distinction, a change of question, or a demonstration that one horn is incoherent, or else a reasoned case that no progress should be expected here and why. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want the structure of the disagreement and where the weight actually rests.
