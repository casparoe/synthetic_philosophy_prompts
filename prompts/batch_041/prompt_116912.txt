I chair the investment committee of a public pension fund, and I need a short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. Our investment policy statement permits the committee to approve a position only where staff can demonstrate that it has a positive expected value, and separately requires us to avoid exposures whose loss the fund could not absorb. Both clauses appear to have been drafted by people who assumed that a position's expected value always exists. That assumption is now in question, and I would like your help in deciding what the committee can defensibly do.

A dealer has offered the fund the following swap. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first head appears on toss n, the fund's settlement is (−1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n dollars per unit of notional. Heads on the first toss therefore pays us $2 per unit; heads on the second costs us $2; heads on the third pays us $8/3; heads on the fourth costs us $4; heads on the fifth pays us $6.40; and so on, the amounts growing as the probabilities shrink.

Our quantitative team's memo values our side at about +$0.69 per unit, obtained by adding the probability-weighted settlements in the order in which the tosses occur, and notes that the fund comes out ahead in two plays out of three. The dealer's term sheet quotes our side at +$0.40 per unit, and the dealer's own pricing memo — a copy of which our staff have seen — shows the dealer's side of this zero-sum trade as worth +$0.40 per unit to the dealer. Our risk officer has filed a dissent. Her argument is that the expected absolute settlement is infinite, that the terms being added are therefore not absolutely convergent, and that the order of summation is consequently not a matter of bookkeeping: she has produced a schedule, a reordering of the very same probability-weighted amounts, that totals about −$4 per unit, and she observes that by choosing the order she can make the total any real number she likes, or send it to positive or negative infinity. Her conclusion is that our rule points to a quantity that does not exist, and that the committee cannot satisfy a rule with no content.

Please draft the opinion. It should end in a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve on stated conditions — with a concrete course of action and a date by which it should be carried out, and it should engage the strongest version of the case on the losing side rather than disposing of it in a sentence. I am particularly interested in how the committee should read its own mandate when the quantity that mandate names is not well defined, and in whether the mandate itself should be repaired. The committee has read the literature; what it lacks is a decision it can defend to the beneficiaries whose money this is.
