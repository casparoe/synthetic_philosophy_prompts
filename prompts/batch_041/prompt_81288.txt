I am at work on a novel about the way a person goes on existing inside the people who knew her, and I have come to the one scene I cannot draft alone. Two of my characters, Ines and Rafe, are philosophers by training and the two oldest friends of a woman who is not in the room. They are arguing about her, and I would like you to write that exchange. I need it to be a real argument — each of them answering the other's actual point rather than delivering a set piece — and I need it to be about her, not about the general problem.

The case, as I have built it. Mira is sixty-eight. Four years ago a slow illness began rearranging her, and the woman it has left is, by every account including her own, not the woman who was there before: not diminished, not cruel, but differently assembled — her loyalties, her sense of what is funny and what is unforgivable, her tastes, all shifted. In her lucid intervals she is articulate about it. She says the woman her friends keep describing is a stranger she is tired of being compared to, and she has asked them to stop describing her.

Ines and Rafe hold between them a portrait of the earlier Mira that everyone who knew her agrees is faithful: forty years of memory, cross-checked over decades, kept in good repair. Ines has said, only half joking, that the earlier Mira is still somewhere — not as a soul, not in any afterlife, but distributed; that the pattern of her is still running, faithfully, in two heads, and that a pattern still running is not dead.

The positions I need them to hold, stated as plainly as I can manage them.

The distributed view. A self is a pattern — a self-referential structure, of the kind sometimes called a strange loop, that can be instantiated in more than one place. Ines takes this literally. The Mira in her head is not a memory of Mira but a continuation of her, one of several now overlapping. If that is so, the earlier Mira has a present tense: she can be wronged by a false account of her, she can be contradicted, she can be disappointed by what the living Mira has become. She cannot act, but she can have interests, and she can be injured.

The single-locus view. A self is a subject of experience; there must be somewhere that the experiences are happening. The Mira in Ines's head is a representation — a good one, forty years in the making, but a representation. It feels nothing, wants nothing, cannot be wronged. When someone slanders the earlier Mira, the injury lands on the living: on Mira, on the friends, on everyone who loved her. And the conflict between the earlier Mira and the living one is a conflict between a woman and her friends' expectations — ordinary grief wearing a metaphysical costume.

What I need from the dialogue. Neither character may be a fool or a mouthpiece; each must land at least one point the other cannot wave away, and each must give ground somewhere, because if one of them walks out of the scene untouched I have written a pamphlet. They must argue about this case — the lucid intervals, the request to stop, the cross-checked portrait — rather than about the general problem of personal identity. Somewhere in the exchange, one of them should be forced to accept a consequence that visibly costs them something. No names of real philosophers, no titles, no citations, no appeals to what anyone has written; nothing may be settled by authority, and the philosophy has to live inside the speech. Roughly a thousand words. End unresolved but not evasive, on a question that is genuinely open rather than on a truce.
