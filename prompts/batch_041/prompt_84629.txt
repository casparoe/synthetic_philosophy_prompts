Take the following as the target claim and give me your credence that it is true: that there is no fact of the matter, even in principle, about the content of a subject's experience at a moment — no boundary in the processing stream whose crossing constitutes a content's becoming conscious, so that "what was she conscious of just then?" can be ill-posed rather than merely unanswered.

Give me a number, then decompose it. I want at least: the probability that the experimental cases usually cited for this claim (color phi, metacontrast, stable disagreements between verbal report and motor response) are genuinely cases of an ill-posed question rather than a determinate boundary that is simply hard to locate; the probability that the claim is a coherent, testable thesis rather than a redescription of ordinary computational processing; the probability that a rival account — global workspace, recurrent processing, higher-order thought — preserves everything the claim gets right while restoring a determinate fact of the matter; and the probability that the dispute is verbal, dissolving once "conscious of" is regimented, say as "would be reported under optimal conditions." Then give me the combination, tell me which term the number hangs on, and name the single result that would move it most.

For what it's worth, I sit near 0.4; if you land far from that, tell me which of your terms most likely differs from mine. And one extra case: if a subject's verbal report and her reaching movement stably disagreed about a briefly shown display, should that raise your credence in the target claim, lower it, or leave it untouched?
