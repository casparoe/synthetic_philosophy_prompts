I am at work on a novel whose central figure is a philosopher of mind — a woman in her sixties who has spent her career on the question of whether a machine can mean anything by what it does — and in one chapter she must explain to a seminar of non-philosophers where Dennett and Searle actually disagree about intentionality. I am not a philosopher myself, and I have no wish to put an indefensible speech into her mouth, so I have been working from a trade book about artificial intelligence and consciousness that sets out the dispute as follows:

The quarrel between Dennett and Searle is usually described as a quarrel about whether machines can think, but it is really about where intentionality comes from. Searle's position is that our beliefs and desires possess original intentionality: they are produced by the brain, they are about what they are about intrinsically, and they would be about it whether or not any observer interpreted us that way. The aboutness of words, pictures, and computers, by contrast, is derived — borrowed from the minds that put the words and the machines to use — and so a computer can never mean anything by itself. Dennett denies that there is any such thing as original intentionality. On his account, intentionality is simply what appears when an interpreter adopts the intentional stance, predicting a system's behaviour by attributing to it the beliefs and desires that a rational agent in its situation ought to have; and the stance can be taken toward anything at all, from a person to a thermostat to the two-bitser, the coin machine that accepts a slug as a quarter. Dennett's answer to the question of whether the two-bitser really believes the slug is a quarter is therefore that it does: the belief is thin and purely instrumental, a convenience for the predictor rather than a possession of the machine, but a belief all the same, and no different in kind from your belief that it is raining, only far simpler. The Chinese room, on this reading, is the behaviourist's mistake turned inside out — Searle hunts for the understanding in the wrong place, when for Dennett there is nothing inside anyone to find, and to ask whether the room or its occupant understands is like asking which part of a whirlwind is the whirlwind. What the whole exchange shows is that "intentionality," as the word is used in this literature, is really just a name for having goals and responding to one's surroundings — which is why Dennett can cheerfully grant it to machines and Searle cannot grant it to anything without a brain.

The passage read as fair to me on first encounter, though I notice that I am temperamentally on Dennett's side, which may be precisely why it did. I should like you to audit it. Go through it clause by clause and tell me what a specialist would accept as stated, what is the sort of simplification a specialist would tolerate in a popular book, and what Dennett or Searle would flatly deny having said. Then, since the audit is ultimately for a work of fiction rather than for a seminar, separate the sentences my character could voice with confidence from those she would have to hedge, qualify, or put in the mouth of an interlocutor she disagrees with; I would rather she sound careful than clever. The paragraph closes by drawing a general moral about what the word "intentionality" is doing in this debate, and that is the claim I am most anxious about: I would like to know whether it follows from anything true in the summary, or whether the summary has quietly redefined the term somewhere along the way. I do not need a reading list or a set of citations — only an account of what I can safely let her say.
