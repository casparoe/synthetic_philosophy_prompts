I'm a third-year philosophy student writing a term paper on unity and composition, and I've hit the point where I need to know whether my own verdicts about cases add up to anything. Our seminar spent two weeks on the regress that seems to threaten any account of what makes a complex thing one thing rather than many: whatever you nominate as the binding — a relation, a structure, a form — looks like one more item sitting alongside the parts, so you then need something to bind it to them, and off you go; and if you say instead that the binding is somehow internal to the parts, it's hard to see what work it is doing that the parts weren't already doing. I follow the argument. What I can't see is where it actually bites, so I wrote down my verdicts on a run of cases and I'm now stuck with a pattern I can't state.

Here they are. A pile of sand on the beach is not one thing, just many grains arranged. The set whose members are my left shoe and the number seven is not one thing; a set is its members considered together and nothing binds them. A water molecule is one thing: the atoms really are held together, and nobody thinks you need a third item, a bond to the bond, before the molecule hangs together. A melody is one thing; the notes are not merely adjacent in time, since each sounds different given what came before and after it. The English sentence "the door is shut" is one thing, even though what makes it a sentence rather than a list of words is only that the words are arranged in a way our grammar permits, and nothing in the words themselves demands that arrangement. A crowd of people who happen to be in the same square at the same time is not one thing. A crowd of people at the same protest, all there for the same reason, is also not one thing — it took me a while to commit to that, since the shared purpose is real, but it is a fact about each of them and not something that makes them a single thing. A liver is one thing; its parts are doing something together, filtering blood, and that activity is what the parts are for. A proposition — say, that the door is shut — is one thing, and here I want to claim something stronger than I can defend, namely that unlike the sentence it is unified by what it is about rather than by any convention, so that if you changed every convention it would still be the proposition it is and the sentence would not.

The tentative principle I've been working with is this: a complex is one thing when the relations among its parts are load-bearing, and merely many when the relations are bookkeeping. Load-bearing means the parts are what they are only in the whole — pull the bond out and you don't have a hydrogen atom plus a stray bond, you have something that isn't a hydrogen-in-a-molecule; pull the note out of its context and it isn't that note. Bookkeeping means the relation is imposed from outside, by us or by circumstance: co-membership in a set, co-location in a square, being strung together by a rule we happen to have.

I can see at least three places this strains. The sentence looks bookkeeping, since grammar is ours, yet I want it unified. The protest looks load-bearing, since the shared purpose shapes what each person is doing, yet I don't want it unified. And the proposition is the case I care most about getting right, and I suspect my principle says nothing about it, since "aboutness" is neither of my two categories. I also can't tell whether the principle is really just the regress-avoiding move in disguise: if a relation is load-bearing, is that supposed to mean it is not a further item needing to be related, and if so, is that a genuine answer or a relabeling?

So what I want is the principle, or a small set of them, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely, with a note on which case motivates each clause. Then tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why, what it implies about the proposition case, and what it says about the case I am genuinely torn on and did not list above — whether a thing can be unified by something that is itself one of its parts, as when an organism is held together by a central organizing part rather than by a relation between its parts. If the honest answer is that no single principle saves everything and I have to give up a verdict, say which one, and say whether the simpler principle that costs me a verdict is better than a complicated one that saves them all.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. I would much rather have one principle stated carefully and tested against the cases than a survey of options.
