I write a column on science and ideas, and last week I sat in on a graduate seminar in decision theory where two panelists got into a dispute about the St. Petersburg game that I have not been able to sort out. The game, as they set it up, is the familiar one: a fair coin is tossed until the first head; if the head first appears on toss n, the payout is 2^n dollars, so the expected dollar payoff is infinite. The first panelist, a decision theorist, said the paradox is essentially solved by the observation that money has diminishing marginal utility: the value of a payout does not grow linearly with its dollar amount, and with a logarithmic utility function the expected utility of the game is finite, so a rational agent will pay only a modest fee. The second panelist, a philosopher of economics, replied that this is not a solution but a restriction: it works only because we have assumed a particular utility function, and one can construct a variant—call it the escalated game—in which the nth-toss payout is 2^{2^n} dollars, so that even logarithmic utility has infinite expectation. The first panelist answered that such a game is physically impossible, since no casino could pay 2^{2^n} dollars beyond very small n; once the payout is capped by the resources of the universe, the expectation is finite and the paradox vanishes. The second replied that this changes the subject: the St. Petersburg game is a thought experiment about ideal rational choice, not a prediction about actual casinos, and invoking physical limits is to concede that the paradox is about practical feasibility rather than about the logic of expected value. The first then said that no rational agent has unbounded utility anyway; a preference ordering that requires infinite expected utility is not a coherent standard for choice. The second answered that unbounded utility is not incoherent per se; it is just a utility function without a finite upper bound, and the real lesson is that expected utility theory needs a restriction on the class of admissible gambles or a distinction between kinds of value that cannot be traded off—not that the diminishing-marginal-utility reply has solved the original puzzle. The first closed by saying that for any real agent with finite lifespan and finite resources the expected utility of the game is finite and small, so the paradox is a curiosity about infinitary mathematics, not a guide to life; the second said that the game is a consistency test, and that if you say it is worth only a few dollars you owe a theory that explains why you would not pay more for a slightly different game.

I am reporting this as a bystander, and I do not have a stake in which of them comes out ahead. What I want is an adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded, not a general essay on the St. Petersburg paradox. Please go through the moves in order—the diminishing-marginal-utility resolution, the escalated game, the physical-limits reply, the unbounded-utility reply, the finite-agent reply, and the consistency-test reply—and for each one say whether it is correct, mistaken, or correct only with a qualification, giving the reason. Then tell me what, if anything, both panelists are missing: in particular, does the escalated game genuinely refute the diminishing-marginal-utility resolution, or does it only show that the resolution depends on empirical assumptions about actual money? Does the physical-limits reply change the subject, or is it a legitimate idealization? Is unbounded utility incoherent, merely a utility function without a finite upper bound, or something else? And what is the best resolution of the underlying dispute about what the St. Petersburg game shows?
