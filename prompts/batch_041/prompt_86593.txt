A national conservation authority runs three instruments that all turn on the word function, and I want to know which theory of biological function the practice commits it to.

First, a register of functionally extinct species: a species is listed once its population has fallen so low that it no longer performs its ecological role, even though some individuals survive. Second, a restoration programme: where a species is lost entirely, the authority may introduce a closely related living species to stand in for it, on the ground that the substitute occupies the same niche and drives the same processes; a site is certified as restored once those processes are measurably running again. Third, an invasive-species schedule: harm is defined as disruption of ecosystem function, with no reference to where the organism came from or how it arrived.

Reconstruct the account of function this practice presupposes. The register looks etiological, since a listed species still possesses the traits it was selected for and merely lacks the role. The restoration programme looks role-based, since it treats function as whatever keeps the process running, whatever the lineage. Are the three instruments consistent, or does the practice rely on two incompatible accounts? If a single account can cover all three, what must it say about a proxy that performs the role more thoroughly than the extinct species ever did? If no single account can, say which instruments have to be rewritten, and how.

Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where it is underdetermined, so that a rival account would license exactly the same behaviour. Finish with the concrete consequences: what the authority would have to stop doing, or start doing, if it adopted the account you reject. Answer in roughly 1,000 words.
