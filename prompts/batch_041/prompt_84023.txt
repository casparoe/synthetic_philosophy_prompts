Hilary Putnam did not live to see the current generation of large language models. Suppose, counterfactually, that he could study them now—systems trained on vast corpora that produce fluent text, answer questions, translate, and appear to refer to objects and properties. Using only the commitments that structure his Twin Earth thought experiment and his semantic externalism, reconstruct the position he would most likely take on the following four questions. First, do the outputs of such a system possess meaning in the way human utterances do, or are they at best causally connected to meanings without expressing them? Second, does semantic externalism imply that an LLM's representations are wide—partly constituted by the environment and the linguistic community that produced its training data—or does the absence of an appropriate causal-historical chain, and of a division of linguistic labor in which the system participates, make the externalist framework inapplicable? Third, if an LLM reliably produces true sentences about water both on Earth and on Twin Earth, would Putnam credit it with the concept water, or only with something like a stereotype that tracks the local extension without grasping it? Fourth, what would he make of the charge that LLMs are merely stochastic parrots—would he see that charge as a relapse into the internalism he rejected, or as a legitimate extension of his own externalism to a new kind of case? I want a committed prediction, not a survey of possible readings: take a definite line on each question and defend it from Putnam's own commitments. Organize the response in exactly these sections: The Putnam I am predicting (roughly 250–350 words), setting out the two or three externalist commitments that do the most work; What is new in the LLM case (roughly 250–350 words), distinguishing what genuinely extends his original examples from what does not; Verdicts (roughly 600–800 words), giving a definite answer to each of the four questions with reasons; and Where this prediction is most fragile (roughly 150–250 words), identifying the assumptions that, if false, would change the verdicts.
