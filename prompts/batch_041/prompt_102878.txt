My reading group's been working through Jain epistemology and a friend of mine has built an arguemnt that I can't stop chewing on, because every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is a car crash.

He wrote it out like this:

(1) Anekāntavāda says reality is many-sided, so no single judgement can capture it. Every claim is true only from one angle or aspect, which the Jains flag with syāt — roughly, 'in some respect'.
(2) So for any claim P, P is true in some respect, and not-P is true in some respect too.
(3) That has to go for every claim, including the claim that anekāntavāda is right.
(4) So 'anekāntavāda is right' is true from one standpoint and false from another.
(5) If a claim is false from some legitimate standpoint, you can't assert it as just plain true.
(6) So anekāntavāda can't be asserted as just plain true — by its own lights.
(7) And worse: the one-sided dogmatist who says only his view is true is, from his own standpoint, correct, and by (1)–(2) his standpoint is as good as anyone's.
(8) So there's no standpoint-free reason to prefer anekāntavāda to any rival at all — including a rival that cheerfully contradicts itself.
(9) So taken seriously, anekāntavāda proves too much: it erases the difference between itself and its opponents, and between sense and nonsense.

I'd quite like to keep (1), the many-sidedness, because it feels right to me, but (9) is obviously a disaster, so something above it has gone wrong. I keep half-remembering bits of the machinery — naya for a standpoint, pramāṇa for (a full valid cognition?), a seven-way scheme of predication (saptabhaṅgī?) that's meant to let you say a thing both is and isn't without landing in contradiction, and something about an omniscient knower (kevalajñāna?) who supposedly sees all the aspects at once — but I've never worked out how the pieces fit, so I can't tell whether my friend is quietly abusing them or whether they really do deliver (9).

Tell me which step to reject. And price it for me: if I drop the step that rescues anekāntavāda from (9), what do I lose about the many-sidedness, and is there anything recognisably like (1) left standing afterwards?
