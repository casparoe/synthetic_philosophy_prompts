My colleague is a hard incompatibilist who keeps insisting that unless an agent could literally have done otherwise, you can't hold them responsible for what they did — and I've come around to thinking that's wrong, for roughly the reason the so-called Frankfurt cases suggest: an agent can be responsible for what they do even in a situation where, thanks to some outside standby mechanism, they could not have done otherwise. I want to practice defending that view against someone who knows the literature far better than I do and who has a much sharper arsenal than my colleague. Please play a committed defender of that position — a philosopher who holds that moral responsibility does not require the principle of alternative possibilities — and stay in character until I say "stop." Give this defender real resources rather than a strawman: it can distinguish the mere availability of alternatives from the actual causal history of an action; it can concede that particular versions of the cases are crude or question-begging; it can grant that the standby mechanism in any given case is stipulated rather than argued for. What it must never concede is the main point, that the absence of alternatives by itself exonerates the agent. Talk like a careful philosopher, one or two paragraphs per turn, no bullet lists, and don't break character to lecture me about the debate from the outside unless I explicitly ask you to. Reply in American English.

Here is my opening move. I want to try a version of the case that I think is nastier than the usual ones, and see whether your defender can handle it without smuggling in determinism. Dana is a mid-level manager deciding whether to sign off on a fraudulent audit that will make her rich. Unknown to her, a warden has installed a system that monitors the neural precursor of her decision; if she were about to refrain from signing, the warden would flood her with an irresistible compulsion to sign. As it happens, Dana signs on her own, out of ordinary greed and a settled willingness to cheat, and the warden's system never fires. Now here is the twist I want you to take seriously: the warden calibrated the system to fire only in those runs where Dana's decision was not already settled by her standing character and reasons at the moment of choice — so by stipulation, in the actual run her signing was a settled, foreclosed event with no openness left in it. My claim is that this case shows nothing, because it cheats. Either her signing really was already settled before the moment of choice, in which case you have simply assumed the conclusion and begged the question against my incompatibilist; or it was not settled, in which case the warden's system would have had to fire and Dana would not be signing on her own after all. Either way, the intuition you want — that she is responsible even though she could not have done otherwise — never actually gets off the ground. How does your defender get out of that without assuming what he is trying to prove?
