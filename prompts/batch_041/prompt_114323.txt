I have come to think that self-deception, understood literally, is impossible: to deceive someone you must know the truth, and to be deceived you must not know it, so the self-deceiver would have to know and not know the same thing at the same time, which is not a hard feat but an incoherence. If that is right, then everything we call self-deception must be something else wearing the name, and the name is a leftover from a picture of the mind as a single room with one shelf for beliefs.

Take the position seriously and tell me what counts against it, ordered from what ought to genuinely shake me to what is merely awkward. I am most interested in the cases I cannot classify. A man arranges his friendships, his reading, and his questions so that the disconfirming fact never gets a chance to arrive. A woman's behavior quietly tracks a truth her words deny. Two people keep a shared story alive because each is willing to believe it so long as the other keeps saying it. Are these self-deception, or are they avoidance, hypocrisy, and mutual flattery? If the latter, I want to know whether I am protecting my thesis by quietly redefining the target, and I want to hear what a serious defender of my view would themselves concede, not only what its critics say.

Then the harder question. Is there anything that could count against my claim, or have I stated it so tightly that nothing could? If nothing could, is that a sign the view is empty, or a sign that it was never an empirical claim to begin with, and does the answer change whether I should hold it?
