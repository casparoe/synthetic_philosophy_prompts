Write a satirical piece in the form of an official press release from a fictional national statistics agency — invent a plausible country and agency name; do not use a real one — announcing that the country is abolishing every self-report wellbeing survey (life-satisfaction questions, mood diaries, the lot) and replacing them with a single Revealed Preference Index, compiled from administrative records of what citizens actually choose: what they buy, where they enrol, which jobs they quit, which contracts they renew. Target roughly 650 words. Hold the deadpan register of a government press office throughout: a headline and dateline, a lead paragraph, two or three quoted paragraphs from the agency's Director, a short section headed "Methodology in brief", one worked example built around a named (invented) citizen, and a closing "Notes for editors" paragraph. Do not let the narrator wink: the piece must never state that anything is a problem, and must never explain its own jokes.

The comedy has to come from features that revealed-preference welfare economics genuinely has, exaggerated rather than invented, so that someone who has taught the subject recognises the moves. At minimum, work in the following, each of which the agency should present as a straightforward procedural virtue:

- The Index has to get from choices to preferences, and finite choice data is consistent with indefinitely many preference orderings; so the methodology section must quietly stipulate that citizens are complete and transitive in their preferences, presented as routine housekeeping rather than as a substantive assumption.
- Because preferences are ordinal, the Index can rank a single citizen's own options but cannot say by how much. Have the agency concede this and then recover magnitudes by valuing each citizen's chosen bundle at national average prices, so that the resulting numbers are in effect weighted by what each citizen can afford. The Director should be pleased about this for reasons that are clearly not the ones stated.
- The Index therefore compares citizens with one another, which requires a further assumption the agency never mentions; let the "Notes for editors" assert that such comparisons are meaningful in a tone suggesting the matter is beneath discussion.
- Citizens who choose little or nothing — those with no options to choose between — register as having little or no wellbeing. The agency should treat this as a data-quality issue with a technical fix.
- Anyone who persists in choosing a constrained or unenviable life scores well, because their choices are stable and mutually consistent; make the worked example a long-term prisoner, a live-in carer, or a monk, and have the agency describe the result as robustness to disappointment.
- The Index cannot be wrong about what people want, since wanting is defined as whatever the choices reveal. The Director should say something to this effect as though it were a guarantee of accuracy, thereby abolishing regret, weakness of will, and the possibility of being mistaken about one's own good.
- Citizens whose choices violate the weak axiom of revealed preference — who pick A when B was affordable, and later pick B when A was affordable — are handled by a procedure the agency calls a consistency audit. Make the outcome disturbing without ever letting the agency call it disturbing.
- The Index is to be used for policy appraisal, which makes cost-benefit analysis circular: a project is worth building if it induces choices, and induced choices are what reveal the preferences that justify it. The Director should announce this as an efficiency gain.

Invent all names, figures, and quotations. Do not cite, name, or paraphrase any real economist, agency, or published work, and do not use real statistics. The piece should be legible and funny to a reader who knows the literature and merely strange-but-followable to one who does not; a specialist should finish it thinking that the author understands the theory and that the satire is fair.
