I spent thirty-four years as a soil chemist — phosphorus, mostly — and toward the end of it I noticed that my whole lab's existence traced back to a process invented by a man who also ran a poison gas program, which is probably why this has been nagging at me since I retired. These days I audit history of science seminars (no exams, which is the apeal) and I keep getting told my instincts are "presentist" — judging people in the past by the standards of my own time — and then, sometimes from the same room, that nobody can escape the standards of their own time anyway, so the whole exercise is a trap. Maybe. But here are my gut calls, and I want to see what rule, if any, produces them. (a) The Vienna physicians who laughed Semmelweis out of the room and helped him into an asylum: I blame them, and not in the soft sense of just saying I'd have done better. The evidence was lying in their own wards, and looking at your own numbers was not a twentieth-century invention. I've heard the revisionists say his statistics were a mess and he was vicious to critics — doesn't move me much either way. (b) Haber: I won't pretend the ammonia process didn't happen or that it hasn't fed a large fraction of the people now alive, and I also won't have a building named after him. Both at once, no tug-of-war. (c) The soil surveyors who spent forty years doing exactly what the colonial agriculture office told them to do: I can't blame them. That was the job they were handed, and they had families. (d) The ones who said nothing — the senior men in the thirties who kept their heads down, kept their chairs and their pensions, never signed anything, never denounced anyone: I blame them more than I blame the (c) crowd, and I notice that this is backwards, since they did less. (e) My old department votes this spring on whether to take the founding chair's name off a prize; he published a pamphlet in 1912 about sterilizing defectives. I voted to keep the prize and add a plaque saying who he was and what he wrote. A friend tells me that's incoherent — either he's honored or he isn't.

So: is there one principle that gets (a) through (e), or do I need two, and what exactly does each clause buy me? Tell me which of my calls I have to give up, apply the thing to a case I haven't listed, and then answer the one I actually lose sleep over: if our standards are just the standards of our own time, is there any sense in which those Vienna doctors were wrong, or only a sense in which we wouldn't do it? Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up.
