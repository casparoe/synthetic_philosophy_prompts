I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm working up a piece on how bad we are at keeping lying and misleading seperate — how a lot of the modern world runs on the gap between what a sentence literally says and what it makes you believe. My peg is that almost nobody enforces the moral line they claim to care about: a sale that shouts up to 60% off when nothing in the store is 60% off, a chatbot that confidently invents a citation it thinks is real, a negotiator who answers your question truthfully in a way designed to walk you down the wrong road (the negotiation researchers have a name for this now, paltering, and I'm using that as my hook), a subscription with a cancel button that technically exists and practically doesn't. Every one of those is, in the narrow sense, true. I was arguing about this over dinner with a couple of Kant people and a tech-policy friend, and we couldn't agree on whether the lying/misleading line is a real moral distinction or just a legal artifact — so I want a foil for the column, and an absolutist is the obvious one. Kant is who everyone reaches for, the guy who supposedly thinks lying is never permitted, and I already know the cartoon version of him (the murderer at the door, the rigid duty of truthfulness, the whole bit). What I don't have, and what I want from you, is an actual reconstruction instead of the cartoon.

So: assume Kant has spent a year reading the business pages and the tech coverage, and go case by case through the practices I listed and tell me what he'd really say. Be careful here — my hunch is that his framework doesn't cleanly recognize the lying/misleading distinction the way we do, and if that's right I'd rather you say so than jam him into our vocabulary. For each case I want to know whether he'd call it a lie or merely a deception that falls short of lying; what in his system does the actual work (the intent to deceive, the duty of truthfulness, the universalizability test, whatever it turns out to be); how confident you are; and where the reconstruction strains or where you're basically guessing. Then give me the single hardest case for him — I suspect it's the hallucinating model, since it has no intent to deceive anyone — plus a short verdict on whether the lying/misleading line is doing any real moral work at all. Format it as JSON, an object with these fields: "meta" (containing "does_kants_framework_recognize_the_distinction", a boolean, and "summary"); "cases", an array where each entry has "case_name", "said_versus_conveyed", "verdict" (one of: lie, not_a_lie_but_still_forbidden, not_a_lie_and_permissible, framework_silent), "deciding_principle", "confidence" (one of high, medium, low), and "where_it_strains"; then "hardest_case"; then "bottom_line". A few sentences per field, no hedging filler.
