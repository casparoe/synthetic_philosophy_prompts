Okay, so my reading group is doing the ethics of killing and a friend of mine (animal-ethics guy, very sharp) dropped this on us last week and I can't shake it. I think it proves way more than he thinks it does, and he thinks the extra bit is just squeamishness.

His argument:

(1) When killing a being is wrong, it's wrong because of what it takes from that being — the good life it would otherwise have gone on to have. So the wrongness of killing is proportional to the amount of welfare the victim would have had ahead of them.
(2) That amount = (time left) × (richness of experience per unit time). A being with a thin mental life loses less per year than a being with a rich one.
(3) How rich a being's mental life can be is set by how much information-processing machinery it's got. Across the animal kingdom the rough proxy for that is brain size, corrected for body size so we're not just measuring how big the animal is.
(4) So the wrongness of killing a being is proportional to its body-corrected brain size. He likes this because it lets you put a number on things — you can even state an exchange rate, so many chicken-years to one human-year.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) says anything about species. They're stated for individual beings, one at a time. So (4) has to hold being by being: if two individuals differ in neural machinery, then other things equal, killing the one with more is worse.
(6) Brains vary alot between individuals of the same species, including ours. Some of that variation is body size, but not all of it; brains also shrink with age and with illness.
(7) So — other things equal — killing a bigger-brained person is worse than killing a smaller-brained one, and a person whose brain has badly atrophied drops toward the bottom of the human range, which puts them below a chimp.
(8) That's nuts. Nobody thinks the person with the smaller brain counts for less.
(9) So something in (1)–(3) has to go — (6) is just biology and (5) is just reading (1)–(3) at face value.

His actual point in the group was the nice half: he thinks it explains why killing a chimp is worse than killing a chicken, and he was using it to argue that brain size is morally relevant. And sure, it seems to deliver that. It just also delivers the ugly half.

I don't want to give up (1) or the chimp/chicken verdict — (1) is the only decent story I have about why death is bad at all, and if I lose the cross-species ranking then brain size stops mattering and his whole paper collapses, which I don't think is right either. My money's on (3) being where the trick is, like "brain size" is quietly doing two different jobs, one across species and one within one. But he says (3) is the only premise doing any work, and if you drop it you drop the chimp/chicken verdict too, which was the whole point of the thing.

Also, and I don't think hes noticed this: the measures don't even agree with each other. Total neuron count puts elephants miles above us. Plain brain-to-body ratio makes a shrew look like a genius. The body-size-corrected quotient is the one that crowns us. And bird brains pack two to four times the neurons per gram that mammal brains do, so a crow's walnut isn't the walnut you'd think it is.
