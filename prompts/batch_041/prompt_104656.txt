I run a monthly reading group for non-specialists — a few graduate students, two retirees, a nurse, a software engineer — and next month the topic falls to me: Samuel Scheffler's claim, in Death and the Afterlife, that much of what we value we value only against the background assumption that human life will continue long after we are gone, and what that is supposed to show about why extinction would be bad beyond the deaths it involves. I do not want you to explain the idea to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it in a ninety-minute session, because I have now twice watched a room leave convinced they had heard an argument that the meaning of life depends on being remembered, which is not the argument.

Give me the plan, and commit to it — one plan, with your reasons, not a menu of alternatives I have to adjudicate.

First, the order. What has to be in place before the claim can even be stated without being misheard, which piece depends on which, and what each step is doing for the next.

Second, the case that carries the whole session. I have been tempted to use a religious order that has stopped admitting novices and knows it will be the last of its line; tell me whether that carries the idea or betrays it, what a carrying case must have and what it must lack, and, if mine fails, what to put in its place and why.

Third, the misconceptions. I already expect "so nothing matters if no one remembers us," and I suspect the group will also slide from a claim about the conditions on value to a claim about how people would feel, and from there to a demand that everyone have children. Name the ones I have not named, and mark exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off.

Fourth, the check. Give me the one distinction the session must press hardest, and a single closing question that tells me whether the group has grasped the structure of the claim rather than memorized it — something that would expose a group that can recite the conjecture but still thinks it is about being remembered.

Fifth, what to leave out at this level, and say so openly.

One more thing I want built in. I would like the session to end by testing the claim against a stubborn case: someone who is told that humanity will simply stop reproducing, and finds their attachments to their projects entirely undiminished. Does that refute the claim or only mark the person as unusual? Leave room for that, and tell me how to keep it from degenerating into a poll of how the group happens to feel.

Keep the session free of reading lists and citations; this group will not chase them down, and I do not want the plan leaning on them.
