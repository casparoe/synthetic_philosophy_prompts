I'm a third-year doctoral student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I've been locked in a running argument with a colleague who works on nominalist reconstructions of arithmetic and analysis. We keep talking past each other, and I've come to think the real disagreement is that neither of us has a clear picture of what an account of mathematical ontology is even supposed to deliver. Before I draft another chapter, I want the specification set out. Please give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of mathematical ontology — and of the truth of mathematical statements — must meet: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the findings from mathematical practice and from empirical science it must remain consistent with, together with the consideration that motivates each requirement. I am less interested in a tidy list than in why each item earns its place, and in which of the things people routinely demand are genuine desiderata rather than commitments of one camp smuggled in as neutral constraints.

Drive the list from cases rather than slogans, and lean on the awkward ones: the apparently indispensable use of mathematics in empirical science; the worry about how we could ever enjoy cognitive access to objects that are causally inert; the status of a theorem proved only by a computer search so vast that no human being will ever survey the proof, yet which scientists go on to apply; the gap between a claim's being true and its being provable, statable, or checkable; and the question whether a theory of ontology owes us any account of mathematical discovery at all, or only of mathematical truth. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I want that part most, since a genuine incompatibility would be a result in itself — and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Then, briefly, how classical platonism, a nominalist reconstruction of the 'no abstracta, only concrete inscriptions and their combinatorial possibilities' variety, and a structuralist or fictionalist alternative each fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard, since I shall run the scoring myself in the reading group. Please write in British English.
