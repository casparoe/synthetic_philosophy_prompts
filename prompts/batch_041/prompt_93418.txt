I'm putting together a thing on dirty hands and I want to run it through a case that isn't Hiroshima for once. Take the British attack on the French fleet at Mers-el-Kébir, 3 July 1940, about a week after France's armistice with Germany took effect. Churchill and the War Cabinet ordered the French squadron at anchor near Oran neutralised -- join the British, sail somewhere out of German reach, scuttle, or be sunk. Admiral Somerville, commanding the force out of Gibraltar, delivered the ultimatum; the French admiral Gensoul wouldn't take it, and Force H opened fire. 1,297 French servicemen died, a battleship blew up, most of the rest were crippled, one fast battleship got away to Toulon. Pétain severed diplomatic relations with Britain a few days later and French aircraft bombed Gibraltar in retaliation. (There's a live historical fight about how much of the offer actually reached Gensoul through the intermediaries -- don't rest anything on that either way, just flag it if it changes the analysis.)

Treat that as a test case for the dirty hands idea: a leader may be required to do something morally wrong for the political good, and the wrongness doesn't evaporate once it's justified -- it leaves a remainder. Now the distinction I want you to run with, which I don't think is standard and which you'll have to work out yourself: the usual dirty-hands story is instrumental. You do the wrong thing as a means to a good end -- torture the guy, save the city. Mers-el-Kébir looks like something else. On the standard telling the point of the killing wasn't any downstream result you could name; it was the demonstration that Britain would do it -- the ruthlessness was the product, not the instrument. Call that constitutive ruthlessness if you like. So: is this dirty hands at all, or a different category that the theory keeps trying to swallow?

Then the part I actually care about: whose remainder is it? Owed to the dead French sailors, to Somerville and his crews who did the shooting, to Churchill and the Cabinet who ordered it, to the office, or to nobody? And is guilt even the right reaction when you're acting in a role -- does Somerville owe anything personally, or is that a category error that the theory bakes in by assuming the agent and the author are one person?
