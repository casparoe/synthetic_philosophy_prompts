I am a postdoctoral fellow in philosophy and economics, and next month I have to lead a single ninety-minute session of an interdisciplinary reading group. The members are PhD students in philosophy, politics and economics: all of them have had a solid first course in decision theory, so they can state the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, they know what a sure-thing argument looks like, and they are comfortable with the idea of a utility representation that is unique up to positive affine transformation, but none of them has ever opened a text in social choice. The topic is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I do not want you to explain the theorem to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and why each must come before the next. I am especially interested in how you would sequence three things that I expect the students to collapse into one another: first, the claim that a social preference relation can be represented by a utility function at all; second, the assumption that the social relation satisfies the same expected-utility axioms as the individual relations; and third, the Pareto-type condition linking social and individual preferences over lotteries. Say explicitly where the theorem does its real work and where it merely inherits structure from assumptions already in place. I want the session carried by one worked case rather than a parade of examples: a small society of two or three people choosing between lotteries, ideally a case in which the intuitive fairness verdict and the verdict delivered by an expected-utility social ordering come apart, so that the students feel the price of the axioms rather than only the elegance of the result. Tell me what that case must have and what it must lack — it should not be a mere two-person two-good exchange, and it should not be so small that the weighting of individuals' utilities looks like an arbitrary stipulation rather than a consequence — and show me how to work it through without a blackboard crowded with notation.

Then give me the misconception map. The students will arrive believing that the theorem proves utilitarianism, and I need to know at what precise point in the sequence that belief should be dismantled rather than merely noted; my own view is that it is defused only once they see that the weights are not fixed by the axioms and that nothing in the result forces the individual utility functions to be measures of welfare at all. They will also conflate the aggregation theorem with the impartial-observer or veil-of-insignorance argument that Harsanyi is equally known for, and I want a way of keeping the two apart that does not consist of saying that one is decision-theoretic and the other ethical. Expect them, further, to treat the result as a variant of Arrow's impossibility theorem with the difficulty removed by fiat, and to be unable to say what the single-profile character of the result does and does not establish — that is, why the fact that the social ordering is a weighted sum of the individual utility functions in the profile at hand does not yet supply a general recipe for aggregating across different profiles. Finally, they will not see why the ex ante/ex post objection bites, so tell me exactly where to introduce it: whether to let them raise it themselves after the worked case or to pre-empt it, and what the objection looks like in the idiom of the case rather than in the idiom of counterexample-mongering. End with the question I should put to them in the last ten minutes that would reveal whether they have understood what kind of result this is — not whether they can state it, but whether they can say what it leaves unsettled — together with a short list of things I should openly refuse to go into at this level and flag as deliberate omissions. Please write the plan in British English.
