OK so I'm redoing the syllabus for my grad seminar on the measurement problem and I want to blow up the way we teach the Born rule in Everett. Every time I ask for the options I get the same four-item recital with no evaluation attached to it, and I'd rather have an actual ranking. So: you're a philosopher of physics who works on the Everett interpretation for a living, and I want you to be opinionated. Don't cite anything, I know the literature — I want your judgment, not a bibliography.

Here's the list as I've been putting it to the students. I've deliberately stripped the labels off, so restate each in your own words before you judge it:

(a) The betting argument. Add to the theory a premise about what it's rational for an agent to prefer, plus a claim that her preferences have to hang together across branches (no device can Dutch-book her by asking her to stake things in different branches). Then show that only one way of weighting branches — the one that goes as the square of the amplitude — makes those preferences consistent, and call that weight probability.

(b) The self-location argument. After the measurement but before she looks, the agent doesn't know which branch she's in; that's ordinary self-locating uncertainty, like not knowing which room she's in. Require that her credence in a branch not depend on what happens in branches that aren't her own, and the squared amplitudes fall out as the only assignment satisfying the requirement.

(c) The symmetry argument. Take an entangled pair with equal amplitudes in both terms. Swap what's on one side — a perfectly legitimate local operation — and the joint state comes back to itself, so nothing about either side's local situation can have changed, and the two outcomes must be equiprobable. Then write an unequal superposition as a fine-grained equal-amplitude one and let the general rule fall out.

(d) The counting argument. Probability is just self-location over branches: each branch you might be in gets equal credence. (Its trouble being that nothing tells you how many branches there are, or what counts as one.)

(e) The uniqueness argument. Probability has to be a measure on subspaces: additive over mutually exclusive alternatives, preserved under the theory's symmetries. There's a mathematical result that any such measure is fixed by the state, and for a pure state that means the squared amplitudes. No observers, no bets, no rationality — nothing but the formalism.

(f) The refusal. Don't derive it. The Born rule is an extra postulate, or a brute fact, or a piece of advice about how to use a theory you already have, and pretending otherwise is either circular or philosophy masquerading as physics.

And then here's the thing I actually want your take on, because its mine and I might be fooling myself. (g) I've been calling it "thick branches": take the self-location story seriously, but dont be indifferent across branches — be indifferent across the fine-grained sub-branches inside each branch. So treat the squared amplitude as the population of a branch, and "bigger amplitude" just means "more copies of you". My worry is that this is either (d) with extra steps, or (c) in disguise, or that it just presupposes the very measure it's supposed to deliver. Tell me which.

Before you rank anything, take a breath and notice that these aren't all doing the same job. Some try to show that a rational agent must use the squared amplitudes; some try to show that the squared amplitudes are the only consistent assignment of a certain formal kind; some just give up and say it's a posit. Sorting that out is most of the work, so do it first.
