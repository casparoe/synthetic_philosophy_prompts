Six years ago I was doing nuclear safeguards work — I spent a year on a team that verified a bilateral materials-accounting arrangement, then moved into export controls — and three months ago I joined a small AI policy shop. The transition has been mostly pleasant, with one recurring irritation: the nuclear analogy is this office's default tool, and I am the only person in the room who has actually stood inside the thing being compared. Last week a colleague laid the argument out in its strongest form, and I have not been able to shake it or to accept it. His claim is that any regime worth having for frontier AI must be Acheson–Lilienthal-shaped rather than NPT-shaped: that the dangerous inputs — frontier-scale compute, the clusters and the fabrication capacity behind them — have to pass under some form of international ownership or custody before anyone agrees to slow down, because a regime that only inspects, counts and sanctions after the fact will be defeated by whoever defects first. He knows the history well enough not to be naive. He reads the collapse of the 1946 plan as a lesson about the veto and about the sequencing deadlock — each side insisting the other move first — rather than as a lesson about the underlying design, and he takes the later treaty-plus-safeguards model that eventually arrived in the late 1960s to be a regime that only worked because the technology had matured and the great powers had already settled into a stable standoff. My own suspicion, which I can't yet defend, is that the analogy is doing all its work in the premise that there is a "dangerous material" at all, and that the AI equivalent is not weights, not chips, and not data centers but something closer to accumulated expertise, which is neither ownable nor verifiable.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than endorse or dismiss it. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of fissile material, of the mines and isotope-separation plants, of the Atomic Development Authority, of the veto-free sanctions clause, of the Soviet counter-demand for disarmament first, and of the inspections regime that followed — and mark each row as tight, loose, or empty. Then tell me which features of the nuclear case the analogy quietly imports into the AI case: the scarcity of the dangerous input, the short list of chokepoints along a long physical production path, the detectability of the relevant activity, the discreteness of a weapon as against a copyable artifact and a continuum of competence, the separability of civilian from military use. Separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and say plainly whether the conclusion survives them — including the awkward possibility that the historical record he is invoking is a record of his preferred design failing and the inspection model he disdains being the thing that eventually held.

Format: open with a single-sentence verdict, then the table, then the analysis; close with what would be a better analogy if this one cannot carry the weight. Eight hundred words maximum, table included.
