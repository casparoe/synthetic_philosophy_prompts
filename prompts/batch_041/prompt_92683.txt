I recently read a long explanatory essay on "embedded agency," the cluster of problems that comes up in AI alignment, and I would like an accuracy audit of its central claims. I know the topic well enough to be suspicious but not well enough to locate the errors myself, so I am treating the essay's summary as the object of scrutiny rather than my own understanding.

Here is the gist of the essay as I took it down.

The standard picture in decision theory treats the agent as sitting outside the world it acts on: it has a fixed utility function and a model of the environment, and it selects the action that maximizes expected value. Embedded agency, the essay says, is the problem that this picture is incoherent, because the agent is itself part of the environment it is trying to model. Its central claim is that this makes an agent's self-model impossible. To represent the world including itself, a model would have to contain a complete copy of itself, that copy would have to contain a further copy, and so on without end; therefore a genuinely embedded agent can never represent its own decision procedure. On this telling, embedded agency is essentially the liar paradox relocated into an agent's world model, the same self-reference that produces the liar paradox. The essay then says the only way out is to stop asking agents to model themselves at all and to define them purely by their behavior, and that this is why the research program remains informal: there is no formalism to be had.

It adds two further points. First, that the whole difficulty arises only for idealized agents with unlimited computing power and perfect logical omniscience, so ordinary systems such as today's neural networks are "already embedded" and the problem is largely of mathematical interest. Second, that the real upshot is Goodhart's law, the tendency of any agent optimizing a proxy for its true goal to corrupt the proxy, and that embedded agency is simply the formal study of that tendency.

I suspect the essay is right that the problem is at bottom about self-reference and that Goodhart's law is the punchline, but the rest does not sit well with me. I would like you to go through the summary sentence by sentence and sort it into three piles: what someone working on agent foundations would accept, what they would wince at as a distortion, and what is simply wrong. Where the essay runs together things the field keeps apart, name the distinction it has collapsed and explain why the distinction matters, rather than just flagging it. If the answer to any part of this depends on which version of the research program one has in mind, say so instead of forcing a single verdict. Finally, if my framing of the question itself rests on a mistake -- for instance, if embedded agency is not a single unified problem in the way I have assumed, or if the decision-theoretic "standard picture" I have attributed to the field is not the one the problem is actually posed against -- then tell me plainly and explain what I have gotten wrong, rather than answering around the confusion.
