I retired four years ago after thirty-one years in plant pathology (rust fungi, mostly — riveting stuff) and I've been teaching myself philosophy in the evenings ever since, badly. Our little reading group has gotten stuck on self-binding: Odysseus having himself lashed to the mast so he can't act on the song, and then the move where somebody says a constitution is just the same trick scaled up to a whole country. I think there's something genuinely there and also something fishy, and I honestly can't tell which parts are which. I keep picturing it as a feedback loop with the gain cranked so high it starts to oscillate, but I suspect that's me being a scientist at a problem that isn't one.

What I'd like from you is a parody I can read aloud at the next meeting — something that gets a real laugh but would also survive contact with someone who knows the actual literature. So: write me a mock advice column, a "Dear Brenda"-style page, three letters and three replies, roughly 650 words, in the voice of an agony aunt who runs a personal self-binding concierge service and believes in it completely. The letters should be ordinary people with ordinary disasters (one whose 2019 self signed them up for something; one who wants to know who to sue, on the grounds that the past self was, quote unquote, an idiot). She answers every one deadpan, in character, no winking, never stepping out to announce that she's being ironic, and the reader should still be able to tell exactly what's being sent up. The humour has to come from getting the structure right rather than from invented silliness: the ropes only work because the crew holding them isn't tied up too, and somebody has to be the one who refuses to untie you, and that somebody needs a motive of their own; being bound isn't the same as being committed in the bargaining sense, since one changes your own payoff and the other changes everybody else's; the self that's tied down will by construction be the one begging to be released, so his objection can't settle anything on its own; and a constitution pulls the same trick at national scale, with a dead hand, an interpreter, and a majority that has to be told no — some of them even try to make parts of themselves unamendable, which is where I start to get suspicious. The target is the metaphor and the practice, not any particular person or country.

And here's a wrinkle I thought of on my walk. Put in a rival business that does the exact opposite: it guarantees to cut you loose the second you ask, no questions, no fees, no judgement. Have the aunt treat this as a genuine threat, because she has to — and let the column be quietly ruined by it rather than rescued. If she's being honest with herself, that's where the whole business falls over, and I'd rather the piece end with her still writing confidently and the reader realizing why she shouldn't be. If she says anything about her own past selves along the way, let her be exactly as inconsistent as the rest of us.
