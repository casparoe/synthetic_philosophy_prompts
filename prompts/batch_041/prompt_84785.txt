For thirty-odd years I did experimental work in condensed matter physics, where a claim earns its keep by surviving an instrument; since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy, and I find myself drawn to the literature on threats and deterrence, which strikes me as the one corner of practical reasoning where the logic is tested against consequences rather than against intuitions. I would like you to write a sustained piece of satire on that subject, and I would like it to be fair: the kind of piece that someone who has actually worked on deterrence reads with a wince of recognition rather than of embarrassment.

The form I have in mind is a customer-facing product brochure and FAQ for a fictional consultancy that sells deterrence postures to governments by subscription. Use the register of an enterprise-software vendor or a management-consulting firm: confident, frictionless, faintly euphemistic. Include an opening pitch, a "how it works" section, three or four pricing tiers, an implementation timeline, a short FAQ, a handful of selected client outcomes, and a closing block of terms and exclusions. Invent the firm's name, and staff the piece with invented officials and invented ministries; do not put words in the mouths of real people, and do not cite or quote any real document. I want the piece to stand on its own without a bibliography, since I intend to show it to people who will not have the sources at hand, and I want a reader who has never encountered strategic studies to be able to follow it.

Everything funny in it should be funny because the underlying logic is right. I would expect the brochure to run up against, at a minimum: the credibility problem — that a threat worth buying is a threat the buyer would rather not carry out; the use of randomness as a substitute for resolve, so that escalation can be automated precisely in order that nobody has to mean it; the difference between deterrence by denial and deterrence by punishment, priced accordingly; the stability–instability paradox, in which the same arsenal that makes major war unthinkable makes minor war thinkable; the reciprocal fear of surprise attack; the problem of delegated launch authority, including the awkward fact that the product works best when its trigger conditions are withheld from the client's own decision-makers; the way deliberation about whether to execute can destroy the very credibility the deliberation was meant to secure; audience costs; moral hazard, since a client who has bought insurance has that much less reason to avoid the crisis; and the position of the seller's own headquarters inside the blast radius. The firm must never concede that it is selling a bluff, and the terms and exclusions must concede it anyway.

Please also coin two or three proprietary terms — a metric, a module, a service tier — and define them with a completely straight face, in the way such documents always do.

Roughly 800 words, deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader and no moment where the narrator steps out from behind the brochure to explain the joke.
