I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been working through Andrew Motte's eighteenth-century English translation of Newton's Principia in the evenings, mostly to see how the mechanics is assembled. The mathematics I can follow; the General Scholium that closes Book III I cannot. I understand that Newton was theologically unorthodox in ways that would have gotten him into trouble, but that fact by itself doesn't tell me what the text in front of me is arguing, and I'd rather understand the argument than file it under biography. Here is the stretch I keep rereading:

"This Being governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all: And on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God παντοκρáτωρ or Universal Ruler. For God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God, not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants. ... He is not Eternity or Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite; he is not Duration or Space, but he endures and is present. He endures for ever, and is every where present; and by existing always and every where, he constitutes Duration and Space."

My confusion is both local and global. Locally: why is Newton so insistent on the difference between being eternity and infinity and being eternal and infinite, and what exactly is being claimed when he says that God, by existing always and everywhere, constitutes duration and space? Is that a metaphysical thesis about what space and time fundamentally are, or is it doing narrower work inside the argument of the Scholium — say, blocking the identification of God with the world while still letting him be everywhere? Globally: the passage appears to ground God's title in dominion rather than in perfection or creative power — God is a relative word, and Deity is dominion over servants — and Newton flatly denies that God is the soul of the world. I want to know what alternative he is proposing, and why it matters to him. Does he need a lord rather than a world-soul in order to keep the physics honest (for instance, to leave room for providential adjustment of a solar system that might otherwise run down), or is the theological position doing work that stands on its own? And how does this picture of God's relation to space and time bear on the objections Leibniz raised against the Newtonian conception of space and of God?

Please give me an explanation I can actually use: walk me through what the sentences are doing, what the target of the "soul of the world" denial is, and what follows from the dominion language. Where people who study this are in broad agreement, say so and say what the agreed reading is; where it is genuinely contested — whether this God is best described as voluntarist, as committed to space and time as divine attributes in some strong sense, or as something else entirely, and how much of the Scholium is theology versus defensive maneuvering against critics of action at a distance — lay out the competing readings and mark them clearly as competing. I would much rather have an honest map of the disagreement than a tidy answer that presents one interpretation as if it were the consensus.
