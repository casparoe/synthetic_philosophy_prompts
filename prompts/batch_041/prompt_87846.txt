I'm trying to write a short piece about Darwin and Turing and it keeps collapsing on me, so I want you to read my notes and give me comments. Here's where I've got to.

Both men do the same trick, supposedly. Paley looks at an eye and infers a designer; Darwin looks at the eye and infers a process with no eye for eyes — no foresight, no plan to make vision. Turing, instead of asking what thinking is and then whether a machine could do it, asks what we'd actually accept as evidence of thinking and then whether a machine could produce that evidence. Both times the explanatory order flips: you stop trying to install the fancy thing (design, thought) at the ground floor and instead show how it precipitates out of something that lacks it.

What I want to argue is that these aren't the same inversion, and that the bumper-sticker version of them — competence without comprehension, comprehension is just competence stacked up — quietly merges the two.

Call the first kind explaining-down and the second explaining-away. Darwin is doing the first: the competence (a swallow navigating to Africa, an eye focusing) is real, and we explain it by a mechanism that doesn't have it. Nothing gets debunked, everything gets explained. I think Turing is doing the first too, at least on the reading that makes the imitation game interesting — passing is evidence of a competence that's really there, and whether there's comprehension behind it is deliberately left open. His suggestion that we build a child machine and educate it rather than an adult one is the same move: a claim about where the competence would have to come from, not a claim it isn't there. But the slogan trades on the second: if comprehension is just a lot of competence, then there was never anything to explain and the question dissolves.

The tell, I think, is what happens to the word "just." Darwin never says the eye is just a light-sensitive patch that got lucky; he says the patch got lucky enough times over enough generations and now it's an eye. The "just" is doing the debunking, and the debunking isn't the Darwinian part. Turing's actually very careful — he doesn't claim the machine understands, he claims that if you insist there's a fact of the matter about understanding then you owe us a test, and he's noticed we don't have one we trust.

Where I'm stuck: is the explaining-down / explaining-away distinction real, or am I just reinventing the is/ought, description/debunking gap with new labels? And does the second inversion ever do legitimate work, or is it always a cheat? I'd like it to be a tool and not just a fence — some cases where explaining-away is the right call — but the only examples I can come up with are ones where I already think the thing being explained away is bogus, which feels circular.

Give me your comments. I don't care whether someone's said all this before, I care whether it holds up, so please don't send me off to read things. I'd like at least one comment on whether the distinction earns its keep or is just decoration, at least one on whether the "just"-test is a real test or just a vibe, and at least one on whether explaining-away ever legitimately works. Write it as exactly five numbered comments, each a sentence or two, with no intro and no conclusion.
