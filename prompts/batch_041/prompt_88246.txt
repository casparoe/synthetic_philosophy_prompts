I am a novelist at work on a book whose third act turns on a quarrel between two characters, and I want that quarrel to be one a careful reader can follow without my having flattened it into a slogan. Both characters are first-rate; neither exists to lose.

The setting is a private betting house that takes wagers on open mathematical questions — whether a given conjecture will ever be settled, whether a particular statement is independent of the axioms it is written in. The house posts its own prices and lets clients bet against them. One character, the house's actuary, designed the pricing rule. The other, a logician who has been quietly taking the house's money, wants to know what the actuary thinks he is doing.

The actuary's line: one can hold graded, defensible degrees of belief about undecided mathematical claims, and the test of such a scheme is not whether it matches some chance process — there is none — but whether any client, however ingenious, can rig a combination of bets that profits whatever the mathematics turns out to be. His rule is built to survive that test. It never leaps to certainty ahead of a proof, it tightens as proofs arrive, and it even prices wagers on its own future prices.

The logician's line, as I understand it, runs roughly so: either the rule assigns probability one to everything provable and zero to everything refutable, in which case it is deduction wearing the costume of uncertainty; or it does not, and then there is some price at which he can guarantee himself a profit. And beneath that sits a second worry: when the actuary quotes odds on a claim's independence from the axioms, is he pricing a fact about numbers or a fact about his own ignorance? A statement's having no proof and a person's failing to find one are not the same thing, and the house's ledger seems to run them together.

Write the dialogue. Let them press each other, concede where concession is owed, and locate the place where they genuinely part company — whether that is a disagreement about mathematics, about rationality, or about what the word belief is doing. They are characters in a novel, not a seminar: let the argument be carried by the exchanges rather than by citation, and give me three exchanges that cut rather than a page that summarizes.
