I convene a mixed reading group — philosophy graduate students alongside machine-learning engineers — and next week's session is on the proposal that an AI system can be kept honest by making it argue. Two systems receive the same question and defend opposing answers before a human judge who is not expected to solve the problem, only to decide which side argued better; each side may challenge any step in the other's case, and a challenged step must itself be defended, and so on. The group will arrive holding two convictions: that truth is easier to defend than to attack, and that the judge's ignorance costs nothing, because the judge only ever has to check one small step at a time. I want the ninety minutes to leave them seeing the obfuscated argument as a structural obstacle rather than a loophole, and I want a plan for the teaching, not the explanation itself. Lay out the order in which the ideas must come, with the dependencies made explicit — in particular, what must already be on the table about the challenge structure before the phrase obfuscated argument can mean anything to them. Nominate the single concrete case that should carry the session. My candidate is a dispute over the value of a large combinatorial quantity in which the dishonest side's derivation is locally faultless at every step, the correct value differs, and the honest side can refute it only by redoing the whole computation and exhibiting the discrepancy; tell me whether that case bears the weight or mislocates the difficulty, and if it fails, what the carrying case must have that it lacks. Then the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface — my guesses are that obfuscation is a species of lying, that a longer debate or a more stubborn judge repairs it, that the judge could simply demand a simpler explanation, and that the difficulty lies in the judge's competence rather than in the shape of the game — with the precise point in the sequence at which each must be headed off rather than merely noted. Close with what I should say out loud that I am leaving out at this level, and one question to put to the group at the end that would separate having understood the obstacle from having merely been told of it.
