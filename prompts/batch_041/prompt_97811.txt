Since I hung up my lab coat a few years ago I've been making up for a lifetime of skipped humanities by reading philosphy, and I've gotten a bit obsessed with the question of who's responsible when an autonomous system does something wrong. Here's the thing that bugs me. Picture the self-driving car that hits someone, or the trading algorithm that blows up a market, or a hospital triage system that quietly mis-prioritises patients — every time, the responsibility seems to slide around without ever landing. The engineer says the thing learned behaviour she never wrote; the operator says he was watching a system that acted faster than any human could react; the company says it shipped a certified product; and if you try to trace it all back to whoever built the thing, the way we trace a drunk driver's guilt back to the decision to drink, the analogy wobbles, because here the downstream behaviour wasn't chosen by anybody. I read somewhere that in these situations the human operator ends up functioning as a kind of crumple zone, absorbing the blame for a machine nobody really steers, and that feels exactly right to me. The other half of what fascinates me is that even if we all agreed to call the machine itself responsible, it would be a completely different kind of responsibility — there's nobody to give an account, nobody who could have done otherwise in the sense that actually matters morally.

Anyway, what I actually want from you is a poem about this. Specifically a sestina — six six-line stanzas and then that little three-line envoi — because the more I look at the form the more it seems built for the problem: six end-words that keep returning, but shuffled into new positions each stanza, so the word that was the agent in one stanza is the object in the next, and the whole thing circles without ever settling. Choose the six end-words so they're the links in the chain (something like design, order, hand, learn, fault, and a word for nobody or no one — your call, but make sure at least two of them are the ones doing the acting early on, so the reader can feel them getting demoted). The envoi should be the moment where all six words are finally in the room together and it's clear that responsibility still isn't anywhere, but please don't let the poem turn into an argument or announce a thesis — it has to stay a poem. I'd like it to be accurate enough that someone who works on this recognises the picture, and I'd rather not have the usual robot-apocalypse imagery. If the form starts fighting the content somewhere, just tell me afterwards in a sentence where, instead of forcing it.
