I'm putting together a handout for a grad reading group on practical rationality (mixed phil/econ crowd) and I want it to be a decision tree, not a list of -isms. Start it at the reader who's just been walked through a money pump: they've got cyclic preferences over three options, someone trades with them three times, they end up exactly where they started minus some cash, and they concede that something is wrong with them. The tree should carry that person from the concession to a specific position on what diachronic rationality actually demands.

The whole value is in the branch questions, so every node has to be something you can answer without already knowing where it lands you. No nodes like "are you a resolute chooser?" That's a leaf, not a branch.

Branch points I want it to use, since these are what we keep tripping over:

- Who or what is the bearer of the requirement — the person across time, or the person-at-a-time? I.e. is there any requirement that your later self be consistent with your earlier self, or only that each slice of you do the best it can with the attitudes it has right then? (Some people hold the slice view and argue that all the so-called diachronic norms are really just norms about what you should do now given what you know about your future selves. If you know the standard formulation of that view, use it.)
- Is being exploitable the same as being irrational? The pump only runs if you actually accept each trade, and each trade only counts as a loss if you're measuring cash rather than preference-satisfaction. So: does the argument reveal a defect in the preferences, a defect in the person's foresight, or just a price you pay for having those preferences?
- Naive vs sophisticated vs resolute. Does the agent treat her future preferences as fixed facts about the world that she plans around — and if so, does she backward-induct from her future self and buy a commitment device whenever she can? Or does she treat the plan she made earlier as something she's entitled to just stick to, even after her preferences shift, with no device needed?
- Do your preferences range over acts (things you can do right now) or over whole life histories and outcomes? And can the same option be ranked differently depending on what else is on the menu?
- Does the pump need the trades available in a particular order, or need you to be unable to precommit, or need you not to know your own future preferences? If the exploit depends on one of those, is that a point about you or a point about the argument?
- What is rationality even consisting in here — maximizing something, having a coherent set of attitudes, or just not being a sucker?

Leaves I want to be reachable, named where there's a name and described in a sentence where there isn't: sophisticated choice; resolute choice; the naive chooser who simply maximizes at each moment; the time-slice view; the view that intransitive betterness can be perfectly rational and the pump is no reductio at all; the instrumentalist view that consistency across time isn't a requirement but a technique — you bind yourself because binding gets you what you want; the flat-footed view that yes, cyclic preferences are just irrational and the pump is the proof; and one more for the person who thinks the puzzle is confused because preferences aren't the kind of thing that has a duration.

Aim for about 12 nodes and 7-9 leaves. If some combination of answers is available in the space but nobody actually holds it, leave the slot open and say in one line whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it's merely unexplored. If two leaves can't really be separated by your questions, say so rather than faking a distinction. Keep the node wording plain — two economists in the group will riot if it reads like a logic textbook. And I dont want it to come out as a personality quiz: every leaf should be a position someone could defend in a seminar, not a vibe.
