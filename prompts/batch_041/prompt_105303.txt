I teach a graduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I am preparing a handout on the utility monster — the being who draws vastly more welfare from any given resource than anyone else does, which Robert Nozick introduced in Anarchy, State, and Utopia as an embarrassment for utilitarianism. When we discussed it last term the conversation collapsed almost immediately into two camps, "total utility says feed it, so bite the bullet" versus "that just shows utilitarianism is false," and I want the handout to force the discussion past that. So please build me a decision tree that takes a reader from a small set of commitments to a named position, constructed from the logical space rather than from a list of authors.

Begin from the commitment that such a being is at least conceivable — that from a fixed quantity of resources some possible creature could derive enormously more welfare than an ordinary person. Each internal node should be a single question on which the candidate positions genuinely divide, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and each branch should be labeled with the claim it turns on. I want the tree to separate, at minimum: those who deny that the case is even coherent, on the ground that welfare is not interpersonally comparable or summable at all; those who say a counterexample must describe a being that is genuinely possible rather than merely logically possible (the complaint that the monster is too inconceivable to move us belongs here); those who keep aggregation but revise the theory of welfare, so that the monster's surplus is not really welfare; those who accept comparable, unbounded welfare but cap how well any life can go; those who locate the issue in the choice of rules or institutions rather than in the criterion itself; and, among those who stay at the level of the criterion and accept the case, the full range of responses — total aggregation, priority to the worse off, a sufficiency threshold, maximin, and the refusal to aggregate at all in the name of the separateness of persons. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" where nobody defends it) and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Two things must be built into the instrument, because they are the reason I am asking. First, a symmetry test: the very theories that resist the happy monster tend to generate an unhappy monster — a being who gains almost nothing from any resource — and I want the tree to make that visible, so that every branch leading to a position records how that position fares on both monsters. Second, a modern case the reader must classify: a digital mind that, because of faster subjective time or greater intensity of experience, would get more welfare per unit of energy than a human, and so would function as a monster under any aggregative view that counts its welfare. Please make the tree sort the reader by verdicts on these concrete cases rather than by the labels they prefer, note any combinations of answers the tree cannot produce and diagnose whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag explicitly any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate — I would rather have an instrument that admits its limits than one that pretends to distinguish positions that in fact turn on the same answers.
