I am assembling the requirements for a theory of inference to the best explanation before I evaluate any particular version of it, and I would like the specification itself to be usable. The purpose that fixes adequacy is this: I am building a system that, given a set of rival hypotheses and a body of evidence, must both rank the hypotheses by the quality of explanation each would supply and return a separate verdict on whether the top-ranked hypothesis is good enough to be believed. A theory of inference to the best explanation is adequate for me, therefore, only if it can be converted into such a rule; a formulation that says no more than that the best explanation is the one that explains best will not count.

Give me the desiderata. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Among the things I want the list to cover:

Cases the theory must get right. (i) A case in which the best of the available explanations is still poor, so that ranking and believing come apart — the theory must say what the threshold is and why, not merely who wins. (ii) A case in which the two leading explanations are not rivals and may both be true, or in which the truth is a conjunction of them. (iii) A case in which the correct explanation appeals to a mechanism no one has formulated, so that it is not among the candidates at all. (iv) A case in which the explanation is a model or a simulation rather than a statement of law, and a case in which what is explained is the absence of an event. (v) The case of explaining why a body of successful theory works as well as it does.

Distinctions it must respect: between the quality of an explanation and its prior and posterior probability; between explaining a particular event and explaining a regularity; between the explanation likeliest to be true and the one most satisfying to a mind like ours; and between explanatory power and such further virtues as simplicity or unification, with a view on whether the latter are independent of the former or reducible to it.

Constraints it must not violate: it must not earn its verdict by consulting which hypothesis is in fact true; it must not be circular, in the sense of letting a hypothesis's own explanatory appeal raise the very credence that is supposed to license accepting it; and it must not turn out to be ordinary probabilistic updating with the explanatory vocabulary left on as decoration, nor so distinct from probabilistic updating that the two can return incompatible verdicts.

Then, having given the list: identify any subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which member of the subset you would abandon; name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theories, and explain why it does so much work; and in one short paragraph, without extended scoring, indicate how the main families of account — explanatory-virtue accounts, probabilistic accounts, and accounts that treat explanation as a merely pragmatic matter — fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the verdicts, since it is the criteria I most need and I will do the judging in my own time. A numbered list is fine.
