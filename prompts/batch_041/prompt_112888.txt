I have an argument from a logic seminar that I can neither refute nor accept, and I want a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

Grant this setup. ZF is a countable first-order theory in a countable language, and by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a countable model if it has any model at all. Granting the further assumption — stronger than mere consistency — that ZF has a well-founded model, we may collapse it and take a countable transitive model M of ZF, so that M's membership relation is the real one and M's ω is the real ω. Inside M, "uncountable" means: no bijection with ω that is itself an element of M.

The argument: (1) M satisfies ZF, and ZF proves that the power set of ω is uncountable; so M contains a set it calls the power set of ω and declares uncountable. (2) M is countable, and M's ω is our ω, so the set M calls the power set of ω is, from outside, a countable set of reals. (3) Hence there is a bijection in the real world between ω and the set M calls uncountable — a bijection M neither contains nor can see. (4) So M's verdict is simply false of that set, and what a model says about cardinality is an artifact of the model. (5) Nothing turns on M: for any model of any theory, cardinality talk is model-relative. (6) Therefore there are no absolutely uncountable sets, and uncountability is a fiction of the theory.

Steps (1) through (3) each look like a theorem to me. My friend says the only exit is to give up Löwenheim–Skolem, which is also a theorem. The reply I keep meeting — that the witnessing bijection exists outside M but not inside it — I understand, and it does not help, since (4) through (6) seem to me to survive it. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked irresistible, and what rejecting it costs. I would sooner keep Löwenheim–Skolem, M, and Cantor's theorem than accept (6), but I want to know the price. Close by saying what would change your verdict, and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
