My old college asked me to give this year's commencement address and I said yes before remebering that I've spent my career arguing that generations aren't real kinds -- that "Gen Z" and "Boomers" and the rest are ad bins, and underneath them there's just birth year, a continuum, plus the fact that people born in different years meet the same events at different ages (the age-period-cohort tangle: you can't pull a clean cohort effect out of it, and the tidy 20-year bins everyone now uses came out of a '90s pop theory of history cycles rather than out of any data). The dean's email says she'd love a few words about "this generation of graduates," and the graduates have been told since middle school that they are one. Room is 400 people: the graduates, their parents, and faculty, several of whom know exactly what I think of generational talk. Write the speech, 12 minutes or so. It has to be honest -- I'm not standing up there saying things I think are false just because it's a nice day -- but it can't turn into a seminar on the identification problem, and it can't be me informing a hall of 22-year-olds that the identity they've been handed is fake, which would be cruel and also wrong, since I do think generation-talk is real as a social fact, the way money is real: people organize around it, employers sell to it, colleges write emails about it. I just don't think there's a kind there. And I don't want to win by stipulation -- quietly redefining "generation" halfway through so my view comes out true is cheating. The valedictorian speaks before me and will do the "we are the generation that" thing. What do I say to the dean afterward if she asks whether I was making fun of her?
