I have been volunteering two evenings a week at a restorative-practice programme in a secondary school, and what I keep noticing is that the script we work from does two quite different jobs in two quite different vocabularies. In the first half — the staff call it the ledger half — the child who has done the harm is asked what they did, who was affected, and what they can do to put it right, and what comes out is almost entirely debt-language: I broke it, I have to fix it, I owe them, how long until it is square again. In the second half — the witness half — they are asked what kind of person they were being in that moment and what kind of person they want to be in this room, and the room goes quiet and the language changes completely: I was a coward, I let myself down, I do not want to be that person. The reparation always gets agreed in the ledger half, and yet it is the witness half where anything actually seems to change. I have been trying to turn this into a position I could defend at a philosophy society, and I would like a consistency check before I present it.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (a) Guilt is the better moral emotion: its object is something I did, it comes with an obvious reparative agenda, and it can be felt and acted on in private, with no one else knowing. (b) Shame is the inferior one: its object is the whole self, it is bound up with how one appears to others, and its characteristic outputs are concealment and withdrawal rather than repair. (c) All the same, the witness half is doing work that the ledger half cannot do, and I do not think that work is dispensable — where a wrong cannot be undone or repaid, and where what is at issue is not a debt but the sort of person one has been willing to be, guilt-language leaves the self untouched; one can settle up and walk away unaltered, and something in the neighbourhood of shame is the fitting response there. (d) Which of the two a person feels is mostly a matter of training: emotional repertoires are cultivated, by families, schools, religious practice and the stories a society tells about what is contemptible, and are not simply given by nature. (e) Because of (d), a culture is not stuck with the emotions it happens to have; it can in principle choose which of the two to foreground, and if (a) is right it should choose guilt. (f) But the choice in (e) cannot be made on the ground that guilt produces better behaviour: if the test is which emotion yields more compliance, less recidivism and smoother institutions, shame will often win, and I do not want to be committed to the view that a culture should cultivate whatever emotion maximises conformity.

What I want is an audit rather than a summary. Tell me which of these pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and rank the real conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. I am fairly sure that something in the set has to go, but I do not know what, and I would rather be told which single revision would clear up the most, and what each candidate revision would cost me, than be told that everything is in tension with everything else. I am also unsure whether (d) undercuts (a) — if my guilt-repertoire was simply installed by my upbringing, in what sense is guilt the better one? — or whether that is a confusion on my part. And if any of the pairs that look inconsistent turn out to be merely verbal, say so plainly, and say which word is doing the damage.
