I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about dominated strategies and my intuitions wont sit still. Two games, both two-player, and in each one I'm the row player.

Game 1: I pick A, B or C; the other player picks X or Y. Payoffs (mine, theirs): A,X (3,0); A,Y (0,3); B,X (0,3); B,Y (3,0); C,X (1,1); C,Y (1,1). C is never a best response to anything they might do. Verdict: C is the worst thing on my list, but I don't think playing it is irrational -- nothing I can actually do beats it, only a coin flip between A and B does, and I don't count coin flips as options I can choose.

Game 2: I pick T, M or D; they pick L, C or R. Payoffs: T,L (3,3); T,C (3,2); T,R (0,1); M,L (3,2); M,C (3,3); M,R (1,2); D,L (1,2); D,C (1,1); D,R (1,2). Verdict: T is out, because M is never worse and is better against R. D is out the same way, and R is out because L is never worse and is better against T. So I'd play M and be happy with L or C from them. Then I ran the same eliminations in a different order -- took D out first -- and T came back and R went away, and nothing about the game changed. Now I don't trust my own verdicts.

Tentatively: a strategy of mine is off the table only if some single other strategy of mine is never worse and sometimes better. Should that be applied once, or over and over? Is it even consistent with what I said about game 1? Tell me what principle actually fits my verdicts, stated precisely, what it says about cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts I have to give up. I'd rather have the principle and its price than a survey of solution concepts.
