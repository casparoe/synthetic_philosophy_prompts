I have spent the past several years working as an applied statistician, building forecasting and causal-inference models, and a recent argument with a colleague trained in philosophy has made me want to see whether the Humean–anti-Humean dispute about laws of nature can be run in the vocabulary I actually think in. Please restate both sides of that dispute — the Humean account of laws as the best systematization of the total pattern of occurrent fact, and the main anti-Humean alternatives, on which laws govern or necessitate the world or express the dispositions of things — as faithfully as you can in my terms: data-generating process versus fitted model, likelihood and goodness of fit versus regularization and simplicity penalties, overfitting, compression and minimum description length, prior and posterior, bias and variance. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of each position translate cleanly into this vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all — in particular, tell me what becomes of the anti-Humean's talk of laws governing the world, and of the Humean's insistence that laws are worldly features of the mosaic rather than features of us or of our models. Finally, say whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary, something the statistical picture simply cannot express, or a sign that the original disagreement was never as deep as it sounded.
