Le Verrier identified in 1859 a residual precession in Mercury's perihelion that Newtonian mechanics could not explain, and the anomaly remained an outstanding problem in celestial mechanics — prompting the failed hunt for an intra-Mercurial planet — until Einstein's general relativity accounted for it in 1915. Analyze this episode through the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory. First lay out what the machinery actually delivers: because the anomalous precession was already certain before the theory existed, its likelihood is the same under every hypothesis and conditionalizing on it cannot raise the probability of general relativity, so the confirmation Einstein took himself to have is invisible to the standard account. Then assess the principal repairs — that the agent learns the theory rather than the datum and conditionalizes on the derivational relation between them; that the real evidence is the finer-grained proposition about the exact magnitude of the anomaly rather than the bare fact of a deviation; and that the case shows confirmation cannot consist in a rise in probability at all. Say which repair the episode best supports, whether general relativity's later success with novel predictions such as light deflection and gravitational redshift makes old-evidence confirmation dispensable rather than genuine, and what, if anything, the Bayesian must concede. Reach a verdict on what the Mercury case shows about the account.
