I run a fortnightly discussion session at my university's ethics society and next week's is mine. The topic I've been assigned is the scale argument for insect welfare -- the one where you take a very low credence that an insect can suffer at all, multiply it by an absurd number of insects, and end up with something that outranks the farmed-animal numbers everyone in the room already takes seriously. Don't explain the argument to me, I know it backwards. What I need is a plan for teaching it in forty-five minutes to a room that's split down the middle: half are animal-welfare people who will nod along and think they already get it (they don't -- they'll nod at the conclusion and then flunk the arithmetic), and half think the whole thing is a joke and that a bug is a little machine. I want the order the pieces have to go in so that the multiplication feels like the boring, obvious move by the time I do it rather than a trick I'm pulling, and I want to know what has to be nailed down before I'm allowed to say the words "low probability of sentience" without the room mis-hearing it as "so it doesn't matter." I'll be using the usual cartoon numbers -- call it a billion billion insects alive at any given moment, tell me if that's even the right order of magnitude to be leaning on -- and I want to add a wrinkle of my own that I think is the actual crux: even if someone insists that each insect, if it can suffer, suffers only a sliver of what a mammal suffers -- a hundredth, a thousandth -- the argument just swallows that as one more multiplier, so any fixed per-individual discount gets swamped by the population ratio. I want them to see that the "probably they feel nothing" objection only bites if the credence is astronomically small, not merely small. So where in the sequence does that point have to come, and what has to already be in place in their heads before it can land?

For the carrying example I was going to anchor the whole session on one object -- a bug zapper on a summer night, thousands of insects going into it while people sit around and watch it like television -- but I'm not sure whether the anchor should be something the room is complicit in or something morally neutral, and whether it should be domestic or agricultural (a field, a harvest). Tell me which, and what the example has to have and has to not have; my guess is it must be countable and must not have a face on it, but say more than that. Then the misconception map, with the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off: the "insects are just automatons / brain size settles it" intuition; the "probability is low so the whole thing washes out" move; the scope-insensitivity one, where the number is so big it stops meaning anything; the one where they think the argument requires certainty that insects are sentient; and the reductio move -- "so by your logic we have to care about bacteria, nematodes, your laptop" -- which I don't think is a reductio at all but I want to know how to handle it live without getting dragged off course. I also want one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether they've actually got the structure (credence times number of individuals times per-individual weight) rather than just feeling bad about bugs, and a short list of things I should openly announce I'm not going to cover at this level. No readings please, I just want the plan.
