You are a philosopher of science and formal epistemologist with a strong interest in how institutions distribute epistemic authority. I would like a rigorous, original analysis rather than a literature review. Please begin by brainstorming the possible commitments at stake, then organize your answer around them.

Consider an institutional practice common in research funding. A funder requires every applicant to sign a certification stating that all claims in the proposal are true and complete. The funder then audits a random 2 percent of funded proposals each year. Its annual report states that no particular funded proposal is likely to contain a material falsehood, but that some funded proposals certainly do. Reviewers are instructed to treat each certified claim as acceptable if it passes the funder's checklist; they are not asked whether the entire set of certified claims across a portfolio could all be true. If an audit uncovers a false claim, the funder does not automatically reopen other proposals from the same laboratory or institution, even when several were funded under the same program. The funder's guidance adds that a signed certification is not a guarantee of truth but a condition of eligibility for funding.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of rational acceptance, belief, credence, and consistency that the practice requires or merely fits well with. In particular, does it presuppose that rational acceptance is not closed under conjunction, so that one may accept each of many claims while also accepting that at least one of them is false? Or does it avoid that commitment by treating the certification as non-doxastic? Does it need a distinction between belief and credence, or between accepting a claim for practical purposes and believing it? Does the signed certification express belief, or is it better understood as a promise, an assurance, or a legal fiction, and how does that affect the puzzle? What is the unit of epistemic evaluation here: the individual claim, the proposal, the portfolio, or the institution? Which rival views of rational acceptance would rule the practice out, and which would leave it underdetermined? Finally, if the funder replaced this practice with a straightforwardly credence-based or probabilistic policy, what would have to change in the certification, the audit rule, and the reviewers' instructions?

Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely makes convenient. You may use the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as tools, but do not simply recite the standard literature about them, and do not provide a bibliography or citations. Aim for a nuanced answer that a philosopher could learn from.
