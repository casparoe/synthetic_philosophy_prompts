I want a sustained piece of deadpan satire in the form of an internal policy memorandum, roughly 700 words, issued by the director of evaluation at a fictional national research-funding body to that body's grant-review panels. The agency has adopted Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes as its official and binding basis for deciding which projects to fund, and the memorandum's whole purpose is to explain how panels are to apply that methodology. Give the agency a bland bureaucratic name of your own invention, and let the voice be that of a competent mid-level administrator who is mildly proud of the new framework and sees nothing at all odd about it. There should be no narrator's wink, no aside, no line that admits the joke; the humor has to come entirely from the content.

Keep the philosophy accurate enough that a philosopher or historian of science would recognize the machinery and laugh in the right places rather than wince. The memorandum should use, and genuinely depend on, the real apparatus: a hard core that the negative heuristic tells practitioners never to attack, a protective belt of auxiliary assumptions that absorbs the anomalies, a positive heuristic that directs which problems the program pursues, and the distinction between a progressive problem-shift, which predicts something new and occasionally gets it right, and a degenerating one, which merely patches the belt after the fact. On top of that, build the agency's own invented administrative vocabulary, categories such as heuristic debt, protective-belt bloat, core-defection risk, and premature monster-barring, along with anything else the conceit needs. The memorandum should specify, in flat procedural prose, how a panel is to grade a program as progressive or degenerating, what precisely counts as a novel prediction for funding purposes, how a laboratory may appeal a degenerating verdict, and why the hard core is exempt from the agency's otherwise strict demand for falsifiable hypotheses and is in fact shielded by a confidentiality rule.

The satire should land the objections that a reader who knows the material will supply on their own, without the memorandum ever voicing them as criticisms: that progressiveness is only visible in hindsight and cannot be scored within a funding cycle, that a program whose core is unfalsifiable by design sits badly with an agency that claims to prize falsification, that a metric defined as novel prediction will be gamed the moment it is announced, that deciding what belongs to the core and what to the belt is a social and political act dressed up as logic, and that no observation ever condemns a program on its own. Let these surface as consequences of the procedures the document lays down rather than as remarks anyone makes.

Include one or two short quotations, both from invented in-universe documents, for example a line from the agency's founding charter and a line from a dissenting panel member's note that the director quotes only in order to dismiss it. Do not quote Lakatos or any other real text, and do not include citations, references, footnotes, or a reading list of any kind.

Write it as continuous prose, the way one would dictate a long memorandum or write a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized labels, no tables. American English.
