Please explain a conceptual issue that has recently become important to me. I am the primary caregiver for my father, who has advanced dementia, and his neurologist has suggested that I sometimes go along with his false beliefs rather than correct them — what some clinicians call therapeutic lying. I am a religious person and have been reading Augustine on lying, and I want to understand the structure of his position rather than just the slogan that all lying is sinful.

Here is the issue as I understand it, and I would like you to clarify whether I have it right. Augustine seems to define a lie as a false signification accompanied by the will to deceive. If that is correct, then two things seem to follow that I cannot reconcile. First, telling the truth with the intent to mislead would not count as a lie, even though it seems to me to be a form of deception; and second, a false statement made with no intent to deceive — say, to a person who cannot understand it, or in a joke — would also not count. Yet Augustine also distinguishes kinds of lies, treating some as more serious than others, and he clearly thinks some false statements are lies even when they harm no one and are told to help. So I want to know: is the intention to deceive the essence of the lie for him, or is it the falsity of the signification, or is it the act of asserting something one believes to be false? And given that he holds all lying to be sinful, how does his ranking of lies by gravity hang together with that absolutism — is it a matter of the lie's object, its motive, or the harm it risks? I would especially like the distinction between an officious lie and a pernicious one explained, and whether that distinction actually does any work in resolving the tension or merely restates it.
