I have no background in economics, but I have been reading about the mathematics of collective decision-making on my own, and I recently finished a short book for general readers that devotes a chapter to what it calls the liberal paradox, attributed to Amartya Sen. I would like a careful audit of that chapter's central summary, which I have written down as faithfully as I can:

'Sen asked whether a society can be both liberal and efficient, and proved that it cannot. He imposed three conditions that look almost too weak to disagree with: that any pattern of individual preferences is admissible; that if every single person prefers one outcome to another, society must prefer it too (the Pareto principle); and that each person must be allowed to decide at least some matters that are their own business. He then showed that the three conditions contradict each other. His famous illustration involves two men and a copy of a racy novel: the prude would rather the book be destroyed than read by anyone, though he would rather read it himself than let the other man read it, while the other man wants to read it and would also rather the prude read it than that nobody did. The three possible outcomes end up circling one another, each socially preferred to the next, so there is no coherent ranking of them at all. The moral is that the Pareto principle and even the most minimal form of liberalism cannot coexist, and since the real culprit is the requirement that social choices be independent of irrelevant alternatives — the same condition that does the damage in Arrow's theorem — the liberal paradox is essentially Arrow's result in different clothing, and the upshot is that liberalism as a political ideal is mathematically incoherent. The one escape is that the paradox depends on people holding nosy preferences about each other's private lives; once those are excluded, Sen himself conceded, the contradiction vanishes and the liberal state is safe.'

Two things make me suspicious. First, I could not reconstruct the circling argument from the example as I have written it down, so I suspect I have garbled something. Second, if a theorem really showed that liberalism is incoherent, I would expect to have heard far more about it, and my impression is that Sen was himself a liberal who spent his career defending something in the vicinity. Could you go claim by claim through the summary and tell me what a specialist would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong — including whether the conclusion about liberalism's incoherence follows from the theorem at all, and what Sen himself took the result to show? I would also like to know how the proposed escape should be assessed: does excluding nosy preferences, or alternatively letting people waive or trade away their rights case by case, actually dissolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate it? Please flag how confident you are in each part of your verdict, and say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different assessment of any of these claims, and where your reasoning is most likely to be mistaken.
