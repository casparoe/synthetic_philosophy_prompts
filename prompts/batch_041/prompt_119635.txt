Our reading group spent a full session last month on the problem of logical omniscience, and two of its members, Ines and Tomas, argued it to a standstill. I was the bystander. I would like a ruling rather than a summary.

The case that set them off came from our own department. A colleague had read all nine clauses of the university pension scheme and said she still did not know whether a year of part-time work would raise or lower her eventual payout. The answer does follow from the clauses by a chain of six short steps; when we asked her about each of the six in isolation she endorsed it, and when we laid out the chain she said the conclusion was still not something she knew, because she had not done the work and would not claim it until she had.

Ines went first. She said the colleague is not merely uninformed but incoherent, and that this incoherence is the whole content of the so-called problem of logical omniscience: if you accept the starting point and each step, you already have the endpoint, you simply have not noticed. On her view the problem is a category error, since it takes a norm of coherence and complains that it fails to describe a psychology, which no norm was ever meant to do.

Tomas replied that this is the sleight of hand, not the diagnosis. What the colleague has are endorsements she would give if prompted, and those are not the states the derivation needs. And even granting the norm, he said, the idealization is not like a frictionless plane: a frictionless plane is still about the same object with one term set to zero, and a puck on ice approximates it, whereas there is no puck on ice for closure. The natural ways of grading a belief set — how many consequences have been drawn, how long a derivation the agent can survey — do not track anything about the agent, and the ones that do, such as how hard a step is to see, cannot be read off the logic at all. So the theory does not describe an ideal reasoner; it describes one who has already finished, which is a different thing rather than a better version of us.

Ines answered that every idealization is unapproached in some respect, since nothing is frictionless either, and that a graded hierarchy is available, namely closure under derivations of length at most n. If n is the wrong parameter, that is an argument for a better parameter, not against idealizing. She added that his objection proves too much, since it would equally show that the rational credence is a fiction: no one assigns probabilities to every proposition.

Tomas said length is the wrong parameter precisely because difficulty is not a function of the logic; a four-hundred-step mechanical elimination is easy and a three-step insight is hard. He then pressed two further points. First, closure is not decorative in these theories — results about updating, and about the relation between knowledge and belief, are proved with it and then applied to us, so it cannot be a harmless fiction in the premises and a fact about actual reasoners in the conclusion. Second, once the theory is patched with awareness or resource bounds, belief becomes sensitive to how a proposition is worded: two agents with the same picture of the world differ in rationality because of their vocabulary, and the theory stops being about what agents take the world to be like.

Ines closed by accepting that consequence. She said a theory of belief ought to be sensitive to how the agent represents things, and that Tomas still owes an account of what is wrong with the colleague. He calls her committed to the conclusion and blames her for not accepting it; but commitment is not belief — if it were, closure is back and his objection dies, and if it is not, then all he has shown is that she is slow, and slowness is not a doxastic failing.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating in a sentence or two. Then answer three questions on their merits: (i) is closure of belief under logical consequence a norm that a reasoner can violate, or an idealization that describes nothing because nothing could satisfy it? (ii) does the frictionless-plane comparison hold, and if not, which disanalogy does the work? (iii) is commitment a species of belief or a different attitude altogether? Finally, say which of the two has the better of the exchange overall, and identify what neither of them said that the dispute needs. I have no interest in who said what in the literature; judge the arguments as they were made.
