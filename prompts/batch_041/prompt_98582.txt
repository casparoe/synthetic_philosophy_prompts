My reading group has spent the term on collective intentionality and joint action, and one member — I will call her the respondent — has been issuing verdicts on a run of cases with a confidence the rest of us find bracing. Two friends walking together and unconsciously adjusting their pace to stay abreast: joint action. A stadium crowd draining through the same exit, each person merely optimizing his own route: not joint action, only aggregate behavior. A jazz duo trading phrases, each anticipating and answering the other: joint action. A corporate board voting to launch a product and the firm then launching it: joint action. Two strangers whom a mover has independently instructed to carry opposite ends of a sofa, each simply obeying, with no shared goal and no arrangement between them: not joint action. Pressed, she insists she is not merely reporting her verbal intuitions, and that a single criterion governs all five verdicts.

Now she has been handed a new case, and I want you to predict her verdict rather than give me your own. Two jazz musicians who have never met are put on a bandstand and told only to play as they please; each independently resolves to shape her playing around the other, each forms the intention that the two of them improvise together, and over several minutes the result is a coherent duet. But they have made no arrangement, promised nothing, and each would be untroubled and only mildly surprised if the other simply stopped and walked off. Predict her verdict, and do it properly: lay out the two or three criteria that fit all five earlier verdicts equally well — for instance a mutual-responsiveness-plus-shared-goal criterion, a joint-commitment criterion on which the parties acquire obligations and entitlements toward one another, and a criterion that turns on a primitive first-person-plural intention rather than on any obligation — say what each predicts about the new case, give your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would most sharply separate the criteria that survive. Please also tell me, separately, which parts of this are settled among people who work on collective intentionality and which are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
