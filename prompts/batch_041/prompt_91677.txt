ok so I run a small reading group for 2nd/3rd year philosophy students. They've done a term of epistemology so they know the brain-in-a-vat stuff, and they've maybe heard the word "transcendental" but only in the transcendental-meditation sense. Next week is 50 minutes on transcendental arguments and I need to plan the session, not deliver a lecture — so I don't want the explanation itself, I want the teaching plan. What goes in what order, what example carries it, where they'll go wrong, that kind of thing.

Heres where I'm stuck. I dont want to run it as "Kant says you can't have experience without the categories, isn't that clever." I want to run it as the general shape: you take something the other person is already doing — asking a question, being wrong about something, making a claim in this very conversation — and you show they can't be doing it unless something they wanted to deny is already in place. And then the interesting part is whether that ever gets you from "we can't help believing it" to "it's true," which is where the whole thing either becomes philosophy or becomes a trick.

So: what order do the pieces have to come in? My instinct is that the thing that has to land first is not the argument form but the idea that some things are conditions of a capacity rather than causes of it — and tied to that, that a creature which could never be right couldn't be wrong either. If that's a bad starting point, tell me and give me the one that should come first instead.

Then the carrying example. I want to build the whole hour on one concrete scenario instead of three, and I'd been leaning on something like: a person who says they don't believe in other minds but still gets hurt when you ignore them — is that a transcendental argument or just a gotcha? If that won't work as the spine, what should, and what does it have that mine lacks?

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up. My guesses: (1) "transcendental" = "transcendent" = spooky/religious; (2) it's supposed to prove the external world exists; (3) it's circular, it assumes what it proves; (4) "so it's just psychology about what we can't help believing," and the follow-up "then who cares." For each one, where in the sequence do I head it off — and is any of them actually a fair objection I should concede rather than correct?

One more thing I want to get right and I'm not sure I'm not just inventing it: I want to distinguish for them the arguments that say "you must believe X" from the arguments that say "you couldn't so much as have the thought that not-X." Tell me if that's a real distinction worth building on or if I've made it up, and if it's real, where it has to sit in the plan.

Finally: what do I openly refuse to go into at this level, and one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether they've got the form of the argument rather than just nodded along at Kant being difficult.
