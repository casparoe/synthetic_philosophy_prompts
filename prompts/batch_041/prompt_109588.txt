I'm putting together a session for a postgrad seminar on the normativity of attitudes, and I need a case built to order. Everyone reaches for the punishing demon or the briefcase of cash when they want a wrong-kind reason, and I've seen those done to death alot, so give me one out of ordinary emotional life instead — no demons, no omniscience, no mind-altering tech, nothing that couldn't happen in a normal week. Two constraints. First, the attitude can't be belief, desire or admiration; those are played out. Second, and this is the bit I actually care about, the usual escape hatch has to be shut: you can't dissolve the problem by saying the consideration is really a right-kind reason to do something — to bring the attitude about, to act as if you had it, to want it — so I want a case where there's nothing the agent could plausibly do or want that the consideration would be a right-kind reason for. (If you can also arrange it so the wrong-kind reason points the same way as the right-kind one, so the distinction isn't doing any verdict work, great — but prioritise the escape hatch.) One paragraph for the scenario. Then do the work: name the attitude and the consideration, and run it past the three broad accounts of what makes a reason wrong-kind — the one that ties it to the object or the fittingness of the attitude, the one that says such reasons are really reasons for a further attitude or action, and the sceptical line that says there's no real distinction here — giving each account's verdict on my case. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if I strip it out. Give me the strongest reply from whichever account comes out worst. And finish by saying where your own reasoning is most likely to be shaky and what would actually change your mind about the verdict.
