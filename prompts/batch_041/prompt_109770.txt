I've been reading up on the Nyaya school's pramanas and I want to pressure-test one of them, so here's some background in case it helps. Nyaya says there are four ways of knowing: perception; inference, which needs a pervasion (a vyapti) linking the mark to what it marks; upamana, usually translated "comparison" or "analogy"; and sabda, the word of a trustworthy speaker. The textbook illustration of upamana runs like this: someone tells you a gavaya is like a cow; later, in the forest, you see an animal that resembles a cow, you recognize it as the thing that was described, and that's how you come to know what the word "gavaya" picks out. The Naiyayika insists that's a pramana of its own — it isn't testimony, because all you were told was a resemblance and nobody ever said what the word means; and it isn't inference, because there's no pervasion to run.

I want a new case, from ordinary life, that does the same job, with these constraints:

- not the gavaya case; no forests, no field guides, and nobody in the story ever states what the term means or points at its referent
- the term should name something you can already see — a plant, a bird, a tool, a kind of person — so that at the end you're looking right at a perceptible object and the knowledge still isn't the perception
- the resemblance you're handed has to be fully statable (not vague, not a "you'll know it when you see it"), and yet it must be impossible to convert into a pervasion, i.e. into a general premise of the form "whatever has feature F is one of those". Saying why it can't be converted is part of the job
- the knowledge has to be real knowledge at the end: you're right, and you're not just guessing
- one paragraph

Then the bookkeeping. Tell me exactly what the knowledge is at the end, what the upamana cognition takes as its object, and why it's neither testimony nor inference here. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I strip that feature out, and — since the whole point is an arguement with a friend of mine who thinks perception and inference are the only two pramanas worth having — give me the strongest version of his reply (he'll say my case is either perception plus inference in disguise, or not knowledge at all) and tell me straight whether it lands.
