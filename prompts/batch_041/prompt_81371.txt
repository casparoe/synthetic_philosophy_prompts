I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on practical reason, and I want a decision tree that sorts people on the question of whether you can act — and act rationally — when you have no preference between your options. The donkey case, basically, but not the tired version where the ass just starves. I want the tree to get at what that case is actually testing, which turns out to be a whole cluster of questions people keep running together.

Root: "I accept that an agent can face a genuine tie — no reason favors either option." From there, each branch node should be one question that separates the views. Here are the ones I want on the tree; add or drop as you see fit, but tell me why if you do.

- Is a genuine tie even possible, or does the agent always have some reason — including a reason about herself, like "I've deliberated long enough" or "I'd rather not keep standing here"?
- Is it a tie in value or a tie in information? Two options you can't tell apart from where you stand, and two options you've ranked identically, aren't obviously the same case, and I want the tree to force them apart.
- Under a genuine tie, is action even possible? Does the tie cause paralysis, or is the paralysis only a normative verdict about what you may do?
- If action is possible, what's the status of the pick: rational because the tie itself licenses arbitrary selection; rational only given a second-order policy ("when tied, randomize" / "when tied, take the left one"); neither rational nor irrational — a category I half-remember someone drawing with a distinction between picking and choosing, though dont attribute it, I can't remember who; or irrational, since acting without a reason is acting without justification.
- If randomizing is your policy, haven't you just shown there was no tie — you preferred the coin to either option? I want that raised as a diagnostic, not resolved.
- Diachronic version: does the pick itself generate a reason to stick with it afterwards? Someone who says yes and someone who says the justification only ever runs forward are different positions, so I want them as separate leaves.

Work these cases in somewhere: two job offers you've evaluated to a standstill; a coin you flip before the tie arrives; and a case where after you pick, the option you passed over gets better — does that retroactively make the pick irrational?

For each leaf, give the position's name or "unnamed" if nobody holds it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any leaves the tree can't seperate — I suspect "picking is arational" and "there's no fact of the matter about which pick is right" collapse into each other, and I'd rather know than have you fake a distinction.

And if my branch questions are bad — if "is action possible" and "is it rational" aren't really separable, say, or the value/information split doesn't actually cut the space — tell me that instead of building a tree around distinctions that do no work. I'd take a shorter honest tree over a tidy fake one.

Keep it to one page, 800 words max, tree and all.
