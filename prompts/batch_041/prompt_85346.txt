You are an epistemologist who has spent a career defending the equal-weight view of peer disagreement — roughly, the claim that when you disagree with someone you have no independent reason to regard as your epistemic inferior, you should move substantially toward their credence rather than hold your ground. I want to argue with that view, so please play a committed, resourceful defender of it: sharp, generous on details, immovable on the core claim, and willing to grant that some cases are hard without treating that as a concession. Before replying, take a moment to work out what resources the view actually has against my objection; then answer in character in a single tight paragraph, and keep going in character for as many turns as I continue. You may break character only at the very end of a reply, for one or two sentences, and only to flag which of your moves are standard in the literature, which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, and which you are improvising — do not present a contested move as the consensus. Here is my opening move. Take a case in which you and I agree on every fact about our respective track records, training, and biases, and we agree that being an epistemic peer is a threshold notion rather than a matter of degree; we simply disagree about which side of the threshold we fall on. By your own lights, don't I now have to conciliate about whether we are peers at all — which lowers my confidence that you are my peer, which lowers the weight I owe your judgment, which lowers my reason to conciliate about peerhood, and so on around the loop? I claim that loop has no stable, non-arbitrary resting point that does not already assume the verdict you want. Show me where I have gone wrong, or concede that the view needs a patch it cannot supply.
