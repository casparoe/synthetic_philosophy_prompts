Thirty-five years in a cell biology lab and I only picked up philosophy after I retired, so if I mangle the vocabulary, thats on me. But this one's been eating at me and I can't find anyone to argue with about it.

We rewatched Blade Runner last weekend, right after I'd been reading up on the higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views where a mental state only counts as conscious if a further, higher-order state represents it. Rosenthal's version says that higher-up state is a thought; the Armstrong/Lycan line says it's more like an inner perception; and then there are the self-representational versions, Kriegel's and company, where the state sort of represents itself rather than needing a separate watcher upstairs. Fine, I follow the shape of it.

Heres my problem. Rachael. She's got a whole childhood in her head, and she doesn't just replay it — she takes it as hers, it moves her, she treats it as a memory of her own life. If that second-order business is what makes a first-order state conscious, then her apparent remembering sure looks conscious. Except the memory is an implant; there was no childhood. And that seems to be exactly the soft spot people poke at these theories — if the higher-order representation can be wrong, or can point at a first-order state that isn't really there, then what exactly is conscious, and conscious of what? I want you to take a real position on whether a higher-order theory can survive Rachael, not just say "interesting tension" and move on.

Second thing. The Voight-Kampff test. It's fishing for empathy — the flinch, the blush, the autonomic tell when you ask about hurting an animal. But if you believe the higher-order story, the mark of the mental is self-monitoring, a metacognitive capacity, not fellow-feeling. So the films test is aimed at a different faculty than the theory says matters. Does the movie know that? Is the whole thing a quiet argument that the theory is wrong and empathy is the real test after all, or is it just trading on "is it conscious" as mood music?

So read the film through these theories. Point me to where it actually dramatizes the live disputes — the misrepresentation problem, the question of what makes a state mine rather than just firing away in there — and where it just coasts. Dont reduce it to the "what makes us human" essay everyone writes. And tell me honestly where the film pushes back on the theory, and whether it ends up showing something the arguments miss. I know the Dick novel goes elsewhere; I care about the movie.
