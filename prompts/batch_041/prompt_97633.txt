I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of law and public policy and I keep getting tripped up by something. Everyone talks about "decriminalization," "depenalization," and "legalization" as if they were three notches on a single dial, but when you look at what actual places do, the picture is weirdly mixed. Portugal is the famous case: using drugs isn't a crime, you get routed to some kind of administrative panel (dissuasion commissions, I think they're called), but producing and selling the stuff stays squarely criminal. Then you have US states where cannabis is legal for adults, so there's now a legal, taxed, for-profit industry with ads and lobbyists, while at the federal level the same plant sits in the most restrictive scheduling bucket. And plenty of the same countries that do all this still put people in prison for a bag of somethign else entirely.

What I want to understand is what these arrangements actually presuppose, philosophically. Take the Portugal-style setup as the main case: what theory of the wrong of drug use — or of where the harm of drugs really lies — does it have to be running on, such that consumption is a matter for a health-and-dissuasion panel but supply is a matter for the criminal law? Is that a coherent position, or does it quietly presuppose that the user is a victim and the seller a predator, and if so is that a moral claim or just an empirical guess about who profits? Then contrast it with full commercial legalization, which seems to presuppose something close to the opposite: that the state can pursue public-health goals by letting a market exist and taxing and regulating it rather than forbidding it. And please disentangle those three words along the way, because I suspect the practice only looks incoherent becuase we're using them loosely — maybe "depenalization" is doing work that "decriminalization" isn't.

Don't just tell me what each country does; I can look that up. What I want is the implicit theory: what each arrangement strictly presupposes versus what it merely happens to fit with, where the practice is genuinely underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the exact same policy, and what would actually have to change if you swapped the presupposed view out for a Millian harm-principle view, or for a plain paternalist one. You're the philosopher here, so play expert, brainstorm a bit before you commit, and then give me a definite verdict — tell me what you think the decriminalize-use-but-punish-supply split presupposes and whether it survives scrutiny — rather than laying out five views and shrugging.
