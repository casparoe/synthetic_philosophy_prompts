I want a piece of deadpan institutional satire: a mock guidance note from a fictional standards body — call it the Office of Performative Standards — announcing that it will from now on issue binding rulings on whether public apologies are valid. Write roughly 600 words in the flat, self-important register of a government circular, with numbered sections and at least one fee schedule; the parties named in its rulings must be invented (a fictional airline, a fictional minister, a fictional football club), never real people or organisations.

The target is the modern appetite for auditing sincerity — the assumption that a public apology can be certified, scored, or struck down against a checklist — and, beneath that, the fantasy that speech acts can be regulated at all. The humour has to come from getting the speech-act apparatus right, so that someone who has read Austin laughs rather than winces. Concretely: keep constatives and performatives distinct; treat an apology as felicitous or infelicitous rather than true or false; and keep misfires (no conventional procedure, or the wrong one, or the wrong party, or the wrong circumstances) rigorously separate from abuses (the procedure goes through, but insincerely, or in breach of the commitment it carries). The bureau may make mistakes, but they must be principled mistakes it can defend from its own premises — not howlers. It must also keep the perlocutionary out of its validity test, insisting that it grades the act and not its effects (whether the apology lands, mollifies, or is accepted), while visibly failing to resist that temptation.

Let the invented machinery do real work: a rubric with named criteria, an appeal route for parties found to have misfired, a category of felicity debt for apologies held valid but defective, and a remedial re-uttering procedure. The reductio should be legible but never announced: by the end the reader should see that a bureau licensed to certify sincerity has committed a category error, and that the line between a defective apology and no apology is carrying all the weight the bureau will not admit to. Stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no narrator nudging the reader, no jokes about the bureau being absurd.
