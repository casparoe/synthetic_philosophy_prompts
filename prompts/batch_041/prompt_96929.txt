I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about technology and politics, and I don't know whether it has a name. The shape of it is this. A piece of infrastructure is built to solve a narrow engineering problem — a bridge, a ticketing system, a file format — and years later it turns out to have settled a question nobody remembers putting: who has to ask whose permission, who is visible to whom, which way of doing things counts as normal. What strikes me is that the technology does not forbid the other arrangement; someone who wanted to could still do it the other way. It is rather that one arrangement has become frictionless and every alternative has become costly, and the difference was never announced, so no one experienced the settling as a decision. Nor is it merely that we grew used to it — the asymmetry was there in the design from the beginning, before anyone had invested enough to be locked in. Is there an established name for this — a design that quietly makes one social arrangement cheap and its alternatives expensive, without requiring or forbidding anything? I suspect it sits near the literature on whether artifacts have politics, but the accounts I have met seem to concern technologies that require, or are incompatible with, a given social order, whereas what I have in mind is weaker than that and yet more than bare compatibility. If there is no name, I should like one, together with an account of what the thing is not: how it differs from ordinary path dependence, from a designer deliberately building a bias into a thing, and from the loose claim that tools shape habits. Tell me how confident you are in what you say, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
