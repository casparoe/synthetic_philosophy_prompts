I am a novelist at work on a multigenerational book whose plot turns on a chance encounter at a railway station in 1870; from that encounter two families' fortunes diverge, and I want the divergence to be intellectually honest rather than merely convenient. To that end, I need you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued past each other for a century: the strong path-dependence camp and the structural-inevitability camp. The first holds that historical outcomes are frequently selected from a set of possible equilibria by early, small, contingent events, and that increasing returns, complementarities, and coordination effects then make reversal costly, so the same broad outcome would not have arrived anyway, or not in the same form. The second holds that broad outcomes are overdetermined by robust structural factors -- geography, demography, technology, ecology, class -- so that even when a particular event differs, the same outcome arrives, perhaps later or under a different description; on this view path dependence is real but shallow.

I want the document they could both put their names to. It should have a numbered agreed-claims section and a disagreements table. In the agreed-claims section, give me what both sides can sign: what path dependence is and is not; what lock-in actually requires, as distinct from mere persistence; the difference between path dependence as a property of outcomes and as a property of processes; the difference between outcome and trajectory; the difference between the claim that the same outcome would have arrived anyway and the claim that it would have arrived on the same schedule; the role of counterfactual baselines and of equifinality; and the limits each view must concede. Do not give me a literature review, citations, or reading lists; I want the conceptual architecture, not the bibliography.

In the disagreements table, each row should be a crux. For each crux, give: (i) a neutral statement of the question; (ii) each side's preferred formulation, written so that side would accept the wording as its own; (iii) the case, argument, or evidence both sides agree would bear on it; and (iv) each side's prediction about that case. I suspect some cruxes are whether increasing returns are common or rare; whether counterfactual convergence is an empirical or a conceptual matter; whether the same outcome can be defined without smuggling in one side's preferred level of description; whether the relevant unit is the event, the institution, or the distribution; and whether the right counterfactual is the nearest possible world or a broad ensemble of worlds. But you should add, subtract, or refine as the logic requires.

To keep the collaboration from collapsing into generalities, I want you to use and test a small taxonomy I have been working with. Call it the hinge/ratchet/stream distinction. A hinge is a contingent event that selects among otherwise live equilibria; a ratchet is a mechanism that makes the selected equilibrium progressively harder to reverse; a stream is a structural force that pushes many possible starting points toward a common destination. Ask whether this taxonomy is genuinely useful, whether the two camps would parse it differently, and where it breaks down.

Then give me, for each side, what it would count as evidence against its own position, and say whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal. Finally, include one concrete test case both sides accept as relevant: two comparable port cities, one of which is destroyed by fire and rebuilt on a new grid while the other is not; over a century and a half, do their institutional and economic trajectories converge or remain distinct? Record each side's prediction in advance. The prose should be formal, exact, and free of rhetorical flourishes; the document should be something a historian and a social scientist could sign without embarrassment.
