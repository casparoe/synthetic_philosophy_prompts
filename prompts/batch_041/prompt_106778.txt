Exam question: State Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge and explain how they are meant to handle Gettier cases. Then explain why, on Nozick's account, knowledge is not closed under known entailment, using the brain-in-a-vat case. Finally, say whether you think the loss of closure is a price the theory can bear.

Student's answer: Nozick proposes that knowledge is a matter of tracking the truth. S knows that p if and only if: (1) p is true; (2) S believes that p; (3) if p were false, S would not believe that p; and (4) if p were true, S would believe that p. Condition (3) is the sensitivity condition, which later writers also call the safety condition; the two come to the same thing, namely that the belief could not easily have been mistaken. Nozick's point is that justification plus truth is not enough: a Gettier subject has a true, justified belief but is not tracking the truth, so the tracking conditions do the work the justified-true-belief analysis cannot.

The tracking conditions are not closed under known entailment. Suppose I know that I have hands, and I know that if I have hands then I am not a brain in a vat. It does not follow that I know I am not a brain in a vat. For consider whether my belief that I am not a brain in a vat is sensitive: if I were a brain in a vat, I would still believe that I was not one, since the vat would feed me exactly the experiences I now have. So the belief fails the condition, and by Nozick's lights I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, even though I do know that I have hands. Hence knowledge is not closed under known entailment.

It is worth noticing how far this extends. Because the brain-in-a-vat scenario is always available as a counterfactual possibility, the same reasoning shows that my belief that I have hands is not sensitive either: if I were a brain in a vat I would believe I had hands. Some critics therefore say that Nozick's theory leaves us with almost no knowledge of the external world, a heavy price to pay for blocking the skeptic.
