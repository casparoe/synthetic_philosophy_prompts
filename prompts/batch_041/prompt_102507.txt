I'm a second-year PhD student working on philosophy of mind and I keep getting tangled up in the debate about what emotions actually are. In our reading group we've been going back and forth between three families of view: that emotions are evaluative judgements (the sort of thing that can be true or false, and that you can in principle be argued out of), that they're perceptions of value (experiences that present something as fearsome or shameful, roughly the way vision presents something as red), and that they're feelings — felt bodily states that don't in themselves represent anything at all. I can recite the stock arguments for each, but I've realised I don't actually understand where the real fault lines are, so I'd like you to lay the whole thing out properly.

The bit I really want is the hard cases, because I suspect they're doing far more work than the textbook summaries admit. Take recalcitrant emotions: I know perfectly well the spider is harmless, I still feel the fear, and it doesn't budge when I revise the judgement — so if emotions just were judgements, what exactly is the thing that's persisting? And how does the perceptual view handle the fact that emotions, unlike ordinary perceptions, seem answerable to reasons and can be called fitting or unfitting — isn't there a gap between an emotion being warranted by the situation and an emotion being justified by what the person happens to believe? Then there's the case of emotions at fiction: I'm moved by Anna Karenina's fate, but I don't believe any of it is real, and I'm not sure any of the three views can say what's going on there without cheating.

Could you explain the issue from the ground up with those cases as the spine of it — what each of the three views is really committed to, where they genuinely come apart (as opposed to where they're just relabelling the same thing), which of the hard cases each can and can't accommodate, and whether any of them can give a decent account of emotional warrant without quietly collapsing into one of the others? I'm not after a literature review or a reading list — I can't get online anyway — I want the conceptual map and a clear-eyed verdict on which view is actually carrying its weight. One thing that naggs me: some people now say emotions aren't natural kinds at all but are constructed out of interoceptive prediction and cultural concepts, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a fourth option or just a clever way of refusing the question. If you can, say where that fits in.
