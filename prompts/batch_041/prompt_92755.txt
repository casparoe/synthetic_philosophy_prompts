I'm in my third year and I'm taking this seminar on ethics at the end of life that has basically taken over my whole brain this term. So here's my situation: my aunt has ALS, she's chosen medical aid in dying (it's legal where we live, she's been approved, the date is the 19th of next month), and she's decided that instead of a funeral she wants one last enormous party while she's still here to enjoy it — she calls it her 'send-off' and she is dead serious about nobody wearing black. She's asked me to give the main speech. So it is very much not a eulogy: she'll be in the front row eating cake and grading me with her eyebrows. There'll be about forty people there, and they do not agree with each other. Her brother is a palliative care doctor who has been openly against this from the start and thinks the rest of us are being railroaded into being complicit in a killing — him and my aunt havent really spoken in weeks. There are cousins who are Catholic and are coming out of love but are definately not at peace with any of it. Her hospice nurse, who she adores, will be there. Also her rowing club friends, who are mostly just here to drink and tell stories about her, which honestly might be the healthiest thing in the room. What I want is a speech that's warm and funny in the right places, that doesn't lie to anybody in the room — no 'she's in a better place', no pretending this isn't happening — but that also doesn't turn into a lecture on the ethics of assisted dying, which she would hate, or into me defending her against her brother from behind a microphone, which would be worse.

The thing I can't stop chewing on is a distinction from week 6 of my seminar. The prof split the request to die into two completely different animals: one is a prediction about the future (things will keep getting worse from here and I don't want to be around for it), and the other is a verdict on a life that's already finished, like a book that's been written and just needs the back cover closed. She thinks the second one is what's actually doing the moral work in most real cases, and that we blur the two together constantly, which is why the debates go nowhere. I'm pretty sure that's exactly what my aunt's brother is missing, and it's also what the cousins need, so I'd like the speech to be quietly built around that without ever putting it in those terms or sounding like I'm citing anything. Two more things: please make the speech treat everyone in the room as witnesses to her death rather than the authors of it, because several of them will be in the room when it happens and the brother's whole worry is that standing there makes you a killer — I want to say something true about that without arguing with him. And keep it to about five or seven minutes spoken, and don't put any quotes or poetry in it, I really don't want to get a line wrong in front of everyone.
