I run the forecast-review desk at a regional water authority: four analysts, none of them trained in dynamical systems, and we are the last stop before anything the authority says publicly about the future. We field fifteen to thirty requests a week, and they all have the same shape — someone needs to know something about the coming behaviour of a river, a reservoir, or the weather over it, and a decision with money or safety attached turns on the answer. The recurring ones are: the crest height at a particular gauging station five days out, to the nearest ten centimetres, because that number trips an evacuation threshold; the volume of snowmelt inflow over the next three weeks; the date the ice cover breaks up at a town downstream, which determines whether we pre-position crews; the first day of a cyanobacterial bloom in the reservoir; and the seasonal inflow total that the allocation decisions are built on. Our statute obliges us to publish a best-available forecast on fixed dates, so an output of "we don't know" is not available to us. The director who signs the statement has no technical background, and an analyst has to reach a verdict in under an hour, working mainly from our hindcast archive — twelve years of runs, twenty-member ensembles, two model upgrades inside that window.

Please give me a decision procedure they can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the specific tests to perform on the archive, the point at which the procedure stops and declares the question unanswerable at that lead time and tolerance, and what to publish in that case that is still useful to whoever has to decide. Have the procedure terminate in one of a small fixed set of verdicts the director can act on. As you build it, distinguish the different things that can make a forecast fail, and be explicit about which of them better measurement would fix and which it would not; about the difference between a system that is unpredictable and one that is undetermined; and about where the procedure should hand the call to the director because what remains is a question about how much risk to accept rather than about the dynamics. Say which of those distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped.

Then run it on three of the cases above — including the one where I expect the honest verdict to be one my director will hate — and mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Tell me where the procedure will misfire: what kind of failure it cannot see coming, and where it will produce false confidence. I don't need references or a reading list; I need the reasoning in plain terms. Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what specialists in this area genuinely disagree about — for instance, whether the practical horizon for day-to-day weather-type forecasts is a fundamental limit or an artifact of present models — and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
