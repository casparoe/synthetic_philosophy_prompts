I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have somehow ended up as the technical member of a small review panel that our company has asked to recommend how to spend a research budget — roughly three researcher-years spread over two years — on the conceptual foundations of mind uploading. The engineering is moving faster than our thinking about it: we have working prototypes of three different replacement protocols, and I keep finding that the questions I can answer with a profiler are not the ones that decide whether what we are building is something a person should be willing to undergo. What I want from you is not a survey of the field but a defended agenda: which open conceptual questions in this area most deserve those three researcher-years, in what order, and why.

To make the question concrete, here is what the three protocols actually do. In the first, a subject's biological neurons are replaced one at a time — on a fixed schedule, over about a year — by silicon units that we have verified are equivalent at the level of input-output behavior and spike timing; the subject is awake for some sessions and under general anesthesia for others. In the second, the same replacement is done in a single uninterrupted procedure lasting a few hours, with the biological tissue discarded as it is swapped out, so there is never a moment at which the subject is partly biological and partly silicon under any schedule we control. In the third, a full non-destructive scan is taken and instantiated on silicon while the original body stays alive and keeps running, so that for a period there are two systems claiming the same memories and dispositions. For each protocol I want to know which conceptual questions it turns on that we cannot settle by measurement, and which of those questions are upstream of the others — the ones where an answer would dissolve or reorder a whole cluster of downstream worries, as opposed to the ones that are interesting but would not change what we build, what we tell subjects, or what the law should do.

Please give me the ranking, the criteria you used, and the trade-offs among them. My own guess at criteria is something like how many other questions depend on a given one, how tractable it is for someone working with arguments rather than instruments, and how much a shift in the answer would change practice — but I would like those criteria defended rather than assumed. Tell me also what kind of argument or finding over the next two years would move a question up or down the list. I am much less interested in a list of topics than in the reasoning that puts one thing above another; and if you think some questions that get a lot of attention in these discussions are actually downstream or idle, say so and say why.
