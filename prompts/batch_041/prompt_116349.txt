I run a monthly journal club for psychiatry trainees and next month I am to introduce Libet's timing experiments to a group who know only the headline that the brain decides before we do. Do not explain the experiments to me; plan how I should present them in a single seventy-five-minute session.

I want the spine of the session to be three questions the group will inevitably run together — whether the intention was conscious when it arose, whether it was the earliest cause of the act, and whether the act was up to the agent — so set out the order in which the pieces must be introduced for those to come apart cleanly, and say what depends on what: what they need to understand about what a readiness potential is a measure of, and about the gap between the reported time of an urge and the reported time of a movement, before any of the numbers can carry weight.

The session should be carried not by the button-press but by a case of my own: someone halfway through signing their name on a form, still talking to the person beside them, who notices that the signature is already under way. Say what that case must have and must lack to do the work, and where it will mislead. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will surface — I expect the unconscious decided it, the intention was backdated and so was never mine, and this settles the free will question — with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. One member will insist that the readiness potential is an artefact of averaging many noisy trials; say where that objection belongs and how much of the lesson survives it. End with a single question I can put to the room to see whether they have grasped the difference between a precursor and a cause, and tell me what to refuse to cover at this level, and why. Please write in British English.
