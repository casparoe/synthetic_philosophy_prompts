Then there's one Quinean who thinks any question about "justifying" logic is a hangover from foundationalism and that the web of belief already answers it, and he says so in about ninety seconds every time. And finally a couple of people from the cognitive science side who study how humans actually reason; for them Carroll's tortoise dialogue is basically an early statement of the psychology of rule-following, and they think the philosophical framing is a distraction.

Heres the thesis I'm defending. The tortoise doesn't show that deduction is unjustified. It shows that the demand for a justification of inference, when it's framed as a demand for a premise that licenses the step, is incoherent — because any such premise would need a rule to be applied to it, so you never get out of the regress. So the regress isn't epistemic, it's constitutive: it tells you something about what following a rule is, not about what we're missing. The upshot is that "is deduction justified?" is a malformed question.

But I don't want to just announce that and sit down, because I think the malformation has a cost. If accepting a rule isn't the same as accepting a premise, then the inferentialists owe an account of what that difference is, and I don't think they have one that isn't just disposition-talk. And disposition-talk is exactly what the cog-sci people will cheerfully accept and the proof theorists will find intolerable. That's my soft spot and I know it.
