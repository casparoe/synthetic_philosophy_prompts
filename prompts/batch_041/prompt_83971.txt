I have never studied philosophy formally, but I have been chewing on one picture for a couple of years and I have written up what I think it shows. I would like your comments on the argument — not a reading list, and not a rewrite. Here is the piece.

Everyone knows Escher's Drawing Hands: two hands on a sheet of paper, each drawing the other. The picture is unsettling because it shows a loop of cause and effect with no starting point. Hofstadter, in his book about Escher and Bach, dissolves the puzzle by pointing at a level that is not in the picture at all — the sheet, and the act of drawing. He calls that the inviolate level, and calls the hands a tangled hierarchy, because a level below determines a level above which determines the level below.

I want to push this further, possibly further than it can go. My claim is that the inviolate level is not a peculiarity of pictures. It is a general condition on any arrangement in which something determines what determines it, and it has a consequence I take to be substantive: nothing inside such an arrangement can occupy its own inviolate level. Any attempt either fails outright or succeeds only by pushing the inviolate level up one step, where it sits just as unreachable as before. Self-grounding is impossible. There is always a floor, and the floor is never something the system can stand on and hold at the same time.

Call the inviolate level the floor. Three cases I take to show the pattern:

1. A constitution containing its own amendment clause. The clause looks like the system writing its own rules, but the clause's authority is not produced by the clause; it is presupposed by whoever invokes it, and if the clause is used to abolish itself, what replaces it is a new constitution whose authority is again presupposed rather than produced.
2. A compiler that compiles itself. The bootstrap is real, but it always runs on something the bootstrapping did not produce — an earlier compiler, a hand-written core, or the machine.
3. A mind revising its own standards of reasoning. It can revise them, and the revised standards can be better. But the revision is carried out under the old standards, so the new standards are never certified by themselves.

Here is my doubt, which is why I want comments. Either the floor is a real thing, something that must exist and does work, or it is a name for the fact that thinking has to start somewhere — in which case I have said nothing and the word is decoration. And there is a third possibility I cannot rule out: that the whole pattern belongs to pictures and not to the world. Escher's hands are impossible because they are drawn; two actual hands each painting the other would be no paradox at all, only two painters. If that is right, the inviolate level is a fact about representations, and my three cases are cases only because I insist on describing them as pictures of themselves.

What I would like from you:

- Where this is wrong or confused, including any term I have used loosely or any distinction I have blurred.
- Whether the central claim is substantive, or whether it collapses into the observation that things have to start somewhere.
- The strongest counterexample, and whether it defeats the claim or merely relocates the floor.
- Whether the picture-versus-world doubt at the end kills the extension to the three cases, or whether the analogy is doing genuine work.
- Which of the three cases is weakest, and whether the third is an instance of the pattern or a counterexample to it — I genuinely cannot tell, and I suspect that answer decides the essay.
- If the piece had to be cut to one paragraph, which paragraph should survive.
