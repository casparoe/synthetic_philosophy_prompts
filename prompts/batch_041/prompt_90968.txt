I lead a small team that translates our written compliance policies into executable checks. The checks run nightly against transaction and case data, and anything they flag lands in a human review queue; a false positive costs queue capacity and a false negative costs us regulatory exposure. I convert roughly forty rules a quarter, I get about two hours per rule, and my only colleagues are three engineers and one lawyer. I have spent enough evenings in the blogosphere's heaps-and-bald-men threads to be convinced that the interesting fact about vague predicates is not that we cannot see where the line falls, but that some of them may have no line to fall — and I now need that distinction to pay rent rather than just be interesting.

Write me a procedure for handling a vague predicate in a rule we are about to compile: 'promptly', 'reasonable', 'material', 'substantially similar', 'without undue delay'. I want it in order — the tests to run, what counts as passing or failing each, where the procedure returns a no, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person. It should keep apart things we currently conflate: genuine vagueness, ambiguity, context-dependence, and a term that is perfectly precise but simply does not reach a case the drafters never imagined. It should say when a bright-line threshold is a faithful sharpening of the predicate and when it is a silent amendment of the rule, and give me a way to measure that — some test over the range of thresholds a fair-minded reader could accept, and what flip rate across our own past adjudications means we may compile, versus what means we may not. Where a step presupposes one of the two main theories rather than the other — epistemicism, on which there is a line we merely cannot locate, versus the supervaluationist picture, on which a statement is true on every admissible sharpening, or on none, and the remainder is left to be settled — say so and fork there, because the sign-off requirements differ: one exit is filling in an unknown and needs evidence and a revisit date, the other is a policy choice and needs the policy owner and legal. Flag which steps are judgments wearing procedural clothing, and note that the text itself is not cheap for us to reopen.

Then apply it to three rules: a duty to notify compliance 'promptly' upon discovery of a reportable incident; a prohibition on 'material' misstatements in a client disclosure; and a data-classification rule whose term must be applied to a technology the drafters did not anticipate. For each, give me the exit the procedure selects, the artifacts it leaves behind, and who signs. Include at least one where the honest answer is that we cannot automate the rule and must keep a human queue — the outcome my program exists to reduce — and do not soften it. Close with where the procedure will misfire: multidimensional predicates, cases where the admissible sharpenings cannot be enumerated without begging the question, and anything else it cannot deliver, followed by a one-page version my engineers can keep at their desks.
