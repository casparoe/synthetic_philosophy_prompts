I write about science and ideas for a magazine and I'm doing a piece on the know-how debate -- the one where one side says knowing how to do something is really just knowing a bunch of facts (plus having the skill on top), and the other side says practical knowledge is its own kind of thing that you can't cash out in facts. Every version of this piece I've read leans on the same two examples and I want to freshen it up, so I made my own list and I need you to sort it.

Here's the list:

1. Riding a bike
2. Flipping a pancake at the right moment, based on the bubbles and the sound the batter's making
3. Doing 47 x 83 in your head
4. Improvising a solo over chord changes you've never seen before
5. Swimming
6. Reading a room well enough to land a joke on one specific person at a party
7. Tying a bowline
8. Catching a fly ball in the outfield
9. Reciting the first 50 digits of pi
10. Getting a two-year-old to fall asleep

Rank these in tiers (S through D is fine, or just a 1-10 score) by how hard they are for the "it's all facts underneath" side to explain. Top tier = they really have to work for it. Bottom tier = they can just shrug and say yeah, that's propositional knowledge, no one's going to fight you on it.

For each one, a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. Then, for whatever you put at the top, give me the strongest thing the facts side can say back and be honest about what it costs them -- because if the same move handles the bike and the pancake and the joke and the pi digits, I don't think there's a real distinction left to write about, and that would be the actual story. If you think one of these cases is carrying more weight than the others, say which and why.

And my editor keeps asking me whats the stakes, so give me one sentence at the end I can actually put in the piece.
