I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I use some of these terms loosely, but I've spent the last couple of years reading around machine ethics — the question of whether and how you would put ethical reasoning into a system that has to make decisions on its own — and there's one argument in that area that I keep running into and that seems completely stuck. On one side are the people who want to give the machine an explicit moral theory it can apply: a set of rules, a utility function, a deontic or defeasible logic, something a human can read, inspect, and verify. On the other side are the people who say that project is doomed because you can never anticipate the situations the system will meet, and who want it to acquire its ethics from cases and feedback instead, the way current learned systems are shaped by training rather than by a rulebook. In the discussions I've followed, both camps concede a great deal — the rule people will admit their systems go brittle at the edges, the learning people will admit theirs can't be audited or guaranteed to do anything in particular — and yet nobody moves on the main question, which is roughly which approach a serious attempt should start from. A colleague of mine who works on verification says you would never ship a system whose behavior you can't reason about; another, who works on learning, says you would never ship a system that only does what you happened to think of in advance. Both of those sound obviously true to me, which is part of why I can't tell what the fight is actually about.

That's what I'd like you to diagnose — not to declare a winner, but to say what kind of disagreement this is. Is it a factual dispute that merely looks conceptual, say about how far formal verification can really scale versus how well learned behavior really generalizes, where the two sides simply hold different empirical bets? Is it a value disagreement in disguise, about what we actually want from a machine that acts in the world — reliability and accountability on one hand, flexibility and coverage on the other — such that the technical arguments are downstream of a preference neither side has stated? Or is it two different questions being answered under one heading? My own hypothesis, and I'd like you to test it rather than just agree with me, is that the camps don't mean the same thing by ethical reasoning: one means deriving a verdict from a principle, the other means having a disposition that was shaped by cases, and so they may be building two different artifacts — a machine that behaves ethically versus a machine that reasons ethically — while arguing as if it were one. If that's right, say so and show me where the equivocation does its work; if it's wrong, tell me what the real crux is.

Finally, given your diagnosis, I want an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress here. Would it take a formal result, a change in what the field is asking, a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, or would it take something neither side currently has — and if the honest answer is that the debate is ill-posed or that no progress should be expected, I'd rather hear that than a diplomatic summary.
