I've been TA-ing a machine learning course this term and something about the whole enterprise has started to bug me. There's this ritual we teach and then practise without ever defending: you split your data into a training set and a held-out test set (or do k-fold cross-validation), you tune on the training set, you report the held-out number as your estimate of how the model will do on genuinely new data, and benchmark leaderboards and paper tables are built on exactly that move. Everyone in the room can also recite the no-free-lunch results in the abstract -- that averaged over all possible target functions every learning algorithm performs identically, so there's no universally best method unless you smuggle in an assumption about the sort of problems you'll actually meet -- and yet the very same people will confidently say that some architecture is just better, or that a method which wins a benchmark has demonstrated a general capability. I can't tell if that's a contradiction or just two different claims wearing the same words.

So what I want from you is this. Treat the practice -- train/test splitting, cross-validation, treating held-out performance as evidence of future performance, ranking methods on benchmarks -- as an object and tell me what philosophical commitments it presupposes. It smells to me like it needs some uniformity-of-nature or exchangeability assumption, a claim that the future will resemble the past in the respects that matter, which is roughly the thing Hume said can't be justified by reason or by experience without circularity; and maybe it also needs a non-uniform prior over possible worlds, since the no-free-lunch results only bite when you average over everything equally. But I don't want you to just hand me the textbook line. Reconstruct the implicit theory, seperate what the practice strictly *needs* from what it merely fits comfortably with, say whether its parts presuppose consistent things (do we secretly accept and deny no-free-lunch at once?), and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival views would license it equally well. Then tell me what would actually have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for a rival -- say a thoroughly Bayesian picture where the uniformity assumption is made explicit as a prior and updated, or a Popperian falsificationist one on which a good held-out score confirms nothing at all. Please write it in British English and keep the jargon in check, I'm a philosopher crashing a stats department, not the other way round.
