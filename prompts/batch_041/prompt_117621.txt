Write a dialogue between two philosophers about a case neither of them has discussed before. The first is an epistemicist about vagueness: she holds that every vague predicate in fact has a precise, fully determinate extension, that borderline cases are cases in which we cannot know which side of the line a thing falls on, and that vagueness is therefore a limitation on our knowledge rather than an indeterminacy in the world. The second is a supervaluationist: he holds that a vague predicate admits many equally admissible ways of being made precise, that a sentence is true when it comes out true on all of them and false when it comes out false on all of them, and that borderline cases are ones where the world itself fails to settle the matter, so that some sentences are neither true nor false.

The case is this. A regional water authority administers a rule stating that a lake is eutrophic, and therefore subject to mandatory restoration, when its phosphorus concentration is high enough. Ecologists have assembled a series of lakes whose phosphorus concentrations rise smoothly from a lake no one would call eutrophic to one everyone would; the concentrations themselves are known only to within a small margin of error. The authority's counsel asks whether the rule can be applied at all without an arbitrary cutoff.

Have the two philosophers argue through, in order: (1) whether the sentence there is some exact concentration at which a lake becomes eutrophic is true, false, or neither, and how each view answers differently, including the supervaluationist's apparent commitment to calling the general existential claim true while no particular concentration is determinately the one; (2) whether the monotonicity principle that no lake with a higher phosphorus concentration than a eutrophic lake fails to be eutrophic holds on each view, and what work that principle does; (3) whether the imprecision of the measurements is merely an epistemic limitation on both views or whether it forces the supervaluationist to distinguish two kinds of indeterminacy, and whether that distinction is stable; (4) whether higher-order vagueness, that is, borderline cases of the borderline, creates trouble for either position; and (5) what each would advise the authority to do, and whether their practical recommendations actually diverge or only their metaphysics does.

Both interlocutors should be equally competent and neither should be a strawman. Each must state the strongest objection to his or her own position and respond to the other's strongest objection. Neither may appeal to authority or to what any named philosopher has written; all argument must proceed from the case itself. Close the dialogue with the single point on which the two agree and the single point on which their disagreement is sharpest. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
