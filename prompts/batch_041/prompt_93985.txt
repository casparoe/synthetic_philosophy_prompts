I am the lead epidemiologist for the causal-attribution unit at a state occupational health agency. When a worker develops a disease that may have come from a workplace exposure, my unit issues a written determination that the exposure did or did not cause the disease. Those determinations go to a compensation board, they get appealed, and about forty times a year one of them decides whether a household keeps its medical coverage and wage replacement. I have a lawyer, an industrial hygienist, and a rotating panel of outside physicians to work with, our exposure measurements are almost always rough and retrospective, we cannot run anything resembling an experiment, and we have to get the determination out in about six weeks. My difficulty is that the philosophers we occasionally consult keep handing me two different pictures of what I am supposed to be establishing. On one picture, I am asking whether this worker's disease would have occurred anyway if the exposure had been absent, which is a difference-making question about one case. On the other picture, I am asking whether the exposure is the sort of thing that makes a difference when you intervene on it within a system of variables, which is supposed to be the better-behaved notion but which seems to change its answer depending on how finely the variables are carved, so that I can flip a determination by redrawing my own diagram. Nobody has told me how to decide between the two pictures, or what to do when they disagree, and I suspect that a great deal of what my unit currently does is one picture wearing the other one's clothes.

Please turn this into a procedure my unit can actually run. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps my hygienist can complete alone and which require the physician panel, and an explicit list of the points at which the procedure stops and hands the file to a human being because the remaining question is one of legal or moral standards rather than of causation. Build the procedure around a small vocabulary the lawyer can follow, with every term defined in plain words, and give it three separate verdicts that it must return independently for each file: that the exposure made a difference in this particular case; that the exposure was part of the mechanism that produced this outcome; and that the exposure raises the probability of the outcome relative to a stated reference class. For each verdict, say what it can and cannot establish, and give me a rule for what to do when the three come apart, including which of them my determination should rest on and which are merely corroborating. Two further requirements, because I have been burned before. The procedure must contain a step where we write down the alternative way of carving up the variables, exposures, and outcomes for the file and record whether that alternative carving would have reversed the verdict, so that the sensitivity is on the record rather than in someone's head. And it must be honest, at each stage, about which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the form.

Then run the procedure on four files we have actually had, and tell me where it strains. The first is a worker with two exposures at levels each of which is independently sufficient for his disease, where he would have been harmed by either alone. The second is a worker whose disease was going to develop eventually regardless, but whose onset appears to have been moved forward by roughly a decade by the exposure. The third is a worker harmed by a ventilation system that was never installed, so the relevant intervention is the removal of a protection rather than the addition of a hazard. The fourth is a worker with a common genetic susceptibility for whom the exposure appears to have mattered only in combination with that susceptibility. I will admit my own leanings: my instinct in the second file is to find no causation, and my instinct in the first is to find causation even though I cannot say which exposure did it, and I suspect the procedure will not let me have both. If it does not, say so plainly and tell me what I would have to give up. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do at all, and which files should never be routed through it in the first place. Write the procedure and the worked cases in American English, and keep the whole thing usable by someone who has never read a page of philosophy.
