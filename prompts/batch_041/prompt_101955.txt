I am assembling a teaching taxonomy for a graduate seminar on the meta-philosophical significance of cross-cultural disagreement in philosophical intuition, and I need you to build the taxonomy itself. The guiding question is: when competent respondents from different cultural backgrounds give incompatible intuitive verdicts about the same philosophical case, what follows — for the case at issue, for the method of cases, and for the assumption that there is a fact of the matter? The instrument should sort positions by what their holders say about that question, not by the labels they favour, so no branch may simply ask whether someone endorses relativism, experimental philosophy, or the reliability of intuition.

Several distinctions matter for my purposes and should do structural work in the tree. First, keep apart three kinds of cross-cultural difference that are usually run together: verdict variation (the same case elicits incompatible answers), confidence variation (the same answer offered with different degrees of conviction or willingness to generalize), and diagnostic variation (respondents agree on the verdict but disagree about which features of the case are doing the work, so they part company on nearby cases). Second, keep apart input-side and output-side explanations of variation: differences in how the case is perceived or imagined versus differences in the rule that maps case features onto a verdict. Third, treat the grouping variable itself as up for grabs at one node — whether the salient partition is culture at all, as opposed to formal philosophical training, the language in which the case is presented, or individual cognitive style — and whether within-group variation swamps between-group variation.

The following leaves are required. One on which variation is conceded but judged irrelevant because philosophical claims are not answerable by intuition in the first place. One on which the finding is treated as a performance error correctable by better probing rather than a competence difference. One on which the variation is genuine but local to the case, licensing a culture-indexed redescription of the case rather than abandonment of the method. One on which intuitions are retained as evidence but treated as defeasible seemings whose evidential weight is calibrated to the reliability of the population sampled. One on which the method is abandoned as a source of a priori evidence but retained as a source of data about concepts. And one that treats the dispute as misconceived because the philosophical case was never a probe of a shared competence. Any further leaves the logical space requires are welcome; give each leaf a descriptive name and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Build the tree from the space of answers first and then locate positions in it, rather than listing names and inventing questions to separate them afterwards. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and distinguish these diagnoses rather than lumping them together. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question a respondent could answer without already knowing the destination, say explicitly that the tree fails to separate them, and state what further distinction would be needed.

Ten to fourteen leaves, no more than four levels deep. Do not provide citations or reading lists; I will attach those myself. Assume the reader knows the standard cases but has not seen them grouped this way.
