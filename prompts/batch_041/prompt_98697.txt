I am preparing a handout for a reading group on time and free will, and I want a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of the literature. Write a decision tree that takes a reader from the unremarkable thought that tomorrow's sea battle either will or will not happen to a named position on future contingents. Each branch node must be one of the load-bearing assumptions rather than a label: whether a future-tensed contingent sentence is already true or false; if bivalence is denied, whether the sentence is indeterminate across the ways the future might still go or true only relative to a later context of assessment; whether the fixity of the past reaches forward to truths that were always about the future; whether a true prediction's truth entails that the predicted event could not have been otherwise. Phrase every node as a question answerable without knowing where it leads. Leaves should be the positions themselves — truth-value gap, supervaluationist indeterminacy, assessment-sensitive truth, the Ockhamist soft-fact move, and plain logical fatalism — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and 'unnamed' where a combination of answers is coherent but undefended. Build the cases the reader is asked about into the nodes: a sealed written prediction, a prophecy, a prediction that depends on someone's later deliberation, and the ancient argument that if your recovery is fated, calling a physician is pointless — the last should land on a leaf distinct from fatalism proper. Flag any leaves your tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why. End with a final line beginning 'Summary:' giving the number of leaves and naming any it cannot distinguish.
