I retired some years ago from marine invertebrate biology and have spent the time since working my way into philosophy of biology — enthusiastically, and not always competently. I would value your help with an argument I overheard at dinner last month. The two people involved, Ilse and Rafael, both understand these matters far better than I do; I took no side then and take none now.

The argument concerned a hypothetical case I had put to them. A certain sessile animal forms flat colonies on rock. A colony begins when two spores from unrelated parents settle side by side and fuse; every cell in the colony descends from one of those two founders, and the colony grows as a mosaic of the two lineages. Whichever lineage arrives first takes the interior, where its cells feed and reproduce; the other is confined to the rim, where its cells cannot feed properly and eventually rupture, releasing a toxin that repels the colony's principal predator — the rupturing cell dies. Which lineage gets which position is settled by arrival order alone, and each lineage is about as likely as the other to arrive first. Lineages do differ in whether they rupture when at the rim: a mutant lineage that never ruptures lives off what the interior brings in. Colonies with such a mutant at the rim are eaten roughly three times as often as normal ones, but within those colonies the mutant lineage leaves far more surviving cells than a normal rim lineage does.

Ilse's position was that this is not group-level selection at all. A colony, she said, is not a reproducing unit but a patch of habitat in which two lineages compete; the rim strategy persists only because the same lineage occupies the interior elsewhere, so what is selected is a strategy conditional on position, which pays across positions. Rafael replied that this is bookkeeping, not biology: a conditional strategy can pay only because colonies with functioning rim lineages survive longer, and no account of the trait's maintenance can avoid that fact. Ilse answered that between-colony differences are the consequence of the strategy, not its cause, and that a selection account should name the entities that vary and are inherited — here, cells and lineages — rather than the vessels they build. Rafael then said that if she counts only entities that replicate, she must conclude that the mutant lineage is fitter than the normal one, which is true inside a colony and false in the world, and that this discrepancy is precisely what a level of selection is meant to capture. Ilse replied that "fitter in the world" is just a sum over cells, which she is happy to compute without positing a colony-level process.

Please adjudicate this exchange as it stands rather than writing a general essay on the units of selection; I already have more reading lists than I can use. Organise your answer under these four headings, in this order:

1. "The case, restated" — no more than 120 words, giving only the facts that do work in the dispute.
2. "Adjudication, point by point" — roughly 600 words, taking the four exchanges in turn and saying who is right about what, and flagging any point at which the argument turns on something neither of them has settled.
3. "What both parties are missing" — about 250 words, including any respect in which the disagreement may be verbal.
4. "Overall verdict" — no more than 150 words: which of the two is closer to the truth about this case, and what would settle the matter.
