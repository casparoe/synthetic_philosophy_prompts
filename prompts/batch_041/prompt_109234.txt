I'm in a small reading group that's slowly working through the philosophy of language stuff -- descriptions, names, presupposition, that cluster -- and last week someone brought up the planet Vulcan as an example and we ended up arguing for like an hour, so now I want a proper writeup of the whole thing. The episode, quickly, so we're on the same page: in the 1840s Le Verrier took the irregularities in Uranus's orbit and worked out where an unknown perturbing planet would have to be, and Galle looked at that spot and found Neptune, which was a big triumph for Newtonian mechanics. Le Verrier then turned to Mercury, whose perihelion precession is a bit more than Newtonian theory predicts (something like 43 arcseconds a century), and made the same kind of move: something unseen inside Mercury's orbit, a planet or maybe a belt of small bodies, must be tugging on it. In 1859 an amateur astronomer wrote to him claiming he'd watched a small dark dot cross the sun's disc that March; Le Verrier took the train out to interrogate the man, believed him, announced the planet as discovered at last, named it Vulcan, and worked out an orbit for it. For decades after, people searched, a few more sightings got claimed and quietly dropped, and then in 1915 general relativity accounted for the whole precession as a spacetime-curvature effect and the planet just stopped being needed. Nobody retracted anything; it faded.

What I want is that episode run through Russell's theory of descriptions, in both directions -- what the theory makes visible about the episode, and what the episode does to the theory. So take a sentence like "the planet disturbing Mercury's perihelion lies between Mercury and the sun," asserted in 1860. On Russell's analysis that's a quantified claim with existence and uniqueness clauses built into its content, so when the searches come up empty the sentence is simply false, and the astronomer who asserted it had a false belief, no different in kind from any other false belief. The Strawson-style line is that existence isn't part of what's said at all but presupposed, so the truth question doesn't even arise and "Vulcan is a planet" isn't false so much as out of bounds. I want a verdict on which of those does better justice to what the astronomers were actually doing -- and I suspect it's messier than either. The scope business is where I think the case gets interesting: "Le Verrier believed the planet perturbing Mercury existed" looks true on one reading and false on the other, and I want both readings spelled out and an argument for which is the one we'd naturally give, plus how "Vulcan" can function as a name at all on Russell's view given that genuine names have to denote, and whether the standard escape routes (it's really a disguised description; it's a name whose reference failed) leave anything unexplained about the fact that it was coined, printed in tables, used in predictions, and then retired without ceremony.

Here's the thing I keep pushing in the group, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than treat it as a quibble. I've been calling the difference between tracking and inaugurating descriptions. A tracking description picks out something you already have independent reason to think is there -- after 1846, "the planet that perturbs Uranus" is tracking Neptune; you use it to say things about an object whose existence is settled. An inaugurating description does the opposite: its whole job is to put a new object into the domain, so the existence claim isn't background, it's the point -- "the planet inside Mercury's orbit" was doing that, and so was "the planet perturbing Uranus" before 1846, actually. The two behave differently under failure, which I think is the tell. If a tracking description fails, you were wrong about the object. If an inaugurating description fails to find its object, nothing much follows, because a failed search is only fatal once someone offers a rival explanation of the anomaly it was posited to explain -- which is exactly what Einstein eventually did, and note that he didn't find a planet, he changed the theory of gravity. Russell's machinery, as far as I can tell, flattens that distinction: both are quantified claims, both false when unsatisfied, same logical form, and the fact that Neptune's description was satisfied while Vulcan's wasn't is a fact about the world rather than about the sentences. So: does the theory have the resources to mark the difference -- through Donnellan, through Kripke's causal picture of names, through scope or presupposition -- or is this something the semantics genuinely can't carry and has to hand off to pragmatics or to a theory of inquiry? And then the second half, which matters as much to me: what does the episode show about the theory -- a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? Tell me where Russell's analysis is simply silent rather than forcing it to have an opinion, and don't bother with reading recommendations or citations, I want the argument.
