So a friend of mine in our reading group has been pushing an argument against the whole modern dichotomy-of-control thing and I cant tell if its clever or just confused. Background, roughly: Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by splitting everything into what's up to us and what isn't — on one side our judgments, impulses, desires, aversions; on the other the body, property, reputation, offices. Arrian wrote it all down, and the modern Stoicism crowd (Irvine especially, the trichotomy guy) has made this the centerpiece, though I'm told the phrase "dichotomy of control" is basically Irvine's coinage and isn't something you'd find in the Greek.

My friend's argument:

(1) A thing is up to me only if it is not the causal product of anything outside me.
(2) My judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions are all causal products of my character, my upbringing, my mood, my body — none of which are up to me.
(3) So by (1) and (2), nothing in the first category of the dichotomy is actually up to me.
(4) Epictetus is a determinist anyway — Chrysippus's fate, the cylinder that rolls when pushed because of its own shape — so he of all people can't have thought up-to-me meant uncaused.
(5) Therefore the Stoics must mean something else by up to me, and whatever that something else is, it isn't a fact about control.
(6) If it isn't a fact about control, the dichotomy is doing no explanatory work; it's a redescription of what we already call our agency, dressed up as a discovery about the world.
(7) And the therapeutic payoff — stop being upset about what you can't control, work on what you can — evaporates, because the line between the two sides was never a real line.

I genuinely can't tell which step is the soft one. I'd rather keep (4) and (5) than (1) if I have to choose; I actually think the determinism point is right and the modern revival tends to forget it. But then I don't know what's left of the dichotomy.

So: which step goes, and what does it cost me? In particular, if I drop (1), what is the Stoic actually claiming when he says my judgment is up to me but my reputation isn't — is that a claim about causation, about what's attributable to me, about what falls within my prohairesis, or something else — and can Irvine's trichotomy (complete control / no control / partial control) survive once we spell that out? Also, is there a weaker version of (1) that's true, and does the argument still run with it?
