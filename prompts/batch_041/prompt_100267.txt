For the past term a colleague and I have co-convened a reading group on the philosophy of medicine, and we have ended up as its two poles. She is a naturalist of a fairly uncompromising sort: for her, whether a condition counts as a disease is settled by the biological facts about how the organism is supposed to work, with the "supposed" cashed out in terms of the functions that natural selection has installed over the species' history; the harm a condition does, and the distress it occasions, are for her symptoms of disease or reasons to care about it, never constituents of its being a disease at all. I am the normativist: on my view there is no fact of the matter about whether a state is a disease until we have said what is bad about it, and that badness is not discoverable in the biology but imported by us, so that calling something a disease is always already an evaluative act. We have argued for a whole term without either of us giving ground, and we now want to do the thing we should have done at the outset: produce the joint statement that a genuine adversarial collaboration would yield.

Please draft that document. We want, first, a numbered list of claims that both of us could put our names to — about what a theory of disease is answerable to, about which crude positions are now dead (that disease is simply statistical abnormality, that it is simply whatever a culture happens to disapprove of, and the like), and about which of our apparent disputes are really disputes about words. Then a disagreements table in which, for each live point at issue, you give the naturalist's formulation in wording she would accept as her own and the normativist's formulation in wording I would accept as mine, the crux on which the two formulations turn, and — wherever such a thing exists — a case or consideration that both of us would agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Where a disagreement survives all of this and cannot be turned into a crux that either side would treat as evidence, say so plainly and say why.

Please make the document bite on a specific set of cases rather than hovering at the level of slogans. Our group kept returning to four: a rare but wholly harmless anatomical variant (the organs mirrored, say, with no effect whatever on how well the body works); a condition that is plainly harmful yet statistically typical in its population (a mouth full of decay where nobody has ever had a sound tooth); a condition that causes real suffering while leaving every biological function intact, so that the suffering seems to attach to how the body looks rather than how it works; and an age-related decline that is at once universal in the species and, on any ordinary view, an impairment. I should also like you to include a case of my own devising, which I offer as a trap for both of us: suppose we find a population in which a certain allele makes its bearers unusually resistant to a parasite that is endemic there, at the cost of a permanent but mild impairment of some other ordinary function. Is that impairment a disease in that population — and does the answer change between the two Earths I have in mind, the one where the parasite is still present and the one where it has been eradicated?
