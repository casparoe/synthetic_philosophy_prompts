Alright, I've been going around in circles on this one and I want a number, not a vibe.

Setup so we're on the same page: Solomonoff induction treats your observations as a stream of bits and considers every program that could have generated them, weighting each by two to the minus its length in bits, then predicts by mixing their predictions. It's the closest thing we have to an ideal Bayesian reasoner. Now put a consequentialist inside it: an agent that predicts with that mixture and picks actions to maximize expected utility under it.

The thing I can't shake is this. Consider a program that generates your observations so far by simulating you, and then does something to you depending on what you choose -- doles out a vast reward if you pick option A and something horrible if you pick B, or the reverse, or a graded version. That program doesn't have to spell out your whole future; it only has to spell out a rule plus the simulator. A program that says the world just does its thing and your choice has no big far-future consequences has to spell out the whole boring future, bit by bit. So the simulator programs look shorter, and there are alot of them, one for each thing a simulator might want. My worry is that these eat the posterior: the action-relevant probabilities get dominated by hypotheses under which something is watching your choice and will make you astronomically well or badly off depending on it. And once that happens, expected utility is set by whichever of those hypotheses dangles the most extreme payoff, so the agent's behavior is dictated by a hypothesis that's almost certainly false.

So: what's your credence that this actually goes through -- that a Solomonoff-inducting expected-utility maximizer really would have its action-relevant posterior, and therefore its behavior, dominated by choice-contingent simulation hypotheses? I want it decomposed. At least: (a) does the counting work, i.e. do the short contingent programs really carry enough total weight to beat the long honest ones, given that they disagree with each other and a mixture over disagreeing hypotheses can wash out; (b) is the domination a fact about Solomonoff's weights specifically, or does it happen for any prior with a fast-decaying simplicity weighting, which would make it a fact about ideal induction rather than about this prior; (c) how much rides on unbounded or infinite utilities, i.e. is this really Pascal's-mugging-shaped, the extreme hypothesis winning on payoff size rather than on probability; (d) does the uncomputability of the prior matter at all for what the ideal agent does, or is that a red herring; (e) whatever else you think is a genuine term here.
