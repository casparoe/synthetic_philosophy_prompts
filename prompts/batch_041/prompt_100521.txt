I have been reading Augustine on lying — the treatise he came to think obscure, intricate, and altogether troublesome — and I keep returning to the way he fixes the definition. His line is that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind": lying is a divergence between what the speaker thinks and what she gives out, so that a person can utter a falsehood without lying (if she believes it) and can lie while uttering a truth (if she believes it false). The fault, he says, is the desire to deceive in the very act of giving out one's mind, whether or not the deception takes. What I cannot settle is what this makes of a case a friend and I keep arguing about: someone is hiding from a person who means her harm, and when you are asked where she is you answer, truthfully, "I have not seen her since breakfast," knowing and intending that this be taken to mean she is not in the house — which is where she is. You have said nothing you believe false, and your words match your mind exactly; but you have arranged for a false belief in the person asking, and you arranged it on purpose. My friend calls this "deception by truthful design" and insists it is every bit as culpable as a plain lie, and worse in one respect, since the speaker can later take shelter behind the literal truth.

The proposition I want a number on is this: a statement whose content the speaker believes true, made with the foresight and intention that the hearer be misled by it, is in itself a lesser wrong than a lie told for the same purpose. My own credence is about 0.25 and my friend's is about 0.75, and I suspect we are not really disagreeing about the case so much as about what does the moral work in lying. I take the load-bearing sub-questions to be (a) whether lying is correctly defined by the divergence between mind and utterance, or instead by the intention to deceive, or by the speaker's belief that what she asserts is false; (b) whether the wrong-making feature of lying is that divergence — the misuse of a truth-directed practice, the corruption of the speaker's own mind — or the imposition of a false belief on a hearer entitled to your epistemic best; and (c) whether moral culpability should track the definitional category at all, or whether the fact that something is not a lie is simply beside the point once we have seen the intention and the effect. If you think one of those three is not actually load-bearing, or that I have left out the term that is, say so and substitute it.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction and no closing summary — each one or two sentences: (1) your headline credence in the proposition, stating how the sub-numbers below combine into it; (2) your probability for the definitional sub-question, with a clause of justification; (3) the same for the wrong-making-feature sub-question; (4) the same for whether culpability tracks the definition; (5) which term your headline number is most sensitive to, which term most plausibly accounts for the gap between my 0.25 and my friend's 0.75, and what argument or piece of evidence would move you most.
