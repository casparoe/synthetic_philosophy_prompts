I'm a high school student writing an essay on historical materialism, and I've run into a problem I can't settle on my own. A great deal of recent history and science writing treats non-human causes as the real makers of history: an epidemic, a volcanic eruption, or a lasting shift in climate decides the outcome of a war, a conquest, or the collapse of a civilization, so that the decisive agents turn out to be microbes, weather, and geology rather than classes and economic forces. Marx died before germ theory became central to historical explanation, and the idea of nature as itself an agent of history is newer still, so this is a question he never faced in these terms. If that whole body of work were put in front of him, what would his position be? Please separate the parts of the geographic and epidemiological determinist thesis he could accept from the parts his own commitments would force him to reject, and then give me a specific account, rather than a vague assertion that both matter, of how he would describe the relation between natural causes and human, especially class, causes. In particular, where would he draw the line between a natural condition that merely shapes history and a natural cause that is itself a historical agent? If you think his framework can absorb these findings without strain, explain how; if it can't, say exactly which part of the framework would have to give way.
