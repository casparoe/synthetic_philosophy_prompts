I'm a journalist — I write about science and ideas for a living — and I've been circling a story for months that I can't get into a shape. Overwork, basically: karoshi in Japan, the overtime caps that came in after the death at Dentsu that finally shamed everyone into legislating, Premium Friday (the leave-at-3pm-on-the-last-Friday-of-the-month thing the government and Keidanren tried to get going, which went the way of most such things), and then the French 35-hour week, which everyone treats as the success story even though they've spent twenty years quietly gnawing holes in it.

What I want is an essay, not a briefing note. Here's the argument I keep failing to write, and I want you to write it and land somewhere.

There are two ways to hold a right to leisure, and people slide between them constantly. The first says leisure is residual: you're entitled to whatever time work hasn't already claimed. On that picture the real right is a right to a limit on work, and its held against employers and enforced from outside — by a statute, a union, a ministry. You don't have a claim to time; you have a claim that someone else's claim be capped. The second says leisure is a rival claim: it stands on its own footing and can beat work's demands, so the 35-hour week is the recognition of something you already had rather than a concession someone handed down.

What nags at me is that almost every real victory for leisure gets sold in the residual idiom. France's shorter week was sold as job-sharing — a labour-market lever, hours as a way of spreading the work around. Japan's version was sold as consumption stimulus: go home at three and spend money, leisure as an economic instrument. Even Weber's Protestant, with his calling, treats unclaimed time as the dangerous stuff — and once the belief drains out, the casing stays hard anyway. (The "iron cage" is Talcott Parsons's rendering of something closer to a shell as hard as steel.)

So: which picture do the actual cases presuppose, and which one can be defended? And who holds the right — the worker, the collective, or the state that both caps the hours and wants the growth? Give me an arguement that reaches a verdict and defends it. I'd much rather have a wrong verdict argued properly than a survey that lays out the considerations and shrugs. Weber, France and Japan all need to be in there; I don't care what order.

Don't send me a reading list, I'll chase the sources myself. British English, please.
