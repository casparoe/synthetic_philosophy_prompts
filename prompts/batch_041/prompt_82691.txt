I would like a single case, built to order, for a specific purpose: to test whether the practical dimension of knowledge attaches to the knowing subject's overall situation or instead to the particular practical question she is using the proposition to answer.

Two views are in play, and I will state them in my own terms so that the case can be assessed against them without appeal to any literature.

Ascriber contextualism: the word knows is context-sensitive, and the epistemic standard that a knowledge attribution must meet is fixed by the practical situation of the person making the attribution.

Subject-sensitive invariantism (pragmatic encroachment): knows has a single, context-invariant content, but whether a particular subject knows depends on how much is at stake for that subject; the higher the stakes, the stronger the evidence she needs.

Call a case threshold-splitting when a single subject, at a single time, with a single body of evidence and a single credence in a single proposition, both knows and does not know that proposition.

Your task is to construct a threshold-splitting case. Constraints:

1. It must be realistic. No science fiction, no demons or evil scientists, no fake barns, no lottery tickets, no brains in vats. The setting should be one an ordinary adult could plausibly be in this month.

2. The subject's evidence, credence, and the reliability of the method by which she formed the belief must be identical with respect to both practical questions. Nothing about the genesis of the belief may differ between them.

3. The two practical questions must be genuinely independent and simultaneously live. It may not be that one question is simply the other with more at stake along the same dimension, and neither question may be about the subject's own epistemic state.

4. The subject must be fully rational and must not be self-deceived or confused about her evidence. She need not notice that the two questions impose different thresholds.

5. Keep the attributor out of it: no third party's stakes, and no conversational standard set by someone else, may be what drives the difference.

6. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

Then analyze what you have built:

(a) State the proposition, the two practical questions, the evidentiary threshold each imposes, and the verdict each yields. Make the difference in threshold concrete rather than gestural.

(b) Say whether the split is a contradiction for subject-sensitive invariantism. If it is not, say what a question-relative version of that view would look like, and how it differs from ascriber contextualism. Say which of the two views, if either, can accommodate the case without modification.

(c) Identify the single feature of the case that does the work of splitting the verdicts.

(d) Say what happens if that feature is removed, and in particular whether the case then collapses into the familiar pattern in which one person's stakes simply rise.

(e) Explain how a defender of subject-sensitive invariantism would try to argue that the case is impossible or that the split is illusory, and assess that move.

Please answer in American English.
