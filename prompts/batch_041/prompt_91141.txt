"Leibniz's Law states that if two things are identical then they have exactly the same properties, and conversely that if two things have exactly the same properties then they are identical. The second claim — that there cannot be two things differing only numerically — is the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, and it is simply the contrapositive of the first, so anyone who grants the first has already granted the second. The principle is a strong one: it tells us that no two objects can be exact duplicates, since duplicates would share every property. In the twentieth century, however, the principle was shown to be false by a now-famous thought experiment. Imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, identical in size, shape, colour, temperature, and material composition, one at each end of an otherwise empty space. They share every property, yet there are plainly two of them rather than one. So no collection of properties can ever distinguish two objects; only their bare numerical distinctness can. This is why most contemporary metaphysicians regard 'being this very thing' as an irreducible feature of an object, something no description could ever capture."
