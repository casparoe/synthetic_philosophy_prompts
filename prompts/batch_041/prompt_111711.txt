I'm a risk analyst at a mid-sized specialty insurer that underwrites industrial machinery, and my job is to produce the single number that goes into a policy: the probability of a specified failure mode per thousand operating hours for a given machine. Underwriters price against it, and our regulator can ask us to defend it years later. The difficulty is that the inputs come from three places that, as far as I can tell, do not mean the same thing by "probability." There is our own claims history for the machine class, which is a frequency in a reference class somebody chose; there is the elicitation we run with consulting engineers, which yields a number that reflects their confidence and nothing else; and there is the physics-of-failure model, which predicts a rate from the mechanism itself and is presented to me as though it were a frequency, when it seems to be a claim about what the machine is disposed to do under stated conditions. At the moment we average the three, which I am fairly sure is indefensible, but I cannot get anyone to tell me what would be defensible instead. I know the standard positions — long-run frequency, coherent degree of belief, and the propensity reading — well enough to be suspicious of my own procedure, but not well enough to write a better one, so please do not spend the answer adjudicating which interpretation is correct; I need the operating manual.

Please write a procedure I can actually run on a file: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which inputs I am licensed to combine with which, and on what assumption; what has to be recorded in the audit note for each input; and, the part I care about most, the point at which the procedure must stop and declare that the number being requested is not the kind of thing it can deliver. Two house constraints you have to work within. Our reserving standards require that anything reported as a rate be accompanied by a stated reference class and a stated reason for thinking the class is the right one. And our audit committee will not accept a range in place of a point estimate, so uncertainty has to be pushed into the point estimate and then explained in words in the note. I am one analyst with about a day per file, and the only person who signs off is our chief actuary, who is a competent frequentist and will not be moved by anything she reads as philosophy.

Then run the procedure on three files. The first is a mature pump model with roughly 40,000 machine-hours and three failures on record, where the physics model predicts a rate about two orders of magnitude lower than the claims data suggests. The second is a newly designed bearing with zero observed failures in service, where the engineers are uniformly confident and the physics model returns a clean rate. The third concerns a failure mode that can occur only under an operating condition the fleet has never yet encountered: the claims data is silent, the engineers disagree wildly, and the physics model says the mechanism is real but rare. For each file I want the verdict the procedure yields, the audit note I would write, and a plain statement of which steps were judgment dressed up as arithmetic. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I will not want to hear; I would rather learn it from you than from the regulator. Keep the procedure itself as a numbered list of steps with the questions in order, end each worked file with a line of exactly the form "Verdict: <LABEL> — <one clause>", where LABEL comes from a fixed set of labels you define and use consistently across all three files, and make the very last line of your answer "Bottom line: <one sentence>".
