Ok so I'm not a philosopher, I did one psych module in college and that's basically it, but there's a thing that's been bugging me for weeks and I'd rather see it as a story than as an argument. You know the little demo where a dot flashes on the left in red, then a dot flashes on the right in green, and if the timing is right you'd swear on your life that you watched it slide across the gap and turn from red to green somewhere in the middle? The problem is that the middle never happened, nothing was ever on the screen between the two dots, and worse, the colour it supposedly turned "halfway" couldn't have been decided until the second flash, which hadn't happened yet at the moment you saw it. So there are two ways this could go. Either your brain stages the whole thing after the fact and then hands it to you as if it were live and in order (the show trial is held in secret and you're only ever told the verdict, so as far as you're concerned there was never a trial at all), or you genuinely did have a jerky red-then-green experience and your memory of it got quietly rewritten a moment later to say "smooth" (the trial happened, you were in the room, someone just went back and edited the transcript). I've seen those two options nicknamed the Orwellian one and the Stalinesque one, I'm fairly sure a philosopher came up with the nicknames, but whatever they're called the thing that gets me is that from the inside, and from every test I can actually think of, they come out completely identical, and that bothers me way more than it probably should.

Anyway. What I want is a short story, maybe 1200-1500 words, where that difference actually matters to somebody and they have to make a call. Here's the setting I keep picturing, use it or change it: a small regulatory office, some near-future version of a medical device authority, and an auditor (call her Ruth, or anything you like) who has to decide whether a clinic's new gadget counts as anaesthesia or as memory-editing. The gadget is used during a nasty but survivable procedure, and it's cheap and popular and the patients all say afterwards that they felt nothing. That's the whole problem. If it's anaesthesia, the existing consent form covers it and the clinic is fine. If it's memory-editing, the pain was experienced, in full, by every one of those people, and then erased, and the form they signed doesn't cover that at all, and neither does the law, and a few hundred people have been through it. Ruth can't get at the experience itself, obviously, she only has the patients' word and the device's logs, and both are consistent with either story. I'd like her to find something that looks like it settles it, and then realise it doesn't. The tech doesn't need to be realistic, invent whatever, just don't have it be magic.

Things I'd ask for: nobody in the story explains the two options to anybody else, no clever character delivers the thesis in a paragraph, and no narrator swoops in at the end to tell us what it all meant. Let it come out through what Ruth does, what she can and can't check, what the patients say when she pushes them, and what she writes in her report. It should feel like a workplace story with a real deadline and a boss, not a philosophy seminar with characters attached. Don't put any quotes or citations in it either, I don't want it leaning on real papers or real scientists. After the story, give me a short structured summary so I can keep track of what I just read, as a JSON object with these fields: "title" (the story's title, a string), "setting" (one line, a string), "stakes" (what turns on Ruth's decision, a string), "evidence_pointing_orwellian" and "evidence_pointing_stalinesque" (each a short array of 2-3 strings, in the story's own terms rather than technical ones), "why_neither_settles_it" (a string), and "what_the_ending_leaves_open" (a string).
