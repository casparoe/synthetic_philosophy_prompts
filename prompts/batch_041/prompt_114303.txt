I'm in a small reading group on practical rationality, and we have spent three sessions circling Kavka's toxin puzzle — the one where an eccentric billionaire offers you a large sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon, where the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but leave you otherwise unharmed, and where the money is already in your account before the deadline for drinking arrives, so you remain free to change your mind afterwards. I've been asked to write up my considered position so the others can attack it, and I want an audit before I circulate it. Here is what I hold:

1. Practical rationality is one standard, not two. The same notion of rationality that assesses what you do assesses the states of mind that produce what you do; I do not accept a separate rationality of the will that can come apart from the rationality of the act.
2. You cannot intend to do what you are certain you will not do. Intending to drink the toxin requires believing there is some chance you will drink it.
3. So in the case as stated I could never collect. I cannot intend to drink, so the money is never mine; and if the money were somehow in my account, I would not drink. My verdict is that I behave sensibly and the puzzle is not about me.
4. All the same, I think the case is a genuine discovery rather than a curiosity. The money is a reason for me — it would be perverse to say the offer gives me no reason at all — but it is a reason I can neither act on nor intend on. What interests me is that kind of reason, not the billionaire.
5. I do not think we should admire the person who can form the intention, if there is such a person. An intention one knows one will not honour is either self-deception or not an intention at all; the trick, where it works, is a defect dressed up as resolve. And the impossibility here looks to me the same shape as the impossibility of sincerely promising something for money: not that the state is hard to produce, but that the reward enters at the wrong place.
6. The same structure is the everyday structure of nuclear deterrence. A threat deters only if it is genuinely intended, and a posture that depends on cultivating intentions to do what one would never do is therefore both morally and rationally repellent. I take that as an indictment of the posture rather than a vindication of intention-cultivation.
7. I have no patience for the idea that there is a distinctive kind of rationality in sticking to a plan after it has stopped being the best plan. That is just someone ignoring a reason that now applies; there is no separate faculty of resolve. (One of my group insists the opposite: that once you have the intention, drinking is rational because you are now committed. I think that is a confusion, but I am not sure which one.)

What I want is not a survey and not citations; I am working from the case itself. Tell me which of these commitments actually pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do, order the real tensions by how much repair work each would take, and for each say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me elsewhere. Then tell me whether the diagnosis changes if the puzzle is a puzzle about rationality rather than about character, and which single commitment I should abandon. Be blunt about where I am helping myself; I would rather be told that one of these is doing no work than be told something reassuring about all seven.
