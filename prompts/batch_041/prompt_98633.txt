A colleague who works in communications has proposed what he calls a single clean line for the ethics of deception: if the false belief is carried by what one literally said, that is a lie; if the audience instead had to do some inferential work — filling in an implicature, drawing a conclusion that was merely left open — then one has only misled, which is a different and lighter matter. He adds that what a sentence implicates is not fixed by its words alone but varies with context and can be cancelled, so the same sentence might amount to a lie to one listener and to mere misleading to another. I have been treating this as settled, and it appears to yield two conclusions that I would like you to confirm.

First, since the prohibition most people accept is a prohibition on lying, and misleading is not lying, an artfully incomplete or carefully hedged statement should always be the morally safer option: the speaker who merely misleads has remained on the permissible side of the line, and any false belief the audience ends up holding is, in an important sense, something they assembled for themselves. Second, since the categorization itself shifts with context and the audience supplies the inferential work, the wrongness of a misleading statement must be audience-relative — misleading a sophisticated listener, who is more responsible for the inference drawn, should be less wrong than misleading a naive one, and there may be no fact of the matter about whether the speaker did anything wrong until we know who was listening.

Are both conclusions correct? I suspect I am conflating where the moral line falls with where the linguistic one falls, so please correct the reasoning if it does not go through, and state clearly what does and does not follow from the fact that misleading is not lying.
