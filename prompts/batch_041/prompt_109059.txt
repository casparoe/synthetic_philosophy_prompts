Three years ago I started a novel about a field biologist who spends a decade watching cuckoo chicks, and somewhere in the middle of the research I got derailed into an argument I can't get out of. Roughly: whether you can say evolution has reasons even when nobody's doing any reasoning. My biologist thinks the cuckoo chick shoves its foster siblings out of the nest *because* that's what gets it fed -- that's the reason, that's why the behaviour is there, and the fact that the chick knows nothing, believes nothing and intends nothing is beside the point. Her colleague says no, that's animism in a lab coat; reasons live in heads, and where there's no head, "reason" is a metaphor you should drop the second you leave the pub. A third friend, who's read more than either of them, says the vocabulary is fine as a prediction trick -- it tells you what the animal will do next -- but it explains nothing, it just redescribes the adaptation in more flattering language. I've now read enough of this fight to notice that all three have heard every version of each other's move and none of them has budged in decades, and I want to know what kind of fight it actually is. So: is this a verbal dispute, where one side means "there is a rationale for this behaviour" and the other means "somebody reasoned," and they're each right about different things? Is it a hidden empirical question about what's happening in the chick's nervous system? Is it two questions wearing one name -- one about the structure of explanation, one about the mind of the bird? Or is it a real disagreement about whether "it was selected for doing X" can ever explain why something does X, with one side quietly assuming all explanation has to be mechanistic? My own guess, and please treat it as a guess, is that it's mostly verbal at the bottom, but that under the verbal part there's a small stubborn disagreement about whether selection-based reasons do any explanatory work a description of the mechanism doesn't already do -- I want to know if that's right.

Here's the thing that made me want to ask. I can get the easy cases to line up: a seedling bending toward light (nobody's home, but you can still say what the bending is for) and a person taking an umbrella (clearly a reason in a head). It's the middle that breaks. Take a bird that has never done it before and was never selected for it, and does something that happens to save its life -- the behaviour is good for it, there was a reason for it in the "that was the smart move" sense, but nothing selected for it and, say, the bird had no idea what it was doing. Or take a machine-learning system trained until it does something clever, where the "reason" for the behaviour is a fact about the objective and the environment, there's no selection, no designer's intention at that level of detail, and definately nobody reasoning. Or, nastier, a case where the rationale and the mechanism come apart entirely: an animal does the adaptive thing for reasons that have nothing to do with why it's adaptive -- it's good for it, but that isn't why it's doing it, and the mechanism story makes the "reason" look like a coincidence. If the camps in my first paragraph disagree about what to say about those three cases, I want to know whether that's the same disagreement or a new one.

So what I'm after is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right: tell me what kind of disagreement this is, working from what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction that dissolves the verbal part, an experiment or result about animal cognition that would settle an empirical part, or an argument that no progress is coming and why not. Keep it honest about the line between what people who work on this actually agree on and what's genuinely up for grabs; I don't want a contested position dressed up as the consensus. And no reading list or citations please, I'm never going to chase them down.
