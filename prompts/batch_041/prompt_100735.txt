I am a novelist, and I am writing a book set inside the present-day benefits system — the waiting rooms, the reassessments, the sanction letters, the telephone queues — and I have reached the point where I must decide what moral weather the story will carry. Rather than invent a conscience for the book out of nothing, I would like to reconstruct one. Please predict, as scrupulously as the evidence permits, what Charles Dickens would have made of the welfare state as it now operates.

The features I have in mind are these: means-tested benefits whose receipt is made conditional on conduct; a regime of sanctions for those who fail the conditions; repeated reassessments of the sick and disabled to establish whether they might be pressed into work; fraud detected less by an inspector's eye than by an algorithm; caps and limits that fall hardest on larger families; and a public vocabulary that quietly sorts claimants into the deserving and the undeserving. None of this lay within Dickens's experience, so the prediction will have to be built from what he wrote, whom he pitied, whom he satirized, and what he plainly believed about how people are reformed or ruined.

I am less interested in a tribute than in a genuine verdict, tensions included. Was he, at bottom, a believer in conditionality — that help should be earned and idleness checked — or would he have recognized the modern sanctions regime as the old workhouse logic under a new name? He distrusted organized philanthropy and put his faith in private benevolence; how does that disposition square with a system administered by the state and enforced by penalty? His sympathy for the poor ran alongside a real impatience with those who would not stir themselves; where does that place him on the modern line between the deserving and the undeserving, and would he draw that line where the present system draws it? And his method was caricature, sentiment, and redemption: what becomes of that method when the system has no visible villain, when the cruelty is distributed through forms, targets, and thresholds, and the line is drawn by no one in particular?

Tell me where he would part company with present-day progressive opinion, and where with conservative opinion, and name one question on which he would be forced into a position he would have disliked. Then show me the book he would write: two or three scenes, with the characters and the choices inside them, that would carry his judgment — the interview, the appeal, the moment of sanction — and say what a character formed by his outlook would do that a merely sympathetic character would not.

Finally, be candid about the seams: mark clearly where you are predicting from his settled commitments and where you are projecting, where the evidence runs out, and where a later reader might reasonably disagree. I do not want a reading list or references — I cannot check them — only the reasoning, as far as it will honestly go.
