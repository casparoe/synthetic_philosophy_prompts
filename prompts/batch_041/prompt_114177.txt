I have been reading the decision-theory threads on the blogs and forums for a long time, and I have lost confidence in my grip on the counterfactual mugging. I can recite the verdict; I am no longer sure I know which feature of the case licenses it and which features are set dressing. I want a construction that separates the two, and I want it built rather than quoted, so please do not name theorists, cite posts, or gesture at the literature. Everything should be derivable from the case as you set it out.

Build a matched pair of decision problems, Case A and Case B, for a single agent, with the following verdict profile. In Case A, causal decision theory refuses and evidential decision theory refuses, while a policy-level or updateless theory pays. In Case B, causal decision theory refuses and evidential decision theory pays, while the policy-level theory refuses. The mirroring is the whole point: reasoning from the act and reasoning from the disposition should each come out right in one case and wrong in the other, with causal reasoning refusing throughout.

Case A must be mugging-shaped. The act, considered on its own, is pointless: it buys nothing in the world the agent actually inhabits. The disposition to perform it, however, has been cashed in, or is being cashed in elsewhere, because some other party's conduct towards the agent is fixed by how the agent's own procedure runs. Case B must have the shape that circulates under the name XOR blackmail: the demand arrives only where the extortionist's forecast of the agent's act disagrees with an exogenous fact the agent cannot see, so that the act cannot move that fact at all, and yet the arrival of the demand is evidence about it.

Requirements. The two cases should share their cast and their surface as far as you can manage — one agent, one other party, one modest payment, one large unobserved stake — and differ in a single structural respect, which you should then name. Draw the settings from ordinary institutional life (a subscription, a letting agency, an insurance clerk, a club, a dead benefactor's estate) rather than from science fiction. No perfect predictors and no simulations of the agent: whatever forecasting appears must be no better than a shrewd, well-informed person could manage. No cosmic arbiters, no oracles, no exotic physics. The sums should be ones a person could actually face. One page per case, prose, no diagrams.

Then, for each case, derive rather than assert the verdict of each of the three theories, with the reasoning; if the profile does not come out as I have specified, say so plainly rather than patching the case until it does. Finish with the single feature of each case that is doing the work, and what each case degenerates into once that feature is removed; whether the paying verdict in Case A survives when the unobserved branch is replaced by a real second instance of the agent's procedure, dealt with separately, and what the replacement changes if it does not survive; and how a defender of whichever theory fares worst would attack the construction.
