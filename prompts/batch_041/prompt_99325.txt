Alright, bit of a long one, and I apolgize in advance for the rambling. I'm a retired soil chemist -- 34 years in a lab, mostly nitrogen cycling, and since I hung up the pipette I've been sitting in on a political philosophy reading group at the local university, which has been humbling in the best way. Last week we got onto the old puzzle about voting: the chance that any single ballot changes the outcome of a national election is astronomically small, so on a straight expected-utility reading dragging yourself to the polling station looks like a waste of an afternoon, and yet almost nobody thinks a person who votes is being irrational, and a good few of us think there's something a bit off about the healthy adult who just shrugs and stays home. I got into a slightly heated argument about it that I'm still chewing on over breakfast, and halfway through I realized I don't actually know what I'm demanding of an answer. So before I read another word on it, I want the spec sheet.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the rationality of voting has to deliver. Which cases it has to get right, and I mean the whole zoo of them -- the nationwide election where my causal footprint is essentially nil, the five-person committee or the tied local race where a single vote is genuinely pivotal, the person who votes purely because it makes her feel like a participant, the abstainer who is simply indifferent to who wins, the voter who casts a ballot mainly to cancel out her brother-in-law's. Which distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse -- between the rationality of the act and the rationality of the disposition or habit or rule, between explaining why people turn out and justifying it, between instrumental and expressive and civic-duty stories, between the question of my own vote and the question of what everyone like me should do. Which results it has to stay consistent with -- that turnout isn't zero, that it drops as the cost and hassle rise, that voting has the shape of a public good with a free-rider problem built in. Give me the consideration behind each requirement, tell me which of them are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, and flag any group you suspect can't be satisfied all at once -- my own hunch, and I'd like you to check it, is that you can't simultaneously insist the standard is purely instrumental expected utility, respect how negligible one vote's causal impact really is, and still come out saying voting is rational or required, so if that trio is jointly unsatisfiable I want to see it spelled out. And tell me which single requirement does the most damage, i.e. rules out the most popular answers on its own. If a couple of the usual contenders -- the expected-utility line, the expressive line, the rule-or-ethical line -- fall out quickly against the list, a sentence or two each is plenty; I'll do the real scorecard myself once I know what I'm grading against.
