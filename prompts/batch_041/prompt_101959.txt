I am a teaching assistant for a first-year graduate seminar in historical theory, and next week I have seventy-five minutes to introduce the idea that germs, climate, and volcanoes can function as historical actors. The students are intelligent but have mostly encountered history as the study of human decisions, and several of them are already suspicious that any talk of non-human agency is either a metaphor or a slide into environmental determinism. A colleague in environmental history and I have been arguing about whether the phrase is a genuine theoretical advance or just a rhetorical upgrade, and I want the session to give students the tools to take a side. I do not want you to explain the topic to me; I want a teaching plan. Assume I know the material and need to know how to sequence it. What has to come first for the question even to be well formed, what depends on what, and where exactly the line between cause and actor has to be drawn before students can see why the phrase is controversial rather than merely fashionable?

To make the session something they cannot simply repeat from a textbook, I want to build it around a distinction I have been calling causal weight versus causal voice. Causal weight is the counterfactual difference an entity makes to an outcome—how much the outcome would change if the entity were absent or altered. Causal voice is whether we narrate that entity as an agent: as something that does, wants, intends, suffers, or responds. My thought is that environmental determinism often comes from collapsing the second into the first—from assuming that a large causal weight licenses a voice—and that the historical actor language is useful precisely when it keeps the two apart. Plan the explanation around that distinction: the order in which the pieces must be introduced, the one carrying example that should anchor the whole session and what it must have and lack (I am inclined toward the Tambora eruption and the year without a summer, but tell me if that example will do the work or if I need a different kind of case), the misconceptions students will predictably bring or form—especially that if something has effects it is an agent, that agency is just a metaphor for causality, and that any environmental explanation is determinism—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. End with the check for understanding that would show me whether they can use the distinction rather than merely recite it, and tell me what I should explicitly leave out at this level and why. Please write in American English.
