I am the rapporteur for a two-day adversarial collaboration hosted by a research institute that funds work on long-run social dynamics, and I need the joint statement that the two participants could actually put their names to. The first is a committed cliodynamician: someone who takes structural-demographic theory seriously as a research programme, who treats secular cycles, elite overproduction, popular immiseration and state fiscal distress as quantities that can be measured and modelled, who works with large coded historical databases of the Seshat type, and who holds that a model fitted to several centuries of European, Chinese and Near Eastern history earns some right to say what is coming next. The second is a committed humanistic historian who is perfectly willing to count prices, wages, heights and parish registers, but denies that this licenses prediction: on this view macrohistorical regularities are largely artefacts of coding decisions, cases are not exchangeable, mechanisms are not stationary across contexts, and the causally interesting work happens at the level of contingency, meaning and local context. They have argued past each other for two days, and I want the document they should have written together instead. Please approach this as an experienced research rapporteur with a philosopher's eye for when a dispute is real and when it is only verbal, and think carefully before you write.

Begin with a short brainstorming preamble, clearly labelled as such, in which you list candidate sub-questions and then discard the ones that are not genuine cruxes — I would much rather you throw away three plausible-looking candidates than pad the final list. Then produce the joint statement itself. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, and I want these to be substantive rather than diplomatic throat-clearing: what both accept about the shape of the evidence, about the distinction between retrodiction and genuine out-of-sample prediction, about measurement error and the construction of a codebook, about what a failed forecast would and would not show, and about which of their remaining disagreements are really about standards of explanation rather than about the facts. Second, a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the cliodynamician's position worded so that a cliodynamician would sign it, the historian's position worded so that the historian would sign it, the observation or argument that both agree bears on the crux, and each side's prediction about what that observation will show. I expect several of the cruxes to sit somewhere near the following: whether constructs such as elite overproduction can be measured invariantly across societies or only within a single historical tradition; whether the nested-periodicity picture — a long secular cycle of a few centuries with shorter, roughly generational instability oscillations sitting inside it — is a discovery about the world or a fitting artefact of flexible model specification; whether pre-registration and out-of-sample testing can settle anything when the variables themselves were selected after the outcomes were known; whether a model that forecasts only aggregate rates of instability, never particular events, has delivered what its proponents promised; and whether the humanistic historian's appeal to contingency is a rival explanation or a refusal to specify one. For each crux, name a concrete test, coding exercise, data-holdout or historical comparison that both participants would accept as probative before they know the result — a blind coding exercise on a polity neither has worked on, a transfer test in which a model fitted to one world region is applied to another, a pre-registered forecast with thresholds fixed in advance, or a structured comparison of two cases matched on the model's variables but divergent in outcome — and record each side's ex ante prediction about it. Where you judge that a disagreement is merely verbal, or that the two positions become compatible once the explanandum is specified precisely, say so explicitly and say which reformulation dissolves it. Do not include reading lists, citations or bibliographies; both participants know the literature and I need the argument, not the references. Use British spelling throughout, and write as though the document were going to be published over both their signatures.
