I'm a philosophy grad student and I've somehow ended up running the "weird results" session in our logic reading group this term — the one where we do a bit of Gödel and then something that makes the mathematicians in the room shift in their seats. This time it's Skolem's paradox. The audience has had one course in model theory, so they know what a structure is, what it is for a sentence to be true in one, they've seen compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem stated, but their set theory stops at "ZFC is a first-order theory and everything is a set." Don't explain the paradox to me — plan how I should teach it. What has to be in place before it even looks like a paradox, in what order, and why each piece needs the one before it; because my worry is I'll say "every consistent first-order theory has a countable model, so ZFC does too" and they'll all just shrug, since they haven't yet registered that "the model is countable" and "the model's set of reals is countable" are two claims living in two different places.

The example I keep reaching for is the countable model M of ZFC and the thing M calls the power set of omega: M says it's uncountable, we on the outside say M's version of it is only countable, and the resolution is that the witnessing bijection isn't an element of M. But I suspect that's exactly the move I want to not lean on, because the second they hear "the bijection isn't in the model" they'll file the whole thing as a cheap trick and switch off. So — should that be the carrying example at all, or is there a better one, and if it is, what do I have to put in place before and after so that the "not in M" step lands as a genuine resolution rather than a debater's dodge? I want them to feel the residue: the Skolemite thought that "uncountable" might have no absolute meaning at all, set against the standard reply that it plainly does and the paradox only looks threatening once you blur inside from outside. And is it worth warming up on non-standard models of arithmetic first, since its the same non-categoricity in a friendlier setting, or does that just hand them a second thing to be muddled about?

Give me the misconceptions in the order they'll actually arise, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off, and one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they've got the internal/external distinction rather than just memorised the slogan. Also tell me what to deliberately skip in a two-hour session, and say so out loud.
