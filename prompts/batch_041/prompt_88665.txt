I'm a novelist and I've somehow agreed to run a 90-minute session at a writers' retreat next month on the Buddhist idea of anatta -- no-self -- for a room of literary-fiction types who think they want it because they're stuck writing characters as fixed little bundles of traits. Don't explain anatta to me, plan how I should explain it. What has to land first, before "there is no self" stops sounding like a moody slogan or a personal insult, and why that piece and not the obvious one? What's the single example that should carry the whole thing -- I keep reaching for the chariot and the river and I suspect both are wrong for this room, so tell me what the carrying case needs to have and lack, and why those two fail here. Which misconceptions will they walk in with or cook up halfway through: no-self equals nihilism, no-self just means the self changes, it's the same thing as Parfit's fission cases, if there's no self then nothing matters -- and at exactly which point in the sequence do I head each of those off? I also want it to read as a real position and not a vibe, so I probably need to give them the fork in how the teaching gets read: as a straight metaphysical claim that there's no self, versus as a practical instruction for loosening your grip, plus the related move where persons are perfectly fine as conventions but don't survive the analysis into what's ultimately real. Where does that fork belong in the order -- before the example or after? And what should I openly refuse to cover in 90 minutes, saying so out loud? Don't send me a reading list, I won't use it. Last thing: give me one question to ask them at the end that shows whether they've actually got it, rather than just been pleasantly destabilised.
