I write about science and ideas, and I am at work on an essay about how we assign responsibility to nations for wars. I want a careful analysis of the Fischer controversy—the dispute set off by Fritz Fischer's claim that Germany bore the primary responsibility for the First World War—through the lens of a theory of collective responsibility. You may take the salient facts as given: Fischer's archival case that German aims were expansionist and that the German government bore a large share of the blame, and the fierce resistance his thesis met from historians who heard in it a revival of war-guilt attributions. What I want is the conceptual machinery. What does it mean to hold a nation responsible for a war? Does the claim require treating the nation as a moral agent, or can it be reduced to the responsibilities of individuals—statesmen, generals, voters, taxpayers? Bring to bear the distinction between causal responsibility and moral responsibility, the distinction between political guilt and moral guilt, the problem of many hands, and the idea that a collectivity can bear a responsibility that none of its members bears individually. Then run the analysis in the other direction. What does the Fischer controversy show about the theory of collective responsibility? Does it vindicate the thought that nations can be responsible, or does it expose that thought as a manner of speaking that does the work of causal explanation while borrowing the rhetoric of moral judgment? Which features of the controversy does the theory illuminate, and which does it distort or leave silent? Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the Fischer controversy supports or undermines the notion of national responsibility, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
