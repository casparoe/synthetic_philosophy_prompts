I am designing a seminar on the moral psychology of party politics, and I want to pair Simone Weil’s late essay on the abolition of political parties with Arthur Koestler’s Darkness at Noon. Weil’s claim, as I understand it, is not merely that parties are corruptible or that some parties become totalitarian, but that the party form itself—the machine for generating collective passion, for subordinating individual conscience to a line, for making truth a function of loyalty—is totalitarian in its essence; hence her unsettling proposal that a free society might need to abolish parties rather than merely regulate them. Koestler’s novel, set in an unnamed state during the purges, follows Rubashov, an old Bolshevik who helped build the Party and is now destroyed by it, and who eventually confesses to crimes he did not commit. I want a reading of the novel through Weil’s lens: where does Koestler dramatize precisely the mechanism Weil identifies, and where does the novel resist or complicate her diagnosis?

Please take seriously both the novel’s political psychology and Weil’s argument. Consider Rubashov’s cell, his diary, his interrogations by Ivanov, his conversations with the other prisoners, his final confession, and the offstage figure of Number One. Does the Party in the novel function as Weil’s machine, or is the novel showing something else—a revolutionary faith, a Hegelian logic of history, a need for sacrifice, a personal guilt—that Weil’s focus on party form misses? Does Rubashov’s individual conscience, such as it is, vindicate Weil’s claim that party membership destroys the capacity for independent judgment, or does it reveal a complicity that precedes party membership? Does the novel’s refusal to name Russia or the Soviets support Weil’s claim that the disease is Continental and structural, or does it universalize in a way that weakens her specific political analysis? Finally, what does the novel add to Weil’s argument that argument alone does not—what does it show about the temptations of party politics that a philosophical essay cannot? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: not reducing Koestler to an illustration of Weil, and not distorting Weil to fit Koestler. Please say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
