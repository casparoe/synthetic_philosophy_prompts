I write about books and ideas for a magazine, and I keep bumping into something I don't have a name for. When a poem or a song is built on a tight formal constraint -- a rhyme scheme, a fixed syllable count, an acrostic -- a translator has to make choices the original author never had to make, and two things seem to happen together. First, the constraint forces a word choice that drags in a shade of meaning that wasn't in the source: a rhyme pulls in a particular image, an acrostic forces a particlar noun, a syllable count kills a qualifier. Second, readers and reviewers of the translation then praise the original for exactly that shade, as if the author had put it there. The translator's forced invention gets credited back to the source.

I once read a whole book that rendered one tiny French poem dozens of different ways, and this felt like half of what it was about, but I never saw a name for the specific move. Is there one? The nearby terms I know are compensation, untranslatability, and the old saying about translators being traitors, but none of those quite catches the part where the translator's necessary addition gets read back into the original as the author's own. If there's no established term, coin one and define it, and tell me where the edges are -- how is this different from ordinary drift in a loose translation, from a deliberate adaptation, or from a translator just padding for the meter? A couple of boundary cases would help. I'm not after a label for its own sake; I want to know what the thing actually is and what it isnt.
