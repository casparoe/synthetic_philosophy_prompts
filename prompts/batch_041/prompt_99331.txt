Ok so I've ended up with a little bit of money and two grad students for about eighteen months, and I get to run a small working group on non-human 'actors' in history — plagues, climate, volcanoes, that kind of thing. The pitch I sold my department was basically: historians now routinely say stuff like the eruption helped kill the empire, or the drought did X, and nobody blinks, but the minute you ask what it even means for a volcano or a germ to *do* something, everyone gets squirmy about environmental determinism. I want us to spend the eighteen months on the questions that actually matter, not rehashing Diamond vs his critics for the tenth time.

So: what are the main open questions here, and which two or three would you put at the top for a group this size and this short on time? Please defend the ordering — I care way more about the reasoning then the list. For each one I want to know why it's upstream of the others (does settling it change how we'd read the rest?), whether it's actually tractable for people who mostly read history and philosophy and not climate models, and what would move it up or down the list — say if some new proxy data landed, or if the philosophers just kept going in circles.

A couple of framings I keep chewing on, and feel free to tell me they're bad: (1) I suspect a lot of the mess comes from conflating three roles a non-human thing can play — pure backdrop or background condition, a trigger that tips an already-loaded situation, and a genuine actor with something like its own aims. Is that distinction worth building the whole project around, or does it dissolve once you look closely? (2) There's a weird asymmetry I can't shake — we're happy to let a pathogen be a cause of a demographic collapse but we get nervous when a climate shift is the cause of a political one, even when the causal chains look structurally similar. Is that asymmetry a real methodological insight or just squeamishness inherited from a bad intellectual history?

One more thing. Some of this territory is genuinely settled and some of it is people talking past each other, and I don't want to walk in assuming there's a consensus where there isn't one. So please flag clearly which parts of what you tell me are pretty much agreed on by people who work on this, and which parts are live disputes where smart people genuinely disagree — don't dress up a contested view as the received wisdom. And don't hand me a reading list, I can find the books myself.
