I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a manuscript where I must decide what the book is actually about. Its setting is a country that has handed its policy decisions to prediction markets — futarchy, in the broad sense — and I have room for one sustained argument and no more. What I want from you is an agenda rather than a survey: among the questions that remain genuinely unresolved about governing this way, which should the novel put at its center, which should it acknowledge in a paragraph, and which should it leave to the theorists? Rank them, and defend the ranking. I am less interested in the list than in the reasoning: which questions are upstream of the others, which can be staged as events rather than explained in dialogue, and which would not change a single scene however they were settled. I am not asking for sources; I am asking for an argument.

So that your answer does not depend on which version of the idea you happen to have in mind, here is the one in my manuscript. The state publishes a single headline measure of national welfare, revised on a fixed schedule. Any citizen may propose a policy. A conditional market then prices one question: will the measure be higher five years from now if this policy is adopted than if it is rejected? If the price crosses a fixed threshold, the policy takes effect automatically, and no body may veto it. Ordinary elections survive only to choose the officials who define the measure and supervise the market. Everything else — courts, parties, a free press — is recognizably our own.

The candidates I have been circling are these. Whether a single scalar measure can survive its own publicity, or whether it will be gamed until it means nothing. Whether the market should price the probability that the measure will be higher or the expected size of the difference, and what that choice does to how a rational citizen bets. Whether a market can coherently price a condition that its own verdict brings about. Whether organized or wealthy actors can profitably distort a price by holding positions in the world the policy would create. Whether the division of labor — values settled by voters, facts settled by markets — survives contact with the fact that whoever writes the measure holds most of the power. And whether a sequence of individually approved decisions can add up to a country no one would have chosen.

Please put these in order, tell me which two or three deserve the book's full weight, add any question I have left out that belongs near the top, and say what would have to be different — about markets, or about people — for your ordering to change. Write in American English and keep the whole reply under 700 words; I would rather have a sharp argument than a complete one.
