Write a joke about the Frege–Geach problem, pitched at someone who has actually worked on expressivism: it should land for them and baffle everyone else. The humour has to turn on the structure of the problem rather than on generic philosophy-nerd material, so no 'boo'/'hurrah' vocabulary, no punchline of the form 'expressivists just can't explain logic', and no closing reveal that the character is secretly a realist. Since the textbook illustrations are worn smooth, hang the joke on this instead: a parent and child arguing about a broken window, in which the child has worked out that a sentence sitting inside an if-clause commits you to no attitude whatever, and therefore offers 'if breaking windows is wrong, then breaking this window was wrong' as evidence that they disapproved of nothing. The joke should turn on the requirement that the embedded sentence mean the same thing as the unembedded one — the point that gives the conditional occurrence its content and makes the argument valid — so that the child's loophole closes the moment the parent supplies the antecedent and modus ponens does its work. Let the parent deliver the last line. Give the joke as a single paragraph, then one or two sentences identifying exactly which feature of the problem it trades on.
