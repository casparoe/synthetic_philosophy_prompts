I want to pressure-test an argument from my reading group before I decide whether to give up on computationalism about consciousness.

(1) A system is conscious just in case it implements the right computation — the one a conscious mind implements.
(2) Any computation can be implemented by a lookup table: a finite list of input-output pairs.
(3) A lookup table is an inert list; consulting a list is not being conscious, and nothing about a list is conscious.
(4) So implementing the right input-output mapping is not sufficient for consciousness.
(5) But any physical system, brain included, can be redescribed as a lookup table that assigns to each of its inputs the output it actually produces — a whole life can be written down as a table.
(6) So whatever disqualifies the table in (3)–(4) disqualifies the brain too.
(7) Therefore either both are conscious or neither is; and since the table plainly is not, the brain is not either.
(8) So no physical system is conscious, computationalism included.

My friend reads (8) as a reductio of (1) and concludes that consciousness must be something over and above computation. But I notice the same reasoning would sink any physicalist story, since every physical system can be tabulated; and I would rather keep (1) than accept (8). Where does it go wrong — in the move from (2) to (4), from (4) to (5), or somewhere else — and if I reject that step, what exactly am I buying and what does it cost? In particular, tell me what work the word "implements" has to do for the fix to hold, and whether the fix leaves the Chinese-nation-style objection live or dead.
