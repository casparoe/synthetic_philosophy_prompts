I host a monthly public interview for a small ethics reading group, and next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent years pushing a line about moral worth that I'd like to actually stress-test rather than let him restate for the fortieth time. His view, roughly: whether an action has moral worth doesn't turn on the act, and doesn't turn on the motive as such, but on whether the agent's concern for the morally relevant feature was load-bearing in his sense -- if you subtracted that concern from the agent, holding everything else fixed, the action would have come out differently. So worth tracks counterfactual dependence on the right concern, not the mere presence of a noble feeling or a correct deed.

That gives me a couple of openings I want to exploit. Huck Finn is one: he helps Jim while sincerely believing he's damning himself, so his sympathy is doing the causal work but his conscience is dead against it, and I want to know whether the view hands Huck moral worth and whether he's happy with that result. The other is the familiar kind of case where a climber lets go of the rope because the thought of the person on the other end of it unnerves him -- the right concern is right there in the causal story, but the causal path is a mess, and I want to see whether a bare counterfactual test can tell that apart from someone acting on the concern properly.

Can you draft eight to ten interview questions? They should press on the actual machinery rather than invite him to re-describe it, and I want at least these four, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give (he's got about three stock moves and I know them all): (1) is worth all-or-nothing or does it come in degrees, and if degrees, degrees of what; (2) can his view tell apart someone who genuinely cares from someone whose concern is real but only fires when it's being noticed -- the person who is generous exactly when there's an audience; (3) the counterfactual itself: what fixes the comparison class and the background conditions, and isn't the whole thing hostage to a psychological claim we can't check; (4) whether someone who acts from a mistaken sense of duty -- believes he's doing right, is in fact doing something monstrous -- can have moral worth. Then one closing question about what case or evidence would actually make him drop the view.

Keep them short enough to read out loud, no jargon dumps, and nothing he can duck by saying the details are still being worked out.
