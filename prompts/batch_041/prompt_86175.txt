I have to give the memorial address at a small workshop that was already on the calendar in honor of my doctoral supervisor before she died, eleven days ago, at sixty-three, of a cancer that gave her a bad final few months. The workshop is going ahead as a memorial session. Her husband and her two grown children, none of them philosophers, will be in the front row; the rest of the room is about thirty philosophers of religion who knew her published work the way you know a text you have taught for twenty years.

Her whole career was a defense of the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, in a form that was hers rather than Leibniz's. She thought the standard reading, on which the actual world wins a competition for the greatest net balance of good over evil, was a category error, because a world is not a heap of goods and evils but a complete series of events, and the only evaluation with a subject matter is an evaluation of the whole series at once, for its harmony and compossibility. Two consequences matter for what I can say on the day. First, no single event, including her illness and her death, can be assessed on its own; only the series can, and the series is the best there could have been. Second, she held that a sentence of the form "the world would have been better if X had not happened" is not false but malformed: it has the grammar of a judgment and nothing for the judgment to be about.

Write the address, about eight minutes spoken. The difficulty is that both audiences have to be served by the same sentences: the philosophers should hear her position stated accurately enough that none of them flinches, and her family should not have a single piece of jargon explained to them. I cannot say anything she would have called false. In practice that removes the ordinary consolations, including anything implying that this world would have been better without this death, and it removes any counterfactual of that form; it does not remove grief, or love, or the plain statement that she suffered. Nor should the address pretend the position is more comfortable than it is. At least one person in that room will be thinking that her view left her no way to protest what happened to her, and the address should not quietly step around that. It should be an address and not a lecture; she would have hated being turned into an occasion for exposition.

If some sentence proves impossible to say under those constraints, leave it out, and then tell me in three or four lines, after the address, which sentence you dropped and why.
