Question 3 (10 marks: 4/3/3)

A lamp has two controls. A fine dial with a hundred positions, and a coarse two position switch, DIM and BRIGHT. The switch is wired to the dial: it reads DIM whenever the dial sits anywhere in positions 1-50, and BRIGHT whenever the dial sits in 51-100. Both controls are connected to the bulb, and either one on its own is enough to fix the bulb's output: on DIM the bulb runs at 40 watts, on BRIGHT at 100.

Someone says: the switch is a cause of the bulb's brightness and the dial is also a cause, so the brightness is causally overdetermined, and we should not say the switch is a cause after all.

(a) Set out the exclusion argument as it is usually run for the mental case. Name each premise and say what a non-reductive physicalist would have to give up in order to get out of it.

(b) Is the dimmer case a good model for the mental case? Say what the analogy is supposed to show and whether it shows it.

(c) Is the overdetermination premise as secure in the mental case as it is in the dimmer case? Explain.

And here's what the student wrote:

The exclusion argument is a reductio of non-reductive physicalism, and it goes like this.

(1) Closure: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. (2) Supervenience: mental properties supervene on physical ones, so no mental difference without a physical difference. (3) Realisation: whenever a mental property M is instantiated it is realised by some physical property P, and P is sufficient for any physical effect E that M is supposed to cause. (4) No overdetermination: physical effects are not systematically overdetermined, so each effect has one sufficient cause. From these, if M is not identical to P, M is excluded from the causal production of E. The non-reductive physicalist is then stuck with the standard dilemma: reduction (M just is P) or epiphenomenalism (M does nothing). To get out they have to reject either closure or the no-overdetermination premise, and most people think the second is the only live option.

The dimmer case looks structurally like the mental case. The switch is wired to the dial, so the dial position fixes the switch position; the switch state is grounded in the dial state, which is the supervenience step. The dial is sufficient on its own for the brightness, which is the realisation step. So the argument should apply and give the conclusion that the switch is not a cause. But that conclusion is obviously wrong. If you flip the switch to BRIGHT the bulb gets brighter, you can watch it happen. So the dimmer case shows the argument proves too much, because it would exclude a cause we can see working. What goes wrong is that the switch is screened off by the dial: once you know the dial position, the switch position tells you nothing more about the brightness. Screening off is basically the exclusion principle in probabilistic dress, and here it is doing something it should not, because the coarse state is the one that makes the difference to the bulb while the fine state carries detail that makes no difference to it at all.

For (c), the overdetermination premise is weaker in the mental case than in the dimmer case. Two controls on one lamp are two separate bits of machinery, whereas the mental and the physical are one thing described twice. And if M is necessarily coextensive with P, every possible instance of P being an instance of M and vice versa, then M and P are the same property, so there is nothing left to exclude. This is why anomalous monism escapes the argument: mental events are causes, so it cannot be that the argument shows thoughts never cause anything.
