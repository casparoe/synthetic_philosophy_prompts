I am preparing a lecture on infinity and motion for an advanced undergraduate metaphysics course, and I have talked myself into a reading of Thomson's lamp that I suspect is too strong but can no longer see where it fails. The lamp is switched at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on, so that by t = 1 infinitely many switches have been performed. There is no last switch. Since the state of the lamp at t = 1 would have to be the state that the final switch left behind, and there is no final switch, it seems to me that no state can be assigned: the lamp is left neither on nor off, and any assignment would be arbitrary. Yet the description plainly demands that the lamp have some state or other, so I want to conclude that the setup is not merely incomplete but inconsistent, and hence that a supertask of this kind cannot be performed at all — infinitely many tasks are approached but never completed.

If that is right, Zeno's runner appears to be in exactly the same position, since she too must complete infinitely many traversals with no last one; yet no one thinks her arrival at the finish line is a contradiction. So either the calculus rescues both cases, in which case there is nothing left for the lamp to illustrate, or it rescues neither, and motion requires denying that a continuum is infinitely divisible. One of my students proposed a variant in which the lamp's brightness is halved at each stage instead of toggled, so that the brightness converges to zero and the final state seems perfectly determinate; I cannot tell whether this shows that the trouble lies with toggling rather than with supertasks as such.

Where does my reasoning go wrong, do the lamp and the runner really stand or fall together, and what is the defensible version of the distinction I am trying to draw?
