I'm building the take-home part of the final for a decision theory course I'm TAing for (mixed advanced-undergrad/grad crowd, about 15 of them, they've done Savage and a bit of game theory but nothing on imperfect recall yet). I want a single essay question on the absent-minded driver that they can't get by just recalling the puzzle and saying "continue, obviously" — which is what half of them will do, and it's not even the interesting part. I'll give them the setup in the question itself so it doesn't hinge on them having memorized it: a driver is going down a road with two intersections one after another. He wants to exit at the second. Exiting at the first gets him 0, exiting at the second gets him 4, and if he just keeps driving past both he ends up with 1. The catch is that he's absent-minded: standing at an intersection he can't tell whether it's the first or the second, and he can't recall whether he's already passed one. So whatever he does at one intersection he does at the other, since from where he sits the two decision points are indistinguishable — continue at both and he scores 1, exit at the first and he scores 0. Fine. But now suppose we let him randomize: exit with probability p at each intersection, same p both times. Then his ex ante expected value works out to 4p(1−p) + (1−p)²·1, which is maximized at p = 1/3 and comes to 4/3 — better than the 1 he gets by continuing for sure. That's the thing I want them chewing on.

So: write me the exam question, a model answer around 700–800 words, and a marking scheme. The question needs to force them past a summary — specifically to (a) say precisely why the deterministic "just continue" argument feels airtight, (b) reconstruct the mixed-strategy argument and where the 4/3 comes from, and (c) take a stand on what's wrong with it (or defend it), by locating the assumption doing the real work. I don't want them just announcing that the mixed strategy is "invalid because he can't tell the intersections apart" — that misses the point, since the whole idea is one and the same bias applied at both. A good answer should get into whether a randomization is a physical event that has to happen at a particular moment versus a description of a plan or propensity, whether an ex ante optimum has to be something the agent can actually carry out, and whether treating the driver as a single chooser across time is what breaks. And I want them to notice that no new information ever arrives, so the usual "wait and see, decide later" story that licenses different choices at different times simply isn't available here.

For the marking scheme, build it around five must-hit points, give each a mark value out of 20 total, and add a one-line note per point on how candidates typically get it wrong. Make sure the scheme actually separates an A from a B: I want to see spelled out which specific moves an A has to contain that a B can get away without. Also — no citations or reading lists anywhere in the question or the answer, these are take-home and I don't want them hunting references, just the argument.
