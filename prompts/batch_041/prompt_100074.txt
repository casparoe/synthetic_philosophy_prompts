I host a small philosophy-of-mind reading group, and last week we spent the entire session on a thought experiment one of us calls the careenium. Picture a closed table—a sort of billiard table—populated by an enormous number of tiny magnetized beads that careen around, bouncing off the walls and off one another and sometimes sticking together into stable clumps. Zoom out and the clumps look like objects in their own right: recurring clump-shapes persist for a while, merge, split, and drift in ways that track regularities outside the table, since the walls become more or less bouncy depending on what is going on out there and thereby feed information back into the beads. Our host then asks who is pushing whom around. Is it the individual beads, every shove ultimately a bead-on-bead collision, with the clumps mere summaries we impose from outside? Or are the clumps themselves the real pushers—so that the higher-level, symbol-like patterns are genuinely causally efficacious and the bead-level story is only the machinery that realizes them?

Two of us took opposite sides and neither would budge, so I would like to see the argument done properly. Please write a short dialogue between two equally sharp, well-informed characters, call them A and B, who disagree about whether the clumps push the beads around. A holds that only bead-level collisions do any pushing and that clump-talk is a convenient compression with no causal power of its own; B holds that the clump level is genuinely causally efficacious, that the patterns are real causes and not merely bookkeeping. I leaned toward B but ran out of answers, so make A's case the strong one it actually is. Let them genuinely engage: each should take up the other's actual points, grant what seems right, press where the other is weak, and reason from the specifics of the careenium rather than trading slogans. Neither should be a strawman and neither should simply win. Keep the whole dialogue to at most 900 words.
