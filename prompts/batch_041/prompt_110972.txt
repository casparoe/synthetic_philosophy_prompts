I want you to treat the following as a position to be stress-tested rather than as a thesis to be defended.

I hold a strong historical-materialist claim: that the economic base of a society — its forces and relations of production — explains everything else, in the sense that once base facts are fixed, superstructural facts (legal, political, religious, aesthetic) add no independent explanatory or predictive power. Superstructural facts are, on this view, screened off by the base: they may be vivid, causally efficacious in the short run, and indispensable to how a society feels from the inside, but they carry no information about the social order that the base does not already carry.

I am not asking whether this is the correct reading of any particular author, and I do not want a bibliography or a survey of who said what. I want a map of the position's exposure: what would count against it, sorted from what should make me abandon it outright, through what would force a serious revision, down to what is merely awkward.

Work through at least the following.

1. What the thesis predicts that its rivals do not. Take seriously two rivals: an explanatory pluralism on which base and superstructure each retain independent causal power, and a mutual-constitution picture on which they are not related as cause and effect at all. What observation would distinguish these from my thesis, and is that observation in practice available?

2. The historical shapes that would embarrass it. I am especially interested in three: (i) a superstructural feature — a legal form, a doctrinal commitment, a contingent political settlement — that persists across a change of base and visibly shapes the new base; (ii) two societies with near-identical bases that diverge sharply in superstructure and stay diverged; (iii) a base that appears to have been selected for by a superstructural constraint rather than the reverse. For each, say whether the thesis can absorb the case by redescription, and what that redescription costs.

3. A specific construction I want you to consider, because I suspect it is where the thesis is most exposed. Suppose we recast the claim as one about screening-off: conditional on base variables, superstructural variables have no residual predictive power for other superstructural variables. Is that recasting (a) equivalent to the original thesis, (b) a weakening no one who held the original would accept, or (c) a strengthening that makes it empirically fragile? Then consider a society in which an arbitrary event — a plague, a charismatic figure, a copied legal code — perturbs the superstructure, and the perturbation propagates for centuries without any corresponding change in the base. What does the thesis predict here, and what would its defenders have to say to keep the case from counting as a counterexample?

4. The internal threat of triviality. If the base is specified broadly enough to include whatever turns out to explain the superstructure, or if the dependence is claimed only ever to hold in the last instance, has the thesis been insulated from refutation? Is that insulation a defect in a thesis of this kind, or a legitimate feature of an explanatory programme? Say honestly which, rather than assuming the flattering answer.

5. What a committed defender, not a critic, would concede as counting against the thesis, and what they would dismiss as question-begging.

I would personally be most moved by a case in which the superstructure demonstrably constrains the base over a long run; treat that as a prior on your ranking rather than as a request to flatter me.

Close with a blunt verdict: is this position well insulated, and does that tell against it?

Please answer in British English.
