I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on explanation and interpretation in deep learning, and I'm working through the midterms. The question I have to grade asked students to engage a small piece of conceptual terrain rather than recite a position: a trained network's internal activations carry information about some property P that a simple linear readout recovers with high accuracy — does it follow that the network represents P? Students were asked for (i) the strongest case that the first claim does not entail the second, (ii) the strongest reply available to someone who thinks a sufficiently good linear readout is already enough to license representational talk, and (iii) what further evidence would actually settle the matter rather than merely relocate it. I've pasted one student's answer verbatim below.

Please give the answer a grade and, more usefully, a point-by-point set of comments I can hand back. I grade on a coarse scale — a strong pass is an A, a competent but flawed answer a B, and anything that misses the substance a C or below — but tell me if a finer calibration is warranted. For each comment, separate what the answer gets right from what it gets wrong or omits, and say whether the problem is grade-bearing or a forgivable simplification; where a step is asserted without support, name the missing premise. I don't want a general impression or a paraphrase of the student's view; I want the specific moves that earn or lose credit, and a closing verdict on whether the grade you would assign is generous, fair, or harsh.

Student's answer:

"The claim that a linear probe can recover information about P is a claim about what is decodable from the activations, and decodability is not the same as use. A probe can succeed because the information is present as a side effect of the network optimizing for something else, without the network ever relying on P when it produces its outputs. So the mere existence of a successful linear readout does not establish that the network represents P. This is the standard deflationary point, and as far as it goes I think it is correct.

But the deflationist overreaches when she concludes that the network has no internal states that stand for anything. Nobody seriously denies that the activations are real, causally efficacious states of the system; the only question is whether we should describe them in representational terms. The interesting middle position is that representation-talk is a matter of what the states are for, that is, their function in the network's computation, and a linear readout gives us good evidence about function, because a direction that a linear classifier can read out is a direction the network has made explicit, and explicit directions are exactly the ones that do computational work.

The strongest reply to the deflationist is therefore causal: if you intervene on the readout direction and the network's behavior on P-related inputs changes in the predicted way, then P is not merely decodable but used, and the representational claim is vindicated. I would add that superposition complicates this, because when features are packed into overlapping directions the network cannot represent any of them individually, which is why sparse autoencoders are needed to recover them. On what would settle the matter, I would say the dispute is really about which interventions count, and that once we agree on the intervention methodology the disagreement becomes empirical rather than philosophical."

That is the whole answer; no notes or rubric were attached.
