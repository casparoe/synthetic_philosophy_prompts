I am designing a ninety-minute graduate seminar session for first-year philosophy and cognitive-science students on the induction of concepts from a handful of examples. They have encountered the problem of induction and can handle elementary logic, but they have no formal background in learning theory or machine learning. I want to use Bongard problems—the puzzles in which a left and a right panel of figures must be separated by an implicit rule—as the vehicle, but not the familiar catalogue of perceptual puzzles; I want the session to turn on a family of variants I have been constructing, and I need you to plan how I should teach the session rather than to solve the problems for me.

The variants are these. First, textual Bongard problems in which each side is a finite sequence of integers and the intended rule concerns the dependency structure of the generator rather than any surface feature of the numbers. Second, Bongard problems with a designated lure: one example on each side appears to satisfy the opposite side’s rule, so the learner must choose between a simple rule with an exception and a more complex rule that fits every case. Third, Bongard problems in which the two sides are indistinguishable under every first-order feature description I supply, but differ in whether the figures were produced by a compositional process or by a holistic one, so the intended concept is historical or etiological rather than perceptual.

The problem I am considering as the spine of the session is this. Left: 1, 2, 6, 24, 120. Right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. The intended rule is not factorials versus powers of two as a list of features; it is that each left sequence is generated by a recurrence in which the nth term depends on all previous terms, while each right sequence is generated by a recurrence in which the nth term depends only on the immediately preceding term. A second panel would extend the left with 720 and the right with 32, and a third panel would introduce a lure: a left sequence whose surface pattern suggests a local rule and a right sequence whose surface pattern suggests a global one. I want the students to feel the underdetermination at the first panel, where both a surface-feature rule and a dependency-structure rule fit, and then to experience the second and third panels as forcing a choice about which kind of concept is being induced.

Plan the session for me. What idea has to be in place before the first panel can even be seen as a problem? What single example should carry the whole session, and why this one or why not? In what order should the variants be introduced so that the distinction between a feature-list concept and a generator-relative concept becomes inevitable rather than stipulative? Which misconceptions should I expect at each stage—for instance, that the rule must be a property of individual items, that any finite sample determines a unique concept, that simplicity is merely aesthetic, or that the lure is a mistake rather than part of the concept—and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? What question should I ask at the end to see whether they have understood the problem of induction rather than merely learned to solve my three panels? And what should I explicitly refuse to explain at this level, saying so, because it would distract from the conceptual work?
