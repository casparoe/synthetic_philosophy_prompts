My friend Priya and I spent like three hours arguing at a bar last night about how old nations actually are, and I woke up still annoyed, so I want to write the document we should have written instead of shouting over each other.

Her position is the Hobsbawm/Anderson one: nations are modern through and through, nationalism came first and the nations are what it produced, and the printing press plus centralized states plus capitalism did the real work. Anything older that looks national is invented tradition — elites dressing up a dynasty in folk costume. My position is more the ethno-symbolist one you get from Anthony Smith: the modern mass nation is new, fine, but it's built out of older ethnic communities — a named people, a myth of common descent, a homeland, shared memories — and calling the whole thing 'invented' just begs the question of where the raw material came from. I keep saying England around 1300-1400 looks like a nation to me; she keeps saying that's just a kingdom with a good publicist. She also says the whole disagreement is people defining 'nation' differently, which strikes me as a bit too easy an exit.

What I want from you is the joint statement that a modernist and an ethno-symbolist could both actually put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims we both accept — about what the evidence shows, about what stands in need of explanation, and about which parts of our two views are actually compatible with each other rather than rivals.
2. A disagreements table: for each live disagreement, the question we're actually disagreeing about, each side's position written in wording that side would accept, the crux (the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us), the case or evidence we'd both agree bears on that crux, and what each side predicts we'd find there.
3. Test cases. Take four or five and give, for each, the earliest date each side can defend for 'this is a nation': England around 1300, revolutionary France, the early modern Dutch Republic or Switzerland, Tokugawa Japan, and one stateless case — Armenians, Kurds, or diaspora Jews, whichever we could both discuss without getting weird about it. Then tell me honestly whether the two dates are competing empirical claims or just different definitions of the word 'nation'. I suspect a good chunk of our arguement was verbal and I want that named rather than smoothed over — but also tell me what survives as a real disagreement once the verbal part is set aside.
4. Defeat conditions: for each side, what would count as evidence against our own view, not just against the other's.

Format it as the numbered agreed claims, then the table, then the test cases, then a short section on verbal vs substantive. Write it in first person plural — it's supposed to be something we'd both sign, and please don't let either side get a formulation it wouldn't actually accept.
