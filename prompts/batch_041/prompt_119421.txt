Szasz held that mental illness is a myth: that what we call mental illness is a metaphor for troubles in living, that a psychiatric diagnosis names no disease unless there is neurological damage to point to, and that a diagnosis therefore bears on a defendant's guilt no more than a claim of demonic possession would. I am a law student clerking for a public defender, and I want to work out what this position implies for a case I am actually handling. My client is charged with a serious assault; he believes, sincerely and immovably, that a former employer is broadcasting his thoughts to strangers, and my supervising attorney thinks an insanity plea is winnable on these facts. In my jurisdiction an insanity acquittal does not end the case: it transfers the defendant to a civil commitment system with annual review, the burden on him to show he is no longer dangerous, and no fixed release date, and his likely stay there exceeds the determinate sentence a conviction would bring. A diminished-capacity route also exists, which could reduce the degree of the offense without triggering commitment. Please set out the most important implications of Szasz's position for how I should weigh these options, and for the criminal law's wider practice of excusing conduct it calls disordered. Be explicit about which implications follow from the claim about disease and which follow from the separable objection to coercion, since I suspect the two strands come apart here. Tell me what would change your mind about the verdict you reach, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
