So I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, mostly long-form, and I've just been handed the chance to apply for one of those small editorial fellowships — six months, a bit of money, no requirement that the piece be about anything in particular except that it has to be a real argument and not just a reported feature. I want to spend it on the commodification stuff, the whole repugnant-markets cluster — kidney sales, paying people for plasma, selling your vote, surge pricing on bottled water after a hurricane — because I've now reported on this from three different angles and I keep coming away feeling that the people I interview aren't actually disagreeing about the thing they think they're disagreeing about.

Here's the half-formed idea I want you to turn into a proper proposal. Whenever someone says a good shouldn't be for sale, the objection seems to come in two very different shapes. One is about the transaction: this particular sale degrades or coerces or exploits someone, and if you could fix that, the sale would be fine. The other is about the market as a standing institution: it's not that any one sale is bad, it's that once there's a price and a queue and a broker, the good has changed for everyone, including people who never buy or sell it — it's now the kind of thing that has a price. My hunch is that a lot of the famous fights in this area, including a lot of the ones that look like they're about coercion or consent, are really fights about which of those two objects the objection is aimed at, and that once you seperate them a bunch of the disputes dissolve and a couple of genuinely hard ones become visible that were hidden before.

So: write me the proposal. State the question so it has a determinate answer, say why the existing positions — the autonomy-and-paternalism people, the corruption-of-meaning people, whatever else is in the room — leave it open rather than settling it, describe the method, what I'd actually do with six months given that I'm a journalist and not a philosopher and my raw material is interviews and cases rather than arguments in journals, and tell me honestly what would count as the project failing. And give me a realistic scope, because I have a tendency to pitch things that are secretly a book. I don't need a bibliography or a reading list, I'll fill that in myself — just tell me the shape of the thing, and who I'd need to be arguing against.
