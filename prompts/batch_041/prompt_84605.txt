Okay so I've been in a running argument with a friend about whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, and I read a long blog post that laid out the case, and now I want to know how much of it is actually load-bearing. Here's the case as the post had it:

Sahlins showed that hunter-gatherers were affluent. Affluence just means your wants are satisfied, and there are two roads to that — produce a lot, or want little. Foragers took the second road, so calling them poor is a category error rather than an empirical finding, and the "subsistence" framing everyone inherits is just ethnocentrism. The empirical meat is the work data: Lee's !Kung and the Arnhem Land groups put in something like 15-20 hours a week, which is less than a medieval peasant and less than a modern office worker, and those numbers have basically held up. They ate a varied diet, they weren't on the edge of starvation, and they didn't have the epidemic disease, the tooth decay, and the stunting that show up once people start farming. So the Neolithic isn't progress, it's a bad trade: more calories per acre in exchange for hierarchy, patriarchy, private property, epidemic disease, and a permanent underclass. Diamond's essay is the same argument with more attitude. Conclusion: farming was the worst mistake in human history, and inequality as such was basically invented by agriculture. And the people who say foragers worked more than 15-20 hours are just measuring differently — if you're not counting food processing as work, you're not measuring work.

How much of that is fair? I'm pretty convinced by the affluence part but the "worst mistake" part smells like it needs premises the post never actually gives, and I can't tell if that's my bias or a real gap. Go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who studies foragers for a living would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what they'd say is flatly wrong. I especailly want to know: (a) whether the definitional move about affluence is doing the work the post thinks it's doing, or whether it's smuggling in a wellbeing claim that then can't be defended the way it was introduced; (b) how much the whole thing hangs on the work-hour numbers, and whether the "they're just measuring differently" line is a real defense or a dodge; (c) whether "agriculture caused inequality" is something you'd defend as a causal claim, a tendency, or just a one-liner that sounds deep; and (d) whether the worst-mistake conclusion follows from whatever survives, or whether it needs extra premises about whose welfare counts and compared to what counterfactual.

And please dont do the "well, it's contested, anthropologists disagree" thing and leave me to sort it out — I want a committed answer. Tell me which of those claims you'd actually stake something on, which ones you'd cut, and if you had to bet one way on whether the Neolithic was a net welfare loss for the people living through it, which way you'd bet and on what grounds. Flag the one or two places where you're least confident in your own verdict, but give me the verdict.
