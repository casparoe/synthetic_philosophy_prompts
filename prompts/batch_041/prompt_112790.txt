I run a small, non-academic monthly discussion group that works through puzzles in decision theory, and I have been thinking about counterfactual mugging on my own for some time. Next month's guest is a decision theorist who holds that you should pay in counterfactual mugging — the case in which Omega flips a coin and, if it comes up tails, asks you for $100, while if it comes up heads it pays you $10,000 on the strength of having predicted that you would have paid in the tails branch. My guest's defense, as I understand it, is that we should assess whole policies or decision-procedures rather than the particular act in front of us, and that the policy of paying comes out ahead when averaged over both branches.

I would like eight to ten interview questions that push well beyond a restatement of that view. At least one should address the incredulity many people feel — the sense that paying $100 once the coin has already come up tails is simply handing money to a stranger for nothing — and at least one should press on a variant I have worked out, which I take to be the crux. Call it the XOR mugging: Omega flips two independent fair coins; if the first comes up heads it asks you for $100 and you must answer on the spot, and if the first comes up tails it does not ask but pays you $10,000 if and only if exactly one of the following is true — it predicted you would have paid in the heads branch, or the second coin came up heads. As far as I can work out, the disposition to pay now leaves the $10,000 at fifty-fifty rather than raising your chance of it, so paying becomes a pure $100 loss. This strikes me as the same structure as the XOR blackmail cases, where the blackmailer ties the threatened harm to an exclusive-or of your payment with a coin flip so that paying ceases to be evidence that the harm will be avoided. I want the questions to force my guest to say whether the policy defense survives this, and if it does not, to identify what was actually doing the work in the original case.

The remaining questions should cover, among other things: what precommitment can mean when there is no earlier moment at which to commit, and whether that is a coherent demand; whether the policy defense leaves the theory unable to say anything about the single case in front of you, and why that is not a fatal objection; whether it matters that in the original case the benefit lands in the branch you are not in, so that the policy is good for a possible version of you rather than for you; whether the XOR variant shows that the original argument turns on a correlation that can be broken at will, and what that implies about how robust the recommendation to pay really is; whether the puzzle depends on Omega being perfect or merely on decision-making being correlated across branches; and how the position handles a blackmailer who is very reliable but not perfect, and whether the answer changes gradually or flips at perfection.

For every question, please give me the follow-up I should ask if my guest gives the answer they are most likely to give. I expect a good deal of insistence that these are features rather than bugs, and of the charge that I am just redescribing causal decision theory, and I want the follow-ups to close those off rather than let the conversation stall. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want questions that will work in the room.
