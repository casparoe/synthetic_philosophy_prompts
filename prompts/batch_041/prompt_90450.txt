Hey — I'm a risk-communication person, and I've been pulled into a lot of these "should we allow X" fights over the years (a new pesticide, a field trial of an engineered crop, a gene drive, a food additive, once a change to a building code). I keep seeing the same move and I don't have a word for it, and I'm not sure one exists.

Here's what I mean. In these arguements, whatever the evidence happens to look like, there's a way of describing it that lands on "so we should wait." If the harm estimate has a wide error bar, someone says the true harm could be much larger than the midpoint, so the uncertainty itself is a reason to hold off. Fine. Then the benefit estimate also has a wide error bar, and the same person says the benefit might not show up at all, so we can't count on it. If the harm estimate is precise and small, that gets read as "you've only studied it under ideal conditions." If the benefit estimate is precise and large, that gets read as "too good to be true, or it'll get captured by whoever funds it." And when you point out that the same evidence can't be read both ways at once, the reply is that precaution means giving the benefit of the doubt to the potential victim, which sounds fine on its own.

What I notice is that this isn't one argument, it's a family of moves that all do the same thing: take whatever the current state of the evidence is and convert it into a reason for the status quo, no matter what that state is. Each move is defensible by itself. Together they mean no possible result of a study could count as a reason to go ahead, which is a strange property for a family of empirical arguments to have. And they only ever run one direction — I've never once seen the mirror image used to argue for acting (nobody says "the harm might not materialize, so let's move," or "the benefit could be bigger than the midpoint, so the uncertainty is a reason to hurry").

I know "precautionary principle" is the obvious thing to point at, but that's usually stated as a threshold — how much evidence you need before acting. What I'm describing is more like a machine for generating reasons that works on any input you feed it. Is there an established name for this? If there is, what is it and how close is it really — I keep getting told it's just confirmation bias, or loss aversion, or status quo bias, and those don't sit right, because they're about the psychology of the people rather than the structure of the argument. If one of them is genuinely closest, say so and tell me exactly where it breaks. If nothing fits, coin something, and give me a definition I could actually use to sort new cases, plus a few cases right on the edge.
