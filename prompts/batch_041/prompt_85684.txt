I am a sound editor, and a recent dispute at work about how to interpret a line of dialogue has sent me back to The Conversation. Harry Caul spends the film trying to recover what two people meant by a recorded phrase, and the film turns on the fact that the meaning may depend on an emphasis he initially misses. Read the film through the debate about authorial intention and textual meaning. Treat the recording as a text and Harry as an interpreter: does the film dramatize the intentional fallacy, or does it show what is lost when we abandon the author's intention? Bring in the distinction between what a speaker meant by an utterance and why they said it, and the difference between an intention recoverable from the work and one only reported after the fact. Consider, too, the idea that interpretation is a form of surveillance—that the interpreter wants to possess the speaker's mind—and say whether the film endorses or indicts that desire. Which scenes show Harry's own intentions shaping what he hears, and where does the film resist being reduced to a case study? End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not.
