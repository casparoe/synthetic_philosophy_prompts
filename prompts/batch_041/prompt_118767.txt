I make short documentaries, mostly local history, and I am currently cutting a piece about a village that was drowned when a reservoir was built in the 1960s: empty rooms, water standing above the lintels, recorded interviews with people who were children when their families were moved out. I want to lay the slow movement of a late string quartet under the whole eight minutes, and my partner — a violist, and the person whose ear I trust more than anyone's — tells me I have the thing backwards. Her argument is not the usual one about mood or atmosphere. She says that music is the only art that copies nothing at all. A painting of a grieving woman is a picture of a grieving woman; a novel about a flood is about a flood; and both of those arts reach us only by way of the idea of the thing they show, so that what moves us is always one remove from the thing itself. Her way of putting it is that the other arts give us copies of copies, whereas music, if it shows us anything, shows us the original. Music pictures nothing and asserts nothing, she says, and yet it moves us more directly and more reliably than either. What we hear in the quartet, on her account, is therefore not the grief of any particular person — not the villagers', not mine — but grief itself, or rather the inner nature of feeling, the substance that all our particular feelings are made of, presented without the concepts that would otherwise let us sort one feeling from another. She thinks this makes the quartet exactly right for the scene and dangerous at the same time: if the music is already the essence of grief, then the images have to earn their place beside it or they become wallpaper. She half-remembers picking the argument up from a book years ago and cannot tell me where, and we have now been arguing about it for three weeks.

What I would like is not a verdict on the quartet but a reconstruction of her position, on the assumption that there is a serious version of it and not only a poetic one. I want to know what it would take for the claim that music expresses feeling without expressing any particular feeling to be more than a flourish — what would have to be true of feeling, and of music, for it to hold — and what such a view must say about the other arts, since her whole case rests on the contrast with painting and literature. I would also like to see the position tested rather than merely stated. The obvious objection is that the same performance can sound sincere to one listener and ironic to another, and that a funeral march can be whistled on the way to a football match; she would say this is a fact about listeners rather than about music, but I want to know whether the position can absorb that objection or whether it has to wave it away, and whether there is any way to tell the difference between a listener who is hearing the music properly and one who is merely projecting.

And then the practical question, which is really what I care about: if she is right that the quartet is grief itself rather than a report of grief, does that give me a reason to trust the music under my footage, or a reason to be suspicious of it? Does it mean the images should be quieter and more factual, so as not to compete with something that is already saying everything, or does the whole idea turn out to be a metaphor that I should not build an edit around?
