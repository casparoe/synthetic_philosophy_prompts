I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and for the closing meeting of a small seminar on teleosemantics I'd like to hand out a poem instead of the usual summary sheet. The view we've been reading — the Millikan–Dretske line, roughly, that a state's content is fixed by the proper function it acquired through a history of selection rather than by its current causal role or its present covariance with the world — has kept tripping us up on the same cluster of cases: the mechanism co-opted for a job it never evolved to do, the signal that fires in the absence of the thing it is supposed to indicate, the creature whose states have no lineage behind them at all. A poem might let us look at that cluster together rather than re-argue it, so I'm asking for one in the voice of the signal itself — not the animal, not the theorist — so that the reader has to feel the strangeness of a state that is about a predator long extinct, or about the wrong thing, or about nothing.

Here is the constraint I care about most, and I'd like you to treat it as load-bearing rather than decorative. Write it as a pantoum, and let the form's central rule — that a line returns in the following stanza and must be re-understood in light of what has since happened to it — stand for the teleosemantic claim that content is inherited from a history of use rather than read off the present moment. Then break the form at precisely the point where the theory says content would fail: pantoums conventionally close by looping back to their opening lines, and I want your last quatrain to refuse that closure, letting a line the form obliges to return simply not return. That missing line should be the severed lineage — the state with no selection history, or the one repurposed for something it was never selected to do — and a reader who has sat through the seminar ought to be able to point at the broken stanza and say "that's the swampman case" or "that's a derived function" without my having to say it first.

Concretely, I want the signal to get three things right: that it is about the distal cause and not the proximal stimulus (the predator, not the ripple that betrays the predator); that it can be tokened wrongly, and that this is a malfunction rather than a quiet change of meaning; and that its aboutness is hostage to a history it cannot inspect from the inside. Please keep it to five quatrains at most, name no theorists or works inside the poem, and resist letting it become a lecture in verse — I would rather have a poem a specialist recognizes as getting the view right than a rhyming précis of it.
