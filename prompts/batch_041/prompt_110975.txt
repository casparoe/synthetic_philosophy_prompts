My grandpa died two weeks ago. I'm 17 and I'm giving the eulogy at his memorial on Saturday and I'm stuck.

Here's the situation. He was a vision scientist before he retired, not famous or anything, but he spent basically his whole career in a perception lab and a few of his old lab people are flying in. The other thing is he's the one who taught me the stuff I want to talk about. There was this one demo he showed me in his garage when I was about 12, and then I rebuilt it for my science fair project, two LEDs, a breadboard, an arduino. It's called the color phi phenomenon. You flash a dot on the left, then a fraction of a second later a dot of a different color on the right. And people dont see two dots. They see one dot moving across, and they see it change color at the halfway point. Which is the problem, because the halfway point is in the middle of the trip, and at that moment the second dot hasnt even been shown yet. So how did you see the color it was going to be? You couldnt of, unless your brain is cheating somewhere.

Grandpa said there's basically two ways to cheat. Option one: your brain sits on the first flash for a beat, waits for the second one, works out the whole story, and only then hands you the finished version. So the fixing happens before you ever experience anything and you never actually had the "wrong" experience at all. He called that the Stalinesque option, like the show trials where the verdict is decided before the trial even starts and the trial is just theater. Option two: you really do experience the first dot, and then a second later your memory gets rewritten so it seems like you saw the color change, fixing it after the fact, like in 1984 where they just change the old newspapers. That one's the Orwellian option. And the whole point is that no experiment can tell the two apart, because they predict that you say exactly the same words when you describe what you saw. So there might be no fact of the matter about when you actually experienced the color change. (I think this is all from a philosopher named Dennett, if that matters. Please dont quote him at me, I never read the book, my grandpa just explained it in the garage.)

Now the hard part. The story everybody in my family tells is that he waited. He was in hospice for about a week, not really talking, and he died about an hour after the last of my cousins got in from out of state. My aunt cried and said he waited for her. Uncle Dan is going to be in the front row, and if I stand up there and say anything that sounds like "well actually we cant know if he experienced that," he's going to be furious, and honestly he'd be right to be, because that's a horrible thing to say at a funeral. But I also cant stand up there and say "he knew you were all there, he felt you come in," because the whole reason I want to talk about any of this is that I dont think we get to just decide that's true. And the lab people in the back are going to be doing the math in their heads the entire time.

So what I want is a eulogy, about five minutes, that actually does this: it uses the color phi thing to say something real about the waiting story, in a way that a room of crying people can follow and that doesnt tell them anything false.
