I teach the medieval survey and every year the flying man passage in Ibn Sina stops me in my tracks -- my students mostly breeze past it, which is worse. Here's the translation we use in class:

"One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence."

I need help getting this straight enough to teach it, so here's what I actually want.

1. Why all the fussy stage directions? Created "at a stroke" and fully formed, eyes veiled, limbs held apart so they don't touch, no perceptible draft of air. Take them one at a time: what does each stipulation rule out, and what happens to the argument if you drop it? (My hunch is that the instant-creation bit is doing way more work than the floating bit, but I can't say why cleanly.)

2. The thing I keep arguing with a student about. He's read a lot of Descartes and says this is the cogito in a robe and turban: the man knows he exists without knowing anything about his body, so the soul is a substance that doesn't need the body, i.e. a proof of immateriality and survival. My gut says thats too fast, but I want the reasoning, not the gut. So: does this passage as written get you all the way to "the soul is an immaterial substance that can exist apart from the body," or only to something weaker -- say, "self-awareness doesn't depend on any particular bodily sensation," or "I can conceive of myself without conceiving my body"? If it's only the weaker thing, what exactly would Avicenna have to add, and does he have it elsewhere?

3. Related, and this is the one that actually nags me: he also holds that the soul is the form of the body, the thing that makes it a living human body rather than a corpse. How does a proof of the soul's independence sit next to that? Is the argument supposed to establish independence of *existence* or just independence of *awareness* -- the self being present to itself without any bodily instrument, so to speak? And what's the strongest objection to the argument, on its own terms? The one my students reach for ("I'm not aware of my liver either, and I definitely can't exist without it") feels too cheap, but tell me honestly whether it's cheap or whether it's the real problem in different clothes.
