I retired from the bench about three years ago — forty years as an analytical chemist, mostly method development — and I've been trying to make up for lost time on philosophy, mostly through a small reading group that meets above a bakery on Thursdays. Last month a woman in the group who actually teaches the stuff told me about something she called the reflection principle (she was clear the name isn't hers). Her version went like this: if you're in a position to know now what you'll believe later, you should just go ahead and believe it now, because otherwise you're insisting that your present self is a better judge than a future self who will have everything you have plus more time and thought. That sounded right to me for about a day. Then I started running cases through it the way I'd run standards through a new instrument, and my gut keeps voting against it. So here are my verdicts. I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to, what it says about the cases I can't call, and which of the verdicts I have to give up. No reading list, please — the stack by my bed is already a hazard.

(1) Sealed envelope. I'm waiting on a replicate analysis of a sample I care about, and I know that tomorrow I'll open the results and come out at 0.9 that the sample is contaminated. Verdict: I should be at about 0.9 right now, before I've seen a thing. It's odd, but I feel like I already have the information.

(2) Before coffee. I know that tomorrow morning, before coffee, I'll read this same argument and think it's a piece of garbage, 0.1 that it's any good. I also know that my pre-coffee self is a crank. Verdict: I shouldn't drop to 0.1, and probably shouldn't move at all.

(3) The pill. A doctor tells me that tomorrow I'll be given a drug that makes people believe, at 0.99, that they grew up in a house with a fireplace, whether or not they did. So I know I'll be at 0.99 about my childhood home. Verdict: obviously I shouldn't believe it now.

(4) The coin. I've arranged that at noon tomorrow I'll settle whether my brother is innocent by flipping a coin, and I'll believe whatever it comes up. Verdict: no. Though I notice this is different from the pill in a way I can't quite state — the coin doesn't even pretend to be about my brother.

(5) Forgetting. I know that by tomorrow the failed replication will have slipped my mind and I'll be back to 0.8 that the effect is real. Verdict: I stay where I am now, 0.3. My future self isn't better informed, he's just leakier.

(6) Rested. I know that tomorrow, after a night's sleep, I'll have redone the error propagation and be at 0.95 that the calibration was off. Verdict: I should go to 0.95 now. And this bothers me, because it has the same shape as (2) — tomorrow my state changes my number — and I trust one and not the other. The only difference I can point to is that in (6) my future self is doing the job better and in (2) he's just in a mood, but that's close to the very thing I'm asking you to explain.

(7) Two glasses of wine. I know that after two glasses I'll put my chances of getting the grant at 0.9, and I know from long experience that wine adds about 0.2 to my estimates of my own prospects. Verdict: I shouldn't go to 0.9; I stay at 0.7, where I already am. Though I can't decide whether the wine is a biased version of a real signal or just noise.

(8) Black box. A doctor says that tomorrow I'll be given a drug she can't characterize. All she knows is that afterwards I'll be at 0.9 that a certain colleague is dishonest. It might be a judgment sharpener; it might be one of those that plants a fixed belief. Verdict: I honestly don't know what to do, and this is the case I most want an answer on.

(9) My gut. My gut has a real record — when it says a grant interview went badly, it's right about 80% of the time — but I have no idea how it works. Tomorrow my gut will say the interview went badly. Verdict: I should move a good deal toward believing it, even though I can't say why. Which seems to contradict (3), since I can't say why that drug works either.

My best attempt at a principle is this: adopt the future belief when the future self got there by doing the job better (more information, more care, a better instrument), and don't adopt it when he got there by something I know is disconnected from whether it's true (a mood, a coin, a drug that plants beliefs). But that leans on the words better and true in a way that seems to assume what I'm asking, and in the black box I can't tell which kind of process is going on, and in the gut case I have the track record without the mechanism. So: the principle, or the two or three that are competing here, stated precisely enough that I can see which case each clause is carrying; what each of them says about (8) and (9); and an honest list of which verdicts I have to give up. If (2) and (6) have to come apart on a line I can't draw from where I'm sitting, tell me that too. I've been going back and forth on this alot, and I'd be happy with a seperate verdict for each case if no single principle survives the list.
