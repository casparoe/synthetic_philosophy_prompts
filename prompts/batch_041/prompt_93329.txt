I'm a third-year philsophy student and I've somehow ended up with the work-in-progress slot at our value theory seminar next Thursday, where I'm presenting an argument I've been kicking around all term about the old torture-versus-dust-specks case. Take the role of a philosopher who's sat through a hundred of these things and knows exactly how this particular room reacts, and think carefully before you write anything — I don't want a summary of my view back, I want a Q&A prep sheet. Here's my position so you know what to shoot at: I think the torture verdict is right (no number of dust specks is worse than one person being tortured), but I don't want to get there by denying that betterness is transitive, because transitivity seems non-negotiable and everyone in that room will pounce on it. So I'm trying to get there by giving up a different thing instead — the assumption that betterness is continuous, i.e. the idea that for any two options, however far apart, there's some number of repetitions of the smaller harm that will eventually outweigh the bigger one. My line is that harms come in two tiers: the ones that are episodes in a life, things you'd live through and remember and that shape who you become, and the ones that fall beneath the threshold of mattering to a life at all, and no pile of the second kind ever overtops a single member of the first. I also want to claim a payoff — if that works, the same discontinuity blocks the repugnant conclusion, which is why I think the population people in the room might actually care instead of just heckling.

The room is about ten people and I know most of them too well. Two are total utilitarians who work on population ethics and know Parfit inside out; they'll want to know whether my threshold is doing any work beyond relocating the repugnant conclusion, and they'll have the standard counterexamples ready to go. One works on vagueness and incommensurability and will insist that I don't need a discontinuity at all, I just need to admit that "better than" is sometimes indeterminate or incomplete between two options. One is a Kantian who thinks the whole specks setup is a symptom of a bad moral framework rather than a hard case. And one is a formal decision theorist who will skip straight to the consequences of dropping continuity — I'm half expecting her to show that my view forces me to trade away any certain good for an absurdly tiny chance of avoiding a life-wrecking harm, and I don't have a good answer to that yet.
