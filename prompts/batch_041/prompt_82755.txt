I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and the week we take up the St. Petersburg paradox has degenerated into an exercise in evasion: my students rehearse the familiar responses, no one stakes out a position, and we adjourn without anyone having been wrong about anything. I want to run the session backward and put my own commitments on the table first, so that the discussion has something to push against. I have therefore written down my honest maximum willingness to pay, in dollars, for the right to play each of the following lotteries exactly once. For the standard game — a fair coin tossed until the first heads, paying me 2^n dollars if that heads first appears on toss n — I would pay about $6. For the same game played against a counterparty whose entire wealth is one million dollars, so that no larger payout could in fact be made, I would pay about $20. For the same game as the standard one, except that the rules stop play after twenty-five tosses, I would pay about $25. For a "super" variant, in which the payout is 2^(2^n) dollars rather than 2^n, I would pay about $6 — the same as for the standard game. For a variant in which the coin is biased toward heads with probability 0.6, I would also pay about $6, though I am aware that this variant's expectation is exactly $6, which makes me suspicious of the coincidence. Finally, for the losing mirror image, in which I pay 2^n dollars if the first heads appears on toss n, I would pay nothing at all to avoid it: I would simply refuse to play, and I would decline even cheap insurance against being compelled to play.

Two of these figures I already suspect are indefensible. In the second and third cases I am reaching deeper into my pocket for a game that is strictly worse than the first, since every outcome of a capped game is no better than the corresponding outcome of the uncapped one; and in the fourth case I am paying no more for a lottery whose tail is unimaginably richer than the standard game's. I want to know what principle, or small family of principles, could underwrite these numbers, and where no such principle can be had without surrendering one of them.

Please state the principle precisely, say what it delivers for each of the six cases, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and then use it to predict my answers in two cases I have not priced: the standard game played one thousand times in succession for a single fee, and the losing game with my total liability capped at one thousand dollars. Be concrete about the mechanism — diminishing marginal utility, a bound imposed by the counterparty's wealth, the weight of the evidence rather than its balance, aversion to imprecise probabilities, a floor on the probabilities I am willing to take seriously, or something else — and do not simply rehearse the received options. There is no need to cite the literature; I know it. I want to watch a principle earn its keep on my numbers.
