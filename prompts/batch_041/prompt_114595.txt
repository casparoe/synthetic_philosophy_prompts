I retired four years ago after 34 years as an analytical chemist — HPLC mostly, plus a decade of arguing with people about detection limits, which I mention because I think that's why this whole thing has got its hooks into me. Since retiring I've joined a philosophy of science reading group that meets Thursdays in the basement of a church near the university (the coffee is geniunely awful; my wife calls it "the cult"), and after last week's session four of us went for dinner and two of the members got into it for about an hour. Ravi is a string theorist who drives over from the physics department, Ellen does philosophy of physics and is much more careful with words than the rest of us, and I was mostly eating and listening and I honestly could not tell you who came off better. Neither of them knows I'm asking you. Here's the exchange as best I can reproduce it: (1) Ravi said the landscape multiverse is a perfectly scientific idea and he's tired of being told otherwise — his case being that string theory isn't a lucky guess, it's what you get when you try to make gravity and quantum mechanics coexist, and it keeps passing internal consistency checks nobody designed it to pass; no rival framework does the same job, every alternative anyone has proposed has failed on some internal requirement, and he said the absence of a serious alternative is evidence, the way it would be in any other field. (2) Ellen said that's a fact about our imaginations, not about the world — the history of science is full of theories that were the only thing going right up until somebody thought of something nobody had imagined, and that's happened often enough that "nothing else works" shouldn't count for much (I think she said there's a whole literature on this argument but I didn't catch the name). (3) Then she went further: she said the multiverse doesn't merely lack a test, it dissolves prediction — to get any probability out of a landscape you need a measure over the vacua, there's no principled one, different measures give different answers, so the framework as it stands says nothing about what we should see; and she added the kicker, that in most of the big measures the overwhelming majority of observers are momentary fluctuations rather than evolved beings, so conditional on the multiverse the probability that our own memories and records are real is minuscule, and a theory that says we're almost certainly wrong about everything including the evidence for the theory has sawed off the branch it's sitting on. (4) Ravi's reply was that the measure problem is a technical gap, not a conceptual one, and not knowing the measure yet is like saying statistical mechanics says nothing until you've specified the ensemble — annoying, but not fatal; and that the fluctuation problem isn't special to the multiverse at all, since any universe that's big enough or lasts long enough has it, including ones Ellen is happy to call respectable, so if the argument works it proves far too much; he also said the LHC null results get used against him unfairly, because the landscape wasn't invented after the fact to explain them, it falls out of the framework and he'd have had it either way. (5) Ellen's answer to that was that a framework which accommodates every value of the constants isn't confirmed by the values we happen to find — if every outcome is equally accommodated, finding this outcome isn't evidence for anything — and that the anthropic move, "we see these values because only these allow observers," doesn't rescue it, because you have to assume a rule about what "I" should expect to observe, a self-location rule, and the two standard versions of that rule disagree with each other about classic cases, sometimes in opposite directions, so the whole apparatus is underdetermined at the most basic level. (6) Ravi said that's a philosophy problem and not a physics problem, and that philosophers disagree about what probability even means in statistical mechanics and quantum mechanics, which nobody treats as a reason to call those unscientific; Ellen said fine, but "the multiverse is unscientific" is itself a methodological claim, methodological claims get argued for by their track record, and the track record favours being stingy about counting "no alternatives" as evidence.

I keep flip-flopping on this and I dont trust my own instincts, so please adjudicate it properly, point by point — who's right about what, not just who won the evening. Neither of them is stupid and neither of them is a crank, which is what makes it maddening, and I'd rather you engage the actual claims than hand me a generic lecture about falsification. Please structure it as follows: "The crux" — one paragraph, roughly 100 words, naming the single disagreement that most of this really turns on; "Move by move" — work through (1) to (6) in order, one short paragraph of about 120 words each, rating the move from clearly wrong through partly right to clearly right and justifying the rating; "What both of them are missing" — one or two paragraphs, roughly 200 words, on the thing neither of them said; and "Overall" — about 100 words on whose position is stronger on balance, and what argument or evidence would flip you.
