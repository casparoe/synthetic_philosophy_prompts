I chair a small grant-making trust. We have eleven trustees and an endowment of about £4 million, out of which any single trustee may authorise a grant of up to £10,000 without consulting the others, provided they write a short justification afterwards. The power exists for speed: we deal with disasters, sudden legal emergencies and other cases where a delay of a fortnight destroys the point of the grant. It worked well enough when there were three of us. At eleven, our own post-hoc reviews suggest that a growing share of what we fund would not have been approved by the board as a whole, and I cannot tell whether that is a fact about the trustees we have recruited or about the arrangement itself.

I have come across the idea of the unilateralist's curse, and I want to know what follows from it for us. As I understand it — and I may have it wrong — the thought is this: when each of a number of people is free to act on their own judgement, and each is liable to overestimate the value of acting, then the more such people there are, the likelier it is that at least one of them will act when the action is in fact a bad one, even though each of them, considered alone, is doing something reasonable, and even though none of them is acting from self-interest.

Please describe the most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for how a body like ours should be governed. I would ask you to take seriously three features of our situation, since I suspect the simplest statement of the idea assumes them away. First, the trustees cannot always confer before acting, and the entire point of the power is that they need not; anything that requires them to confer defeats its purpose. Second, the fund is a common pool: every grant one trustee makes reduces what the others can do, so the cost of a bad grant falls not only on the trust but on the beneficiaries whom a different trustee would have helped. Third, some of our grants are irreversible once made — a payment transferred, a commitment entered into — while others can be paused, revisited and clawed back.

I should say plainly that none of my trustees is self-interested; each authorises a grant because they sincerely believe it is the best use of the money, and each cares about our mission as much as I do. If the idea implies that a body of conscientious, well-motivated people of this kind is especially prone to a certain error, I would like to understand why.

In particular, I would like to know: whether the idea predicts that a trust like ours errs mainly by acting when it should not or by failing to act when it should, and what would let me tell which of the two is actually happening; whether making the trustees more diligent or more devoted to the mission would help or merely alter the pattern of error; whether the size of the body and the level of the unilateral limit should be set by the same considerations, and in which direction; what the alternatives to unilateral authorisation are — I have heard quorums and consensus requirements proposed, and I suspect both are worse than they sound for a body that exists to move quickly — and what each alternative costs in the currency we actually care about; and how far a trustee should discount their own judgement once they know how many others are free to act.

Where the implications depend on assumptions we do not satisfy, please say so rather than smoothing them over. I do not need a reading list; I would rather have the reasoning. Please write in British English.
