I'm in the middle of an argument with a physicist friend that has grown into a short essay for a reading group on statistical mechanics and information, and I want to write my way to an actual verdict rather than a survey of positions. The dispute concerns what the information-theoretic treatment of Maxwell's demon has actually established. My friend's line is that the demon is a closed case: any demon that measures, records, and then re-uses what it has learned must eventually pay Landauer's price -- at least kT ln 2 of heat per bit, with T the temperature of the environment it dumps into -- when it clears its memory, so the second law is safe, and its safety is a theorem of physics. My instinct is that this is too quick. In most textbook treatments, I think, the demon is quietly handed an external, non-physical ledger: it is allowed to record and consult information without itself being part of the system whose entropy is being audited. If you instead insist that the demon is a finite physical system embedded in the same world as the gas, then what saves the second law looks less like a fact about erasure and more like a fact about the demon's own entropy budget; and if you allow the ledger to be infinite, or to be wiped by something outside the system, you have assumed the conclusion.

Write that essay. It should weigh the main ways the demon has been exorcised -- the measurement-cost argument, the erasure-cost argument, and the older trapdoor-and-fluctuation argument -- and then concentrate on a variant I keep returning to, because I think it is where the real disagreement lives: a demon whose memory is never erased. It writes each measurement to a fresh cell, and when the cells run out it simply stops; demon, filled memory, and gas are then left to equilibrate together. Nothing is ever erased, so no Landauer cost is paid anywhere inside the experiment, and yet I take it that the second law is not violated. I want you to say what does the work in that case if erasure does not, and whether the answer is a fact about thermodynamics or only a fact about bookkeeping. Consider as well what happens if the memory is allowed to be infinite, or held at a temperature approaching absolute zero, and whether either variation changes the verdict or merely relocates the cost somewhere you have not looked.

Don't end on a non-committal note. Reach a conclusion and defend it: either the demon really is defeated by the physics of information, in which case say precisely which step does the defeating and why that step is not circular; or the exorcism is at bottom a promissory note dressed up as a theorem, in which case say what a genuine demon would have to do and why we should or should not think it impossible. If your conclusion is conditional -- the second law survives for finite demons and only for finite demons, say -- make the conditions explicit and argue for them. No references or reading lists, please; I care about the argument, not the literature.
