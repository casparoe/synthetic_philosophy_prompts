Bit of an odd request, but here's where I'm at. I grew up in a town of about 1,800 people, my dad drove a grain truck, and I'm now a data analyst at a hospital system in a big city — married, mortgage, the whole deal. Went home for Christmas and my brother made some joke about me being "fancy now" and it turned into a two hour fight about whether his life or mine is the better one, and honestly neither of us could say what we were actually disagreeing about. My mom thinks I look down on them now. My wife thinks my family is anti-intellectual. I don't know what I think, but I can't stop picking at it.

What I want from you is a taxonomy — a tree — of the positions people actually hold on this question, where every branch point is labeled with the question that splits it and every leaf is a position with a short descriptive name and one sentence about the ugliest commitment it has to swallow. I don't want a reading list or citations, and please don't name philosophers at me; I want the shape of the disagreement, not the bibliography. Phrase every branch question so somebody could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — I want this to sort people by what they'd actually say about the cases, not by what they'd call themselves.

Things I want the tree to get right:

- Keep the geography (rural/urban) and the class (working vs. professional) as seperate axes, because in my family they're fused and I suspect they come apart in ways that matter. If a leaf only exists because somebody is quietly sliding between the two, say so.
- Don't let it top out at "everyone has their own values, live and let live." The first branch should be whether there's any fact of the matter here at all, and the branch under that should separate "one is better," "they're equal," and the fourth option, where two ways of life are genuinely comparable but neither is better and they're not equal either (I've seen this called parity and I don't fully understand it, but I want it as its own leaf, not folded into relativism).
- Adaptive preferences need to be a branch, not a footnote — the idea that if someone is content with a narrow life because nothing wider was ever on offer, that contentment might not count as evidence the life is good. That one splits the tree hard and I want to see exactly which leaves it kills.
- Same with "who gets to give reasons": does the side that can defend its way of life in an argument have a real advantage, or is being able to articulate your values just a skill that professional-class people happen to have? I want both readings on the tree, plus the leaf that says the professional outlook's supposed neutrality is itself a values-laden ethos rather than a neutral vantage point.
- One more: whether the fact that none of us chose our values in the first place makes criticizing anyone's values illegitimate. I keep hearing this from both sides and I want to know what it actually commits you to.

Cases I want used, at least these: (1) my cousin taking a steady warehouse job over a "career" job that requires a move and two years of unpaid internships; (2) my nephew spending a bonus on a truck versus putting it in a college fund; (3) me paying $2,300 for a one-bedroom and calling it freedom, versus my mom living where there's one grocery store and everyone knows her business; (4) the complaint that my job doesn't produce anything anyone actually needs.

Aim for ten to fourteen leaves, and if two of them collapse into one, just say that instead of padding. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree definately can't tell apart, and for the branches that come out empty, tell me whether nobody lives there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to work it out.
