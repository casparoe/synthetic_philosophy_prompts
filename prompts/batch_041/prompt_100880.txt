I teach a seminar on self-knowledge and I keep getting tangled up every time we hit Moore's paradox — you know the shape of it, "it's raining out but I don't believe it's raining," and the mirror version "I believe it's raining but it isn't." The thing I tell the students every year is that neither sentence is actually a contradiction, strictly speaking the first one could be true and so could the second, and yet saying either out loud is absurd in a way that feels almost like a contradiction. What I want from you is a tier list of the main candidate diagnoses, ranked by how much of the phenomenon each one really explains, with a sentence or two of justification for each placement. (A colleague and I spent a whole lunch on this and left more confused than we started, so I'd rather see you commit to an ordering than hedge.)

Here are the ones I usually throw on the board, in my own loose shorthand, so we're working with the same list: (a) the idea that uttering the first half already expresses and commits you to the belief, so the second half is a kind of pragmatic self-contradiction even though the two contents are perfectly compatible; (b) a purely normative story, where assertion answers to a belief- or knowledge-norm and the second half simply advertises that you're breaking the norm, so what's absurd is the flaunting rather than any conflict of content; (c) a transparency / first-person-authority story, on which from the inside I can't separate "is p true" from "do I believe p," so disavowing the beleif while asserting p forces me to treat my own mind as an object of third-person prediction, which I can't coherently do about myself; (d) an audience-directed, self-defeating story, where asserting p is meant to hand you a reason to believe p and the second half yanks that reason away, so the speech act eats itself; (e) an evidential story, where I normally take my own beliefs as tracking the truth, so the conjunction forces me to treat my own doxastic state as evidence against p, which is bizarre; and (f) something I half made up and want you to tell me whether it's even distinct from the others — that the two halves answer to different directions of fit, one a claim about the world and one a claim about me, and the absurdity is just the clash of those two registers.

Two follow-ups, since I think they're where the real action is. First, does your ranking shift when we switch between the two forms, "p but I don't believe p" versus "I believe p but not p"? I suspect some of these accounts only do the work for one of the two, and if that's right, tell me which ones come out lopsided. Second, if any one of these turned out to be the whole story, which would most change what you think the paradox is even about — which one, if true, makes it a problem about language, which about the nature of belief, and which about first-person authority?
