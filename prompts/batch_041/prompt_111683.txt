I have no academic training in philosophy, but for several years I have been reading histories and survivor testimony of mass atrocities, and one question has come to dominate my thinking about them: whether it is possible, or whether it is obscene, to rank one historical evil against another. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of historical and comparative judgment, and to think carefully and at length before answering. Please begin with a brainstorming pass: lay out the candidate principles that might govern these judgments, and say where each of them strains, before you commit to a considered answer.

To give you something to work with, here is what I actually think, case by case, going by my reactions rather than by any theory. I am quite comfortable saying that a massacre that killed a few hundred people is not as bad as a genocide that killed millions. I am not comfortable ranking the Holocaust against the Rwandan genocide, even though the death tolls differ enormously; asked to choose, I feel I am being asked to do something indecent. I think the centuries-long enslavement of millions of Africans in the Atlantic world was worse than any single genocide, because it was sustained across generations. I think that saying a swift massacre was in some way kinder than a life of enslavement is obscene, even if there is a sense in which it is true. I think a deliberate attempt to annihilate a people is worse than a famine of the same death toll that nobody intended. But I also think the great Bengal famine of the 1940s, which killed millions, was worse than many deliberate massacres that killed far fewer. I think ranking atrocities is fine in a courtroom that has to decide reparations, fine in a history book, and obscene as the premise of a joke. I think it is obscene to say that all atrocities are equally bad. And I am fairly sure that ranking atrocities is acceptable only so long as it does not amount to ranking the worth of the people who suffered them, though I cannot see how to keep the two apart.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: whether it is obscene to say that the destruction of a people's language, religion and way of life, without killing them, can be as bad as killing them.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I can apply them myself. For each principle, tell me which of my verdicts it captures and which case forced each of its clauses. Then tell me, honestly, which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should give that verdict up. Show me what your preferred principle implies about cases I have not mentioned, including at least one where I would probably find its implication repellent, and say something about the case I keep going back and forth on. If the honest answer is that my question is really two questions that I have been running together, say so, and separate them.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I will look up the literature myself, and I would rather have your reasoning than a map of it.
