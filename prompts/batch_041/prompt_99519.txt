Hey, I'm working on a short paper on Henry Sidgwick and his sideline in psychical research, and I want someone to poke holes in the draft before I show it to my advisor. What I'm pasting below isn't the full essay yet, just the skeleton of the arguement plus the parts I'm least sure about, so please comment on it as if it were the real thing.

The claim: people usually treat Sidgwick's involvement with the Society for Psychical Research (he was a founder and its first president back in 1882) as either a quaint Victorian eccentricity or as a private hunt for consolation, i.e. the man could no longer subscribe to the Thirty-Nine Articles, resigned his Trinity fellowship over it in 1869, died an agnostic, and went looking for ghosts to fill the hole. I think both readings are too shallow. My reading is that the psychical research was continuous with the central problem of The Methods of Ethics (1874), namely the dualism of practical reason: egoism and universal benevolence each look rationally defensible, and nothing above them settles the conflict. Sidgwick didn't need immortality exactly, he needed a morally governed universe, some structure in which the gap between what's good for me and what's right gets closed. Psychical research was the empirical arm of that project. Survive death, live in a cosmos with a moral order, and the dualism dissolves, because the individual's long-run interest and the demands of morality converge. Yes, I know the standard objection, that Sidgwick kept theological assumptions out of ethics proper and kept the research in a separate compartment, but I want to argue the compartment wall is thinner than it looks.

Here's where I want to push. I claim there's a structural mismatch between what psychical research could in principle deliver and what Sidgwick needed. Survival evidence at its best supports something like: the personality persists. That's a claim about the duration of the self. What the argument needs is a claim about the governance of the world: that virtue is eventually rewarded, that moral progress can go on without limit, that the universe is not indifferent to how you behave. Different claims, and the first doesn't even make the second probable. You could persist forever in a cosmos that doesn't care what you do. And suppose you did establish the governance claim. I think you'd be worse off, not better: morality would then be re-grounded in prudential calculation about cosmic rewards and punishments, which is precisely what Sidgwick didn't want, and the position collapses back into a refined egoism. So the whole thing either under-delivers or self-destructs.

Second thing I want comments on: I compare this to Kant, who just postulates the immortality of the soul so that the highest good is achievable. My line is that Sidgwick takes a postulate and converts it into a research program, braver in some ways, but it makes his ethics hostage to empirical results in a way Kant's never is. Not sure if that's a real contrast or if I'm dressing up a platitude.

So, four asks: (1) where am I getting Sidgwick wrong? (2) is the under-delivers-or-self-destructs dilemma actually a dilemma, or is there an obvious escape hatch I'm missing? (3) does the Kant comparison earn its keep, or should I cut it? (4) be honest, is this interesting or just clever? Write your comments in American English, and no need for reading lists or citations, I just want the argument worked over. Don't be gentle, I'd rather hear the objections now.
