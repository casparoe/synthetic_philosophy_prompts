I run the qualification lab at a small firm that certifies sealing, coating, and polymer components for harsh environments. Our recurring question — asked dozens of times a week — is whether a given component actually has a disposition we care about: the disposition to resist corrosion under a specified exposure, to hold pressure at temperature, to stay flexible after thermal cycling. Test results do not settle this by themselves. A component can fail because it lacks the disposition, or because something in the rig blocked the disposition from manifesting; it can pass because it has the disposition, or because some unrelated property happened to produce the same observable result. And sometimes the very act of applying the trigger changes what the component is disposed to do, so the test destroys the disposition it was meant to measure. I want a decision procedure, grounded in the metaphysics of dispositions, that a junior technician can run on a raw test result and that produces a defensible written verdict.

Treat the following vocabulary as fixed for our shop, and build the procedure around it. A mask is a condition that prevents a disposition from manifesting when its trigger occurs without altering the disposition itself; masks may be intrinsic to the component or imposed by the rig or environment. A fink is a condition that the trigger itself brings about and that alters the disposition, so that once triggered the component no longer has the disposition, or has a different one. A mimic is a distinct property that produces the same observable manifestation the disposition would have produced, yielding a false positive. A reverse fink is a condition under which the trigger removes an interference, so that the disposition manifests only because something that would have blocked it was destroyed. Our verdict set is exactly four labels: disposition present, disposition absent, inconclusive because masked, and inconclusive because finked. A verdict of present or absent must be justified without appeal to how much we want the component to ship.

The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, where a branch terminates, and where it hands the decision to a person. Make it usable in about thirty minutes by someone with no philosophy background, running on a completed test record, with a senior engineer signing off on any escalation. It must say explicitly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It has to handle all of the following: dispositions whose manifestation is probabilistic rather than all-or-nothing; multi-track dispositions, where a single underlying property would show up in several distinct tests; cases where the trigger and the mask are the same event; cases where the trigger is irreversible, so the obvious move — repeat the test with the suspected interferer removed — is unavailable, and the procedure must say what to do instead; and cases where the only evidence available is a passing test, so the question is whether the pass reflects the disposition or a mimic.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a polymer seal that passed our standard cold-bend test in the lab but cracked in the field at a temperature well inside the passing range; decide whether the lab result, the field result, or neither establishes the disposition. Second, a coating that failed an accelerated salt-spray test, where the failure could plausibly be a rig artifact — decide whether we are entitled to call the disposition absent or must call it masked. Third, a component we have been shipping for years on the strength of passing tests, where on review every passing test admits a plausible mimic; I expect this one to end in a verdict we will not like, and the procedure should be able to deliver it anyway.

Finish with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will misclassify, the inputs it cannot supply for itself, and the point at which the remaining question is about acceptable risk rather than about what the component is disposed to do. Do not ask for citations or reading lists; the output should stand on its own as a working document for the lab.
