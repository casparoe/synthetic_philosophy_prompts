I have been asked to open a reading group on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and the chapter I have been given to present is a popularising one. Before I stand up in front of people who know the literature far better than I do, I should like an accuracy audit of it. The substance of what the chapter says is as follows.

Its first paragraph runs roughly like this: 'Aumann's agreement theorem shows that two rational agents who share a prior — that is, who begin with broadly the same background beliefs — cannot agree to disagree. If each knows how the other is reasoning, then whatever probability one assigns to an event, the other must assign the same. Disagreement between rational people is therefore always the product of irrationality or of private information, and once the private information is pooled it must disappear. This is why the persistent disagreement we observe in markets and politics is better explained as a failure of rationality than as an honest difference of opinion.'

Its second paragraph claims that the same logic governs games: 'If it is common knowledge that every player is rational — by which I mean that everyone knows it — then in any finite game of perfect information the backward-induction outcome is the only one consistent with that common knowledge. Hence in the centipede game the first player takes the money at once, cooperation in a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma is impossible for rational players, and promises exchanged in such settings are not credible. The theory thus explains why so many real negotiations collapse into mutual defection.'

I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in the second paragraph, though I cannot say where, and I am equally prepared to be told that I am misreading terms that are being used in a technical sense. I should rather not go in and accuse the chapter of an error it does not make.

What I want is the audit itself, not a summary of your own. Go through the two paragraphs sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, what a game theorist working on epistemic foundations would endorse, what they would wince at, and what is simply false. Where a sentence distorts, say precisely what has been run together — which two claims the chapter has merged, or which substantive assumption has been smuggled in as though it followed from rationality alone — and say what a defender of the underlying results would concede and what they would insist upon. Tell me where the chapter is right as well as where it is wrong, and which of the two results each of its claims is actually entitled to draw on. Finally, say whether the closing claims about markets, politics and negotiation follow from anything true in the two paragraphs, and if they do not, what would have to be added for them to follow.
