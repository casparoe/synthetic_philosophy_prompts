I'm in a seminar on early modern philosophy, and a friend and I have spent a few evenings trying to reconstruct the strongest version of the cogito — the inference from my thinking to my existing — rather than just repeating the textbook gloss. We built the following argument ourselves, and I now genuinely cannot tell whether it is a proof or a trap:

(1) The proposition that I do not exist cannot be entertained without the entertainer of it existing; the act of denying one's own existence undermines itself. (2) So whatever else may be uncertain, the existence of the thinker is certain at the moment of the act of thinking. (3) That certainty is indexed to the act: what cannot be denied is the occurrence of thinking, not the persistence of any particular owner of the act. (4) But no act of thinking is instantaneous — to think that I doubt, I must already retain the doubt I am judging about, so the thinking that is certain is already spread over time. (5) Whatever is spread over time and is the subject of its own acts is a substance, i.e. a thing existing in its own right that could in principle exist without a body. (6) Therefore the cogito proves not merely that something thinks but that I am an immaterial, persisting soul. (7) Yet if the pattern in (1)–(5) is sound, it will work for any state I indubitably have at the moment I have it: my being in pain would prove that I am essentially a feeler of pain, and my imagining a winged horse would prove that I am essentially an imaginer of winged horses. (8) I cannot be essentially all of these things, so something in (1)–(5) is wrong.

My friend insists the culprit is (3), and that we should read the cogito as securing only a momentary occupant of the act of thinking and never an owner of it; I suspect the slide is really somewhere around (4) and (5), though I can't articulate why, and I worry that giving up (3) costs more than he thinks, since it threatens to leave the cogito too weak to be worth having. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (2) if I can — I want the cogito to prove something — but not at the price of (8). So tell me which premise looked innocent and why it looked that way, then price the alternatives: for each candidate step I might give up, what do I forfeit in the rest of what I wanted the cogito to establish, and which loss is the least bad?
