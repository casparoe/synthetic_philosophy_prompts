I'm presenting a paper at a small workshop on the metaphysical views of twentieth-century logicians, and I want a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion afterward. My thesis is that Gödel's belief in an afterlife is not a sentimental footnote to his logic but a consequence of two doctrines he held jointly: first, his anti-mechanism — the argument, drawn from the incompleteness results, that the mind is not a finite machine — and second, his rationalistic optimism, the conviction that the world is rationally organized and that reason can in principle decide every meaningful question. On my reading, anti-mechanism makes the mind's survival at least possible, and rationalistic optimism upgrades that possibility to something we should expect, so that his ontological argument and his scattered remarks about a hereafter belong to one package rather than to a private eccentricity.

The audience falls into three groups. Two Gödel scholars — a proof theorist and a historian of logic — who know the Nachlass and the secondhand reports of his conversations and are allergic to reading his informal metaphysical remarks as a system. Four or five analytic philosophers of religion, mostly theists, who would like to recruit Gödel as an ally but whose own picture of the afterlife is personal, gratuitous, and bound up with resurrection and grace. And two naturalists who think the whole thing is wishful thinking dressed up in logic.

Prepare me for the questions: the objections I'll actually get from each group, phrased the way that group would put them, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by who raises them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front, and finish with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
