I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm running the problem sessions for a large introductory decision theory course. Every year the same puzzle comes back — two envelopes, one holds twice what the other holds, you pick one at random and are then offered the other — and every year I get a fresh version of it from a student, from my officemate, or from someone at a reception, usually with a confident and mutually inconsistent solution attached. I've started keeping a list of my own verdicts on the variants, and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether they hang together or whether I'm just reporting whatever feels right case by case. I've read the standard treatments, and they all dispatch the simplest version and then stop; what I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that covers the whole list below — stated precisely enough that I can apply it to cases that aren't on the list — together with a frank statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Please write your answer in American English.

The list. (1) I look in my envelope and see $10. The host tells me, and I believe him, that the other envelope holds either $5 or $20, each with probability one half. Verdict: swap; this one seems obvious, and I'd defend it to anyone. (2) I don't look, and all I know is that one envelope holds twice the other. Verdict: swapping is a wash — my situation is perfectly symmetric between the two envelopes, so nothing can be gained, and I'd say the same whether or not the swap is offered. (3) A friend's argument: let x be what I hold; the other envelope holds 2x or x/2 with equal probability; so the expectation of swapping is 1.25x, which exceeds x; therefore I should always swap. Verdict: the conclusion is wrong, since it contradicts (2), but I've never been able to say more than 'the two cases aren't equally likely,' which feels like a restatement of the problem rather than a diagnosis. (4) Now suppose the smaller amount is chosen uniformly from the whole dollars 1 through 100, the pair (m, 2m) is formed, the envelopes are shuffled and handed to me, and I may look before deciding. Verdict: there is a definite right answer, and it depends on what I see — I should swap if I see any amount up to and including $100, and keep if I see anything above that — and yet a blanket policy of always swapping is still exactly a wash. I brute-forced this, and the look-then-decide rule beats both always-swapping and never-swapping by about nineteen dollars in expectation, which I find surprising, because it means that merely looking at a number, with no new information about the mechanism, is worth something. (5) Finally: I look and see $32, and this time nobody tells me anything at all about how the amounts were chosen — all I know is that one is twice the other. Verdict: here I feel the pull of my friend's argument from (3); I seem to have no reason to think the other envelope is likelier to hold $16 than $64, so I should swap. But I also believe (2), that looking at a number cannot turn a wash into an improvement, and I can't hold both.

So what principle, or principles, does this list add up to? I want the actual diagnosis of the argument in (3) — not the observation that its conclusion is false, but the step that fails, and why something like that step succeeds in (4) and fails there. I want to know whether (5) forces me to abandon (2), or (3), or neither, and in what sense. And I want the principle's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: the same setup as (4) with the smaller amount uniform on 1 through N for arbitrary N, and then with N unknown to me; a version in which the host keeps offering me the swap after each refusal, so that I could swap back and forth indefinitely; and a version in which the smaller amount is drawn from a distribution with infinite mean, so that both envelopes have infinite expected value. Where my verdicts conflict, tell me which one has to go, and show me how the surviving principle handles each of the five cases rather than only the ones it was built for.
