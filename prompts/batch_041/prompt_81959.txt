Ok so I'm running a session for a weird cross-listed seminar, half lit students and half history-of-philosophy students, and I want to read The Island of Doctor Moreau through Descartes on animal minds. Not the cartoon version where Descartes says animals are machines and that's that. I mean the version that's actually in the texts, where he seems willing to let animals have sensation, fear, hunger, anger, while still insisting they don't think, and where the two tests he offers for a mind are genuine language and general-purpose adaptability rather than any particular bit of behaviour. Moreau's island is basically a vivisectionist's utopia built on something like that premise: the creatures he makes can talk, they recite a law to each other, they ask whether they aren't men, and then most of them slide back into what the book treats as their old nature. I want a reading that takes the novel as a novel and Descartes as philosophy and says honestly where each pushes back on the other. Does the creatures' speech and rule-following refute the beast-machine on Descartes' own terms, or does Wells cheat by giving them human bodies first?
