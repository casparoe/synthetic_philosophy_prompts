For the last several weeks a small reading group I sit in on has been working through the philosophical literature on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals, and at dinner after the final session someone remarked that the whole debate seems built to be funny and yet nobody has ever heard a good joke about it. I would like you to fix that, and to write me a joke rather than an essay. The engine I want you to work with is the claim the group spent two hours failing to settle: what makes an original valuable is nothing you can see or hear in it but its causal history — that this canvas was covered in paint by that hand, in that workshop, in that tradition — with the consequence that a forgery succeeds exactly to the degree that it makes that difference imperceptible, so that the better the forgery, the more forcibly it demonstrates that the appearance was never what we valued; a perfect forger is therefore the strongest possible evidence for the view that origins matter, and the art world's response is to punish him for proving it. A related phenomenon we kept circling, and which may serve you just as well: an exposed forgery recovers its interest the moment it is relabeled, since the same object with a new and true story attached becomes worth looking at again, now as a document of the forger's skill and of our credulity; some museums keep a debunked forgery on view for precisely that reason, and some leave the empty frames of stolen pictures hanging on the wall, as though the label and the place had been doing the work all along. A third possibility, if you can make anything of it: restoration, and the point at which a heavily repaired original has become a convincing fake of its own earlier self.

Please give me two or three versions, each short enough to be told aloud in under a minute — a setup and a punchline, or a brief exchange between a formalist who insists that only the surface matters and a contextualist who insists that the history does — and after each one a single sentence saying which of the points above the punchline turns on, so that I can see whether you have aimed at the right target. The joke has to be funny to someone who actually knows the debate: the punchline should turn on the content of the positions rather than on the thought that art experts are pretentious, that modern art is easy to fake, or that critics invent their verdicts; it should not require the listener to recognize any particular artist, museum, or scandal, since I want to be able to tell it at a seminar without a footnote; and it should be clean enough for a seminar dinner, with nothing put into the mouth of a real person.
