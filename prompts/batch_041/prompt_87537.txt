I'm a phil PhD student working on Smith's moral psychology, and I keep coming back to something that obviously didn't exist in his day: subscription apps that sell you a moral second opinion. You describe whatever you're about to do -- fudge a colleague's data, ghost a friend, rat out a coworker -- and the app gives you a verdict, where the app is trained on how a big pool of strangers judge stories like yours. So it's the impartial spectator, except it's paid, machine-run, and made of aggregated strangers. Would Smith have seen that as finally delivering the spectator we're meant to be imagining -- more detached than our friends, less self-serving than our own inner voice -- or as corrupting the whole mechanism, since on his account we're supposed to do the imagining ourselves and be moved by the pull of mutual sympathy? Take a view and defend it: what would he have latched onto, where would he have refused to update, and which parts of his own account would he have to give up or stretch to get his verdict out? And do you think he'd say the app tracks praiseworthiness, or just praise?
