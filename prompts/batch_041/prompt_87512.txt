A friend of mine in a policy seminar keeps saying nuclear deterrence is just a game of chicken: two drivers racing at each other, whoever swerves first loses, and the winner is whoever can convince the other guy he'll never swerve. From that he concludes the smart move is to throw your own steering wheel out the window -- predelegate launch authority, cultivate a reputation for being a bit unhinged, make your own side the one that can't back down -- and he says this is basically what Schelling showed about brinkmanship. I want an audit of it. Give me the correspondence table first (who's driving, what counts as swerving, what the crash is, what the steering wheel is, what the road is), then tell me where the picture breaks. Be specific about which breaks actually matter for his conclusion and which are just window dressing -- I suspect the ones about repeated play, private information about who's really resolved, third parties standing on the sidewalk, and the fact that a crash doesn't crown a winner but ends the game, all matter a lot. And I have a nagging feeling that deliberately leaving part of the outcome to chance is not the same move as welding your wheel in place, so say whether the analogy is even representing the right thing. Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the chicken picture carrying the whole argument? Also tell me what a better analogy would be, and what it predicts for the case he keeps skipping -- why two nuclear powers might still happily shoot at each other in some faraway country while the big crash stays unthinkable.
