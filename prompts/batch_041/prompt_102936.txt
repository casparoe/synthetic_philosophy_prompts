I teach a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of probability — sixteen students, all of them fluent with expected value and comfortable with a little calculus, none of them trained in measure theory. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the St. Petersburg problem with their hands rather than read it off the page: by the end they should be unable to say whether the fair price for the lottery is very large, very small, or simply undefined, and they should see that the difficulty is not merely about diminishing marginal utility. Please design the activity and its debrief.

Here is the scaffolding I would like you to develop, adjusting the mechanics wherever my version is clumsy. Stage one: a casino with a finite, publicly posted bankroll — the house can pay out at most a stated large sum before it goes broke. Students take turns running the house and playing against it, and the lottery is the familiar one that pays 2^n with probability 2^-n. Run enough rounds that the empirical payoffs pile up at the bottom and almost everyone walks away with a pittance while the infinite expectation sits in plain view on the board. Then put the group in role as a pricing committee: they must agree on a single posted price for a ticket and defend it against the rival factions in the room. Stage two, which is the part I care most about and which I have not seen staged before: after the first lottery, offer a second lottery that stochastically dominates the first at every outcome while retaining the same infinite expectation, and require the group to rank the two and to justify the ranking by expected value. They should find that they cannot, and that the reasoning which told them the first lottery was worth any finite price now tells them the second is worth the same — and then a third, and a fourth, without end. The intuition I want them to arrive at is that the paradox is a symptom of a deeper breakdown in comparing options of infinite expectation, not a puzzle about risk aversion that a concave utility function can wave away.

Please give me: the rules and materials; the payoff scheme in tokens or points, with nothing staked that costs real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the roles or factions; the exact mechanics of the second-stage ranking and how the facilitator keeps it from collapsing into a shouting match; and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto a part of the paradox and onto the standard families of responses — capping the bankroll, bounding utility, restricting attention to finitely additive probabilities, treating the expectation as simply undefined — so that the students leave with the menu of moves and their costs rather than with a single verdict. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially where the arithmetic or the role-play will bog down, and how to recover. And be candid about what the activity cannot do: name the feature of the problem that no finite game can reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
