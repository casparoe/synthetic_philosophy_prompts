Hey — phil PhD student here (value theory, mostly metaethics and practical reason). I need a poem to kick off a grad workshop on incommensurable values and hard choices, and I'd rather it not be the usual fork-in-the-road mush. Can you take on the role of a poet who actually knows the value-theory literature and write me something?

Think carefully and start by brainstorming, just a few lines: what poetic form or device could do structural work here, rather than the poem merely talking about the idea? I don't want it to announce that values are incommensurable; I want its shape to make that felt. My rough thought is a poem built on two measures or two refrains that never rhyme with each other and never resolve into a couplet — no real volta, no closing turn that adjudicates — so the form keeps trying and failing to convert one good into the other's currency. Take that as a starting point, not a spec.

Use a concrete case. Something like a conservator who has to choose between stabilizing a fresco so it survives the next century and leaving the artist's surface alone out of respect for the thing as it actually is. Pick your own if you have a better one, but keep it specific and unglamourous.

Length: 14-20 lines, no fixed form required, but keep whatever constraint you choose consistent. It should be accuarate enough that someone who works on this stuff nods instead of wincing, and good enough to read aloud to people who don't.

Then, in a short note after the poem (not part of it): say how confident you are that the poem is getting the ideas right, and flag the one point you're least sure of — including if you're leaning on a contested reading of what incommensurability amounts to, or of where the line falls between incommensurable and merely hard-to-compare.
