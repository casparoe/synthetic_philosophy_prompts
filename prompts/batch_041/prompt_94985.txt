So I'm in a philosophy of economics reading group and somehow I've ended up being the one running the session on revealed preference and what "utility" is even supposed to mean — and the problem is everyone walks in already able to recite the standard line about Samuelson making preference a matter of choice and the whole thing being about representation theorems, and then we all nod and go home. I want a handout that actually forces people to commit, so I've decided to make it a decision tree instead of the usual list of discussion questions. Here's what I want: a branching tree where every internal node is a plain-language question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf is a named position on what utility is. Build it from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and then drop the actual positions into that space, with the empty slots flagged and briefly diagnosed (is nobody there because the view is incoherent, or just unattractive, or just unexplored?). If two leaves come out indistinguishable on this tree, say so out loud instead of papering over it.

The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order I think they cut deepest: (a) is a utility function a description of something actually in the person's head, or just a compact way of summarizing their choices — i.e., is the thermometer analogy the right one; (b) can a person be wrong about their own preferences, or does preferring just mean being disposed to choose that way; (c) if there is something in the head, is it hedonic (quantities of pleasure and pain) or desire-ish (degrees to which states of affairs are preferred), and can those two come apart in a single case; (d) do the numbers carry cardinal meaning — can we add them up and compare them within or across people — or are they purely ordinal; (e) is the whole vocabulary of preference doing any explanatory work at all, or is it a wheel that turns nothing, in which case I want a leaf for the eliminativist who says we should just talk about choice dispositions and stop. I'd like six to eight leaves, and I don't want the obvious labels just lined up in a row — I want the tree to seperate views that sound identical but answer (a) and (b) differently.

Don't worry about citing anyone or giving me reading, I just need the structure and the plain-English phrasing of the nodes — and please make each node a question about a concrete case or a straight yes/no claim, not "do you believe in hedonic utilitarianism?", because the whole point is that people should discover their answer rather than grab the label they already like. Oh, and one more thing: a couple of the leaves should be positions nobody in the group has ever heard of, so people can't just pattern-match to the four views they already know.
