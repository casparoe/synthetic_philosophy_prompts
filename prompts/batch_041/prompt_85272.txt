I'm deep in a novel — 300 years, one stretch of drained fenland, one family, a plot of land that keeps sinking after they pump it dry in the 1650s and nobody notices for two centuries. And I keep running into the same wall, which is that I need some account of how history actually moves before I can decide what my chapters are even about. Where do I put the camera? Is the storm of 1810 the story, or the fact that the soil was going to waste away under them regardless of what anybody did in 1810?

So before I read any more Braudel (I've got the big Mediterranean book and I keep bouncing off it), give me the spec. What would a theory of historical change in that long-duree vein actually have to deliver to be worth building a book on? I want the requirements list — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each one, and your call on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just house preferences of the Annales crowd.

Things I already know I need it to do, so you can tell me if I'm wrong about any of them:

- hold several timescales at once without secretly reducing one to another, and say how a slow variable actually produces a fast event, not just that they sit side by side;
- explain persistence as well as change with the same machinery — why the inheritance customs survive three revolutions and the dialect survives the railway, while other stuff turns over in a decade;
- tell me when an individual is causally load-bearing and when he's just the mouth the structure talks through, without going limp on both;
- give me something like a measure of how much of what happened had to happen. I need to be able to write "this could have gone otherwise" and have that be licensed rather than me just being wistful;
- not contradict what the soil scientists and the climate people and the demographers actually say about my slow variables, and not turn geography into destiny, because the same marsh gives me a very different village in 1700 and 1900;
- be clear about whether structure/conjuncture/event are three kinds of thing or just the same events seen at different focal lengths, because my chapters depend on the answer;
- keep my people's own reasons in play — I can't write a novel where motives are froth;
- and, this is the one I care about most and never see on anybody's list: be usable. It has to tell me what to leave out, where to slow down, where the hinge is. A theory of history that can't be narrated isn't any use to me.

Then two things. First, flag any pair of these that I can't have together — I suspect at least two of them are in flat contradiction and I'd rather find out now. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most damage, i.e. knocks out the most candidates. You can score Braudel-style geohistory, old-fashioned event-and-great-men narrative, and the pure contingency school against the list if it's quick, but the list is the deliverable, dont bury it.
