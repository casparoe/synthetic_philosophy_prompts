Someone I argue with keeps invoking the expanding circle — the historical sweep from abolition to suffrage to animal welfare — as though it settled what we owe to present-day AI systems, and he adds a sharper premise: that because the circle has no natural boundary, any proposed stopping point is arbitrary, so it must keep widening. I suspect the slogan is doing several jobs at once and that at least one of them is empty. Lay out the distinct precise claims the phrase could be making — a descriptive generalization about the past, a normative injunction, a prediction, a claim about the essential structure of moral reason (that it is constitutively universalizing), or a near-tautology about whatever "we" happen to include — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which readings, if any, actually license his two inferences: from past expansions to future ones, and from the absence of a natural boundary to an obligation to keep expanding. I want a menu to take back to him, not a verdict on the metaphor.
