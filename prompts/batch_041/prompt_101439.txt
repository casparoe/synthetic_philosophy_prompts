I cover philosophy of science for a small online magazine, and I've been sitting on an idea for a while that I'd like to turn into an actual proposal — it's for one of those public-philosophy micro-grants, six months, not much money, and the deliverable is a short working paper plus a piece for a general audience. Here's the half-formed thing. Every time I've watched philosophers fight about Hempel's raven paradox, the fight seems to be about two different questions at once: whether a non-black non-raven (a white shoe, a red herring, whatever) *confirms* "all ravens are black" in the logician's sense, and whether you should actually raise your credence in the hypothesis when you learn about the shoe. And I've started to think the whole thing turns on a distinction nobody quite names — between confirmation as a relation that only needs an evidence sentence and a hypothesis, and confirmation as something that also depends on how the evidence was gathered, i.e. the sampling plan or what partition of the world you were checking against. The or-trick where you rewrite "all ravens are black" as "everything is either black or a non-raven" makes the paradox vivid, but I suspect it also makes it artificial, because it silently changes what counts as a "trial."

Can you write me the proposal? I want the question stated so it has a determinate answer — something like: under what conditions, if any, does an observed non-black non-raven raise rational credence in "all ravens are black," and does the answer depend on making the sampling procedure explicit? Then say why the existing camps (the Bayesian "it confirms a tiny bit" people, the "it's irrelevant because of the reference class / the shoe is a red herring" people, and whoever thinks Nicod's criterion should just be abandoned) don't already settle it, what I'd actually do month by month, and what would count as the project failing — I'd honestly rather have a clean negative result than a fuzzy essay. Scope-wise keep it small and real: I'm one person, part-time, no lab, and I'd rather the method be "build a small library of explicit-sampling cases and see which principles survive" than anything requiring formal proofs. Don't do citations or a reading list, I'll graft those on later.
