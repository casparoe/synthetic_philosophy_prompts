For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on time and thermodynamics — roughly eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute session, a room with a whiteboard, masking tape, index cards, and a deck of cards, and no computers — design a runnable activity rather than a demonstration. The students themselves should be the micro-constituents of a physical system, and the rules should be arranged so that they collide with a specific situation before it is named. The microdynamics they execute must be exactly reversible, and they must prove this to themselves by running the system backwards and landing on the precise starting state; yet nothing in those dynamics may single out a direction. The asymmetry they eventually locate has to be in the boundary condition at one end of the run, not in the rules. A second thing they should feel from the inside: their own recollections are physical records sitting on one side of the very boundary condition at issue, so memory cannot be used to break the tie.

Constraints:

- Runnable by a single facilitator with the listed materials, no apparatus beyond paper and tape. State the setup and the floor plan.
- The update rule must be a genuinely invertible map on the microstate. Do not assume that a naive simultaneous-move rule with collisions is reversible; either specify an update scheme whose invertibility is exact and say how you know it is, or use a scheme in which the state is a permutation and each step is a fixed invertible shuffle. Sketch a lattice-gas-style default (students as particles on a taped grid, holding cards that bear directions) and give the permutation scheme as a fallback if the physical version proves ambiguous at the edges.
- There must be a blind, individual commitment round — a bet or a sealed guess, with stakes that need no money — in which students say which of two presented snapshots came later, with a substantial minority of items drawn from runs whose special low-entropy condition sits at the far end. Their default heuristic (more spread out means later) must fail on enough items that the failures, not the successes, become the hinge of the debrief.
- The system must begin in a state everyone independently agrees is orderly and end in one everyone independently agrees is disorderly, without the facilitator adjudicating which is which.
- No lecture up front; the target thesis should not be announced as the topic before the debrief.

Deliver: a phase-by-phase timeline with minutes; materials and setup; the update rule written out precisely enough to execute, with the invertibility check; the betting round's items, stakes, and scoring; and a debrief that maps each phase onto a component of the analysis — the time-reversal symmetry of the laws, the specialness of the initial condition, the counting or typicality basis of entropy increase, the fact that the reversal itself required constructing an extraordinarily special microstate, and the direction-of-inference point about records and memory. Close with an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce, at minimum: the measure-theoretic justification for calling entropy increase typical; the cosmological question of why the boundary condition is what it is; the fact that a twenty-particle system will show fluctuations and recurrences that a system of order 10^23 will not; and the difference between statistical typicality and the practical impossibility of fine control.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including at least: where the collision bookkeeping will break down and how to pre-empt it; the students who will call the reversal a cheat, and how to use that reaction rather than defuse it; and what to do if the betting round turns out to be too easy.
