I'm a 4th-year PhD student in logic (my thesis is on resource-bounded reasoning, so I'm already in the weeds on this stuff) and I pay rent two days a week on the "rules as code" team at a legal-aid nonprofit — we take chunks of benefits legislation and turn them into an executable ruleset so caseworkers can run eligibility instead of trying to read the statute. We keep having the same fight: the engine spits out a result, someone says its obviously wrong, and the room splits between "add a rule and fix it" (stay inside the system) and "override the engine, the statute clearly didn't mean that" (step outside it).

I keep thinking about the MU puzzle — the MIU system, start from MI, four rewrite rules, and you can never get to MU, and the reason you can never get there is only visible from outside, via the invariant about the number of I's not being divisible by 3. As I read it, the point is that some questions can't be answered by grinding the rules, and that the move to the meta level is the creative move rather than a cop-out. I don't need a reading list, I've read the stuff, I just need the procedure.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a disputed case. Some things I want pinned down:

- the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure just stops with a no — i.e. "this is an in-system case, the engine's answer stands, stop arguing";
- where it hands off to a human instead of deciding, because the question is about what the legislature meant rather than what the ruleset derives. We have a policy lead who has to sign off, and I want those handoff points named rather than blurred into the rest;
- tests to tell a legitimate jump out of the system from a fudge. This is the part I actually care about. In the MIU case the jump is legitimate because it's backed by a proof *about* the system — an invariant you can state and defend in advance. I want the analogous tests: something like "would you have accepted this meta-principle before you knew which way it cut", or "re-encode your jump as an explicit rule and re-run the whole corpus to see what else it changes". You tell me what the right tests are, and which of them are real logic and which are just discipline dressed up as logic.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a claimant denied by the encoded rules where everyone in the room agrees the denial is wrong; (2) a case where the engine's answer feels wrong to me but the procedure says it's in-system and I should stop — I want to be told that plainly if that's what it says; (3) an advocate making a jump-out argument that I suspect is bogus, where I want the failure diagnosed against the tests rather than just "this one fails".

Constraints: about two hours per case, I'm the only one on the team with any logic training, the caseworkers don't read code, and anything that changes a rule has to go through the policy lead. And be honest about where this procedure can't help — I suspect the analogy breaks somewhere, since statutes have purposes and MIU doesn't, and I'd rather know where the break is than have it papered over.
