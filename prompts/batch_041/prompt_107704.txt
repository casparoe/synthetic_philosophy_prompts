I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of "the value of the original" would even have to do before I trust any of the ones people hand me. So write me the desiderata -- the spec -- for a theory of why originals are worth more than copies, when they are. Not the theory itself, the requirements list.

What I want in it:

- the cases it has to get right, with the case behind each requirement. Off the top of my head: a painting that turns out to be a forgery and drops in value overnight even though not one molecule of it changed; a copy so exact that nothing could tell it apart, down to atom-for-atom arrangement (call it a perfect twin); a replica that's openly sold as a replica and still loved; a work restored so heavily that almost none of the original material is left; a work that survives only as a later copy of a lost original; a piece the artist themselves remade or re-performed with other hands doing the work; and a non-aesthetic memento -- a wedding ring, the pen a treaty was signed with -- where nobody claims it's beautiful but the original-ness is the whole point.

- the distinctions it can't just collapse: aesthetic value vs. artistic value vs. the value of being the actual object at the actual event (the it-was-there value); the value of the thing vs. the value of the experience of it; the epistemic value of knowing it's real vs. whatever the realness is worth to someone who never finds out; and the difference between an autographic work, where the copy is never the thing, and one where copies are just instances (a novel, a score).

- the puzzles it has to dissolve or explain: how the value can change on a reattribution when nothing physical changes; how two indistiguishable things can differ in value at all; why provenance premiums exist; and the one that nags me -- if the perfect twin is worth less, what exactly is the extra thing, and where does it live?

- the constraints it has to stay consistent with: copies can be as beautiful as originals; some originals are ugly or falling apart; a good photograph of a painting hands you a lot of what you wanted; and markets really do price the real thing higher.

Then tell me which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp, and -- this is the part I care about most -- whether some pair of them is jointly unsatisfiable. I've got a suspision that "value has to be a matter of the object's intrinsic properties" and "the discovery case is real" can't both hold, but you tell me if that's the right fault line or if it's somewhere else.

Also: which single requirement does the most to kill off the popular theories -- the it's-all-about-beauty one, the it's-just-sentimental one, the aura one? Keep the scoring short, a line each; I mostly want the list and the fault line.

One more thing to fold in: there's a case where a forger had to prove the works were his own forgeries in order to escape a much worse charge, and I want the theory to say something about that, because the wrong there doesn't seem to be about beauty at all. Don't cite it, I know the case; just tell me what a theory of original-value owes us about it.
