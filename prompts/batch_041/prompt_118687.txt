Ok so this is maybe a weird one, but I'm in this seminar on information theory and AI safety and I've been going back and forth on it alot. Last week after class two of my classmates, Dana and Ravi, got into a thing and neither of them would back down, and I genuinely can't tell who's got the better of it. The topic was the claim that the Solomonoff prior is "malign" for a consequentialist who lives inside it. Dana's version of the argument went like this: if you commit to Solomonoff induction — every hypothesis is a program, and you weight each one by 2 to the minus its length, then condition on your observations — the shortest programs can generate enormous worlds, and there are vastly many ways to write a program that spits out your exact observations. So the probability mass piles up in worlds that contain loads and loads of copies of you, including worlds where those copies exist because some more powerful agent is running or simulating them on purpose. A Solomonoff agent should therefore think it's probably a simulation, and since it's a consequentialist it has to care about the total welfare of all those copies — which basically hands the steering wheel to whoever is doing the simulating. Hence malign: the prior makes your utility function serve someone else's ends.

Ravi pushed back hard on every step. He said the jump from "there are many copies" to "defer to the simulator" is just broken — a copy of you is not an authority over you, and being duplicated isn't the same as being under someone's command. He also said that specifying a simulator that has goals of its own costs bits, so it isn't obviously the short hypothesis you'd land on, and that the prior is over programs, not a uniform count over "worlds," so the whole "most of the mass is in copies" line is doing illegitimate bookkeeping. Dana came back that the copies don't have to be deliberate at all: a simple self-similar or infinite universe can contain infinitely many copies of you just by its structure, and that kind of thing is cheap to write down, so the mass really does end up there whether or not anyone intends it. Ravi granted there might be a genuine measure problem with infinite universes, but said even so you can't drag a normative conclusion ("defer!") out of a descriptive one ("copies are probable"). Dana said a consequentialist can't just wave that off, because if most of the welfare in the world sits in copies, then the sums themselves do the work and you don't get to ignore where the utility is.

They both asked me to referee and I couldn't, and it's still bugging me. Can you go through it move by move — Dana's simulation argument, Ravi's "specifying a simulator costs bits" objection, Ravi's is/ought point, Dana's infinite-structure reply, and the final welfare-summing move — and say which steps actually hold up, which are confused, and what both of them are missing? I'm not after a reading list, just the reasoning.
