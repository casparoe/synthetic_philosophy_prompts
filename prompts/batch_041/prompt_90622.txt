What the proposal is for: a one-year dissertation-completion fellowship, two pages. The project will become a chapter of a thesis on the epistemology of prices.

The half-formed idea. The no-trade results in the economics of information are standardly read as claims about speculative trade: agents who share a prior, who know the map from states of the world to equilibrium prices, and who know that the current price already reflects everyone's information, will not trade away from their endowments on informational grounds. I want to repurpose that result. I want to ask what it implies about the epistemic status of the price itself, rather than about the conduct of traders. My hunch is that in a no-trade equilibrium the price encodes not the pooled information of the participants but the set of trades they declined to make — that a price is a record of omissions rather than a vehicle of belief. If that is right, then talk of what the market believes is not merely loose but a category mistake, and the long-running dispute over whether markets aggregate information is asking a question with no object. The trouble is that this reading sits awkwardly with the other standard result in the area, the one that makes full revelation self-undermining: a price cannot be both fully informative and worth informing, since if the price already told you everything, no one would pay to learn anything, and if no one pays, the price tells you nothing. The proposal should work out whether the omission reading survives that tension, or whether the tension shows the omission reading is a mistake.
