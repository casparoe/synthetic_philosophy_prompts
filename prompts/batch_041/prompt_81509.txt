I've been retired from the lab about six years now — cell physiology, mostly ion channels, nothing to do with brains — and I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, mostly by podcast, which I gather is a bit like learning surgery from YouTube. Anyway, there's this episode I keep coming back to on the "hedonistic imperative" — the idea that we ought to use biotech to get rid of suffering, all of it, eventually. The host summarized the whole position roughly like this, and I want to know how much of it I should trust:

"So the hedonistic imperative is basically classical utilitarianism with a lab coat on. Pleasure is the only good and pain the only bad, so the moral goal is to maximize the total amount of pleasure in the universe. The people pushing it think we'll soon be able to rewire the brain so everyone's permanently euphoric — engineered bliss — and that this is obviously what we ought to do. It's a nice thought, but the obvious objection is that a life with no suffering at all would be meaningless: we'd have no motivation to do anything, we'd just sit there grinning. And since you can't have pleasure without pain to contrast it against, the whole project kind of refutes itself. Oh, and it only really applies to humans, which is a bit parochial."

That's roughly it. My instinct as a biologist is that some of this is muddled — "pleasure" and "pain" are doing an awful lot of work in that paragraph and I don't think they're the same kind of thing at all, pain as a signal versus suffering as how it feels seem definately distinct to me — but I don't have the philosophical vocabulary to say exactly where it goes wrong, and the host sounded very confident. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me: what a serious defender of this position would accept, what they'd wince at, what's just wrong, and whether the "meaningless life" and "no contrast" objections actually land or are just asserted? I have a nagging feeling the last line is backwards, and I'm inclined to think the "refutes itself" bit is too quick, but I'd like to be told if I'm the one being sloppy. No reading list please — no citations, I won't be able to track them down anyway, just tell me where the summary is fair and where it isn't.
