I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm commissioning a short story for a special issue on social categories — the question being whether generations are real kinds or useful fictions.

Write as a novelist with a serious amateur's grounding in the philosophy of science and in demography: someone who has read enough about natural kinds, social ontology, and age-period-cohort analysis to be dangerous, and who cares more about texture and dramatic pressure than about showing off. Before drafting a single line, think the problem through on the page. Brainstorm at least three distinct scenarios that could carry it; for each, note which position it would stress-test and where the drama would actually come from; then choose the one that lets the ideas emerge out of conflict among characters rather than out of a narrator's exposition. Only then write the story.

Here is the conceptual terrain I want the story to inhabit.

First, age, period, and cohort are entangled. A cohort is defined as a period minus an age, so from a single cross-section you cannot cleanly separate what people did because they were young, because the times were what they were, and because they happened to be born when they were. Every claim of the form "this generation is like X" is a claim about one of those three things, and the data usually cannot arbitrate between them. One of your characters should feel this as a professional wound — a discovery that a finding they have staked something on cannot be identified at all.

Second, the boundary problem. Generational labels are drawn by pollsters, consultants, marketers, and journalists, sometimes retroactively, and the same band of birth years can look like a coherent kind in one dataset and dissolve in another. Naming is an act, not a discovery, and the act has authors with interests.

Third, the loop. Labels are not merely descriptive; people adopt them as scripts, and the script can produce the very behavior the label predicted. A thoroughgoing nominalist about generations can still insist they matter, because fictions can be causally potent.

Fourth, the concession that makes the argument interesting. It is possible to grant durable cohort effects — formative exposure to some technology, entering the labor market during a deep recession, the timing of a school system — without granting any shared essence to the people so affected. The question worth dramatizing is not real versus fake, but which kind of realness survives scrutiny.

To keep the story from drifting into vague meditation, give it a concrete institutional hinge where a boundary has to be drawn and someone has to live with the result: a payout keyed to birth year, an eligibility rule, an exhibit, an admissions policy, a marketing settlement. Requirements: at least one scene in which the technical dispute is rendered as drama rather than lecture; at least one scene in which a label changes a character's understanding of themselves; a story readable with pleasure by someone who has never encountered the identification problem, but which a demographer would recognize as being about it. Do not name real scholars, studies, books, or publications — invent your people and institutions, and do not lean on citation. Do not let the narrator settle the question by fiat.

Deliver the story at 1,200 to 1,800 words, followed by an author's note of roughly 150 words stating which position the story leaves standing and why you chose the scenario you did.
