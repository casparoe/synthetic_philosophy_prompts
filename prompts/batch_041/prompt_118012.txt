I am the outgoing director of a small foreign-policy research institute I have led for nineteen years, and next month I give the farewell remarks at our annual dinner. The dinner doubles as the launch of our new program on the uses of history in statecraft, so I am expected to bless the thing warmly. I want the speech to do something harder than that: to argue, without insulting the trustees who paid for the program, that the analogies it will catalogue are as a class more dangerous than useful — and then to say what historical reasoning can actually do for a working analyst, because I do not want to send a room of practitioners home with nothing but skepticism.

Write the speech. The audience is split. Roughly half are analysts and diplomatic historians who know the Munich and Vietnam debates in detail, who have sat through a dozen briefings built on power-transition arguments of the kind usually labelled the Thucydides trap, and who will notice immediately if I caricature any of these. The other half are trustees, spouses, and two retired ambassadors who are there for me rather than for the argument, and who will stop listening if I deliver an essay.

The speech should be built on a specific set of claims rather than on a general mood of caution: that the source case is always already interpreted, so an analogy smuggles in a contested reading of the past along with whatever it is meant to prove; that choosing the comparison is the entire inference and is done before any reasoning begins, which means the analogy can never be tested by the reasoning it licenses; that a single vivid precedent cannot do the work of a rule because the counterfactual is never available; that analogies are far better at generating options and at diagnosing a situation than at forecasting; and that the costs of over-applying and under-applying a precedent are asymmetric, which the literature on lessons routinely fails to notice. The speech must itself use at least one analogy that does real work, and must acknowledge out loud, without turning it into a joke, that it is doing the thing it warns against. Do not quote anyone and do not name historians or their books: I cannot check such references, and a misattributed line in a farewell speech would be worse than no line at all. Do not resolve the tension by declaring historical reasoning worthless; end with something an analyst could actually use.

About a thousand words, continuous prose meant to be read aloud, no headings or bullet points, dignified throughout. After the speech, add a short note that is not part of it and not to be read aloud, identifying which of its claims are settled among people who study this material, which are genuinely contested, and where the speech has quietly taken one side in a live disagreement — in particular, do not let any reading of Munich or of Vietnam stand as though it were the consensus.
