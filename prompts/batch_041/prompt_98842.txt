I've been hanging around the heritability arguments on the rationalist-ish bits of the internet for years now, and there's one line that keeps turning up in a couple of variants: heritability isn't a measure of how genetic a trait is, and the close cousin, heritability is a population statistic and says nothing about individuals. First time I met it was in a thread about twin studies where someone deployed it to say the whole research programme is conceptually confused, and then last month I saw it used in a completely different way, to shut down a discussion of group differences on the grounds that you can't go from a within-group heritability to a between-group claim. Both times everyone nodded. I can't shake the feeling that those two uses are doing quite different work and that at least one of them is smuggling something in under the cover of an obvious truth. I did a bit of behavioural genetics in my undergrad so I'm not totally green, but every time I try to state exactly what the slogan commits you to I end up with three half-formed readings that contradict each other.

What I want is a proper disambiguation, laid out as a menu I can actually keep, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or flat false? Which ones are just analytic consequences of how variance decomposition works, and which ones are real empirical or conceptual theses? I'd like the individual-versus-population point seperated out from the genetics point, because I suspect they're doing different work and get welded together for rhetorical convenience. I'm also confused about a few specifics and would like them slotted into the relevant readings rather than bolted on at the end: whether heritability genuinely tells you nothing about intervention, given that it's also the number you plug into the breeder's equation to predict how a population responds to selection; what changes when you swap between the twin-study estimate, the SNP-based estimate, and the "missing heritability" gap between them; what happens to a heritability estimate when you change the environment, change the population, or change the trait's prevalence; and whether the classic two-fields-of-wheat illustration is an argument about causation or just about the arithmetic of variances, because I've seen it sold as both.

Finally, tell me which reading is most likely being invoked by each of my two people, and which readings would actually license the conclusion they drew -- I want to know if the twin-study sceptic and the group-difference sceptic can both be right on the same precisification or whether one of them has to give ground. And please write it in British English, I'm keeping all my notes that way.
