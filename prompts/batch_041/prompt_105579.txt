Right, I need an actual number out of you, not a shrug. I've tried to make the setup concrete enough to argue about.

Take a pretty ordinary altruistic act: someone in a rich country gives £1,000 to a charity that scores well on near-term, measurable grounds — anti-malaria bed nets, say. The near-term effects (lives saved, illness averted) we can estimate with the usual error bars. The worry is what comes after. The kid who doesn't die grows up, has kids, shifts the local economy, and so on, and a century or two down the line the causal chain has fanned out so far that — so the argument runs — our estimate of the *sign* of the downstream effect (net good or net bad overall, every consequense counted) is no better than noise. Call the point where that happens the fog line, and call the claim that the fog line is reached well within the relevant horizon for ordinary acts the fog thesis. The literature calls the general problem cluelessness, if that helps orient you, but I care about the concrete case.

The proposition I want your credence in: for acts of this kind, the total long-run value of the consequences is sign-indeterminate, in the sense that no finite reasoner could justifiably hold a credence above 0.5 that the act is net positive once all downstream consequences are counted. Pick a reading of the horizon and of 'justifiably', and stick to it. I'm sat at about 0.65 myself.

- the probability that long-run causal chains from small acts are genuinely chaotic and sign-mixed, rather than systematically favourable or unfavourable;

- the probability that the fog only sets in far enough out (10,000 years, say) that near-term reasoning survives and the whole worry is academic;
- anything else you reckon belongs.
