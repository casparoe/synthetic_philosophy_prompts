I teach a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's later epistemology and the hinge-epistemology literature that has grown out of On Certainty, and I am drafting the take-home final. I want a single essay question that a well-read student cannot answer by reproducing the standard exegesis — the riverbed and hinge metaphors, the Moorean examples, the claim that some propositions stand fast because everything around them holds them fast. The question should force students to do something with the machinery rather than recite it.

Build the question around the following case, which I have been using in lecture. A structural biologist treats the output of a particular class of instruments as beyond question: she does not doubt the readings, does not seek independent confirmation of them, and would regard a colleague who demanded that she justify her trust in the instruments as having misunderstood how research is done. Her attitude is not naïve. It was formed over years of the instruments behaving reliably and of her training in how they are calibrated and repaired. Pressed, she could in fact reconstruct a long track record and, if she chose, an inductive argument for her confidence. So the proposition that this class of instruments is reliable looks like something she could justify; and yet, in her practice, it functions the way Wittgenstein says a hinge functions — it is not something she believes on the strength of evidence so much as something she thinks and works from.

The question should turn on the tension between that case and Wittgenstein's remark that justification comes to an end not in a kind of seeing but in our acting. In On Certainty he writes: "Giving grounds, however - justifying the evidence - comes to an end. But the end is not certain propositions' striking us immediately as true, i.e. it is not a kind of seeing on our part; it is our acting which lies at the bottom of the language-game" (OC204). A hinge acquired through evidence and reasoning — call it a scaffolded hinge — appears to sit awkwardly with that picture.

Write the essay question so that a strong answer must do three things: first, state as sharply as possible what distinguishes a hinge proposition from an ordinary empirical hypothesis, using the biologist to test the distinction and saying where the analogy strains; second, decide whether the existence of scaffolded hinges refutes the claim that hinges are exempt from rational assessment, or whether the hinge epistemologist can absorb them, defending the verdict against the strongest objection the student can construct; and third, address the normative consequence — if some hinges are more defensible than others, say what within a broadly Wittgensteinian picture could underwrite that ranking without collapsing hinges back into hypotheses, and if nothing can, say what that costs the view.

Set it as a 90-minute essay. Tell students that no secondary sources or citations are required — I want their reasoning, not their bibliography — and that they should quote or paraphrase Wittgenstein only where it does argumentative work. Then give me a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that totals 20 points, identifies the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one at each stage, and adds a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs marks at each stage.
