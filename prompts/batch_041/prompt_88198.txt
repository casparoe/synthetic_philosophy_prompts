In my reading group on paradoxes, one participant answers every puzzle with the same one-liner: that the Liar sentence is neither true nor false. He offered it again when I produced a sentence asserting only that it is not true, and once more for a sentence asserting that it is either false or neither true nor false, as though the phrase settled each case. I want the slogan treated not as a single position but as a menu. Set out the distinct precise theses it could be asserting, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical — for instance, that such a sentence is not truth-evaluable at all; that it is truth-evaluable but falls into a gap; that it takes a third value within a non-classical semantics; and whatever further readings are worth separating. For each reading, say what it commits its holder to, whether it is defensible on its own terms, and what it would do to the two harder sentences I raised. Then state which reading the slogan's everyday users most plausibly intend, and which readings, if any, actually dispose of the harder cases rather than merely restating a treatment of the original Liar.
