I chair the physical-sciences grants panel of a national research council, and at Thursday's meeting we must settle a proposal that has split our referees down the middle. The applicants want funding to investigate whether the laws of physics permit a computation that no Turing machine could perform. Their apparatus, in outline, would place one part of a computer on a worldline along which it can complete an unbounded sequence of operations — an open-ended search, say, for a counterexample to a conjecture — while a second part, following a different worldline, waits only a finite time and then reads off whatever the first part has produced. If the first part halts, the reader learns that it halted and what the answer was; if it never halts, the reader is somehow to learn that too. The applicants concede that whether any such arrangement is physically realisable is precisely what they propose to find out, and that a null result would interest them as much as a positive one.

Our referees divide three ways. One holds that the identification of effective calculability with what a Turing machine can do is a settled result, so a proposal to construct a counterexample is incoherent and should be declined on that ground alone. A second holds that the same identification is at most a conjecture about the physical world, so that testing it is ordinary science and ought to be funded. A third holds that it is neither a theorem nor a hypothesis but a stipulation — an explication of what we are to mean by effective calculability — and that no experiment could confirm or refute it either way.

Please draft the decision memo I shall circulate before the meeting. It should reach a clear recommendation — fund, decline, or fund subject to stated conditions — with reasons the panel and the applicants can both engage with; say what would count as success or failure for a project of this kind; and give the strongest of the three referee positions its due rather than dismissing it. I should also like the memo to say whether the panel ought to adopt a standing policy for proposals of this foundational character or to judge them case by case, and to be honest about what the panel is and is not competent to decide.
