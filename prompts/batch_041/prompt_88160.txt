I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the relation between morality and the divine will, and I want my students to see what a genuinely constructive encounter between two entrenched positions looks like. Please draft the joint statement that the following two parties could both put their names to after a long, good-faith collaboration: a committed theological voluntarist, who holds that what makes an action obligatory or good is, at bottom, God's commanding it; and a committed non-voluntarist, who holds that moral truths are true independently of any will, divine or human, so that God — if there is a God — commands as God does because it is good.

I want, first, a numbered set of claims both can endorse about the Euthyphro dilemma itself: its logical form; what it does and does not establish; the distinct senses of arbitrariness, independence, and explanatory priority on which it turns; the difference between a divine command's being the causal source of a moral fact and its being the constitutive ground of that fact; and which of the familiar replies actually engage the argument rather than change the subject. These claims must be neither vacuous nor slanted toward either party.

Second, I want the residual disagreement stated as precisely as possible, in wording that each side would accept as a fair rendering of its own view.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. Please assess, rather than simply adopt, two candidates of my own: (a) whether grounding the good in God's nature instead of God's will escapes the dilemma or merely relocates it; and (b) whether the dilemma can be posed in a modal form — asking not what God in fact commands, but what would be good had God's will or nature been otherwise — and whether that modal version is a fair test or a trick. Add any further cruxes you regard as genuine.

Fourth, for each crux, set out a case, argument, or thought experiment that both parties would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. Work through, if they are apt, two cases: a command whose content strikes most of us as monstrous, and a command that appears morally indifferent.

Finally, tell me which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal — turning on different senses of grounding, dependence, or explanatory priority rather than on any substantive disagreement — and say why.

Please do the reasoning in the document itself rather than surveying a literature, and do not include citations; I want a text the two parties could sign, with the agreed claims numbered, the residual disagreements in a table, and the cruxes and their tests in a clearly separated section.
