I am in the third year of my undergraduate degree and am currently taking a small seminar on consciousness and the metaphysics of mind. Next week I am responsible for opening and leading the discussion on panpsychism, and the fifty-minute block I have to fill is meant to be about the combination problem. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the plan for giving it. The ten or so students in the room will have done the assigned reading and will therefore arrive fluent in the vocabulary, which is exactly the danger: fluency will feel to them like comprehension. Most are default physicalists who treat panpsychism as a curiosity, one is an eliminativist who will announce that the whole debate is confused, and one will say at some point that the combination problem is just the hard problem again and therefore tells us nothing. My aim is that they leave able to state the problem in their own words and to see why it is a live difficulty rather than a refutation.

So: tell me the order in which the components have to be introduced, and why each one depends on what precedes it, and what has to be postponed because it presupposes something the room does not yet have. I am especially unsure where the motivation for panpsychism belongs. My instinct is that if I state the problem first it will sound like an internal quarrel among people who have already swallowed something absurd, and that nobody will feel its force until they have felt the pressure that makes the position attractive in the first place; tell me whether that is right, and where exactly the hinge falls.

Then the example that is supposed to carry the session. I have two candidates and want your judgement on which, if either, should do the work, what it must have and lack, and how to repair it. The first is a test by analogy with quantity: two masses make a mass and two forces make a force, and this works because the items composed are magnitudes of one kind, so there is a fact about the sum; I want to ask the room what the corresponding single kind would be for two experiences, given that experience appears to have no unit. The second is a committee: an assembly of people, each of whom is unquestionably a subject, which is nonetheless not itself a subject, whereas I, who am equally an assembly of something, am one. The difference between those two cases is what the example is meant to make unavoidable. One of my flatmates objects that the committee is rigged, because a committee's beliefs are an institutional fiction, and I cannot tell whether that objection destroys the example or is the very thing the example needs to surface. If neither candidate should carry it, say what should, and say why the obvious fallbacks will not serve.

Also give me the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, each with the precise point in the sequence where I should head it off. My own provisional list, which I would like corrected and extended: that panpsychism says ordinary objects think; that micro-experiences are miniature minds with miniature thoughts; that the combination problem is a question for neuroscience about how the brain binds features into a single experience; that subjects add up the way masses do, so combination is unproblematic; that emergence is the obvious solution, when emergence is one of the things at issue; and that the combination problem refutes panpsychism. I take that last one to be false, because every neighbouring position pays a bill of the same shape — emergentism pays brute emergence, physicalism pays the hard problem, and the reverse strategy of beginning with one cosmic subject and deriving the many smaller ones pays a decomposition problem of its own. I want the session to end on that symmetry rather than on a verdict, and I want the room to feel it rather than be told it, so tell me where in the sequence it can be made visible and how.

Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end that will show whether they have the problem rather than merely the terminology, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain in fifty minutes and say so — a refusal that looks like evasion is worse than a hurried sketch. My own candidates for the refuse pile are the technical taxonomy of varieties of combination, the question of what fundamental physical properties are like intrinsically, and anything connecting this to quantum mechanics, but I would rather have your judgement on where the line belongs. Rough timings for each stage would help me pace it.
