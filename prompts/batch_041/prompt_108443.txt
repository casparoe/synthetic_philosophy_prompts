I was at a gallery opening with two friends last weekend, Mira and Dev, and they got into it for like forty minutes over a single piece, and I've been chewing on it since. The piece: the artist somehow got the gallery to let her exhibit its own storage room as her work. You go through an unmarked door off the main space and you're standing in a cramped back room full of other artists' paintings wrapped in bubble wrap and stacked in crates, and the only thing she added is a wall label outside with her name and a title. She didn't move anything, didn't arrange anything, didn't add a single object — she just designated the room as the piece. Mira thinks it's obviously art. Dev thinks it's obviously not. They asked me to referee and I said I'd think about it, partly because I lurk on LessWrong and have a standing prior that alot of these fights are at least half verbal. Here's their exchange as best I can reconstruct it; I'm trying to report both sides fairly and I honestly don't know which one I think is right.

(1) Mira: designation plus context is enough. Once something is put in a gallery and offered for attention as a work, it enters the artworld and that's what makes it art — same basic move as a readymade. Dev: the readymade at least was an object that was *chosen* and relocated; here she made nothing and didn't even choose the contents, other artists did. If bare pointing counts, then literally anything counts, and a word that applies to everything does no work. (2) Mira: that's the old essentialist demand for one shared necessary feature, and it's a mistake — 'game' has no single feature common to all games either, just overlapping similarities, so 'art' can be the same kind of cluster concept. Dev: family resemblance still has to exclude *something* or the term goes slack; and if everything can be art, the real question just slides over to whether it's *good*, and he thinks it isn't — the entire effect depends on you already knowing you're in a gallery, which is a cheat. He said (I think this is the 'acquaintance principle' but don't quote me) that aesthetic value should be graspable from the thing you're actually looking at, not from a label you read on the way in. (3) Mira: that principle is false for tons of canonical art — you can't appreciate a Renaissance altarpiece without knowing the iconography, or a satire without knowing what it mocks. Dev: fine, but those works still *do* something when you experience them; strip the label off this one and it's just a storage room, and a piece whose whole content is 'hey, look, we're in a gallery, isn't that wild' is a comment *about* art rather than a work of art — that's criticism, not art. Mira: things can be both; half of modern art is about art, and this gesture only even reads as art because there's a tradition it's inheriting from.

What I want from you: go move by move through those four numbered exchanges and say who's right about which specific claim, where a claim is true but doesn't do the work its author thinks it does, and what each of them should concede.
