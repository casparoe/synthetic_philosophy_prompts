I've been rereading Collingwood's The Principles of Art and want your best reconstruction of a verdict he never had to give. On his account, art proper is the expression of an emotion: the artist does not know the emotion in advance and comes to know it only in the expressing of it, which is why his cleanest case is the poet whose poem simply arrives unbidden. Whatever is made to a prior plan, by a technique, with a raw material and a finished product in view, however skilful, belongs to craft — and the error of treating art as a species of craft is the one he devotes much of the book to undoing.

Now the case he never saw. Someone fine-tunes an image model on a single painter's entire corpus, then prompts it through long iterative loops, generating dozens of candidates and keeping the one that finally feels right — a reaction they could not have specified in advance and would not have predicted. A friend of mine calls this Collingwoodian expression in a new medium, since the inability to state the target beforehand is precisely the structure Collingwood demands. I think that is too quick, but I want your case rather than mine.

So what would Collingwood say? Who, if anyone, is the artist in that loop, what would he have to concede, and where would his framework have to break rather than bend? Lead with the verdict, then the argument, then how confident you are and what would move you. Skip the page references, and give it to me as continuous prose — no headings, lists or bold.
