I keep going back and forth on whether "generations" like Boomers or Gen Z are real kinds or just an convenient label, and I want you to compare two ways of defending them and tell me which one actually survives.

Take, first, the plain demographic view: a generation is just a birth cohort, everyone born inside some window, and any talk of "Gen Z values" is really just age effects or period effects wearing a costume. On this view the label has no causal oomph at all, it's bookkeeping. Second, take the view that generations are genuine social kinds, roughly the Mannheim-ish line: a cohort only becomes a generation once it shares a formative experience and starts to act on that shared identity, so the label loops back on itself and partly makes itself true, people read the trend pieces about their generation, marketers sell to it, and it hardens into something that behaves like a real kind.

Heres the thing I can't get past: age, period and cohort are linearly dependent. If you know someone's birth year and the year you're measuring them, you already know their age, so a model with all three in it is unidentified unless you bolt on an extra assumption, and that assumption is quietly doing all the explanatory work. Which means a big chunk of "generational" findings might just be artifacts of whichever constraint the researcher happened to pick.

So compare the two views for me, say which one can survive the identification problem, and then tell me whether the looping/self-fulfilling part of the second view adds anything that isn't already in the first. If generations are only real to the extent people beleive in them, is that enough to call them real kinds, or does it just make them fashions? Give me your honest read, and flag anywhere I'm sliding between "cohort" and "generation" without noticing.
