I'm putting together something on Du Bois and art, and I want to try a counterfactual that only makes sense after 1963, so no searching, just reasoning from what he actually wrote. Setup: in 1850 a Harvard zoologist, Louis Agassiz, had daguerreotypes made of an enslaved man named Renty and his daughter Delia in South Carolina — shirtless, posed front and profile like specimens — as evidence for his racial theory. The plates sat in Harvard's Peabody Museum for over a century. A descendant, Tamara Lanier, sued in 2019; the Massachusetts courts held that a photograph belongs to the photographer rather than the subject, though they let her emotional-distress claim go forward, and in 2025 Harvard settled and agreed to transfer the plates to the International African American Museum in South Carolina. Now hold that next to Du Bois's own photographic practice — the roughly 500 images he helped assemble for the Exhibit of American Negroes at the 1900 Paris Exposition, his "typical Negro faces," the counter-portrait project — and then add what's genuinely new: models trained on enormous scrapes of historical photographs, pictures of enslaved people included, that will generate endless fresh types on demand, with nobody's consent and no author to hold responsible.

So: what would Du Bois have said? I want an argument, not a vibe. Start from his 1926 Crisis essay on the criteria of Negro art. Would he treat ownership of an image as the real question, or would he think property-in-the-image is a liberal individualist frame that concedes too much, and that the fight is over who gets to author the collective image of Black life? Would he read generative models as just the latest installment of white-authored portraiture, or as something his 1926 position can't handle — because his answer to racist imagery was a truer image made by the people depicted, and a machine that manufactures images with no depicted people at all breaks that move? If I'm loading the question by assuming he'd care about consent, say so. And if predicting a dead man's verdict here is just a parlor game — if the honest answer is only that we can say which conclusions are continuous with his commitments and where the record gives out — then say that plainly instead of handing me a confident verdict. Flag where you're leaning on a contested reading of him, especially the tension between his insistence that art is unavoidably propaganda and his insistence in that same essay on Beauty and on the artist not being a mere pamphleteer. I don't want to recieve a tidy answer that just says he'd oppose it because racism.
