QUESTION: Consider the following case. Ruth is 58 and was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's four years ago. Two years before her diagnosis, after nursing her own father through the last years of his dementia, she signed a notarised advance directive stating that if she ever developed dementia severe enough that she no longer recognised her family, she wanted no life-prolonging treatment — no antibiotics, no artificial nutrition, no hospitalisation — and wanted to be kept comfortable at home. She discussed it with her two children at the time and was, by all accounts, clear and unambivalent. She is now in the moderate-to-severe stage. She does not reliably recognise her daughter, though she is often cheerful, enjoys music and pudding, and when she recently had a urinary tract infection she swallowed her antibiotics without protest and said she wanted to feel better. Her physicians judge her not competent to make medical decisions. She now has aspiration pneumonia. Antibiotics would probably resolve it; without them she will likely die within a week or two, though not necessarily uncomfortably if she is kept sedated. Her son wants to follow the directive and keep her at home with comfort care. Her daughter wants to treat the pneumonia, arguing that her mother is happy, doesn't seem to be suffering, and that the person who signed that document isn't the person sitting in that chair. (a) Reconstruct the two most prominent positions on whether an advance directive of this kind retains its authority after the person who wrote it has lost the capacities that made her autonomous. (b) Say how each position would resolve Ruth's case, and what the disagreement between them actually turns on. Be explicit about what work the idea of the earlier self is doing in each position.

STUDENT'S ANSWER: Both positions agree that an advance directive is an exercise of autonomy; they disagree about whose autonomy counts. On the precedent-autonomy view, associated with Dworkin, we distinguish between experiential interests — interests in having pleasant experiences and avoiding pain — and critical interests, which are interests in having a life that goes well as a whole, and which depend on the person's own convictions about what makes life worth living. Ruth's directive expresses a critical interest: she had a considered view about what kind of life she could tolerate. Because critical interests are what give a life its shape, they don't evaporate when someone loses the capacity to hold them in mind; the earlier Ruth, so to speak, outlives the later one for purposes of authority. So the directive governs, and Ruth should stay home with comfort care. The daughter's argument, that Ruth is cheerful and enjoys her pudding, appeals to experiential interests only, and that's exactly the sort of consideration this view says can't be decisive. On the current-welfare view, defended by critics of precedent autonomy, the person in the chair now is the patient, and what matters is her present experience. The earlier self's preferences are at best evidence about what the later self would want; they don't bind her, because the authority to direct one's treatment belongs to a competent person and Ruth is no longer competent. Her daughter is right that the woman who signed the document is not the woman who now reaches for the spoon. Where a directive is silent about a particular intervention, the standard move is substituted judgment, i.e. asking what would be in the patient's best interests right now, which is why the daughter's appeal isn't frivolous. Applying that here, the current-welfare view would treat the pneumonia, since Ruth isn't distressed and antibiotics let her go on having the experiences she currently enjoys. So the disagreement really turns on personal identity. If we accept a Parfitian picture on which psychological continuity comes in degrees, Ruth's earlier and later selves aren't the same person, and the earlier self has no more authority over the later one than a stranger would; the current-welfare view is what you get if you take that seriously. If instead we think the person persists, the directive binds. I'd add that the directive is a refusal of treatment rather than a request for it, and refusals and requests have the same standing in advance care planning, so Ruth's document is enforceable on either view. My conclusion is that the case can't be decided without settling the metaphysics of personal identity first.
