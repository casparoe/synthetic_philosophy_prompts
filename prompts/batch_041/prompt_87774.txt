I am a doctoral candidate in the history of science at work on a methodological chapter about causal explanation in environmental history, and I have run into a conceptual snag that I have not been able to state to my own satisfaction, let alone resolve. A colleague in my department argues, with some force, that pathogens, climatic shifts, and volcanic eruptions ought to be treated as historical actors in their own right rather than as the backdrop against which human beings do the historically interesting things. She denies that this is a mere figure of speech, and she thinks that historians who reserve agency for intentional human beings are smuggling in a metaphysical commitment they would not defend if it were made explicit. I find something right in the vicinity of her view, but our conversations keep stalling: I suspect we are using cause and actor in senses that never quite meet, and I would like a clear statement of what is actually at stake. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of causation and the philosophy of history, and treat this as a conceptual problem rather than as an invitation to survey the literature.

The case we keep returning to is schematic, and perhaps that is part of the trouble: a population with no prior exposure to a pathogen; a harvest failure following an unusually cold decade; an imperial administration that responds to the resulting distress badly and late. My colleague wants to say that the pathogen and the climate are parties to the outcome rather than its setting, while I want to say that the administration is where the explanation really lies. Neither of us can say why we disagree. So I would like you to explain the following. First, what is the difference between the claim that an eruption caused a famine and the claim that the eruption acted? Does the second claim add anything beyond metaphor, or does it stake out a genuinely different kind of assertion about what belongs in a historical explanation? Second, much of the resistance to this way of talking seems driven by the spectre of determinism: the worry that once non-human factors are admitted as agents, human deliberation begins to look like an epiphenomenon and history becomes a story about climate and microbes in which people are merely the medium. Is that worry well founded, or does it rest on an equivocation, in particular on sliding from the claim that something shaped what happened to the claim that it made what happened inevitable? Third, my colleague sometimes says that a volcano or a pathogen did real work in a given episode. I cannot tell whether that is a claim about causal efficacy, about explanatory salience, or about how credit and blame should be apportioned, and I suspect these come apart.

Before you answer, take a moment to lay out the candidate distinctions you think are doing the work here, and flag any that turn out to be red herrings. Then explain each contested claim carefully, stating explicitly any distinction you rely on and showing how it resolves the question at issue. Where you take the dispute to be merely verbal, say so and explain why; where you take there to be a substantive disagreement, characterise it as precisely as you can, including what would have to be true for each side to be right. A compact and sharply argued explanation would serve me considerably better than an exhaustive survey.
