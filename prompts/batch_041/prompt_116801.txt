I have spent twenty years in commercial mediation, and for some time now I have been trying to write up a pattern I keep running into, without much success. I read a fair amount of philosophy in the evenings, so I recognise some of the neighbouring territory, but I cannot find the thing itself.

There are people in my trade who, as a matter of settled temperament, cannot accept a bad settlement — not that they refuse, but that they are unable. If the numbers are poor they cannot bring themselves to sign, and they will go to a costly and uncertain hearing rather than do it. Everyone at the table works this out within half an hour. These people do markedly better than the rest of us: the other side, whose advisers are paid to be realistic, folds early, and clients seek them out. The part I cannot account for is what happens when a colleague decides to become one of these people. The decision is sincere and the resolve is real, and it does not take. Everyone can tell that the refusal is being performed as a tactic, and once they can tell, it is worth nothing.

Three further things I have noticed, though I cannot tell whether they are essential to the pattern or accidents of my corner of it. First, it appears to be self-erasing under its own description. The people who have it are the last to know that it is doing them any good; one of them told me that when he began thinking about it in these terms, he started losing. I can see the mechanism perfectly clearly, and I cannot have any of its benefit. Second, it does not scale. If everyone at the bar acquired it, no case would ever settle, the courts would seize up, and the advantage would vanish — so it is something I want for myself and would not want for my profession, and I cannot tell whether that makes it a virtue or merely a good seat. Third, it is not the familiar case of a person known for keeping their word. That one runs through reputation and can be adopted deliberately: resolve today to keep your promises, and in a few years you will be believed. Here, resolving today is precisely what fails. The disposition has to have been there already, and to be known to have been there already, before one wanted anything from it.

Is there a name for this class of advantage — the ones available only to those who are not trying to get them, where the trying is the very thing that spoils it? I can see that it sits near Parfit's hitchhiker and near the sailor who has himself bound to the mast, but neither has quite this shape: the hitchhiker has one stranger to convince on one occasion, and the sailor gets his advantage from a device, which is available to anyone willing to be tied up. What I am after is an asset made of one's own character, which dissolves on contact with the thought of it, and which could not be wished on everyone at once.

If there is no established term, I would rather have a careful one than a clever one, and I would want it drawn tightly: what must be true of the disposition for a case to be an instance of it, what falls outside, and where the boundaries lie with ordinary reputation, with commitment devices, with costly signalling, and with the more general thought that some traits are rational to have and irrational to act on. I would also like to know whether the self-erasure is essential to the phenomenon or merely a fact about the people I happen to work with. Please answer in British English.
