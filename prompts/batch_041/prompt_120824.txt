You are a philosopher working on moral disagreement and practical ethics. I need advice on a concrete decision, so please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the competing considerations before giving your recommendation.

I chair a small land trust that is deciding whether to sell part of a parcel to a developer. One group on the board cares most about protecting a wetland; another cares most about building affordable housing. The proposed deal gives the developer most of the land for housing, while the trust keeps a conservation easement over the wetland and receives a payment for future acquisitions. Almost everyone agrees that each side ends up better off than under no deal, even though neither gets what it most wants. A board member argues that this shows how to handle deep disagreements generally: each side should concede on what it cares less about in exchange for concessions on what it cares more about, and the outcome is legitimate because it is mutually beneficial. I am uneasy. Some things seem not to be the sort of thing one should trade at all, and I worry that the mutual-benefit test would license bargains that harm people or places not at the table. A colleague has suggested a distinction that may help: between merely giving up some of what you value and giving up the authority of a value over a whole domain; she says only the latter is a moral loss, while the former is ordinary resource allocation. Is that distinction sound? Is the market analogy the right way to think about this kind of disagreement? What are the limits of treating compromise as a mutually beneficial exchange? And how should I decide whether to vote for the deal?

Please be explicit about which parts of your answer reflect settled positions among philosophers who work on this and which parts are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. You may refer to arguments and positions, but I do not need citations or a reading list.
