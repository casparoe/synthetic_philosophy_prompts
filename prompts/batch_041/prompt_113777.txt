I'm helping run the first session of a probability seminar and I want to open with a poem instead of a slide, because the whole point of Bertrand's chord puzzle is that the picture does something a proof doesn't. Here's what I'm after.

A poem in three stanzas of six lines each, then a two-line coda. The three stanzas have to be formally identical -- same rhyme scheme, same meter, same shape -- I want them to look like the same poem wrote out three times, so that if you blanked the numbers you couldn't tell which stanza was which. Each stanza is the same experiment: a chord is thrown at random into a circle, and we ask whether it comes out longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. But each stanza reads "at random" a different way -- once the two endpoints slide freely around the rim, once it's the chord's distance from the centre, once it's the midpoint landing somewhere in the disc -- and each stanza should end on the number that reading actually gives (that's 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, one per stanza, in whatever order feels least like a punchline). Don't label them, don't explain them; the sameness of the form should be doing the arguing.

Then the coda, two lines, and it definately shouldn't pick a winner. I want it to sit in the fact that the same appeal -- no reason to prefer one placement over another, so count them all equal -- is what's working inside each stanza, and it comes out different every time. If you can rhyme the coda back into the first stanza's rhyme so the whole thing closes on itself, please do.

Accuracy matters more than prettiness; a statistician should be able to check this line by line. After the poem, give me one short paragraph on where it had to fudge the math or lean on a contested reading -- and if my framing is off, just say so instead of writing it anyway. I have a nagging feeling that calling all three of those the same appeal to indifference is already fudging something, but I can't put my finger on what.
