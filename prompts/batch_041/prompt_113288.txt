I'm TAing a seminar on ancient philosophy this term and my professor has done something slightly unhinged with the syllabus: he's paired Cicero's De Senectute with King Lear, and he's convinced the two are in a real argument with each other. I can't tell yet if that's a genuine insight or just the sort of thing people say when they've taught the same course for fifteen years, so I want to actually work it out. The setup, as I remember it (correct me if I've mangled it): Cicero has old Cato speak for old age, and Cato takes the usual complaints one at a time and knocks them down — that old age takes you out of public life, that it wrecks the body, that it strips away the pleasures, that it puts you within sight of death. His answers are roughly that the old can still serve with counsel and judgment even if not with arms, that the goods of the mind don't rot when the sinews do, that the pleasures it takes away were the ones nobody should have been chasing anyway, and that being near death is either nothing to fear (if we're extinguished there's no one left to be harmed) or something to welcome (if we aren't). A lot of it is also just practical advice about how to be old well — don't be querulous, don't be grasping, keep doing something useful, don't insist on the trappings of a role you've given up. And the man wrote it when he was already old himself, then got himself killed in the proscriptions a year or so later, which I find either very funny or very sad, I haven't decided.

So what I want from you is a reading of King Lear against that dialogue — as a test case, not an illustration. Lear is the exact old man Cicero is warning about: he wants to hand off the work and keep the hundred knights, the title, the deference, the whole apparatus of being king, and he can't understand why anyone would object. I'd like you to press on that, but I don't want it to be a one-way street where the play just confirms the essay. Some things I keep circling and can't resolve on my own. Cicero is confident that the mind holds up in old age, and the play is full of the opposite fear — the dread of losing your wits entirely, of being reduced to something that isn't a person anymore; how bad is that for Cato's case? Cato's good old age seems to assume a republic that still wants his advice and an old man who still has standing, and Lear's whole catastrophe is that once he's given the power away he's simply superfluous — is there anything in the dialogue for being superfluous, or is that a gap the play walks straight into? Gloucester, who loses his eyes and then seems to understand his situation better than he did with them, looks like the cleanest test in the play of the claim that the mind's goods survive the body's ruin — tell me whether he's a vindication of Cato or a refutation of him, and don't fudge it. And the ending, where the one thing the old man wants back is the one thing the dialogue never even considers worth mentioning — does that break Cicero's consolation, or does it just show that Lear never learned to be old the way Cato says you must? I'd also like to know whether Kent and the Fool count as the kind of counsel the old are supposed to be good for, since the play is obsessed with who gets listened to. Be honest where the play resists the reading — if Lear refuses Cicero's terms altogether, say so and say what that refusal shows. Then finish with a couple of sentences on what the play knows about old age that the dialogue, arguing as it does, can't get at; that's the part I actually want to bring to section.
