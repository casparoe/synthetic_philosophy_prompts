I'm in the last weeks of a seminar on Madhyamaka and comparative philosophy of logic, and for our closing meeting each of us has to bring in a short poem that does some of the work of the reading rather than just gesturing at it. I'd like help writing mine. The aim is a poem that enacts the catuṣkoṭi rather than names it.

What I have in mind is a poem of four stanzas, four lines each, on a single ordinary subject. A river is the obvious choice and I'd rather avoid it; something like a scar, a bus route, a nickname, or a debt would be better. Each stanza should run the four lemmas on one predicate of that subject. The fourth line of each stanza is the one that gives me trouble: I don't want it to read as a fourth alternative sitting alongside the other three, because on the reading I find most persuasive it isn't a further position but the point at which the question stops getting traction. If that reading of the fourth lemma is contested enough that the poem can't honestly be written as if it were settled, please say so up front rather than smoothing it over.

Two constraints beyond that. First, the poem should not quote or closely paraphrase the opening verse of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā or any other canonical text; I want it built from the structure, not from borrowed lines. Second, it should stay away from the stock examples — no chariot, no fire and fuel, no dream, no illusion, no moon in water. Those have done their work already and would make the poem feel like a recitation.

Please add a short note at the end, a few sentences, on where the poem takes liberties with the tetralemma or leans on a contested reading — Prāsaṅgika versus Svātantrika, or the four-valued-logic reading versus the therapeutic one — and on whether the form I've asked for actually suits the idea or whether I've let the shape dictate the philosophy. If the whole request rests on a confusion — for instance, if enacting the catuṣkoṭi in verse isn't possible without collapsing the fourth lemma back into the third — I'd rather you said so plainly than produced something that papers over it.
