I'm in a third-year phil of religion seminar and this week we read David Lewis's paper on penal substitution — the one where he argues that we do actually beleive in penal substitution, not just religious believers but everybody, because we already accept cases where one person takes on the punishment or cost owed by somebody else, and we don't think whoever imposed it acted unjustly. His point, as I read it, is that the standard objection (you can't punish an innocent person in place of the guilty, that's just scapegoating) is too quick, since our own practice already treats substitution as legitimate when the substitute steps in willingly and the relationship is the right kind.

I want to practice arguing the other side. Don't strawman yourself: you can concede that people genuinely volunteer to take on burdens for others, but you'll insist that this is not punishment, it's something else — assuming a debt, bearing a cost, taking a risk — and that the doctrine needs the substitute to bear punishment specifically. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep every reply to one paragraph, 150 words max, and don't give me reading lists or citations, just argue.

My opening move: fine, you concede the volunteer takes on the burden. But in the cases Lewis gives, someone steps in and takes the punishment that was owed by someone else — and we say they were punished, and we don't think the one who punished them did anything unjust. So our concept of punishment already has room for substitution in it, and the objection can't be that substitution is conceptually impossible. If the atonement is still a problem, it has to be some other feature of the divine case. Which feature, and why doesn't it show up in the ordinary cases?
