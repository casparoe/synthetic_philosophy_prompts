Me and my housemate have been having the same arguement for about a year now, and we finally decided to do the adversarial-collaboration thing properly instead of rehashing it at the kitchen table every Sunday, so I'm hoping you'll draft the document we should both be able to sign. The setup: I've basically talked myself into Simone Weil's position on parties. As I read her, a party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion, it's built to exert collective pressure on the thinking of each person inside it, and its first and in the end only aim is its own growth, without any limit — and on her account belonging to one forces you into untruth as the price of membership, so the existence of parties isn't a regrettable but necessary evil, it's just flatly an evil. What she wants in their place isn't a better party but something much looser: journals and reading groups you sit near rather than sign up to, proximity instead of adherence, and candidates who stand as themselves instead of as the bearer of some organization's line. My housemate spent four years as a party organizer and thinks that's a fantasy for people with no dependents — she says parties are the only thing that's ever let anyone who isn't a landowner coordinate at scale, that the growth-seeking Weil hates is exactly the mechanism that keeps a program honest from one election to the next, that discipline is the price of being able to promise anything and be held to it, and that a world of independent candidates and little journals is either noise or rule by whoever already has a platform. We've gotten far enough that we each think the other is describing something real and then over-generalizing it, and we're both the kind of people who've read too many blogs to enjoy watching ourselves do that.

So write the joint statement. Start with what we can both honestly affirm — about what parties actually do to the people inside them, about whether the growth claim is even an empirical claim that could be false, and about what's fundamentally at stake here (is the metric one person's grip on the truth, or a population's capacity to govern itself, because I suspect we've been scoring the same facts against different scoreboards). Then the disagreements, each one phrased the way the person on that side would phrase it so that they'd actually say yes to it — my guess is the big ones are whether the lying is intrinsic to the party form or downstream of something contingent like mass membership, single-member districts, or the specific way parties turned into electoral machines in the twentieth century, and whether her alternative is a real institutional proposal or a thought experiment meant only to purify our sense of what's wrong. Then for each crux, the case or argument we'd both agree actually bears on it, with each of us on record about which way we expect it to come out before we look — she'll want to point at how often a party-line vote is really the leadership's preference imposed on members who privately dissent, and I'll want to point at what happens to representation when the label stops carrying any information at all. And if part of this turns out to be a fight about words — whether party means the mass-membership thing Weil was surrounded by or any durable coalition that coordinates — say which part and why, because I'd bet at least one round of this was purely verbal.

One thing though: write it as continuous prose. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no tables, no bold — just paragraphs, like a long email you'd send a friend, because we're going to read the thing out loud to each other and the second I see a table I stop taking it seriously.
