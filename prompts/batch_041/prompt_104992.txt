For the past several years, since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been teaching a second-year undergraduate course on rationality and choice. I am midway through grading the midterm and would like your help with one script. The examination question and the student's answer to it appear below.

Please assign the answer a mark out of ten, on the understanding that seven is a solid pass. Lead with the mark itself, then give the itemised comments that justify it. I care about the specific defects rather than a general impression: for each one, identify the sentence or step at fault, explain why it is defective, and state what a fully correct answer would have said in its place. Where a point is genuinely contestable rather than simply mistaken, say so and indicate what turns on it. Please keep the whole assessment to four hundred words or fewer.

Examination question:

(a) Using a three-option example, set out the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences and state the assumptions on which it relies. Then consider an agent who strictly prefers L to M and L to N, but who is unable to compare M and N — she strictly prefers neither to the other and is indifferent to neither. Does the same style of argument show that she can be money-pumped? If so, describe the pump; if not, say what would have to be added to the situation for one to exist.

(b) State the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem, list the axioms it requires, and explain what the theorem does and does not establish about the claim that a rational agent must maximise expected utility.

(c) A classmate objects that lexicographic preferences are perfectly rational, and that they therefore discredit the whole framework. Evaluate the objection.

Student's answer:

(a) Take an agent whose preferences run in a cycle: she prefers B to A, C to B, and A to C. Suppose she starts with A. Because she prefers C to A, she will pay one cent to swap A for C. Because she prefers B to C, she will pay a second cent to swap C for B. Because she prefers A to B, she will pay a third cent to swap B for A. She is back at A with three cents less, and the sequence can be repeated without limit. The argument needs only that her preferences remain fixed throughout the episode and that she prefers having more money to less.

The same reasoning disposes of incompleteness. If the agent cannot compare M and N, then she does not prefer M to N, so when a trader offers to exchange M for N for one cent she has nothing against the trade and will accept it; and since she equally does not prefer N to M, she will accept the reverse trade as well. She can therefore be pumped in either direction.

(b) The von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem states that if a preference relation over lotteries is complete, transitive, continuous, and independent, then it can be represented by a utility function, unique up to a positive affine transformation, whose expectation the agent maximises. Independence requires that if A is preferred to B, then any mixture of A with a third lottery C is preferred to the corresponding mixture of B with C; this is just the sure-thing principle under another name. What the theorem shows is that an agent who is rational in the ordinary sense will maximise expected utility. The axioms simply are the requirements of rationality, so someone who violates independence is irrational, as the Allais paradox illustrates.

(c) The classmate is right. Lexicographic preferences, on which any gain in one dimension outweighs any amount of another, are entirely rational, yet they violate continuity and admit no utility representation. Expected utility theory is therefore refuted.
