I'm putting together the Q&A for a small online philosophy-of-medicine seminar series, and next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent years arguing for a higher-brain (neocortical) criterion of death — roughly, that you die when you irreversibly lose the capacity for consciousness, so a patient in a persistent vegetative state on a ventilator is dead, while someone in a locked-in state is alive. She's also got a second claim I'd like to draw out, and it's the reason I booked her: she thinks the higher-brain criterion actually rescues the dead donor rule rather than threatening it, and that the standard "irreversible" wording is a mistake that ough to be replaced by "permanent". I've drafted some questons but they're soft, and I don't want her just restating the book at me.

Give me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on:

- the somatic-integration objection — the fact that bodies diagnosed brain-dead can go on maintaining temperature, healing wounds, fighting infections and even carrying a pregnancy for months — and what she says to the claim that this shows the brain isn't the organ that keeps an organism alive as a whole;
- the fact that her view makes a vegetative patient dead while keeping someone in a minimally conscious or locked-in state alive, a line that seems to rest on an unobserved remaining capacity rather than on anything you can see, and whether that line is sharp enough to carry the weight of life and death;
- how you'd actually certify loss of the capacity for consciousness at the bedside — what test or observation would she accept, and what she says to the worry that there isn't one yet, so the criterion can't be applied;
- the "permanent" versus "irreversible" distinction, and whether a definition of death should be hostage to the possibility that some future treatment restores cortical function;
- whether she's telling us what death *is* or what we should *do* — i.e. whether this is a conceptual claim about biology or a moral and policy proposal dressed up as one;
- the transplant angle: whether her view would change who can donate and when, and what she makes of the complaint that brain-death criteria are a rationalisation of organ procurement rather than a discovery about death;
- the ontology — whether death is an event or a process, and if it's a process, what makes any particular threshold non-arbitrary.

Please make the questions turn on the content of the view rather than on her biography or how she came to it, and please mark clearly where the answers are genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want a rundown that presents the higher-brain criterion as though it were the settled position, and I don't want the real disagreements smoothed over into a tidy consensus. Where something is settled — that the 1981 US President's Commission went the other way, or what the standard bedside tests for brainstem death actually involve — it's fine to say so plainly; where it isn't, say who's on which side and why. No reading lists or citations needed, I just want the questions.
