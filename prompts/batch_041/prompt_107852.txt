You are a philosopher of science who knows both the demarcation literature and its critics well. Think carefully, and open with a short brainstorm of the possible sources of an impasse in a debate like this one before you commit to a diagnosis.

I am preparing a seminar on the demarcation problem and keep hitting the same wall. The debate seems to me stalled in a particular way: each proposed criterion — falsifiability, puzzle-solving within a paradigm, novel prediction by a research programme, a community's institutional norms — is refuted by a counterexample drawn from a different kind of case, and the counterexample is then treated as decisive. Falsificationists point to a doctrine that can absorb any observation; critics point to the historical sciences, to theories whose central commitments are shielded by auxiliary hypotheses, or to respectable programmes that make no risky predictions. Nobody moves.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than assume, is that the impasse is largely an artifact of three different jobs being run together under one heading: (1) sorting a finished body of work into science and non-science in a way that matches what competent practitioners would say; (2) supplying a procedure a non-expert — a court, a school board, a journalist — can apply to a live, contested claim; and (3) saying what a practice must do to be a good way of finding out about the world, even if the criterion condemns stretches of what we call science and matches no practitioner's classifications. Falsificationism, on this reading, is a poor answer to (1) and (2) and a substantive answer to (3); the classical counterexamples aim at (1); and the conclusion that the problem is a pseudo-problem follows only if (1) was the job all along.

Assess this. For each major position and each standard objection, say which of the three jobs it is really addressing, and then diagnose the disagreement itself: is it substantive, is it about what a criterion is for, or are several parties answering different questions under one name? Close with what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result — or an argument that none should be expected. Engage the actual moves rather than surveying the field, and say where my three-way division itself papers over a real disagreement. No reading list; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
