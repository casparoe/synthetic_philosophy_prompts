I'm a grad student in a marine bio lab and somehow I've ended up on the tiny committee rewriting our animal-care protocols, and I keep tripping over something I can't quite name. We keep zebrafish, trout, and a rack of crabs and crayfish. Every finfish gets an approved euthanasia method — buffered MS-222, or electrical stunning for a big batch — and we log time-to-unconsciousness and everything. The crabs and crayfish just go in the freezer or straight into boiling water and nobody files a thing. What nags at me is that when we do measure something it's the same kind of thing in both cases: we'll point to elevated cortisol or erratic swimming as evidence the trout is suffering, but when the crabs avoid a noxious stimulus, or the crayfish lose their startle reflex after a shock, the lab sort of waves it off — invertebrates are different, they don't have the wiring, it's just a reflex. And the sustainability certification we're applying for has this odd scoring sheet where a species collects "sentience points" for nociceptors, opioid-type receptors, and flexible learning, and anything under the threshold score is treated as though its welfare isn't even a category we track. Oh and we're told to say "harvest" and "processing," never "kill" or "slaughter" — apparently that's in the style guide.

I want to know what our practices are actually committing us to philosophically, not what the paperwork claims. What view of moral status does this whole pattern presuppose — does it require believing that welfare only counts where there's conscious experience, and that finfish have it while crustaceans don't? Or is it really an evidential policy, like "we're not confident enough yet, so we don't regulate," which would be a totally different kind of commitment? Is there one coherent theory underneath, or is it two theories quietly coexisting — a threshold view about evidence in one place and a view about value in another — and do those two pull apart? I'd also like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to tell me where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories would license exactly what we do. Then: what would have to change — in the protocols, in what we measure, in whether we count death itself as a harm versus only felt suffering, in the euphemism problem — if we swapped the implicit view for one where crustacean suffering is real but death is no harm, or for one where any death of a sentient-ish animal is a harm regardless of pain? Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
