I keep circling back to the insect welfare thing and I think I finally want to just write it out as an essay and land on a verdict. The standard move goes: insects are the most numerous animals by an enormous margin, so even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is very small, the expected amount of suffering (or wellbeing) summed across all of them swamps everything else, and insect welfare should dominate our priorities. I want to stress-test that multiplication and see if it actually holds up.

The thing that keeps nagging me is the grain of the probability. The argument treats sentience as a property of individuals and multiplies the probability across them. But what if it isn't like that? If sentience tracks some architectural feature that a whole clade either has or lacks -- shared by all its members -- then the number of individuals doesn't multiply anything. It's one bet repeated, not a vast number of indepedent draws. So the scale argument looks like it needs a fairly strong and usually unstated assumption: that sentience is realized per-individual and roughly independently. I want you to make that assumption explicit and then decide whether the argument survives without it.

Second thing: I can't shake the feeling that the two factors are anti-correlated. The theories of consciousness that make insect sentience likely tend to be permissive -- any system with the right kind of recurrent processing counts -- and those same theories tend to make each instance thin: less integrated, less to lose, maybe a much smaller moral weight per insect. Whereas the theories that would give a single insect real moral weight are exactly the ones that make it unlikely. If that's right, the big product might collapse. But I'm not sure it's right -- maybe richness and moral weight come apart, or maybe the permissive theories can still assign full weight.

Take a view: does the scale argument survive once you spell out the grain assumption and take the anti-correlation seriously? Then tell me what follows practically -- should someone who cares about animal welfare actually be putting money and attention into insects, or is that a mistake? Dont end on a fence. And say what would have to turn out true about insect neurobiology for your verdict to flip.
