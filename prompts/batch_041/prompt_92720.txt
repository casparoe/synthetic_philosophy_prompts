I need a two-page research proposal for a departmental workshop in political theory, and I'd like you to develop a half-formed idea of mine into something with a determinate question. The idea: three live reform proposals — extending the vote to resident non-citizens, lowering the voting age, and compulsory voting — are argued about in three largely separate literatures, but I suspect they are three probes of one underlying question about what the franchise is. On one familiar account it is a right held by those with a stake in the outcomes of decisions; on another it is something attaching to those subject to the law; and a third treats the vote less as a right than as a duty or a public office one holds on behalf of others. These pull the reforms apart in interesting ways. If the franchise is grounded in subjection to the law, extending it to resident non-citizens and to younger citizens follows fairly directly, but compulsory voting then needs a further and not obviously available premise — that subjection generates not only a claim to a vote but an obligation to cast it. If the franchise is grounded in affected interests instead, all three look harder to reach, and the third hardest of all. The suspicion I want tested is that the three reforms cannot be underwritten by a single principle, and that each of the familiar principles picks out a different subset of them.

Turn this into a proposal. The central question should be stated so that it has a determinate answer — something along the lines of whether one principle can both fix who ought to have the vote and settle whether those who have it ought to use it, or whether the three reforms in fact rest on incompatible accounts of the franchise. Say why the existing positions leave this open rather than settling it: the boundary principles that are usually offered for who counts, and the right-versus-duty framing that is usually offered for compulsory voting, each look sufficient within its own debate and silent about the other. Lay out the method — what I would actually do, month by month, across a semester — and justify that approach against the obvious alternative of writing a straight comparative survey of the three debates. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give a realistic scope for the time available. Keep it to two pages' worth.
