Kafka's A Hunger Artist ends with the faster's admission that he fasted only because he could never find a food he liked, and with the panther that takes over his cage. I should like a reading of the story through Nietzsche's late dietetics: the chapter of Ecce Homo in which he ranks nourishment, place, and climate above anything a thinker merely believes, together with his notion of great health as something to be won rather than enjoyed. My thesis, which I would like you to test rather than illustrate, is that the story is a Nietzschean diagnosis and not a Nietzschean allegory: it shows what the ascetic ideal becomes once its metaphysical pay-off has been withdrawn, so that the regimen persists while the prize does not.

Take the story's apparatus seriously: the forty-day ceiling the impresario imposes, the spectators whose interest depends on the faster's denial, the move to the circus and the straw, the night watchers, the panther. Where does Nietzsche's regimen illuminate the faster's practice, and where does the story refuse it? Above all, if the ascetic ideal is a will to power turned against life, does a man who gains nothing by his denial — no purity, no mastery, not even an audience at the end — still fall under that description, or does his fasting fall outside it? And does the panther settle anything, or is it too convenient a figure of health to bear the weight placed on it?

I am preparing this for a reading group. Open with a single sentence giving your verdict on the thesis, keep the whole piece under 900 words, and close with a short paragraph on what the story sees that the theory alone cannot. Where the Nietzschean reading distorts Kafka, say so plainly.
