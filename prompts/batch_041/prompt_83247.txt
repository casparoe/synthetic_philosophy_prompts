Last week a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about who ought to have the vote, and we ended no closer to agreement than we began. He defends a package: the franchise should follow residence and subjection to the law rather than citizenship, so that long-term residents who pay taxes and are bound by the law are included; the voting age should come down to sixteen; and voting should be compulsory, enforced by a small fine. My objections were all case-by-case — that a sixteen-year-old who follows politics reasons better than a forty-year-old who does not; that a resident who can be deported but cannot vote is ruled without being represented; that compelling a vote treats a right as a duty — and he answered, fairly, that these were intuitions in search of a theory. I want the theory before we resume.

So give me the specification: the desiderata that any adequate theory of the franchise must satisfy. For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp rather than a genuine constraint. I am most interested in the requirements that pull against one another, and I suspect at least one subset cannot be jointly met: admitting only the competent; treating all adult citizens as political equals; drawing a bright line at some age; and treating the vote as a right that may not be compelled. Tell me which subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and what has to give.

Three cases belong on the list whether or not they fit comfortably. First, adults under guardianship whose condition makes informed voting very unlikely — any competence-based account must treat them consistently with the way it treats children. Second, the reflexivity of the question: any change to who votes must be enacted by those who currently vote, so a theory that grounds the franchise in the community's own decision appears circular. Third, a proposal to hand certain technical decisions not to the electorate but to a randomly selected panel of citizens, which trades the principle that everyone decides for the principle that a representative sample does.

Close with a brief verdict on how the main families of answer fare against your list — where each breaks, not a survey. The list matters more to me than the scorecard.
