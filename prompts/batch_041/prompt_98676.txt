I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and there's a fight I keep running into that never seems to go anywhere. It's about national character — whether you can say that whole countries or peoples have stable, describable psychological traits, that the French are like this, the Brazilians like that, and so on. On one side you've got people who insist it's a real thing you can study and even put numbers on, and they point to cross-national surveys, giant personality datasets, and the fact that some of these differences keep showing up no matter how you slice the data. On the other side you've got people, mostly in the academy, who think the whole idea is either incoherent or poison. Incoherent, they say, because there's way more variation inside a country than between countries, and because how somebody fills in a one-to-seven "I'm talkative" scale depends on who they're mentally comparing themselves to, so a five in Tokyo and a five in Toronto aren't the same five and the comparisons are garbage. Poison, they say, because even if you could measure it, generalizing about peoples has a track record and the track record is ugly.

Both sides have heard the other's arguments a hundred times and nobody budges an inch. What's funny is that the exact dimension scores academics roll their eyes at are in every management consultant's slide deck and every airport bookstore pop-psych title, so the thing is radioactive in one building and common sense in the next.

My working theory — and I want you to push on it, not just agree — is that the reason this never resolves is that the two sides aren't actually answering the same question. One is answering an empirical question about whether the differences are real and measurable; the other is answering a normative question about whether it's ok to make the claim; and the phrase "national character" is doing double duty and hiding the seam between them. But it could also be that underneath there's a genuine methodological fight (are the stats actually any good, or is this all aggregation artifacts), or a genuine conceptual one (is "a country has a character" even a coherent claim, or a category error, like asking for the character of a city or a corporation?).

So can you actually diagnose this for me instead of just taking a side? What is each camp really committed to — where do they differ about facts, where about standards of evidence, where about values, and where is it just words? And then tell me honestly what would count as progress here. Is there a study, a distinction, a reformulation of the question that would actually move it, or is this one of those fights that cant be settled and everyone should stop pretending otherwise?
