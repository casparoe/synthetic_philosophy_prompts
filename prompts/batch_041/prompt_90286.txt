I'm a philosophy prof and I teach a seminar on myth vs. history -- Troy, Arthur, the Exodus -- and I keep running into the same one-liner from two collegues who use it to opposite ends. One of them says 'where there's smoke there's fire' every time a student asks whether there's a real war behind the Iliad, or a real war-leader behind Arthur, or a real escape behind the Exodus; he treats the sentence as a licence to go looking for a historical kernel. The other one throws the same sentence back at him as an accusation: in exactly these cases the smoke is the only evidence there is for the fire, so the slogan just names the fallacy she thinks he's committing. I cant tell which of them is doing violence to it, and my suspicion is that it's both.

So give me the menu of precise claims that sentence could be making. I want the whole spread: the readings so weak that nobody could deny them, the ones that are substantive and actually defensible, and the ones that are just false. For each one, say whether it would get either of my collegues the conclusion they want out of it.

Two things to keep in view so the readings don't come out hand-wavy. First, 'the smoke' could be any number of different things -- the bare existence of a tradition, a specific detail inside it, the fact that somebody bothered to write it down, the fact that it took hold in a particular place at a particular time -- and the slogan means something different depending on which of those you pick. Second, keep the tunnel case in play, because it's my favourite test of the whole idea: every school I've ever taught at has a story about a sealed-off tunnel under the building, and every so often one of them turns out to have an actual tunnel. I want a reading of the slogan that explains why that example feels like it counts for the slogan and against it at the same time.

Exactly five numbered readings, a sentence or two each, no intro and no closing paragraph, and skip the reading list -- I don't need anyone's citations, I need the distinctions.
