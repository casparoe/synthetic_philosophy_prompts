I recently came across a short encyclopaedia entry on the Hart–Devlin debate and want to know how much of it to trust before I rely on it. Its account of the two positions runs roughly as follows:

"Devlin and Hart disagreed about whether the criminal law may enforce morality. Devlin held that immoral conduct is intrinsically wrong and that society has a right to uphold virtue, so the law may criminalise conduct on the ground of its immorality alone; he added only that the convictions of the reasonable person should be the standard, so that the law need not defer to the merely eccentric or the oversensitive. Hart answered with Mill's harm principle: conduct may be restricted only where it harms others, and therefore the law may never regulate conduct merely because it is immoral. Since there is no evidence that a shared morality is required for a society's survival, and since Hart's principle is the one that liberal legal systems have largely adopted, Hart's position has prevailed."

Three things about this strike me as possibly off, but I cannot tell whether the fault lies in the entry or in my own expectations, and I would rather not simply assume that Hart won because Mill was right.

First, is the entry's Devlin a fair reconstruction? My impression is that his case was not that immorality as such is wrong and therefore punishable, but something more sociological, about a shared morality being part of the fabric of a society and therefore something the society may protect; and that the reasonable person was less a liberal safeguard than the figure whose sentiments fix the content of the morality that is to be enforced. If that is right, then the entry has quietly substituted one kind of legal moralist for another, and I would like to know exactly what has been swapped in and what it changes.

Second, does the entry overstate Hart? "The law may never regulate conduct merely because it is immoral" is a much stronger conclusion than I remember being attributed to him, and it makes his objection to Devlin turn wholly on an inherited principle rather than on an argument he supplied. I want to know what his actual case against Devlin was, and whether he had a distinction available to him that the entry simply omits — something about the difference between the morality a society happens to accept and a morality that could be defended.

Third, does the closing inference survive? Even if every sentence about the two positions were accurate, "liberal legal systems have largely adopted Hart's principle, therefore Hart's position has prevailed" appears to change the subject from which view is correct to which view is popular. I also doubt the description itself, since I have the impression that contemporary criminal law still prohibits some conduct on grounds that look moralistic rather than harm-based.

Finally, a friend claims that Devlin's argument is structurally the same as a much more recent one — that certain kinds of speech or information should be restricted not because they harm any particular person, but because they corrode the shared norms a democracy needs in order to function. He says this shows Devlin was making an argument we still take seriously, merely in different dress. Is that comparison fair, or does it inherit the same ambiguity as the encyclopaedia entry?

Please go through the entry sentence by sentence and tell me, for each claim, whether someone who works on this debate would accept it, wince at it, or call it a distortion; indicate which of the distortions a defender of Devlin would resist and which a defender of Hart would resist; and say whether any conclusion the entry draws can be salvaged once the misdescriptions are corrected. Where the entry flattens a distinction that matters — I suspect there is more than one kind of legal moralism in play, and possibly more than one ground for criminalisation besides harm — set the distinctions out and tell me what turns on them.
