Okay so I'm in an aesthetics seminar this term and we've spent the last four weeks on whether a morally bad work can still be a good work, and honestly the room hasn't moved an inch, and I don't think it's because anyone in there is dumb.

Here's the shape of it. Camp one — call them the separatists — says aesthetic value and ethical value are just different currencies. A novel can be cruel and also be a masterpiece; you only have to add the two up when you're deciding what to spend your evening on. Camp two says no, the moral stuff gets inside the aesthetic: if a work's cruelty is doing real structural work — if the whole point is the way it makes you complicit, or the voice it builds out of that cruelty — then the moral defect is an aesthetic defect, not a seperate debit on another ledger. And camp three (two people, and they're loud) say everyone's assuming moral flaws can only subtract. Sometimes a work's willingness to be monstrous is exactly what makes it powerful; we'd think less of it if it were nice about it.

Every seminar runs the same loop. Camp two does the move where a work is flawed because it prescribes a response you can't honestly have — you're supposed to feel for the narrator and you can't, and that failure is inside the experience of the work, so it's an artistic failure. Camp one says that's just taste dressed up as logic, and anyway you can have the prescribed response while disapproving of it. Camp three says the example is rigged. Then somebody brings up the artist's life and we burn forty minutes on whether we're even allowed to care, which is obviously a different question and we all know it.

Last week the TA handed out a sheet that split moral defects into three kinds, and it's the only genuinely new thing anyone's said all term: a work can be morally bad in what it depicts, in what it asks you to go along with (approve of, enjoy, identify with), or in what it simply takes for granted that you already accept. And she added a second pair on the sheet: the value of the work, versus the value of what the work does to you while you're inside it.

My hypothesis — tell me if it's dumb — is that this isn't one disagreement but about four stacked on top of each other. Some of the fighting is just equivocation, because "immoral art" is covering all three of the TA's categories at once and "good art" is covering at least two different things. Some of it is a real disagreement about whether value is one currency or several. Some of it is a disagreement about which cases count as data in the first place — whether your inability to enjoy a cruel book is a fact about the book or a fact about you. And underneath all that I think there might be an empirical question nobody's actually testing, about whether moral disapproval really does block aesthetic appreciation or whether people just report that it does.

**What kind of disagreement this actually is** (~600 words): the diagnosis. Verbal, substantive, different questions under one name, hidden empirical question, or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for — and which parts of the debate are which, since I doubt it's uniform.

**Where the words are doing the work** (~250 words): the specific places where "immoral" or "good" shifts meaning mid-argument, and who's guilty of it.

**What would count as progress** (~300 words): given your diagnosis, what would actually move this — a distinction, a case, a study, a change of question — or an honest argument that nothing will.

**Where you'd put your money** (~100 words): not which camp is right, but which camp is closest to being right about what the fight is even about.
