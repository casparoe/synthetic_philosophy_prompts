I am assembling an application for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in decision theory and applied ethics, and I need a three-page research proposal from you. The half-formed idea I would like turned into something defensible concerns what I have been calling, for lack of a settled name, the surrogate-goal defense against acausal threats. The setup is this: an acausal threat is one that operates only through the threatened party's beliefs about what the threatener would do, with no causal channel in either direction — the threatener cannot make the threat credible by any action, and the threatened party's compliance or refusal cannot feed back into the threatener's incentives in the ordinary way. The proposed defense is for the threatened party to adopt, as a genuine goal of their own, the pursuit of some outcome other than the one being threatened, chosen precisely so that the threatener's threatened action would no longer move them; if the threatener knows this, the threat becomes toothless, since carrying it out would no longer secure what the threatener wanted. What I want to work out is what has to be true for that defense to be rational rather than mere wishful thinking, and what adopting such a goal actually commits one to.

Two things make me think the question is genuinely open rather than settled by the usual machinery of precommitment, credible threats, and deterrence. First, there is a stability puzzle about sincerity: the defense seems to require that the surrogate goal be real rather than feigned, since a threatener who suspects you are only pretending can discount it — yet in an acausal setting your mere policy or disposition is itself something the threatener reasons about, so it is unclear whether the distinction between genuinely having the goal and merely being disposed to act as if you had it can even be drawn, and if it cannot, whether the defense collapses into something trivial or into something impossible. Second, there is an ethical question that I suspect is usually left implicit: adopting a surrogate goal may amount to making the welfare of others a matter of indifference to you, and I want to know whether that is a permissible thing to do, whether it matters that the indifference is adopted deliberately as a defensive measure, and whether the answer changes when the threatened outcome is catastrophic rather than merely costly to you.

Please write the proposal so that it does the following. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, rather than gesturing at "the rationality of surrogate goals." Explain why the existing treatments leave it open, in terms of positions and arguments rather than a literature survey. Lay out the approach you would actually take — I expect this to be mainly conceptual analysis supplemented by some simple decision-theoretic modelling, and I would want at least one worked toy case carried through from start to finish — and say why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give a realistic scope for twelve months, including an honest account of which part is most likely to prove too large. Do not include a bibliography or any citations; I will fill those in myself, and I would rather the proposal not depend on particular named sources.
