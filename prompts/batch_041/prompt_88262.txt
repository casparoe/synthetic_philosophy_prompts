Alright, I need a thought experiment for something I'm writing on mental causation, and I want it built to order rather than whatever example everyone recycles.

What I want: a case where, on a difference-making / interventionist account of causation, the mental property comes out as a genuine cause while its physical realizer comes out as a bad cause or no cause at all — even though the physical story is fully deterministic and complete, so the exclusion argument's sufficiency premise is satisfied to the letter. The whole point is that the case should force the exclusion argument to lean on something other than sufficiency, and I want to see which of its other premises actually does the work.

Constraints:
- Realistic. A kitchen, a commute, an argument with someone, a form being filled in. No brain scans, no neurons, no zombies or Twin Earth, no sci-fi devices.
- The scenario in one paragraph, told as a story about people doing something, not as a list of variables.
- No crude overdetermination: one causal chain to the effect, not two. The realizer has to be sufficient for the effect, not a partial or contributing cause.
- Don't get the result by positing emergent mental forces or new laws; the only laws in the case are ordinary physical ones plus whatever you need to describe the mental side.
- Don't just assert that the mental property is a cause. It has to fall out of the tests you run.

Then do the work:
1. Lay the case out the way an interventionist would: name the effect variable, the mental property, the physical realizer, and anything else you need, and say for each candidate cause what the surgical intervention looks like and what happens to the effect.
2. Say exactly why the realizer fails or does badly and the mental property passes. Name the condition doing the work — proportionality, specificity, stability, whatever it is — and be honest about whether it's one condition or several bundled together.
3. Run the exclusion argument on the case. Say which premise it needs to get to "the mental property isn't a cause", and whether that premise is independently plausible or is basically the conclusion restated.
4. Collapse test: change one feature so the two levels come apart (e.g. the realizer can be swapped around while the content stays fixed) and show the verdicts flipping. Tell me which feature was load-bearing.
5. Counterattack: how does someone who thinks the exclusion argument is right anyway — they grant the higher-level property is a cause but deny it's a distinct one, or they say the difference-making test is too weak to settle anything — go after this case? Strongest version, not a strawman.
6. Finally: would the case survive if the mental property were a very fine-grained content (a determinate) rather than a coarse one (a determinable)? I suspect thats where it gets shaky. And if the case ends up making the realizer's causal status depend on the mental property rather than the other way round, say so plainly instead of hiding it — I'd rather know thats what I'm buying.
