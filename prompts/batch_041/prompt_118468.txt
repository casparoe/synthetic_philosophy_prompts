I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm midway through a feature on the Allais paradox — the famous pair of choices in which most people, offered a guaranteed million on one hand and a lottery that pays a million with near-certainty but might pay five million on the other, take the sure million, and then, in the second problem, take the lottery over the smaller, surer chance. I know the standard line: taken together, the two answers violate the independence axiom, because each problem contains the same 89% branch and the axiom says a branch shared by both options should cancel out. What I did not anticipate was how quickly my sources convert that into an argument that proves far more than I am willing to print. A game theorist I interviewed gave me the following, and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny before I quote it.

(1) A requirement of practical rationality is immunity to a money pump: there can be no finite series of trades, each of which you would voluntarily accept because you prefer the outcome to what you already hold, that ends with you certainly worse off than you began. (2) The Allais answers are not immune — because you prefer the sure million in the first problem but the lottery in the second, a bookmaker who knows your pattern can offer you a run of swaps that you accept one at a time and that leave you holding less than you started with. (3) So the Allais answers are irrational. (4) And since those answers are precisely what the independence axiom forbids, independence is a genuine requirement of rationality, expected utility theory is vindicated, and everyone who reports the Allais intuition — including the economists who admit to sharing it — is simply in the grip of a bias, the way one is in the grip of an optical illusion.

It is that last move that makes the argument prove too much for me: it recasts a widespread and, to my eye, sensible preference as a defect in the person who has it, and it leaves no space for a theory on which risk-sensitivity is anything but a mistake. I suspect that one of the steps is carrying more weight than its wording lets on, but I cannot tell which one, and I honestly cannot say what a money pump really requires — so I cannot judge whether the bookmaker's sequence actually goes through. Tell me which step fails, why it looked convincing in the first place, and what it costs to give it up; and please commit to a single diagnosis, with reasons, rather than laying out every reading and leaving the verdict to me.
