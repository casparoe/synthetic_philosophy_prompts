Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of an evening about the precautionary principle, and I came away realizing that neither of us could state clearly what the principle actually commits you to. I would like your help reconstructing the philosophical commitments it presupposes.

Here is the practice I have in mind, as it operates in European Union food-safety and chemicals regulation. When a substance is suspected of causing serious harm but the scientific evidence is inconclusive — the studies are few, the mechanisms plausible but unconfirmed, the effects difficult to measure — regulators may restrict or ban it anyway, and they assign the burden of proof to the producer to demonstrate safety rather than to the regulator to demonstrate harm. Contrast this with how the same regulatory system treats a new pharmaceutical: there the producer must supply positive evidence of both safety and efficacy, and approval is withheld until that evidence meets a demanding statistical standard. Absence of demonstrated harm never gets a drug onto the market, yet it is apparently sufficient to keep a chemical off it. And when later evidence fails to confirm the suspected hazard, the restriction is frequently left in place regardless.

What I want from you is an account of the implicit theory. What view of evidence does this pattern presuppose — does it treat the absence of evidence as itself a kind of evidence under specified conditions, or does it reject that inference while nonetheless licensing action on mere suspicion? What view of rational action under uncertainty does it require: straightforward expected-value maximization, a maximin-style rule, a deontological constraint against imposing uncertain risks on others without their consent, or an asymmetry between errors of commission and errors of omission? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — cost-benefit analysis, ordinary Bayesian updating, a harm-minimization framework? And are its parts consistent with one another, given that the pharmaceutical case seems to show the system does not operate on a general anti-risk prior at all?

My friend's position is that the precautionary principle is nothing but risk aversion dressed in legal language, or a political device for shifting the burden of proof onto parties who are expensive to inconvenience. My own suspicion is that it presupposes a substantive moral asymmetry between causing harm and failing to prevent it. Please adjudicate between these, but do the harder work first: separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same regulatory behavior. If the presupposed view turned out to be replaceable by an expected-value or a consent-based framework, say concretely what in the practice would have to change.
