ok so I just spent two hours arguing with my friend Sam about whether it would be a good thing if there were more people in the world — he's a pronatalist, thinks more happy humans is obviously better, wants child benefits and cheap housing for families, the whole pitch — and I came out of it not really knowing what I think. Can you audit my position for me? I'll write down everything I've got and you tell me what doesn't hang together. Sorry in advance, it's a mess.

1. The repugnant conclusion is repugnant. A world of ten billion people living lives that are barely worth living is not better than a world with a few million people who are doing great. Honestly I want to say it's worse. If that's what being a totalist amounts to then count me out.

2. But I'm not an averagist either. Averagism says a world with one ecstatic person in it beats a world with eight billion happy ones, and that adding someone whose life is worth living makes the world worse just because they drag the average down — plus, if you could do it painlessly, it'd be an improvement to get rid of everyone below average. Sam says look, total or average, pick one, there is no third thing. That smells like a cheat to me but I can't say why.

3. Existence isn't itself a benefit or a burden. The world isn't better just for containing more happy people in it, and it isn't worse just for containing more either. What's good is making the people who are already here better off. I'd say the same about me: if I'd never been born, the world wouldn't have been worse off and my absence wouldn't have been a loss to anyone, including me — and I'm still glad I'm here.

4. Adding a person whose life is worth living can't make the world worse. You take a world, you add someone who's glad to be alive, you haven't wronged anybody and there's more happiness around than there was. I can't see how that's a downgrade.

5. Better-than is transitive. If A beats B and B beats C then A beats C. That's not a controversial claim, it's just what the words mean.

6. A reason has to be a reason for somebody. "There would be more total happiness" isn't a reason unless it cashes out as a reason for a particular person. Sam thinks that's sentimental mush, but I think it's the only thing standing between me and (1) being false.

7. My cousin is deciding whether to have a kid now or wait. She's got a condition that clears up in about a year; if she conceives now the kid will have a lifelong painful condition — lots of hospital, real suffering, but still a life worth living, the kid would be glad to exist. If she waits, she has a kid with none of that. I think it's flat out wrong of her to go ahead now. But I can't work out who it's wrong to — the kid she'd have is fine, and the kid she'd have later doesn't exist in the world where she doesn't wait. So it feels like I'm saying she did something wrong to nobody.

8. And I don't actually mind the child benefit stuff Sam wants. If the state pays people to have kids, I don't think that's an outrage, even though I don't think more people is better in itself. Sam says that's just (3) contradicted in practice. I said those are different questions, but I couldn't explain the difference to him.

What I want from you: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, how bad each real one is — rank them, don't just list them as equally bad — and for each one, what I'd have to give up and what that costs me. If some of them can be saved by restating them more carefully, say how. But don't just tell me it all works out, I'm pretty sure some of these are definately doing something to each other.
