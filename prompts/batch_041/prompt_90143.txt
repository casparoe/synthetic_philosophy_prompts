I belong to a small discussion group — six or seven of us, mostly not philosophers, meeting fortnightly — and this term we have been working through the chapter of Franklin's Autobiography in which he sets out what he calls his bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection: the thirteen virtues with a short precept apiece, the little book ruled in red ink into columns for the days of the week, the decision to give each virtue a week of strict attention in turn, the scheme of employment for the twenty-four hours, and the Humility entry added at the end after a Quaker friend told him he was generally thought proud, overbearing and rather insolent. We have now reached the point where the group has to decide whether to run the thirteen-week course ourselves, and two of us have landed on opposite sides of that question in a way that has become more interesting than the practical question. I would like you to write the dialogue that ought to take place between them, with each of them given a real case.

The first should defend the method on grounds that would survive Franklin turning out to be a scoundrel: the thought that knowing what is right is not where the difficulty lies — habit and inclination are — so that moral improvement is a problem of training rather than of insight, and that a person can be trained by a deliberately designed regimen, one item at a time, with a written record, in the way a hand is trained by copying engraved letters. Let that character make the strongest version of the case: that the method's value does not depend on reaching the perfection it aims at, that Franklin's report of his own failure is evidence for the method rather than against it, and that even the appearance-first route to Humility — putting on a manner with violence to natural inclination until it becomes easy and habitual — is simply what habit-formation looks like from the inside. The second should press that the project measures something other than what it claims: that a ledger of spots tracks what is countable and noticeable, that the list itself contains no virtue whose exercise essentially requires another person's good beyond refraining from wronging them, and that Franklin's own concessions — that he acquired the appearance of humility and not much of the reality, that a spotless character would be a kind of foppery in morals and would make him ridiculous, that a benevolent man should keep a few faults to keep his friends in countenance, and that the scheme of Order is unworkable for anyone whose hours are not his own — are not incidental embarrassments but symptoms of what kind of project this is.

Neither character should be a fool or a cynic, and neither should win by reciting the other's misfortunes; both are competent and arguing in good faith. I would rather they paraphrase Franklin's text than quote it, since I do not trust either of them to get the wording right and I want the argument rather than a dispute about phrasing. Let the exchange turn at least twice on a specific feature of the project — the choice of virtues, the one-at-a-time sequencing and what it assumes about whether the virtues are separable, or the move from examining one's conduct to keeping a score — and let each of them concede something real under pressure. Do not have them cite scholars or works; I want the reasoning, not a reading list. End with a short note, clearly marked off from the dialogue, telling me which parts of this disagreement are settled among people who have studied the Autobiography closely and which are genuinely contested, and flagging anywhere the dialogue has leaned on a contested reading as though it were the plain sense of the text.
