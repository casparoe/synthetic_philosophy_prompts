I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a historical novel set in a provincial German town between the wars — a family of printers, one son a socialist, a daughter married into the civil service — and I'm stuck in a recurring argument with my editor, who wants the book organized around a single decisive man whose choices bend everyone else's lives. My instinct is the opposite, and it's Tolstoyan: the essay that closes War and Peace argues that we mistake the visible gestures of the figure at the front for the cause of the movement of the mass behind him, and that a real account of history would need something like a calculus of infinitely small, individually unaccountable acts. The case my editor keeps throwing at me, though, is one Tolstoy could never have seen: the Third Reich and the Holocaust. Would the camps have come anyway, under some other leader, or does everything hinge on that one man's obsessions?

So what I want from you is not a summary of Tolstoy's philosophy of history but his verdict on the counterfactual and on our way of asking it: whether he would have treated the question as well-formed at all, whether he would have called the Holocaust inevitable (his determinism strikes me as something other than fatalism, and I'd like that distinction to do real work here), and what he would have said to the historians who divide into those who trace it to one man's intentions and those who trace it to the competitive radicalization of an apparatus that no one controlled. Where would he have had to concede that this case strains his framework? Give me your best reconstruction, and be explicit about where it is most likely to go wrong: tell me which consideration about Tolstoy, or which feature of the case, would most likely overturn your verdict on what he would have said. American English, please, and no more than four hundred and fifty words.
