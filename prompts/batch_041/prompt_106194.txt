Two friends of mine in a reading group got into an argument about Pascal's stuff on justice and force, and they've asked me to referee but I honestly can't tell who has the better of it. I'll lay out the exchange as fairly as I can.

We'd been going through the Pensées, and the bit where Pascal says justice without force can't do anything, and force without justice is tyranny, so you have to put the two together -- make what's just strong, or make what's strong just. Also the fragment where he says justice is whatever has been established, so our established laws get treated as just without being examined, just because they're established.

Dev's take: this is might-makes-right with extra steps. He says the "make what's strong just" move is the giveaway -- it's just whoever holds power getting to call it justice. And the "justice is what is established" line seals it: there's no independent standard, established law is the standard by definition, and Pascal even says we shouldn't examine it. So Pascal is a legal positivist at best, and a cynic at worst, and either way the doctrine rationalizes whatever regime happens to be in charge.

Mara's take: that's a misread. She says Pascal is running a distinction between orders -- the body, the mind, and charity -- and there's no proportion between them, so you can't derive political authority from spiritual truth and you can't enforce inner justice with coercion. When he says justice is what's established, he's describing how we in fact treat custom as the ground of law because we can't agree on first principles, not claiming there's nothing above custom. She points out he also holds that there are natural and immutable laws, just very few of them, so he's not a pure positivist.

Dev comes back: fine, but if the natural laws are few and unenforceable, and the operational rule on the ground is obey what's established, then in practice it's indistinguishable from positivism. He says the critical edge Mara wants just isn't there -- you can have all the secret thoughts you like, you still obey. He says this is exactly the structure of every authoritarian apology: power is power, and justice is whatever power says it is, but we'll call it a mystery of the orders so nobody feels bad about it.

Mara's reply: the critical edge is precisely in the orders. Political power can't reach the order of charity, and that's why the powerful can be judged by something above them. She says the whole point of his address on the condition of the great is that the great have no natural superiority -- their title is a human institution, accident and custom, not merit -- so they can be told they have no natural right to esteem, even while you bow. That's a demotion of power, not a sanctification of it.

Dev: but the demotion is only in the "secret thought." Externally you still obey, and there's no license for resistance anywhere in the picture. So for anything that actually matters politically, Pascal lands on the side of established power. He says Mara is confusing a private consolation with a political critique.

Mara: obedience and thinking-it-just are different things, and separating them is what lets Pascal deny that power justifies itself. And "make what's strong just" is an imperative -- it tells the powerful they have a job to do, conforming their force to justice, not a description saying they already have.

They've stopped speaking to each other over this. What I want is a move-by-move adjudication, not a winner overall: which of Dev's moves are right, which of Mara's are right, and where each of them is smuggling something in. In particular: is Pascal a positivist, a natural-law theorist, both, or neither? Does the doctrine leave any real room for criticizing or reforming an unjust established order, or does it collapse into obedience-plus-private-grumbling? And is there something both of them are missing about what the two-orders thing is even supposed to do in the argument?
