I am writing a seminar paper on the epistemology of testimony, and I keep returning to the Salem witch trials of 1692 as a case that neither of the two standard positions seems able to handle cleanly. Take the following as the facts you need, and do not worry about filling in more of the history than this: in Salem Village a group of young women began reporting fits and visions in which they were tormented by the specters of their neighbors; their reports were received as testimony about events nobody else could observe and were decisive in bringing people to trial; the court admitted spectral evidence, that is, testimony that the accused's shape or spirit had appeared to the afflicted; the accused's own confessions, which several made and which saved their lives, were treated as confirming the accusers' reports, and those confessions implicated further people in turn; there was essentially no independent, non-testimonial evidence of the alleged witchcraft; several leading ministers, Increase Mather among them, objected that spectral evidence could not by itself convict anyone, since the Devil could assume the shape of an innocent person; and by the time the proceedings collapsed, nineteen people had been hanged, one man had been pressed to death for refusing to plead, the court had been dissolved, some of the accusers had admitted fabricating their charges, and the colony had entered a long season of retraction and apology.

What I want is a two-way analysis, and I would like it kept disciplined throughout. First, take the reductionist position, the view associated with Hume and pressed by those who follow him, on which testimony carries no independent epistemic weight and a hearer is entitled to accept a report only to the extent that it can be reconstructed out of perception, memory, and inference together with background evidence about the speaker's competence and honesty, and tell me what a court guided by that position should have done at Salem: what would have counted as the independent evidence, whether the total absence of any such evidence should have ended the prosecutions before they began, and whether a reductionist can even make sense of the use made of the confessions, given that a confession is itself testimony and this one was extracted under threat of execution. Then take the anti-reductionist position, the view running from Reid through Coady and defended by those who hold that a hearer is entitled to trust a speaker in the absence of positive reason for doubt, and tell me what it says about the same court: whether the accusers' reports were entitled to acceptance by default, whether their mutual agreement amounted to genuine corroboration or was merely one source repeated, and what the anti-reductionist must say about spectral evidence, which is testimony about an experience no one else can check and which was, in this case, the only thing on offer. Be explicit about who counts as a testifier at each stage, what the hearer's total evidence was, and where each theory simply falls silent rather than being stretched to speak.

The second half matters more to me. Salem looks at first like a straightforward vindication of reductionism, since the court accepted testimony without independent support and hanged the innocent, but I suspect that verdict is too quick: a reductionist court would have had nothing whatever to work with, and the reductionist's own checks, whether the speaker is honest, whether the speaker is competent, whether the speakers agree, were in a sense all satisfied, since the accusers were numerous, they agreed with one another, and many of the accused confessed. So I want you to consider whether the failure here is a failure of the anti-reductionist default of trust, or a failure of something both views share, namely the assumption that agreement among many testifiers is itself evidence, when the testifiers are not independent and are responding to the same expectations, the same interrogations, and the same social pressure. Bring in the further question of whose speech was admitted as testimony at all: the accusers' reports were credited, the accused's denials were read as further proof of guilt, and the standing to be believed tracked gender, age, and social position. Does either position have the resources to explain that asymmetry, or does it need an account of credibility, of who gets listened to and why, that neither reductionism nor anti-reductionism supplies? End by saying which of the two, if either, you would want a court to adopt, and what would have to be added to it to make it safe in a case like this one.
