You are an experienced examiner in normative ethics, accustomed to setting finals for a strong upper-level undergraduate or taught-master's cohort. I need your help drafting one. Work carefully, and before you compose anything, spend a few lines brainstorming where the crux of the question genuinely lies — what a student would have to get right that a fluent summary of the literature would not.

Some background. A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the cluelessness objection to consequentialism: the thought that the remote causal consequences of almost anything we do are so diffuse and chaotic that we cannot know whether our actions do more good than harm, and that a criterion telling us to maximise the good therefore either cannot guide us or guides us badly. We kept talking past each other, and I want the exam to force students to confront the point we never settled.

Here is the distinction I want the question built around. Call it the difference between input cluelessness and output cluelessness. Input cluelessness is ignorance of the causal facts: we do not know what our act will set off downstream. Output cluelessness is a problem that would survive even if every causal fact were in: the values of the consequences do not obviously compose into anything that can be totalled or ranked, so there may be no fact of the matter about whether the act was for the best. I want students to see that the standard reply — replace "maximise the good" with "maximise expected good" — is an answer to the first problem and not to the second, and to work out whether that asymmetry matters.

The question should also make them wrestle with a thought experiment I have been chewing over. Call it the Cup of Tea. I buy a cup of tea; the causal ripples are unbounded, so input cluelessness is total; suppose, further, that we could enumerate every downstream effect and still could not say whether the sum was positive, so output cluelessness is total too. The question should ask whether consequentialism can say anything at all about the Cup of Tea case, and if all it can say is "act so as to maximise expected good, where the expectation is taken over an aggregate that may not exist", whether that is a substantive criterion or a dressed-up permission to do as one pleases.

Please write, in British English:

1. The essay question itself — a single question, answerable in roughly 75 minutes, that cannot be answered by reciting the received positions on cluelessness. It should compel a verdict on whether the input/output distinction rescues consequentialism, merely reframes it, or exposes it, and it should require candidates to say what work the consequentialist criterion is still doing once we concede that we cannot apply it.

2. A model answer of about 600 words, written as a first-class student would write it rather than as an encyclopaedia entry.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 that names five or six must-hit moves, the marks attaching to each, and, for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme must separate an A from a B: state explicitly which moves a B answer can miss and still pass, and which one or two moves an A answer must have and a B answer characteristically lacks.

Do not ask students to cite anything, and put no references in the model answer — the examination is closed-book and I do not want anyone rewarded for name-dropping. Where you need a label for a position, coin a neutral one rather than leaning on an author's name.
