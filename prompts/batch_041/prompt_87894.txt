I am the research and evaluation analyst for a mid-sized urban school district — the only person on staff who reads the studies behind the proposals that reach the deputy superintendent. I have one graduate statistics course behind me, no access to paywalled journals, and no time: three or four times a month, a vendor pitch, a board member's public comment, a grant application, or a parent letter cites a heritability figure for a cognitive or behavioral trait and draws a conclusion about what the district should do. My working instinct, after two years of this, is that every such citation is a red herring. I would like a procedure that keeps me honest, including in the cases where that instinct is wrong.

Write me a decision procedure I can run on a single proposal in under two hours, producing a two-page memo, using only the documents in front of me and arithmetic no harder than a ratio. It should be usable by someone who knows the phrase "heritability is a population statistic" and not much more, and it has to survive scrutiny from a board member who does know more. Do not send me to the literature; I cannot get it, and a reading list is useless to me.

The procedure should take the form of ordered questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It must, at minimum, force me to distinguish four different things a heritability number might be asked to do — explain why individuals within a population differ, explain why two populations differ, predict what happens if we change the environment, and predict which individuals will respond to a change — and then to identify which of the four the proposal actually needs and whether the estimate on offer could possibly support it. It must require me to state the population and the range of environments the estimate came from, and to treat an estimate drawn from a narrow environmental range as uninformative about any intervention that widens that range. It must handle the equal-environments assumption and the gap between twin-based and DNA-based estimates without requiring me to adjudicate either. It must keep "heritable" and "unchangeable" apart in a way I can say out loud in a memo without delivering a lecture. And it should end in one of a fixed set of verdicts: irrelevant to this decision; relevant but insufficient; relevant and cutting against the proposal; relevant and supporting it; or not assessable on the available evidence.

Then run it on three matters I am currently sitting on. First, a vendor proposing an intensive one-to-one decoding program for struggling readers, on the argument that a twin study puts the heritability of reading difficulty near 0.7, so the difficulty is intrinsic and only an intensive program can reach it. Second, a board member arguing against funding a resilience curriculum because grit is substantially heritable and teaching it is therefore futile. Third, a proposal to reserve a new reading intervention for children with a particular response profile, supported by evidence that the size of the intervention's effect varies across children in ways that track a heritable characteristic. For each, say what the procedure yields, which step is doing the work, and where I am at risk of overclaiming. The third case should come out in a way I will not like; do not soften it to spare my instinct.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire — the situations in which a well-designed study would overturn its verdict and nothing in the memo would let me see that coming — and what it cannot do at all.
