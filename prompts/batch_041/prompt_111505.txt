Hey, I'm a third-year PhD student in archaeology (Mediterranean prehistory, mostly lithics and depositional stuff) and I keep having the same argument with a friend in my department who does paleogenetics, and I want to actually work out what I think on paper instead of re-litigating it over coffee. The argument is about whether we can ever legitimately get from stuff to beliefs — not to behavior, beliefs — and I have a concrete case in mind that I think makes it less of a seminar abstraction. My advisor is publishing a rock-shelter deposit: forty-odd small clay figures, all female-form, all with the head snapped off in roughly the same way, laid in a pit lined with red ochre, and sealed under a hearth that shows one episode of use and nothing after. No domestic debris anywhere in that unit. Two occupation layers up, in a much later phase, the same figure type turns up in a child's burial and also, unceremoniously, in a midden next to what look like gaming pieces. My advisor reads the pit as evidence of a belief that the dead return, or that persons get reborn. A visiting colleague says you cannot infer a belief from a deposit, period — you can infer that people did something costly and patterned, and calling that belief is just projection. A third person in the lab says it's taphonomy, that the heads simply break that way. And the old worry hangs over the whole thing: the further you get from diet and technology, the shakier the inference, until you're telling stories about people who can't correct you.

So here's what I want. Take the role of someone who works seriously on archaeological inference and the philosophy of the historical sciences, think carefully before you start and maybe jot your way through the case first, and write me an essay that takes a position on the controversy: is inference to past belief ever warranted from material remains, and if so, under what conditions? Don't hedge into an it-depends-on-context non-answer — I want a verdict, and I want it to bite on my case specifically: say what my advisor gets to claim, what the colleague gets to claim, and which of them has to give something up. I'm half-convinced by a test I've been calling the costly-and-independent test (a deposit licenses a belief attribution only if the behavior is costly, recurs across contexts that vary independently, and isn't fully explained by practical ends), but I want you to stress-test it rather than just apply it — if it collapses, show me where, and tell me the strongest thing the skeptic gets right even if you rule against him.

Format it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no closing paragraph, with point five carrying the verdict. American English, please. And skip the citations and reading list — I want the argument, not a bibliography.
