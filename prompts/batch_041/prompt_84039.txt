I'm a novelist, three books in, and I keep a notebook of poems I write when I'm stuck between drafts. Most of them are bad, but a few people I trust say otherwise, so here we are. Over Thanksgiving my brother-in-law, who's a philosophy postdoc, got me going about the thing Bernard Williams does with a man who jumps in the water and saves his wife rather than the stranger who is also drowning, and who then, when someone asks him why, says well, she's my wife, and in a situation like that a person saves his wife. Williams's complaint is that the second half of that sentence is one thought more than the act needed, since she's my wife was already carrying the whole thing. I've been obssessing over it for a month. What gets me isnt the ethics, its the grammar: there's a thought that actually holds the act up, and there's a thought you add when you have to be answerable to somebody who wasnt standing there, and those are two different thoughts even when they come out in the same words. I've been calling the first one the load-bearing thought and the second one the covering thought. In my notebook version the stranger in the water is a kid, which I know wrecks the example as an example, but it makes the covering thoughts more desperate, and desperate is what I want. Also, the guy shouldnt come off as a creep — he's decent, that's the whole problem.

So write me the poem. I'm picturing the additive kind, like the house that jack built, where each stanza repeats everything that came before it and then adds exactly one new line: the repeated lines are the act, and every new line is another covering thought laid over it, and the poem should get a little harder to breathe in as it goes. Voice: the man himself, some years on, saying it out loud to somebody who isnt going to let him off the hook — a dinner party, a kid of his own, a therapist, your call. Eight to ten stanzas, I'd guess. Two things I care about more than anything else: the catchphrase everyone quotes about the extra thought should never appear, and neither should the words integrity, impartial, or utilitarian — I want the form to do it, not the vocabulary. And the final added line has to be the one that actually answers the objection, the one that would satisfy a committee, so the poem ends with the act completely buried under the defense of it and the reader feeling the burial happen.

The test I'd apply is this: somebody who's read Williams should be able to point at exactly which added line is the one too many, and say why the lines before it are fine; somebody who's never heard of him should just read it as a poem about a man who did one thing fast and then spent the rest of his life explaining it. If the additive form fights the material, tell me and try something else — a glosa, a run of couplets that keep restating themselves, whatever — but keep the growing, one-clause-at-a-time thing, because that's the whole poem. And no citations obviously, it's a poem.
