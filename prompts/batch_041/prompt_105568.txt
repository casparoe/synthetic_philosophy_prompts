I serve on my university’s Moral Dilemma Review Board, and I need help predicting how our board would rule on a new case given our past precedents. Our board classifies submitted cases as either genuine moral dilemmas or not genuine moral dilemmas. Here are the precedents. In the Transplant case, a surgeon can save one patient with a heart transplant or save five patients with a different organ from the same donor; the one and the five have equal claims; we ruled that this is a genuine moral dilemma. In the Promise case, a person promised to meet a friend at the airport but encounters a stranger having a heart attack on the way; the friend will be mildly inconvenienced if stood up, the stranger will die if not helped; we ruled that this is not a genuine moral dilemma because the promise is not morally binding in that situation and the emergency cancels it. In the Two Promises case, a person promised to attend a sibling’s wedding and promised to help a neighbor move out of an abusive home on the same day; the two promises are equally weighty and incompatible; we ruled that this is a genuine moral dilemma. In the Whistleblower case, a person can either lie under oath to protect a colleague from being fired or tell the truth and get the colleague fired; we ruled that this is not a genuine moral dilemma because lying is not morally required and truth-telling is. In the Dying Friend case, a person promised a dying friend to destroy a letter revealing a secret, but the letter is also evidence that would exonerate an innocent person in prison; the duty to keep the promise and the duty to exonerate are equally weighty; we ruled that this is a genuine moral dilemma. In the Self-Sacrifice case, a person can either donate a kidney to a stranger or not; we ruled that this is not a genuine moral dilemma because donating is supererogatory and not required. In the Two Children case, a parent can save only one of two children from a fire; both have equal claims; we ruled that this is a genuine moral dilemma. By supererogatory I mean beyond the call of duty; by incommensurable I mean that neither option is better than the other. In each genuine-dilemma case, our written opinions have said that the two options are incommensurable and that no moral principle resolves the conflict; in each non-genuine case, we have said that either one option is not morally required or one option is clearly weightier.

The new case before us is the Confidential Letter. A lawyer promised a client to keep a document confidential. The document would exonerate a wrongly convicted person. The duty to keep the promise is slightly more weighty than the duty to exonerate, because revealing the document would endanger the client’s life. The lawyer cannot both keep the promise and exonerate the prisoner. This is the first case we have seen in which both options are morally required but one is weightier than the other. I need to decide how to vote in a way that is consistent with our precedents, and I want to understand what rule our board has actually been running.

Please predict the board’s verdict on the Confidential Letter. Then identify the rule the board seems to be running, and at least two other rules that fit all of the earlier verdicts equally well. For each rule, state what it predicts about the new case. Say which rule you think is the best reconstruction of the board’s practice and why. Identify the closest earlier case as an analog, and state what principle the board would be adopting if it rules that the new case is a genuine dilemma and what principle it would be adopting if it rules that it is not. Finally, name a further case that would best distinguish the rules you have identified.
