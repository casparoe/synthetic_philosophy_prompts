For several years I have been trying to run my own decisions on Herbert Simon's principle of satisficing, and I want to settle the normative question before I commit to it any further. My understanding is that satisficing means choosing an option that is good enough rather than the best one available, so that the satisficer knowingly accepts a worse outcome than she could have had; what licenses this is the cost of looking, since search takes time and effort and at some point it stops being worth another look. On this reading, satisficing is a concession to limited attention rather than a principle in its own right, and the aspiration level, the bar an option has to clear, is just a rough stand-in for the point at which the expected value of further search falls to zero. I am currently applying all this to a search with a real deadline, deciding how long to keep looking before I take a place to live in a city where the listings turn over quickly, which is what makes me want to get the principle straight.

A colleague of mine takes the reasoning to its conclusion. She points out that because a satisficer stops at the first option over the bar, she is guaranteed to leave value on the table, and that the only defensible rule is to keep looking until one more look is expected to gain nothing. She also says the aspiration level cannot be derived from anything: nothing tells me whether my bar should sit at eighty percent of my ideal or ninety-five, and any answer I give will express how much searching I feel like doing rather than a fact about what is rational. She adds that the cost of searching can simply be folded into the utility of the outcome, in which case satisficing turns out to be maximizing with an unusual utility function, and the whole idea of bounded rationality adds nothing to the theory of choice.

I find this hard to resist, and yet I suspect Simon was getting at something that survives it. So I would like you to tell me how to set my aspiration level in a principled way, if there is such a way; how I might go about converting myself into a maximizer, or, if that is not something one can do, what the closest attainable substitute is and what I should expect to give up by remaining a satisficer; and whether my colleague is right that good enough is always second best to best, since if she is I would rather learn it now.

Please answer in American English, in several paragraphs of continuous prose. Do not use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables. Write it the way you would write a letter to someone who had put this question to you in earnest.
