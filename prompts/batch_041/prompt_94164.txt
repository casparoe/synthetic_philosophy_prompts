I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long piece about the universal basic income that has migrated from the seminar room to the campaign trail. Before I file it, I want to understand what the leading version of the policy is quietly committed to, because the case its advocates make and the way they have designed the thing do not obviously line up.

The version I have in mind is the one with the most support: a monthly cash payment to every citizen, paid to individuals rather than to households, with no work requirement, no means test, and no conditions attached, and financed not from general income-tax revenue but from a sovereign wealth fund built out of the proceeds of the country's natural resources — the citizens' dividend model. Its defenders describe it as an enlargement of freedom and an affirmation of everyone's equal standing.

But the design carries commitments the freedom talk never mentions. Paying the wealthy as well as the poor implies that the payment is not really about need. Paying individuals rather than families implies that the person, not the household, is the unit that justice counts. Funding it from resource revenues rather than from taxes on labor implies that the wealth in question belongs to everyone in common rather than to those who produced it. And setting the payment below what it takes to live on implies that paid work is still expected of the able-bodied.

Reconstruct for me the theory of justice, freedom, desert, and ownership that this design presupposes — the picture of the citizen's relation to the state and to the national wealth that would have to be true for the policy to hang together. Tell me which of these commitments the design strictly requires and which it merely accommodates, and where the same design would be equally at home under a different underlying theory. Flag any two commitments that sit awkwardly together — whether, for instance, the common-ownership story about the funding can be squared with the individualist, desert-denying picture the unconditional payment seems to assume. Then say what would have to change in the policy if we swapped the presupposed view for its most serious rival, a conception of justice built on reciprocity and contribution rather than on equal ownership. Skip the literature and the citations; I want the implicit theory made explicit and tested for coherence.
