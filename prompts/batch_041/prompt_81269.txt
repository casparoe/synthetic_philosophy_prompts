I write a little satirical column for a newsletter that goes out to maybe forty people, mostly econ and philosophy grad students, and I've been stuck for a week on a piece about Good's theorem -- the result that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can't be made worse off *in expectation* by getting costless information, provided the menu of actions she's choosing among is fixed and her utility doesn't depend on what she happens to believe. What I want to skewer isn't the theorem, it's the way people wave it around like a hall pass: information can't hurt, we're only telling you for your own good, you'd want to know, rationally speaking. Because the fine print is doing all the work and nobody reads the fine print. It's a claim about expectations ex ante, not about how it feels to actually open the envelope; it assumes you'll update correctly and that you simply can't decline to look; and it goes quiet the moment the news changes which options you have or what you care about, or the moment you're the kind of agent who'd rather not know -- there's a whole literature on that, information avoidance, the ostrich effect, people who won't check the portfolio or take the test. A decision theorist should read my piece and think ok, they get it, and never once think I'm claiming the theorem is false.

So here's the ask. Write me a deadpan parody of a member-benefits letter from a made-up insurer, Pangloss Mutual, announcing a new "Total Information Benefit" that quietly enrolls every policyholder by default. Around 600 words, in the voice of the company's Chief Bayesian Officer -- warm, sincere, faintly menacing corporate register, no winking at the reader, no jokes that break character. He should cite the theorem as the reason the program can't possibly harm anyone, and the humor has to come out of the actual structure of the result rather than out of made-up math: ex ante versus ex post, the fixed-menu-of-actions assumption, the belief-independent-utility assumption, the difference between an agent who can commit to ignoring the disclosure and one who can't. Let the fine print carry some of it -- a clause about the exact moment the disclosure arrives relative to your enrollment window, a clause about premiums that now depend on what you believe, a line dismissing members who opt out as irrationally information-averse. The target is the argument and the sales pitch, not any real company or person. And if you can land one genuinely sad beat -- some member who'd have been better off not knowing and has no way to say so without sounding like she's disputing a theorem -- I'd love that.
