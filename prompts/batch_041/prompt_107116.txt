I have never studied philosophy in any formal setting, but I have spent a great deal of time discussing it with two friends who have, and I would like your help settling an argument they went back and forth on recently. The question was whether a person can reasonably retain full confidence in a conclusion after learning that someone who appears equally careful and equally well-informed reached the opposite one.

A began: "If I discover that someone with the same evidence and comparable intelligence disagrees with me, I have to lower my confidence. I do not get to be the one person who happened to get it right; the fact that a competent person landed elsewhere is itself evidence that the question is harder than I thought, or that I have slipped somewhere."

B replied: "That cannot be right. The bare fact that you disagree with me tells me nothing about the subject; it tells me something about you. If I have gone over my reasoning and I am confident in it, your arriving at a different answer is simply you being wrong. And notice what your rule does to itself: plenty of equally careful people reject the rule that I should split the difference whenever someone disagrees. By your own principle you should lower your confidence in that principle. So either the principle eats itself, or you quietly exempt it because it is yours."

A: "I do not exempt it. I do lower my confidence in the split-the-difference rule, a little, for exactly that reason — and then I go on using it, because lowering my confidence is not the same as giving it up. A rule can survive being applied to itself."

B: "Then it is a rule that can never be firmly held, which is absurd. And there is a second problem. You keep speaking as though a disagreement were evidence about the world. But my evidence that my faculties are working is precisely what is in dispute. Compare the case in which I know I have taken something that makes people overconfident: there I have a reason, independent of the question at hand, to distrust my own confident verdict, and so I should distrust it. A person who merely disagrees with me gives me no such independent reason. It is circular to treat his disagreement as evidence that I have erred, since whether I have erred is the very thing in question."

A: "But you have just conceded the drug case. So you agree that evidence about my own condition, rather than about the subject, can properly lower my confidence in a conclusion I would otherwise hold. Once you grant that, the only remaining question is whether another person's disagreement is a source of such evidence. And it plainly is: another mind working on the same problem is a fallible instrument, just as my own mind is, and its output is data about how likely a careful reasoner is to get this sort of question right."

B: "No — in the drug case I have independent reason to think my judgment is impaired on this occasion. In the disagreement case I have no independent reason at all; I am simply being asked to treat someone's contrary conclusion as a strike against my own, when the only thing that could justify that treatment is a verdict on the very question we disagree about. That is not evidence; it is a mirror."

That is where they left it, each convinced the other had begged the question. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move — A's opening, B's self-undermining objection, A's reply that a rule can be applied to itself and survive, B's drug-case comparison and circularity charge, A's attempt to turn the drug case against B, and B's final distinction — saying for each move whether it is sound and, if not, exactly where it goes wrong. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing, and which of them has the better of the dispute overall.

One request about how to answer: I have no way to check the current state of opinion on any of this, so please separate clearly what is settled among people who have thought about it carefully from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. If the exchange turns on a distinction that is itself disputed, say so rather than assuming one side's framing.
