So I'm in this reading group on epistemology and we keep rehashing the peer-disagreement stuff and honestly I'm bored of it. What I actually want is an essay on the deeper thing underneath: can it ever be rational to believe something while also believing that you're not justified in believing it? Epistemic akrasia, basically — the belief that p plus the belief that your belief in p is irrational.

The reason the usual examples feel cheap to me is that they all have the higher-order evidence sitting *outside* the first-order question. You took a pill, the pill made you worse at logic, end of story. I want you to take seriously a case where the two are tangled together — where the very fact that you're unreliable in this domain is itself a reason for thinking the conclusion is false, not just that you got it wrong. I've been calling this "entangled" higher-order evidence in my notes, no idea if that's a real term anyone uses. Does the akrasia even survive in that case, or does the higher-order stuff just collapse back into first-order evidence and the whole puzzle evaporates?

So: write me an essay that takes a side. Weigh the arguments for the view that akrasia can be rational against the arguments that it can't, and then actually land somewhere — including on whether the entangled case is genuinely different from the clean one. Don't do the thing where you say it depends on your theory of justification and then stop.

And please be honest about the state of play: tell me which parts of this are actually settled among people who work on it and which are live disputes where smart people just disagree, and don't sell me a definately-controversial position as if it's the consensus.
