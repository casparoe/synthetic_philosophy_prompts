I am writing a short argument to the effect that the most common defensive move against the problem of consequentialist cluelessness fails precisely in the cases that matter. What I need from you is a designed case that isolates this failure.

The problem, as I understand it, is that for most ordinary actions the full set of downstream consequences is not merely unknown but unassignable: there is no defensible probability distribution over the long-run effects, so the expected-value calculation a consequentialist would need cannot be completed. The defensive move is to note that if the unknown effects are symmetric — mean-zero noise around the knowable effects — then the knowable effects dominate in expectation, and the agent may decide as if the tail did not exist. My claim is that this move is unavailable when the cluelessness is biased: when an action's unassignable downstream effects are not noise around zero but are systematically skewed in one direction, so that the direction, though not the magnitude, is something the agent can see. I want a case of exactly that kind.

Specifications:

- The scenario must be realistic and one a thoughtful person could actually face — a workplace decision, a civic judgment, a professional call — not a trolley and not a science-fiction setup.
- There must be two live options, A and B. On the knowable, near-term consequences, A is clearly better and B clearly worse.
- A's unassignable downstream consequences must be systematically bad and B's systematically good, in the biased sense just described: the direction is visible, the magnitude is not, and no probability distribution over the tail is available. The bias must be produced by a plausible mechanism a reader will accept — for instance, A involves setting a precedent, normalizing a practice, or retraining a norm-following system, so that whatever else happens the practice propagates — rather than being asserted by stipulation.
- The scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph.

Then do the following work:

1. State the verdict of a narrow consequentialist, who evaluates only the consequences the agent can assign probabilities to, and the verdict of a wide consequentialist, who insists on including the unassignable tail; give the reasoning for each.
2. Say whether the two verdicts differ, and if so in which direction, and whether the wide consequentialist's verdict is determinate or collapses into "cannot say."
3. Bring in a rule consequentialist, a deontologist, and a virtue ethicist, and state their verdicts, showing whether their reasoning even engages the tail. The point is to see whether the case makes the act-consequentialist's verdict track the tail while the others' do not — and, if the rule consequentialist escapes, whether that is because of the rule or merely because rules inherit a smaller tail.
4. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens if it is removed — in particular, whether making the cluelessness symmetric collapses the case back into the standard "ignore the tail" verdict.
5. Give the strongest reply available to whichever theory comes out badly, and say whether it succeeds. For example, a defender may argue that a direction the agent can see is by definition not clueless, so the case trades on a confusion; or that the bias was smuggled in by an implicit constraint.

Do not stipulate the verdicts; they must fall out of the theories. And do not let the bias arise from the agent's own bad character or from an obviously vicious action — the action whose tail is skewed must be one a reasonable person could defend on the knowable consequences alone.
