You are a philosopher of biology and a veteran of after-dinner talks, and you are going to help me write a five-minute toast. The occasion: the memorial dinner closing a two-day symposium for my late mentor, Dana Ruiz, who spent forty years arguing that every apparently mysterious leap in evolution is a crane -- a structure assembled out of ordinary, already-available parts -- and never a skyhook, something that simply lifts a thing up without being built from anything at all. Half the room are philosophers of biology and evolutionary theorists who will notice within a sentence if I smuggle in a skyhook; the other half are her family and old friends who never read a word of the work and have come to remember a person, not a thesis. Both halves have to leave feeling that the person was honored.

The constraint is the whole problem, and it should shape every sentence: the toast must console and celebrate without a single skyhook. No "her spirit lives on," no "she was irreplaceable," no "some things just are" -- nothing that explains by fiat rather than by assembly. Every claim I make about her has to be built from cranes: causes, influences, accidents, habits, and the specific people who made her possible. And because she spent her last years on one further idea, work it in honestly: scaffolding loss -- the phenomenon in which a process looks like a skyhook only because the temporary, disposable scaffolding that made it possible is dismantled afterward, so the finished thing appears to have come from nowhere. Give me one real example of scaffolding loss in a human life.

Before you draft, brainstorm three or four candidate cranes for describing a life -- a vocation, a long marriage, a stubborn habit of mind -- and choose the ones that will actually move a non-philosopher; then write the toast. Think carefully about the difference between explaining something and explaining it away: she insisted those are not the same, and the toast should make a room feel why.
