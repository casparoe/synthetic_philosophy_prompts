I have been working on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism—the objection, often associated with Rawls, that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons. I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to show that the objection is untenable. I would like your help diagnosing where it goes wrong, if it does.

The argument:

(1) The separateness of persons objection holds that utilitarianism is defective because it aggregates benefits and burdens across distinct persons, treating the gains of some as compensating for the losses of others.
(2) Consider a case in which an agent can save either one person from a painful death or five other people from equally painful deaths, and cannot save all six. Any moral theory that says the agent ought to save the five is aggregating across persons, since it weighs the deaths of five against the death of one and reaches a verdict on the basis of the total.
(3) Therefore, if the separateness of persons objection is correct, we cannot say that the agent ought to save the five rather than the one.
(4) But it is plainly true that the agent ought to save the five.
(5) Therefore, the separateness of persons objection is false.

I am inclined to keep (4) and to give up (1), but I am not sure that is the right move. I would like to know which premise or step you would reject, why it looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I am interested in whether the objection can be stated in a way that does not have the consequence in (3), and if so, what work the phrase "separateness of persons" is doing in the revised statement.
