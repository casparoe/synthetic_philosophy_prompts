I am a doctoral candidate working on the moral and legal standing of mass atrocity, and I have been defending a position that I would like you to stress-test rather than endorse. The position is this: genocide is a morally distinct category, not merely an especially severe species of mass killing or of crimes against humanity. Its distinctiveness lies in the fact that it is directed at a group as a collective subject rather than at individuals taken severally -- the perpetrator aims at the destruction of a shared identity that partly constitutes each member's social world -- and this is why the wrong of genocide is not exhausted by the sum of the wrongs done to its individual victims. I also hold a second, more contentious claim: that this moral distinctiveness is independent of the well-known arbitrariness of the legal definition. The 1948 Genocide Convention protects only national, ethnic, racial and religious groups, requires an intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part precisely as a group, and leaves out the cultural and economic destruction that Lemkin's original conception of the term included; I regard those features as defects of the legal instrument rather than evidence that the underlying moral category is confused.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against it? I am interested in defeaters of every kind -- conceptual objections, cases the view cannot describe without distortion, consequences it is committed to that turn out unacceptable, results showing that it collapses into a rival. Rank them from what should be decisive to what is merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a committed defender would themselves concede rather than dismiss as a critic's caricature. It would also help to say what the position explains or predicts that its rivals do not. Two things I would particularly like you to press on. First, does the group-as-such wrong do any independent moral work, or is it a reification that the wrongs to individuals already fully capture? Second, has the position been insulated against disconfirmation -- in particular, if defenders retreat to a moral category that floats free of the legal one whenever the law proves under-inclusive or arbitrary, is there any atrocity the view could not accommodate, and if not, is that a fatal concession or an acceptable feature of a moral as opposed to an empirical thesis? I am also uneasy about the politics of naming, and I want you to tell me whether the fact that the label tracks the interests of those who apply it tells against the category itself or only against its use. I would find the answer most persuasive if you could identify a case in which the position's commitments force it to say something its defenders would find unwelcome.
