I'm about 80 pages into a historical novel set in 17th-century Italy and one of my characters is an astronomer who keeps paraphrasing Galileo at dinner parties, so I've been reading up on him. My main source is one of those big popular histories of science (the author escapes me) and here's how it sums up Galileo's whole thing about scripture, which I've probly repeated a dozen times by now:

"Galileo's view was that where the Bible and science disagree, the Bible is simply wrong, because it was written by ancient people who knew nothing about astronomy. He said the point of scripture is to teach us how to get to heaven, not how the heavens work, and that it talks in loose everyday language rather than technical truth — he called this accommodation, basically a diplomatic fudge. From all this he drew the conclusion that scientists, not theologians, have the authority to say what scripture means, which is why the Church had to shut him up. The Church's answer was that scripture's plain sense has to stand no matter what science comes up with, so Galileo's real crime was putting faith under the thumb of physics."

I suspect the last bit is unfair to the Church and the whole thing sounds too tidy, but I honestly can't tell where the seams are. Could you go through it clause by clause — what a Galileo scholar would nod at, what's a popularization that's half-right, and what did he explicitly deny? And does the closing claim, that the Church came down on him because of his teaching about interpretation, actually follow, or was the fight narrower than that? I don't need a bibliography or a reading list, I just don't want my astronomer saying things the real guy would have winced at.
