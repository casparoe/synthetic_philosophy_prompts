I spent thirty-five years as a bench and field scientist before retiring, and philosophy has become my second education. The problem that pulled me in was testimony: I had trusted colleagues, technicians, and instruments for decades, and I wanted to know what, if anything, made that trust rational. I have now read enough of the reductionism and anti-reductionism debate to see that the two sides keep talking past one another, and I would rather have the document they should have written together than read another exchange of that kind.

Please draft that document: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist about testimony. Take the positions as follows, and say at the outset if stating them this way already loads the dice. The reductionist holds that a hearer's belief formed from what she is told is justified only if she has non-testimonial grounds for it — a track record, an independent check, an inference to the speaker's reliability or to the reliability of testimony in general. The anti-reductionist holds that testimony is a basic source of justification, so that a hearer with no positive reason for suspicion is entitled to accept what she is told, with no requirement of supporting non-testimonial evidence.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. Include claims about what the dispute is not about, and about what each side concedes to the other's best case.
2. A disagreements table. Each row: the sub-question, the reductionist's formulation, the anti-reductionist's formulation — each phrased in words its own side would accept as an accurate statement of its position, and neither so hedged that it becomes vacuous.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect the first is whether the default is a positive reason or merely the absence of a defeater, and the second whether the reductionist's demand is a condition on the order of justification or only on the order of learning; tell me whether I have identified them correctly.
4. For each crux, the case, argument, or evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction stated in advance. Where a crux turns on how hearers actually reason, say what sort of empirical result would bear on it.
5. A closing section on whether any part of the disagreement is merely verbal — in particular, whether justification in the reductionist's mouth and entitlement in the anti-reductionist's are answering the same question.

Please treat the following as stipulated material for section 4, not as claims to be disputed. (i) A colleague reports a measurement you cannot repeat, taken with an instrument you have never used; you have no independent check and no particular reason for doubt. (ii) A technician relays a reading from an unattended sensor, and the chain from sensor to bulletin passes through several hands, none of them known to you. (iii) A child acquires her first beliefs about what words mean and what is dangerous, before she has any track record of anyone's reliability. (iv) A community whose members are, in a given domain, uniformly and unknowingly unreliable. I would also like you to test a distinction I have been trying out — between a hearer's having positive credentials for a source and her merely lacking disqualifying grounds against it — and to say whether that distinction, or the local/global distinction, is what actually separates the camps, or is a red herring.

I do not need citations, names, or a reading list; I want the formulations themselves to do the work.
