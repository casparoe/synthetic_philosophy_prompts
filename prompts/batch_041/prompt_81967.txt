So two people in my reading group have been going at each other for like three weeks about IIT — integrated information theory, the Tononi thing, consciousness = phi, the amount of integrated information a system has. I run the group so I sort of have to referee and I can't. I've read way too many rationalist blogs in my life and I can usually spot when two people are talking past each other, but this one I genuinely can't call. Can you go through it move by move?

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Raj, the philosophy guy: IIT is the only game in town that says what consciousness actually *is* rather than just correlating it with brain stuff. Phi isn't a hypothesis, it's a definition — it tells you which systems are conscious, full stop. Nobody can compute phi for a human brain; that's an engineering problem, not a conceptual one.

(2) Mel, who does ML: You can take any system IIT says is conscious and "unfold" its causal structure into a plain feedforward network with identical input-output behavior over time, and IIT gives the unfolded one a phi of zero. So no experiment that only looks at what a system *does* can ever tell the two apart. And that's not a quirk of IIT, it hits any theory that says consciousness depends on causal structure instead of behavior. So either there's some non-behavioral way to test it, or it isn't testable.

(3) Raj: Fine, so test it non-behaviorally. We do — perturbational complexity, zap the cortex and measure the echo. Low in dreamless sleep and anesthesia, high in waking. That's a substrate-level measurement, not a behavioral one.

(4) Mel: That's circular. PCI is calibrated against what we already assume is conscious. If IIT is what's telling you the awake brain has high phi, then using PCI to confirm IIT is using the theory to check the theory.

(5) Raj: IIT also made a call nobody else made: the cerebellum has several times more neurons than the cortex but almost none of the looping connectivity that generates phi, so it should contribute basically nothing to consciousness — and people born missing most of a cerebellum are largely fine. That's a hit. Then there's the grid thing: IIT implies a simple 2D lattice of logic gates can have more phi than you do, and that scaling the lattice up just adds more consciousness. Everybody hates that result. But it's a prediction, and a theory that sticks its neck out that far isn't pseudoscience.

(6) Mel: Sticking your neck out with a prediction nobody can check isn't a virtue. And the grid thing is a reductio — a camera sensor with the right wiring becomes a somebody. Also, a hundred-odd neuroscientists signed that open letter a couple years back calling IIT pseudoscience, and their complaint wasn't about the math, it was about the claims people were making off the back of it.

(7) Raj: That letter was mostly about coma-patient stuff and people annoyed at the adversarial collaboration. And the core charge was "this can't be falsified," which is just the unfolding argument again with worse manners. String theory gets called a research program, not pseudoscience.

(8) Mel: String theory at least is testable in principle. If your theory says two systems are behaviorally identical and differ in consciousness, and there's no possible observation that could ever separate them, you're not doing empirical science, you're doing metaphysics with equations.

Where I get stuck is that Raj's answer in (3) and Mel's in (4) both seem fine to me and they can't both be. Same with (5) through (7).

Then say who's more right overall on the big question of whether IIT is unfalsifiable, and what both of them are missing.
