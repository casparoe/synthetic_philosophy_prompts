I'm in a third-year seminar on cooperation and conflict — we did Hobbes and then a lot of fairly standard game theory (prisoners dilemmas, cheap talk versus costly signals, the security dilemma where two states spiral into an arms race even though neither of them wants a fight) — and my response paper for the end of term is on the dark forest hypothesis as it's presented in Liu Cixin's trilogy. I've read all three books twice and I keep flip flopping on whether they actually believe it. As I understand the thing, it runs on three axioms (survival first, civilizations expand, total matter is fixed) plus two further worries, the chain of suspicion and the possibility of a technological explosion, and out of those you get the conclusion that any civilization that announces itself is finished. What bothers me is that those two further worries aren't the same worry. The chain of suspicion is about intentions — we can't know whether they mean us harm, and that's the version everyone repeats. The technological explosion is about capabilities: they might be perfectly harmless now and able to wipe us out in five hundred years, which means the argument doesn't need them to be hostile at all and would licence a strike even against someone we were certain was decent. So is the hypothesis a claim about not knowing other minds, or a claim about not knowing the future, and which of the two does Liu actually dramatize? I'd like the reading to keep them apart instead of letting them blur into each other, because the second one seems much stronger and much stranger and I don't think people notice that.

Second thing that's nagging at me: the deterrence era reads like a counterexample sitting inside the story's own logic. Luo Ji gets a credible threat across four light years with no prior trust whatsoever and it holds for decades, and the swordholder arrangement works right up until Cheng Xin refuses to pull the trigger — which is a failure of nerve, not of communication. If a signal can work even once, then the impossibility of communication isn't doing the work the hypothesis needs it to do, and the load-bearing assumptions might be somewhere else entirely: that listening is cheap and speaking is fatal, and that a first strike is always cheap and decisive. Nobody in the books ever argues about that, it's just granted. And then Liu hands humanity a fix that looks exactly like a costly signal of harmlessness — the black domain, wrapping yourself in lowered light speed so that you can never leave — which suggests the dark forest is only an equilibrium as long as there's no way of proving you aren't a threat. I can't tell if that's a hole in the hypothesis or a piece of it, and I'd like you to take a position.

Third thing, and this is the one I most want pushed on: near the end of the third book we finally see a strike from the other side, from the hunter who fires it, and he thinks about the whole business roughly the way you'd think about clearing a blocked drain. Everyone talks about the dark forest as a tragedy, a terrible thing forced on basically decent beings. The novel doesn't show it as that at all — it shows routine, a job, a bit of maintenance. Does that shift what the thesis even is? Is the claim about what a rational agent must do, or about what an institution will do once the moral distance is big enough that the other party stops counting as a someone? And here's the other half of it: the trilogy is itself a broadcast. It put this idea into millions of heads and made the phrase the default thing people say about aliens. If the hypothesis is true, writing it was about the most irresponsible act imaginable, and if enough people believe it then cooperation is dead and it becomes true whether or not it ever started out true. I don't want that treated as a clever gotcha, I want it thought through — is the novel a performative contradiction, or a demonstration that the whole thing is self-fulfilling? What I need in the end is an essay-ish reading of the trilogy in terms of the hypothesis as a philosophical thesis about what rational agents are permitted or required to do when they can't know the intentions or the future capacities of the other side. Use the scenes — Ye Wenjie's reply, the wallfacers, the ships that eat each other out in the dark, the standoff, the black domain, the strike from the hunter's chair — but read them rather than listing them. Say where the books back the thesis, where they quietly refute it, and where the philosophical frame does violence to the novels; then say what the novels get at that the argument alone can't, because that's the part I actually need. Its meant to be about 1200 words, argument rather than survey, and don't bother with page references or citations, I've got all three books on my desk.
