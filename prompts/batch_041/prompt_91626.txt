I am a third-year PhD student in early modern philosophy, and in three weeks I am presenting a chapter of my dissertation at a departmental seminar. The chapter argues that Spinoza's defence of democracy is best understood not as a defence of majority rule or popular participation but as a structural claim about the conditions under which a commonwealth can secure the freedom to philosophise and the exercise of reason. On my reading, the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus treats democracy as the most natural form of state because it comes closest to preserving the natural right that each person retains under the social contract, while the unfinished Tractatus Politicus reveals a tension: Spinoza's account of aristocracy is more developed than his account of democracy, and his exclusion of women from political power sits uneasily with his broader democratic principles. I also want to connect this to his metaphysics: the conatus doctrine and the identity of right and power make his politics naturalistic, but I think this naturalism does not collapse into a crude realism, because the free man's reason provides a normative standard internal to his account.

The audience will be mixed. There is a Spinoza specialist who knows the TTP and TP inside out and will press on the consistency between the two treatises, especially on the role of the multitude and the limits of absolute sovereignty. There is a Hobbes scholar who will ask whether Spinoza's democracy is just Hobbesian sovereignty with a different front, and whether the natural right retained by subjects is anything more than a residual liberty. There is a Dutch historian who will want to know how much of Spinoza's position is a response to the rampjaar and the fall of De Witt, and whether I am reading later republican anxieties back into the text. There is a political theorist from the republican tradition who will object that my account is too thin on civic virtue and participation, and that a democracy without those is not a democracy at all. There are also two analytic philosophers who will ask about the normative status of Spinoza's claims: is he describing what is, or prescribing what ought to be, and if the latter, what is the source of the normativity? I expect these different audiences to talk past one another, and I need to be ready for each in their own vocabulary.

Please act as an expert in early modern political philosophy, with deep knowledge of Spinoza's political treatises and their reception, and prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me. Begin by brainstorming the likely objections each of these audience members will raise, then order the eight or so most damaging and most likely objections by how much damage they would do to my thesis, not by how easy they are to answer. Phrase each objection the way that particular audience member would phrase it, using their vocabulary and concerns. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft or where I am most vulnerable. I also want you to identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is so dangerous and what I might say if it comes up anyway. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I need to be able to answer from what I know in the room. Think carefully about the differences between the TTP and the TP, and about how Spinoza's metaphysics constrains his politics, but keep the prep sheet practical and usable under pressure.
