A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about slurs and got nowhere, even though I could not produce a single case on which our judgments differed. Her view is that the offensiveness belongs to the word: it travels with the expression itself, which is why a slur in quotation marks, or in the mouth of a member of the group it targets, still leaves a residue, and why the neutral paraphrase does not. Mine is that the offensiveness is something speakers do with the word rather than something the word does by itself: some slurs are affectionate between insiders, some are offensive even from insiders, a slur can be used with no contempt at all while contempt can be conveyed with no slur at all, and what the word contributes to what is said, and to what is true, looks just like what its neutral counterpart contributes. If we agree about all of that, what kind of disagreement is left? Is one of us offering a theory of the word and the other a theory of the act of using it, under the single heading 'meaning'? Are we disagreeing about what a semantic theory is for -- truth conditions versus standing constraints on use -- so that the two accounts never actually collide? Is there a hidden empirical question, about whether hearers process the derogation automatically or derive it, that one of us ought to be running experiments on? Or do we simply count different data -- truth-value judgments versus judgments of offensiveness and permissibility -- so that each of us is answering evidence the other does not accept? My own suspicion is that the long stall in the literature is not about the words at all but about what a theory of meaning is supposed to explain. Diagnose the disagreement as precisely as you can, and tell me what would count as progress, including the possibility that none should be expected.
