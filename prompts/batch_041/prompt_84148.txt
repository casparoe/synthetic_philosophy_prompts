The exam question was:

Problem 3 (20 points). A randomized, double-blind trial tested a new drug for a chronic condition. 180 participants were assigned to drug (n = 120) or placebo (n = 60); the primary outcome was clinical improvement at 12 weeks.

Overall:
- Drug: 59/120 improved (49.2%)
- Placebo: 38/60 improved (63.3%)

Because the drug's known side effects could themselves affect how patients feel, the team also recorded, at week 4 — after treatment had begun — whether each participant had reported a treatment-related adverse event, and split the results:
- Adverse event reported: Drug 24/80 (30.0%); Placebo 5/20 (25.0%)
- No adverse event reported: Drug 35/40 (87.5%); Placebo 33/40 (82.5%)

(a) Explain what is going on in these numbers.
(b) The trial statistician proposes reporting the two stratum-specific comparisons as the trial's main result, on the grounds that they "control for" adverse-event status. A colleague argues that the overall comparison is the one to report, because that was the pre-registered primary analysis. Whose position is better supported, and why?

The student wrote:

"These numbers are a textbook instance of Simpson's paradox, and the first thing to note is that nothing has gone wrong arithmetically. The pooled comparison and the stratum-specific comparisons are both computed correctly from the same 180 participants; the reversal is a real feature of the data, not a rounding error or a coding mistake.

The mechanism is straightforward. Adverse events are strongly associated with the outcome — patients who reported an adverse event improved far less often than patients who did not, in both arms. And the drug arm contains a much larger share of adverse-event patients (80 of 120, about 67%) than the placebo arm (20 of 60, about 33%). So the overall comparison is heavily weighted by a group of patients who were destined to do poorly, and the drug's benefit is masked. This is exactly what Simpson's paradox is: an association that reverses when a third variable is taken into account.

Part (b). The statistician is right, and the colleague's reasoning is weak. Adverse-event status is a classic confounder: it is associated with the exposure and independently associated with the outcome, and it distorts the crude comparison. Stratifying on it removes that distortion, and once we do, the drug is better in both strata — by five percentage points in each. The data therefore show that the drug's effect depends on whether a patient has an adverse reaction: it helps a great deal in patients who tolerate it and only modestly in those who don't.

The colleague's appeal to pre-registration is beside the point. Pre-registration is a safeguard against fishing for results, not a criterion of causal validity; the pre-specified analysis can still be the confounded one. The deeper lesson is that the data alone cannot tell us which comparison is correct — the choice depends on assumptions we bring, not on the numbers — so the decision between the two comparisons is ultimately a matter of which estimate we find more interpretable, and the stratified one is clearly more informative. The trial should therefore report the drug as effective."
