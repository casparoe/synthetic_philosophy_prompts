Writing a piece on the "cluelessness" problem that the longtermism people keep going on about — the worry that for basically any action, the long-run consequences are so chaotic that you can't know whether it did more good than harm. I've come out against it and I want you to try and change my mind. My case: consequentialism is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure, so at most cluelessness shows we don't know which act is right, not that there's no fact of the matter — and "we can't tell" isn't an objection to a theory of what's true. And on the deciding side we're not clueless in any deep sense anyway: assign probabilities, maximize expected value, same as any other messy practical choice. If our probabilities are bad, that's a fact about us, not about the theory. I'd also split the thing three ways — can't tell whether it's net positive at all, can't tell which of two good options is better, can't tell by how much — and say only the first is even a candidate threat, and only in cases where the expectation is undefined rather than merely unknown (the heavy-tail stuff), which is rarer than people claim. So: what would count against all this? I want the real defeaters, ranked by how much they should move me, including the ones that the people who make these moves themselves would concede. And be straight with me — have I built something that no evidence could touch, and if so is that a problem, or is that just what a criterion of rightness is supposed to be?
