I've been trying to pin down the antinatalism debate by running a historical counterfactual that is more than a personality quiz. Schopenhauer already argued that this is the worst of all possible worlds, that satisfaction is negative because it is only the removal of a pain while pain is positive, and that suicide, far from denying the will, affirms it more strongly than ordinary living does. Benatar's asymmetry holds that the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when no one enjoys that good, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived by it. Give me a reasoned prediction of Schopenhauer's verdict rather than a summary of either position: where would he endorse the asymmetry, where would he refuse it, and what would he say the argument actually establishes? I especially want his reaction to the second and fourth rows. If pleasure is only the removal of pain, does that make the harm of coming into existence worse than Benatar says, or does it disqualify the presence of pleasure from counting as a straightforward good and thereby leave the asymmetry without one of its terms? And would he accept comparing a life to a never-existing person at all, or would he object that the absence of pain is being treated as a good state when there is no subject, no will, and nothing to be in that state? Close with whether he would sign on to antinatalism as a conclusion, or only to something weaker like the wisdom of not adding new sufferers, and note any point at which I am importing a modern welfare framework he would reject.
