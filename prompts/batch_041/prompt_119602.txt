I'm on the research advisory subcommittee for my kid's school district (about 9,000 students), and I need to hand the board a short written opinion before Tuesday's vote. I've been reading the SSC/LessWrong corner of the internet for fifteen years, so I'm the one who volunteered to draft this, which was definately a mistake.

The case: the district's accelerated math track admits by a single test. A group at the state university ran a twin analysis over district records and came back with the test score being roughly 65% heritable in this sample. One parent group wants that number in the public FAQ, on the grounds that it shows the admissions gap isn't something the district is doing wrong. The equity office wants us to never mention heritability at all, on the grounds that any such talk is a motte-and-bailey for nothing can be done here. The superintendent just wants the yelling to stop and asked our subcommitee to recommend what the district may and may not say.

So: draft the opinion I'll circulate. I need a concrete recommendation (cite it, don't cite it, or cite it under conditions I can list), the reasoning behind it, an honest steelman of whichever side loses, and one paragraph short enough to read out loud at the meeting. No citations or reading list, I'll add those myself.

Things I want you to actually dig into instead of giving me the standard heritability-isn't-destiny paragraph:

- The 65% is a ratio of variances in one population at one time, and the population is our district in the environment we currently run, which includes the magnet program itself and the tutoring expansion we rolled out last year. So part of what that number reports is our own policy. Also: it says nothing at all about any single kid, and I want that said plainly.
- Our analyst pointed out that if the district actually equalized the measured inputs, the estimate would probably go up, and she's worried people will read the higher number as genes mattering more now. I think that's the sharpest thing anyone has said in these meetings and it should be in the memo.
- The twin design leans on the assumption that identical and fraternal twins get equally similar environments, and, this is the part I keep failing to explain to people, the environmental measures in these studies (parenting, peers, stressful life events) are themselves partly heritable, since kids help pick and shape their environments and share genes with the parents providing them. Which makes the clean genes-vs-environment split in our FAQ worse than wrong, it's incoherent.
- One committee member wants us to report, instead of the 65%, the heritability of the change in scores across the tutoring rollout, or the heritability of the program's effect. Tell me whether that's a coherent quantity at all, and if it isn't, what we should be reporting instead.
- The decision the board actually faces is what we do next year about admissions and tutoring. So I want the memo to say what the heritability figure would have to be informative about in order to bear on that decision, and then say whether it is.
- For calibration: average height in rich countries went up by a lot over the last century while staying just about as heritable as ever. Use that however you want, or don't.

The equity office's position should get its strongest form, not a strawman, I have to sit next to these people. And give me the two-sentence version of the bottom line I can use when someone accuses us of hiding science, because someone will.
