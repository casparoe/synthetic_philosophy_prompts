Ok, I'm writting the final for my upper-level seminar on the units of selection — mostly seniors, a couple of MA students, half of them from the bio side — and I need one essay question that actually discriminates. I'm so tired of grading gene's-eye-view vs. Dawkins-and-Gould karaoke: replicators and vehicles on one side, "for the good of the species" on the other, everyone reciting the same arguments they half-remember. I want a question where the labels get you nowhere.

Treat yourself as a philosopher of biology who's taught this seminar a dozen times and refereed in the area. Before you write anything, brainstorm the candidate cruxes — the three or four places where a student's understanding actually shows — and tell me in a couple of lines which one you picked and why. Then write the thing.

The question has to be built on a case, and the case is mine, not from the literature, which is the point. Rough version: a made-up colonial insect, the marsh skimmer. Nests of ~30 adults share a chamber. One allele, call it S, makes its bearer secrete an antifungal compound into the chamber wall. Secreting is metabolically expensive — bearers lay about 8% fewer eggs — but chambers with lots of secretors suffer far fewer mould wipeouts over winter, so more adults survive to spring. The non-secretor allele s pays nothing and still gets the protected chamber. Cheaters do well inside a nest; secretor-heavy nests do well against the mould.

Version A: nests are ephemeral. Everyone disperses each spring and next year's nests are random re-samples, so nest composition isn't heritable at all.

Version B: nests persist and split. A colony buds off a daughter chamber that inherits the mother's composition more or less intact, so secretor frequency is a stable, heritable property of a lineage of nests.

Three things I keep telling them to keep seperate, and they never do: (1) what's the bearer of the adaptation — the product question; (2) what entity's differential reproduction is doing the causal work — the process question; (3) what's actually being copied down the generations — the inheritance question. And then the audit: for any claimed unit of selection, name the entity whose differential reproduction does the work, and say what evidence would show that the lower-level accounting screens off the higher-level one, or that it doesn't. Nine times out of ten they answer one of those three questions when they've been asked another, and that's exactly the failure I want the exam to catch.

I also want them to have to commit to something. Is "group selection" in version A a fact about the world or a choice about how to bookkeep? Do A and B get the same verdict or different ones? What finding would flip their answer? No fence-sitting.

Deliverables: (1) the essay question itself — 90 minutes, closed book, so it has to be answerable from the case plus what they already know, no literature recall required; (2) a model answer of about 650 words I can hand the TA, with an actual verdict, not a survey of positions; (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks out of 25, and for each one a line on how candidates typically blow it. One of those points should be about what a formal variance partition can and can't settle — the point being that you can always run the partition at either level, so the arithmetic never decides the causal question by itself. And give me one explicit A-vs-B separator: the single move that has to be there for an A but that a strong B answer can live without. Don't ask them for citations, they'll invent them.
