I'm a science journalist who writes long features about ideas, and I've been commissioned to write roughly five thousand words explaining predictive processing and the free energy principle to readers of a general-interest magazine: intelligent, curious, comfortable with abstraction, but with no mathematics and no neuroscience beyond school biology. I've read widely enough to be dangerous and to keep losing the thread. So I don't want the explanation itself — I want you to plan the explanatory arc of the piece.

Start with the dependency structure. The pieces I could include are: prediction error minimisation; the notion of a generative model; precision, attention, and the weighting of prediction against input; active inference, on which action rather than belief is what does the error-reducing work; the free energy bound itself, and why it is an upper bound on surprise rather than a measure of it; Markov blankets, and the claim that they mark out anything that manages to persist; and the extension of the whole apparatus from brains to life in general. Which order must these come in so that each step feels like the answer to a question the reader already has? Which can I cut without loss, and which look cuttable but are load-bearing? For the two or three hardest steps, tell me what a reader will conclude if I introduce them before their prerequisites are in place.

Then the carrying example. My editor's instinct is to open with the claim that the brain is a prediction machine, and my own was to build the piece around binocular rivalry; I suspect both are mistakes, the first because it's the sentence every popular treatment already opens with and the second because it's a laboratory curiosity rather than something the reader has lived. Tell me what single example should carry the piece, what properties it needs — something the reader has experienced, that admits of a wrongness that gets corrected, that can be extended from perception to action and eventually to the organism as a whole — and why the obvious candidates fail.

Then the misconception map, ordered by when readers will actually form the misconceptions rather than by when critics raise them. My working list: that free energy here means the thermodynamic quantity; that the theory says the brain performs Bayesian inference in a literal computational sense; that it entails we are hallucinating and never in contact with reality; that because the theory says we minimise surprise it recommends the quietest available room; and the meta-objection that a framework which can accommodate any result explains none. Which of these are genuine hazards for a general reader and which are straw men that only critics attribute to the theory? Where in the sequence should each be headed off — and is there one I shouldn't raise at all, because raising it turns the piece into a piece about the critics rather than about the idea? I'd also like your judgement on whether to present the framework as an empirical claim about brains or as a formal claim about any system that manages to keep existing, since I suspect the piece collapses into confusion if I blur the two.

Finally: what should I openly refuse to explain at this level, and how do I say so without appearing to dodge the hard part? Give me one question a reader should be able to answer by the end — something that distinguishes having understood the framework from having learned its vocabulary — and one thing I should be able to say to a sceptical neuroscientist afterwards that I could not have said before.
