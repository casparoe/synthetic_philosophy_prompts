I'm an epidemiologist by training, and I think about causation almost entirely in the vocabulary of the sufficient-component-cause model and modern causal inference: causal pies and component causes, necessary versus sufficient causes, DAGs and the do-operator, potential outcomes, attributable fractions, mediation and path-specific effects, and the gap between the causes of a population's disease burden and the actual cause of a single case. Restate for me the debate among historians and philosophers of history about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Work through the standard puzzles — an event with two sufficient causes, preemption, the demand that a historian name the cause of a war or a revolution when many conditions were jointly required, the contrast between outcomes felt to be inevitable and outcomes felt to be contingent, and the suspicion that any why-question about the past is implicitly contrastive — and show how each looks once translated. Then give me the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For each piece of residue, say whether it marks something my framework cannot represent, or a place where the historians' formulations are doing work a causal analysis was never meant to do. Where the translation looks suspiciously complete, say so and tell me why that should make me uneasy rather than pleased, and flag any point at which you have quietly imported my framework's assumptions — well-defined interventions, stable units, a target population — and called it translation. Finally, tell me which of your residue verdicts is most likely to be wrong, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about it.
