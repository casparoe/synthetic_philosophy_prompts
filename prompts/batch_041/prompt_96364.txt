You are a philosopher of science working on explanation, reduction, and the unity of the sciences, and you have a reputation for turning conceptual distinctions into instruments that non-philosophers can actually run under time pressure. Work carefully and in stages. Before you write the deliverable, spend some private effort brainstorming: enumerate the different ways an explanation can look like it is doing work while leaving the phenomenon exactly where it was, and enumerate the different things people might mean when they call an explanation reductive. Also, before you begin the procedure itself, write out for yourself what would have to be true for a charge of either of the following to be decisive rather than merely rhetorical. Do not show me this preliminary work; use it.

The situation. I am a program officer at a foundation that funds interdisciplinary work on cognition and behaviour. I screen about ten pre-proposals a month and write a one-page recommendation on each, which goes to a review panel; I can send a proposal back to the applicants for revision, but I cannot reject one outright, and the panel decides. My panel chair has asked me to stop doing two things. First, waving through proposals that explain a phenomenon by naming a capacity. Second, being impressed by proposals that dissolve a phenomenon into something smaller and announce that the problem is solved. The panel has settled on a working vocabulary for the two failures, and has asked me to apply it mechanically enough that I can defend my recommendations to applicants who will be annoyed. I need you to build the machinery.

The vocabulary, which you should treat as the fixed input and may refine but not replace. It comes from Daniel Dennett's discussion of cranes, skyhooks, and greedy reductionism; I am not giving you any other literature, and nothing in your answer may depend on a source I cannot check.

A skyhook is an entity, capacity, or process introduced into an explanation to do the work, where the capacity to do that work is left unexplained. The proposal says, in effect, and then X does the rest, where X's ability to do the rest is the very thing that needed accounting for. Two points matter. Being unfamiliar, mental, holistic, emergent, or irreducibly relational does not make something a skyhook; neither does being posited rather than observed. The mark of a skyhook is that the explanatory load rests on a capacity that the theory itself never generates, so the mystery has been renamed rather than addressed.

A crane is a multi-step account in which whatever does the work at each step is available from the previous step, or independently licensed, or at least explicitly a target for further work. Cranes may be baroque and may posit new entities. What makes something a crane is that the steps compose: you can see how the output of one step is the input to the next, and no single step silently carries the whole load.

Greedy reductionism is explaining a high-level phenomenon by jumping directly to the lowest-level description available, so that the intermediate levels where the phenomenon actually lives are skipped. The diagnostic sign is not that the low-level facts are false. It is that they are true and insufficient: you cannot get from them to the phenomenon without the missing levels, and the explanation either quietly substitutes a different and easier phenomenon or denies that the original phenomenon exists.

Three things the vocabulary must not be allowed to do, and you should design around them. It must not be possible to dismiss a proposal merely for positing something whose ultimate origin is not derived from anything deeper; some posits are legitimately treated as fundamental, and the test is whether the theory licenses that stopping point, says what the posit is doing, and says what would count as its failing, not whether the posit has a pedigree. It must not be possible to win a case by pointing out that a phenomenon is multiply realizable or that the levels are not neatly nested. And the verdicts must be graded: a proposal is rarely a pure skyhook or pure crane, and the useful output is often that one step of an otherwise sound chain is doing all the work.

My constraints. I have thirty minutes per proposal, I have no expertise in most of the subfields I read, and I am not entitled to adjudicate whether a proposal is empirically correct. My job is only to assess the architecture of the explanation: what is doing the work, what is being left undone, and where the applicants have stopped. My panel chair will sign the one-pager with me and has no patience for a procedure whose steps are really just invitations to admire the theory. Applicants are often senior people and will contest every step, so each question in the procedure must have a stated standard for what counts as an adequate answer and a stated consequence if the answer is missing.

Deliver the following.

1. A decision procedure I can run in thirty minutes, as a numbered sequence of questions in a fixed order, each with: what I am looking at, what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as an evasion (with two or three concrete examples of evasive answers, since I will meet them), and what happens next. Include the stopping conditions: where the procedure terminates in a crane, where it terminates in a skyhook, where it terminates in greedy reductionism, and where it terminates by handing the decision to the panel as a matter that is not settled by facts about the explanation at all. Where a stopping condition requires a value judgement, mark it in the text and say what the judgement is.

2. A short battery of tests for the greedy case specifically, since it is the one I expect to misapply. Give me three or four probes that distinguish an explanation that is low-level and correct from one that is low-level and greedy. The central one should be a request for the composition step, that is, an explicit demand for the account that carries you from one level to the next. Include at least one probe that is designed to catch a legitimate multi-level reduction and clear it, and say how I can tell that the probe has been cleared rather than merely deflected.

3. Worked applications to all four of the proposals below, each walked through the procedure question by question with the verdict stated. At least one of the four should come out against the first impression a sympathetic reader would form, and I should be able to see from your walk-through exactly which question turned the case.

Proposal A. Human infants acquire their first language far too fast, and on far too thin and degenerate evidence, for general-purpose learning to be doing the work; therefore the brain contains a device whose internal structure already is the structure of human language, and the research programme is to specify those internal rules more exactly and locate their neural implementation. The proposal does not address how the device came to have that structure, nor how development assembles it; the device's having the structure is the starting point, and the applicants describe this as a necessary idealization.

Proposal B. Workers in a social insect colony treat nestmates differently from non-nestmates. The proposal traces a chain: individual variation in the waxy layer on the cuticle; learning of the colony odour during a sensitive period in the first days after emergence; comparison of incoming odours against a learned template; a graded aggressive response above a threshold; and, over generations, selection on the odour-producing chemistry and on the learning window, since colonies with mismatched recognition thresholds lose resources to intruders. Each link is said to be documented in other species and measurable here.

Proposal C. Music moves us because the passages that give listeners chills reliably produce dopamine release in the striatum, measured by displacement of a radioligand. The applicants conclude that the pleasure of music just is dopaminergic reward, and that the search for anything more is romanticism. The proposal does not address why these particular acoustic patterns, rather than any others, recruit the reward system, nor how musical structure at the level of phrases, expectations, and resolutions relates to the reward events, and treats such questions as outside its scope.

Proposal D. Institutions persist because participants share a bonding property that holds a collective together across defection and turnover; the property is defined operationally as whatever it is about a population that makes its institutions stable when they would otherwise unravel. The proposal's plan is to measure this property, compare it across cases, and show that it predicts institutional durability. The panel is enthusiastic, because it promises to unify work in three literatures at once.

4. A closing section, honestly written, on what this procedure cannot do. Specifically: the misfires I should expect, including the case where a good explanation is flagged because it is unfamiliar and the case where a bad one passes because it is told in many small steps; which of the questions are judgement calls dressed as tests, and what the judgement is that they conceal; what a panel member who rejects the whole crane and skyhook vocabulary would say in response to the procedure and whether that objection can be met or only noted; and what I should tell an applicant who asks what they would have to add to a skyhook-flavoured proposal to make it fundable, given that I am not entitled to tell them what to think.

Throughout, write for someone who has never read a word of philosophy of science and will be running this with a proposal in one hand and a clock running. Define any term you introduce in the sentence in which you introduce it. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to particular authors or works; assume I cannot look anything up and that the procedure has to be self-contained. Do not hedge with a long survey of the literature you are not citing. If you reach a point where the procedure cannot be made to work without a distinction you cannot define plainly, say so there and tell me what to do instead.
