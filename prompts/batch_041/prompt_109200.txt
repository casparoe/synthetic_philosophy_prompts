I teach a second-year applied ethics module — 18 students, one 90-minute seminar — and I'm bored of running habitat loss as the usual "biodiversity good, extinction bad" debate. I want a simulation where the students manage a bit of habitat themselves and then have to stare at the welfare numbers their own decisions produced. The wild-animal-suffering angle should land on them as a consequence of choices they made, not as something I announce.

Rough shape I've got, but improve it and make it actually runnable: four or five teams, each gets a plot and a hand of species cards. Each card carries three numbers: (1) young produced per adult per season; (2) the fraction of those young that survive to breed; (3) a per-individual lifetime welfare score from -10 to +10, where 0 is a life barely worth living. Make sure every deck has at least one boom-and-bust r-type — something like a fish or a mayfly that scatters thousands of eggs and loses essentially all of them to predators and starvation within days, so the doomed young are sitting on a clearly negative score and only a handful ever make it to a decent adult life. And at least one slow K-type — a bird or small mammal, few young, most of them surviving, positive score. The "welfare ledger" for a plot is just the sum over every individual it produces: doomed young (negative) plus survivors (positive). Teams keep a running total across rounds.

Each round a team picks one action for its plot: leave it alone, clear/develop it, or intervene (cull a species, feed, contracept, whatever). Run four or five rounds. The thing I want them to hit without me saying it: the teams that "protect" the habitat will usually end up with a much more negative ledger than the teams that develop it, because an intact habitat keeps churning out huge numbers of short, bad lives. Let them argue about that for a bit before anything is named.

Then I need the debrief to do real work. It should map what just happened onto the actual idea — that the net welfare of an ecosystem is only the sum of the welfare of the individuals in it, that there's no extra "ecosystem welfare" on top, and that number of lives and quality of lives can pull in opposite directions. I want them to see that "habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering" is not a gotcha, it's a live position, and to feel why it's tempting and why it's also shaky. Have them separate total welfare from average welfare, since the two come apart in the game and someone will confuse them. And push them on the weird one: whether bringing into existence a life that turns out to be net negative is a harm to anyone at all — they should notice the ledger quietly assumes yes and that this assumption is doing a lot of work. End by naming the two things that make the whole exercise unstable: nobody can actually measure a wild animal's lifetime welfare, and the parameters were made up by me.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — where the teams will refuse the numbers, which cards will get called unfair, who'll insist insects don't count or that a fish doesn't suffer, what happens when two teams pick different actions and compare ledgers — and how I should handle each without rescuing them. And be honest at the end about the one feature of the idea this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.
