I run a fortnightly discussion group on consequentialism and aggregation, and we keep circling back to a single case: you may either allow one person to be tortured for fifty years, or allow an unimaginably large number of people each to suffer one dust speck in the eye — a speck apiece, so trivial that no one would notice it a moment later. Most of us, on reflection, say we would prevent the torture; one or two insist the arithmetic is undeniable and the specks must be prevented. We have been at this for a year, nobody has moved, and I have begun to suspect we are not disagreeing about the same proposition at all. I would like you to act as a philosopher working on axiology and the ethics of aggregation and to give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Begin by thinking the dispute through carefully before you commit to a line: lay out, as concretely as you can, what each party is actually committed to — the totalist who bites the bullet, the person who holds that certain harms may not be traded off against any number of trivial ones, the one who thinks the case is a trick of scale and our intuitions simply fail at astronomical magnitudes, and the one who thinks the setup is ill-formed because a harm beneath the threshold of experience is not a harm at all. Then tell me what kind of disagreement this is: a substantive disagreement about value, a disagreement about which facts bear on the case, a disagreement about what a decision procedure may reasonably be asked to do, or two groups answering different questions under one heading. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the sticking point is neither the arithmetic nor the separateness of persons, but something about how we individuate harms and whether an imperceptible one counts.

I have two variants that I think isolate the question, and I would like you to say whether they change your diagnosis. First, replace the unmanageable number with a large but finite one — say, a figure so long that writing it out would take more digits than there are atoms in the observable universe — so that no one can wave the problem away as a feature of an impossible quantity. Second, and more importantly, put every speck on a single person, spread across a life long enough to hold them all, so that all the trivial harms accrue within one life and the objection about the separateness of persons has nothing to bite on; the alternative is still one person tortured for fifty years. If the impasse survives this, it is not about aggregation across persons, and if it dissolves, that tells us what was really doing the work. I am interested less in which verdict you favour than in what these two variants reveal about the structure of the disagreement.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result about how harms combine, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Please be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled among people who know this literature and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus; where you are unsure, say so rather than smoothing it over. I do not need reading recommendations or citations — reason it out from the cases themselves.
