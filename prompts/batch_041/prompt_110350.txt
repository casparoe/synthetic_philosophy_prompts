I'm a fifth-year PhD student and I've been dragooned into writing one of the essay questions for the take-home final of our department's upper-level epistemology seminar — the term's topic is higher-order evidence and disagreement, and the professor who normally writes the exam is on leave and basically told me "make it good, don't make it a memorization test." Which is exactly the problem: every question I draft is answerable by someone who's memorized the labels conciliationism and steadfastness and can parrot the self-undermining objection in two sentences. What I actually want is a question that forces them to take a stand on the one issue I've never fully been able to settle myself, partly because it hit close to home when a friend of mine (a radiology resident) described second-guessing a call she was confident about after reading a study about overconfidence in her specialty.

So take on the role of a philosopher who teaches and examines this material at a high level — someone who really knows the epistemology of disagreement and higher-order evidence — and think carefully before writing anything; maybe start by brainstorming where you think the real crux is, then draft. The question I want them to answer is roughly this: does the possibility of rational epistemic akrasia give the conciliationist a stable reply to the self-undermining objection, or does it just relocate the problem? And I want them to run it through a case, so please build the case into the question. Here's the case I've been sketching, feel free to tighten it: Priya is a radiologist who, on the basis of a scan and her reading of it, is confident in diagnosis D. She then learns of a well-designed study showing that radiologists like her are systematically overconfident on D-type calls, which is higher-order evidence that her first-order reasoning is unreliable here. But the study's sample was drawn from a population of unusually ambiguous cases, so its bearing on her particular call is itself uncertain — and she then reads a sharp methodological critique of the study, which is, so to speak, higher-order evidence about her higher-order evidence. I want the question to make them say whether that little regress matters, whether the "levels" are a real hierarchy or a fiction we impose, and whether the akrasia move survives once you notice the regress.

Please write me (1) the exam question itself — 90 minutes, closed book, graduate level, and it must not be answerable by reciting the standard positions or by summarizing the literature; it has to make them commit to a verdict and defend it; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that a strong candidate could plausibly produce in that time; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 5 marks each (25 total), where each point names the specific move a top answer has to make, and a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong. Three things I specifically want the marks to hang on: they have to distinguish epistemic akrasia from mere level-splitting (and I've noticed students definately conflate these), they have to state the self-undermining objection precisely enough that you can see why it targets conciliationism in particular, and they have to name at least one serious cost of the akrasia reply rather than just asserting it works or fails. Also please don't ask them to cite anything or list who said what — it's closed book and half of them will guess, I'd rather the marks come from the moves. And I'd like the marking scheme to seperate a genuine A from a B, so be specific about what the B answer is missing, not just vague stuff like "less depth."
