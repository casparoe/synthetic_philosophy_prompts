The argument about the frame problem among my colleagues has run for years without either side giving ground, and what I want is a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another overview. The engineering side says the family of difficulties was tamed in practice long ago: successor-state axioms and nonmonotonic formalisms handle inertia, qualification and ramification have workable treatments, action-description languages made it routine, and learned dynamics models make it moot, so what remains is bookkeeping. The other side, taking its cue from the phenomenological critics of classical AI, answers that only the bookkeeping version was ever solved and that the real problem is relevance: no formal system tells an agent which of the indefinitely many facts about its situation matter here and now, which is why the difficulty reappears with each new formalism. Meanwhile the people now building agent systems describe their trouble over deciding what to put into the model's context and memory as the frame problem returning, and I cannot tell whether that is the old problem or the first one under a new name. My hypothesis, and I hold it only as a hypothesis, is that the stalemate is an equivocation between a problem about the economy of representations and a problem about relevance, and that successes on the first keep being quietly counted as progress on the second. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath that no clarification of terms will dissolve? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side says, and what would count as progress here: a formal separation or impossibility result, an argument that the relevance version is not a computational question at all, or a principled reason to expect no convergence.
