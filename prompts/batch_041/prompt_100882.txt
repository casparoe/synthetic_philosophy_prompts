I want to pressure-test higher-order theories of consciousness, and I'd rather do it against a live opponent than read a summary. So: play a committed higher-order theorist for me — the kind who holds that a mental state is conscious just in case it's the target of a suitable higher-order representation of it. Sharp, not a strawman. Concede small points when I land them, but dont give up the core claim, and stay in character until I say stop. One paragraph per reply so it feels like a back and forth, and argue like a philosopher, not like a textbook reciting the objections and replies.

Here's my opening move, and skip the rock and the zombie, I've heard those.

Take a person with a totally ordinary first-order pain state in the left hand — injury, nociceptors, the works. But suppose we've rerouted things so the higher-order representation of that pain isnt generated by the pain. Instead an implant generates it, reading the experimenter's console, and it fires "pain, left hand" whenever the experimenter flips a switch, timed to coincide with the real pain. The person frowns, says ouch, my hand. Now which horn do you take?

If you say yes, there's a conscious pain, then it looks like being represented is all that matters — the higher-order state doesnt have to be about the first-order state or caused by it — and now I can make you conscious of states you dont have just by driving the higher-order state from outside, which sounds like the theory has quietly lost the very phenomenon it was built to explain. If you say no, then it wasnt the representing that made the state conscious, it was some causal or functional tie between the two states, and then the higher-order representation isnt really what's doing the work after all — the tie is. So which is it, and what's the principled line that keeps you off both horns?
