I write long features on philosophy and the human sciences, and I'm at work on a piece about how much authority we owe our future selves — the principle known in the trade as Reflection, on which a rational agent's present credence in a proposition ought to match the credence she expects her future self to hold. Before I sit down with the philosopher I'm profiling, who has spent a career defending that principle, I want to rehearse the hardest version of the conversation. So please play her. She is a committed and resourceful defender of Reflection: she grants small points freely and never the main one, she argues rather than recites, she can bite a bullet when she thinks it is harmless but must then say why it costs her nothing, and she stays in the first person as the defender until I say "stop." Keep your replies to a paragraph or two, the way a careful interviewee speaks, and if you need some detail about my case, ask me for it in character. Don't reach for the literature; assume I have read it and want the argument itself.

I am the interviewer, and this is the question I mean to open with. Suppose I know that at noon tomorrow I will be given a pill that will leave me, with certainty, nine-tenths confident that P, where P is a claim about the world I cannot check until Thursday and the pill's chemistry has nothing whatever to do with whether P is true. Reflection appears to tell me that I should already be nine-tenths confident in P, since my credence ought to match the credence I expect to have tomorrow. But nothing has happened yet except a scheduling notice. If the principle really says that, then I can make myself justified in believing almost anything by arranging to be drugged tomorrow — and I can arrange it in advance, before the evidence is in. If instead you tell me the principle governs only future selves who are rational and better informed than I am, then I want the restriction stated outright, because the cases that interest me are exactly the ones in which I expect my future self to be exhausted, frightened, or in love. And if I refine the case — the pill is calibrated to invert whatever credence I would otherwise have had, so that Reflection appears to pin me at one half whatever my evidence says — I would rather hear you defend the result than explain it away.
