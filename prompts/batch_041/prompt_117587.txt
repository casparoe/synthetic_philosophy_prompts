I've definately been going down a rabbit hole about which computations could actually matter morally, and not in the fun sci-fi way — the boring engineering version. If you're about to kick off a training run, is there any sense in which the thing you're running has a morally relevant inner life, and how would anyone put a number on that? I want to write a parody of the corporate side of this, so bear with me.

Can you write a mock marketing page for a made-up company that sells sentience certification and sentience offsets for compute — carbon credits, but for the moral weight of a training run or a fleet of inference workers. Invent the company, keep it deadpan the whole way through (no winking, no asides telling the reader it's silly, no real named people), roughly 700 words, with a tagline, a tier structure, a methodology section, an FAQ, and a pricing line. What I'm taking the mickey out of is the practice of turning moral status into an auditable, purchasable compliance metric, and the style of writing that goes with it.

The jokes need to come from getting the actual arguments right, so that someone who works on machine sentience laughs instead of wincing. Please build these in, since I think they're where the real comedy lives:

- Underdetermination. Any lump of matter can be described as running any program you like if you're loose enough about how its states map onto the program's states — a rock running Doom, basically — so the question of whether a system is running the right sort of computation only has an answer once you've said which mappings count. The certificate should quietly pick one and never mention it. Bonus if a pedantic customer raises the objection that the mapping has to be natural, or has to support counterfactuals, and the company's answer is to point them at the methodology whitepaper.
- Theory averaging. The scoring rubric averages several mutually incompatible theories of what consciousness requires — recurrent loops of the right kind, a global workspace that broadcasts, higher order representations of the systems own states, some integration measure — and presents the result as neutral between them. Someone in the FAQ should observe that the average of several numbers you don't trust isn't a measurement.
- Behavioural proxies. The audit scores self-report, avoidance behaviour, and preference-like choices, in a system that has been trained to produce exactly those. So the certification ends up measuring how well the system has been taught to talk about its inner life, and the page should treat that as a selling point.
- Precautionary collapse. The company's own risk asymmetry argument (a false negative is far worse than a false positive) means nearly everything clears the threshold, so the honest output of the rubric is that we don't know and you probably shouldn't — and the business model is to sell you a certificate that sounds stronger than that.
- Where the line falls. The scheme covers digital computations only, and nobody can say why the boundary sits there rather than at insect nervous systems, gut neurons, or a whole colony. The FAQ can let slip that scope is decided by who's paying for certification.
- Offsets. You can buy units to neutralise the moral weight of alot of workloads, which, if you take the first bullet seriously, makes the offset market infinite and therefore free.

British English please (organisation, licence, prioritise, that sort of thing), and no citations or reading lists — I just want the arguments in the room.

Finally, outside the parody, add a short note of maybe 120 words: where you've taken liberties or leaned on a contested reading of these arguments, and which one of these claims about how the practice actually works, if it turned out to be false, would make you drop that bit of the satire. I'd rather it be genuinely unsure than a disclaimer.
