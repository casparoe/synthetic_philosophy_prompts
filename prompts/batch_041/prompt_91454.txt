A colleague of mine in the philosophy department has been working through cases of vagueness with her graduate seminar, and I have been keeping a tally of her verdicts. I would like you to forecast her verdicts on the next batch, and I want to understand the rule she is actually running rather than the one she advertises.

Here is the record so far. On the plainly clear cases she is conventional: a single grain of sand is not a heap, a hundred thousand grains is a heap, a man with a full head of hair is not bald, a man with no hair at all is bald. She insists that the sorites premise — that if n grains make a heap then n-1 do too, for every n — is simply false, not a harmless idealization; she says the argument trades on a false universal. She holds that "either 37 grains is a heap or 37 grains is not a heap" is true, and she is emphatic that classical logic is not up for revision on account of vagueness. She also grants that there is some number of grains such that that many make a heap and one fewer does not — she says the existence claim is true even though no one can produce the number. What she will not do is say, of any particular middling case, whether the predicate applies; when pressed on 37 grains specifically she declines to call the sentence true and declines to call it false, and she treats that abstention as principled rather than a failure of nerve.

The next cases are these:

1. A single sentence, S: "Thirty-seven grains of sand arranged in a loose pile make a heap." Is S true, is S false, or is S neither true nor false?

2. The sentence "Either S or not S." True or not?

3. A sentence about a middling case that is itself the subject of a middling case — what she would call a borderline case of a borderline case: "There is a determinate fact of the matter about whether 37 grains make a heap." True, false, or neither?

4. A sentence that predicates a middling value of an object in a context engineered so that the conversational standards are unusually high: "Given how we are using the word right now, 37 grains make a heap." True, false, or neither?

Predict her verdict on each, say which of the following rules she is implicitly running — sharp-but-unknowable bivalence (there is always a fact of the matter, we simply cannot get at it), supervaluationism (the sentence is neither true nor false when precisifications disagree, though its classical compounds can still be true), a degree-theoretic account (the sentence has an intermediate degree of truth), or a contextualist account (the predicate's extension shifts with the conversational standard) — and identify which of the four cases would most cleanly separate the rules that your best guess leaves standing. I care less about which label she would accept than about which cases her stated verdicts actually pin down, and where the record so far genuinely underdetermines the answer.
