I'm a third-year philosophy student (also doing a minor in econ) and next Thursday I have to present my term paper at our work-in-progress seminar, which is a weird mix: mostly causal decision theorists from the econ department who are allergic to hand-waving about correlation, two Kantians from the ethics reading group who keep asking whether everything is just universalizability with extra steps, one evolutionary game theorist who thinks every norm is replicator dynamics in a trench coat, and my supervisor, who works on team reasoning and is probably my most dangerous questioner. My paper defends a claim about superrationality: that it's not a seperate decision theory or a disguised moral principle, but what you get when you take causal decision theory seriously and add what I've been calling the 'same-wiring' assumption -- roughly, that if two agents' choices are outputs of the same reasoning process, then each agent's choice is evidence about the other's, and that this is a logical/algorithmic correlation, not spooky backward causation. I distinguish two versions: 'mirror' superrationality, where the agents know they're running the same code (two copies of the same AI, or two people who've both read the same argument and are symmetric), and 'shadow' superrationality, where they don't know but their reasoning is correlated because it was shaped by the same selection pressure or training environment. I think the shadow version is the interesting one and can handle one-shot PD without requiring identical agents or altruism. I use a couple of examples: two instances of the same LLM playing a one-shot PD, and a human and an AI that were both trained on the same corpus and use the same heuristic. I also say something about why this isn't just evidential decision theory in a hat, but I'm definately not sure that part is airtight.
