Me and a friend got into a long, kind of dumb argument last night about pain asymbolia -- the thing where people with certain brain injuries feel pain, will tell you it hurts, but it just doesn't bother them; they don't pull their hand away, sometimes they laugh. My friend's line was that this proves the badness of pain isn't in the sensation itself, it's some separate reaction that the injury knocks out. I half agree, but then I started wondering what Bentham would have made of it. He's the guy who supposedly thinks pleasures all differ only in quantity and not in kind, and who puts pain and pleasure as the two masters running everything. So: would Bentham say the asymbolic patient isn't really in pain at all, or is in a genuinely intense pain that somehow scores zero on the calculus, or something else? And which way of reading him does each answer commit you to -- is the badness of a pain the felt intensity of it, or the grip it has on you that makes you avoid it? Last bit: if it's the grip, what happens to the hedonic calculus when you're adding up a whole population -- do those patients' pains just drop out of the sum? Give me your best reconstruction of what Bentham would actually say, not a survey of the asymbolia literature.
