ok so my history and philosophy of science reading group is doing Malthus this week and I have to bring a "philosophical profile" — basically where I stand on how you're supposed to judge a theory like his — and I'd like to know if it actually hangs together before I get picked apart. here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) A theory that makes a dated prediction, and the date rolls around and the thing doesn't happen, is falsified. That's definately what testability amounts to; if nothing could count against it, it isn't saying anything.

(2) Malthus's mechanism was basically right — unchecked population runs up much faster than food supply, so any population that gets ahead of its food gets dragged back down — and the reason his dates missed is stuff he couldn't have seen coming, like the demographic transition and the yield jumps from modern agriculture. So he was right but early rather than wrong.

(3) A theory whose central mechanism gets vindicated should count as corroborated even when the quantitative predictions miss. The mechanism is the theory; the numbers were just where he happened to hang it.

(4) But you never rescue a theory from a failed prediction by bolting on an auxiliary hypothesis whose only job is to explain the failure away. That's the textbook ad hoc move, and it's how research programs go senile.

(5) Long-run predictions are the best kind of test, because you can't fudge them — you hit the date or you don't, and nobody can quietly revise the claim in the meantime the way they can with a short-run forecast.

(6) I'm a realist about the forces he named — the pull of population against subsistence is really there, not a useful fiction. But I'm not a realist about his numbers; the particular ratios and dates were artifacts of his moment and shouldn't be read as describing anything.

Someone in the group kept saying Malthus was "falsified on schedule" — the failure showed up exactly when he said the reckoning would, which is a weird kind of failure and not the same as a theory that just gradually stops touching the world. I've been leaning on that distinction ever since. Related: I want to say the mechanism was right because population pressure was the rate-limiting factor for most of the period he was writing about, and the things that broke the prediction were unpredictable in kind, not just in size — but I can't tell if that's a real criterion or just me special-pleading on his behalf.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, how bad is each clash, and which one has to give? I suspect a couple of them can't all be true at once but I can't see which, and I'd rather find out from you than in front of the group. If any of them only look like they conflict, say so and say why. And for each real clash, tell me the cheapest fix and what it costs me.
