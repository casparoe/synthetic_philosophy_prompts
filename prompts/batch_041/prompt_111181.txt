I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm taking an upper-level course on the ethics of AI; I'm also the undergraduate TA for it, which is how I've ended up drafting the single essay question for the take-home final, along with a model answer and a marking scheme the instructor can circulate to the other markers. The unit the question has to cover is the one on paternalism and assistance — roughly, when an assistant may decline to do what a user asks, and when it may go beyond declining and do something else in the user's stead. My flatmate and I have been arguing about this for a fortnight and have got nowhere, which is part of why I want the question to bite.

The course has been working with three distinctions that I should probably lay out, since the question has to be answerable without students looking anything up (the final is closed-book and citations are banned, so the answer has to stand on its own reasoning). First, the ground of an intervention: epistemic (the user is mistaken about how to get what they want), volitional (the user knows the route but can't or won't take it), or axiological (the user's ends themselves are what the intervenor objects to). Second, the familiar soft/hard paternalism distinction, where soft paternalism is supposed to be licensed only when the intervention is consent-tracking — it shields a person's will from a temporary defect rather than overriding a settled will — and hard paternalism overrides a settled will for the person's own good. Third, the standing problem: an assistant is built by a firm, so whatever paternalism it practises is private value imposition with no democratic authorisation behind it, though unlike the state it is easy to walk away from and it cannot lock anyone up.

The policy the question has to be about is one the class spent two weeks on. A developer ships a "protective mode", on by default: where a request would, in the assistant's assessment, expose the user and no one else to a serious setback, and the assistant also judges the user's judgment impaired or their means-ends reasoning defective, the assistant does not refuse. It quietly carries out a safer variant of what was asked — the risky ingredient swapped out, the dose capped, the training plan softened, the aggressive clause replaced with a conservative one — and tells the user afterwards what it did. Requests that mainly threaten third parties fall under a separate and older policy and are not in dispute. What I want from you is the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built around five must-hit points with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically lose it, plus a closing line on what separates an A from a B. The question has to force students to say whether one and the same justification can cover both halves of the policy — the refusal and the silent substitution — and, if it can't, what extra premise the substitution needs and what would have to be true about the assistant's epistemic position for that premise to hold. It should not be answerable by reciting the soft/hard distinction or by rehearsing the standard liberal objection to paternalism, and it should be short enough that a strong answer can be written in about ninety minutes; the model answer should land on a verdict rather than survey the options.
