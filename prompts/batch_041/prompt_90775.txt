For the past year I have been attending a small Anglican parish after fifteen years of believing nothing in particular, and I find that I cannot give an honest account of what changed. I did not acquire a new belief in the way I acquired the belief that the kettle is on; nor does it feel like a decision to adopt a vocabulary, which is roughly how my brother — a philosopher of mind, and an atheist — describes what I have done. When I say, in the liturgy, that Christ is risen, I am not reporting an event I could in principle have witnessed, and yet I am not merely expressing a hope or recommitting myself to a way of life. Both of the descriptions on offer, the propositional one and the expressivist one, seem to me to miss the thing itself, and I have begun to suspect that the difficulty lies with the descriptions rather than with my experience of them.

My brother and I have been arguing about this for months and the argument goes nowhere, which is why I am asking you rather than him. We have both read enough Wittgenstein to know the moves: religious language as a distinct language-game with its own internal criteria of correctness; the suggestion that the believer and the unbeliever may not be contradicting one another at all, because they are not playing the same game; the thought that a man might have a picture framing the whole of his life while someone else, looking on, cannot share the picture and cannot refute it either. I have also read the standard objection, that this renders religious belief invulnerable — a fideism dressed up as a grammatical observation — and that it cannot accommodate the plain fact that believers take themselves to be saying something true: true enough to die for, true enough to condemn others by, true enough to lose their faith over. My brother presses that objection hard, and I have not yet found an answer to it that does not either surrender the grammar or surrender the truth.

What I would like from you is a dialogue between two people who are both good at this. One holds that Wittgenstein's remarks on religion are best read as locating religious belief within a practice that carries its own criteria, so that the request for evidence does not arise in the way the critic supposes it does. The other holds that this reading, however attractive, cannot survive contact with how believers actually speak and behave, and that some form of realism about religious claims is required if faith, doubt, conversion and apostasy are to be intelligible at all. Neither should be a caricature and neither should win by being handed the easier lines; each should land at least one genuine hit on the other, and each should concede something under pressure.

I would like the exchange to turn on two concrete cases rather than on generalities. First, a believer who reports that God has told him to do something monstrous: I want to see whether the internalist can distinguish a misuse of the language-game from an unfamiliar but genuine move within it without simply appealing to what the rest of us happen to find acceptable. Second, a believer whose faith collapses after reading history or after the death of a child: I want to see whether that transition is best described as a change of belief or a change of life, and what each interlocutor must say about the other's account of it. They should also disagree explicitly about whether conversion is better described as coming to see a fact or as coming to inhabit a practice, and about whether the idea of God's reality can be given any content on the internalist's account or is being quietly abandoned by it.

Somewhere in the dialogue I would like one of them to press a point that is my own half-formed worry and which I have not seen put quite this way: that an appeal to a language-game is a description of how a form of words is used, and descriptions of use are perfectly compatible with the users being systematically mistaken about what they are doing; so the internalist cannot move from the fact that these are the words people use to the conclusion that this is what the words mean without an argument he has not yet given. If the realist has a good reply, I want to see it. If he does not, I would rather the dialogue ended with the disagreement sharpened than falsely reconciled, since an honest impasse would be more use to me than a settlement I do not believe.

Please write the dialogue in British English, with both speakers arguing in their own voices rather than trading summaries of positions. Then add a short paragraph in your own voice saying which of the two you think has the better of it, and naming the one point on which you take the losing side to be plainly right.
