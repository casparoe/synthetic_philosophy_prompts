I'm on the committee for our PhD comp exam in philosphy of history and I need one take-home essay question on contingency — the for-want-of-a-nail thing — that candidates can't pass just by reciting the usual chestnuts or gesturing at chaos theory. Here's a setup I made up, so they can't have read it (and they can't search, so don't ask for citations): a historian claims to measure how contingent an outcome was by simulating the period and counting what fraction of tiny random perturbations — a general sleeps an hour late, a courier's horse goes lame, a letter arrives a day late — flip the outcome. He calls the resulting number the outcome's contingency score. Build the question around that. It should force them to work out whether the number actually tracks contingency or something else (sensitivity to initial conditions, fragility, plain luck), whether the set of perturbations is well-defined or quietly smuggles in the possibilities the actual world happened to contain, and whether a high score really licenses "it could easily have gone otherwise" rather than "my model is unstable." Then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a 20-mark scheme, where the scheme names the specific moves an A answer has to make that a B answer can skip — e.g. separating epistemic from metaphysical contingency, noticing the measure problem, and saying what a defensible contingency claim would have to assert.
