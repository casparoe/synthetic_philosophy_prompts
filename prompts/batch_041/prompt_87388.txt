Something thats been bugging me about the free-tuition debate and I cant get a clean answer to.

Germany, Denmark, Sweden, Norway: no tuition, the state just covers it. US and England: you pay, often a lot, usually with a loan that hangs around for decades. Fine, different countries, different choices. But three things about the actual arrangements dont hang together for me and I want to know what theyre committed to.

1. In the Nordics tuition is free but living costs arent. You get a grant and a chunky loan for rent and food. So the state has drawn a line: instruction is a public thing we pay for, your own upkeep is your own business. Why there? What view of education, or of adulthood, puts the lecture hall on one side of the line and the groceries on the other?

2. The UK used to be in the free camp and moved to the fee camp inside one generation, and even today Scotland doesn't charge while England charges a lot. Same country, same universities, different answers. If the regime can flip like that without much else changing, was the old one actually presupposing anything, or is it just budget politics wearing a philosophy costume?

3. The English 'loan' isnt really a loan -- you only pay it back above some income, and whatever's left gets written off after a few decades, which is not how a loan works. Meanwhile in the US the sticker price is close to fictional for a lot of students; hardly anyone pays it. So one system calls a tax a debt, and the other posts a price that isnt a price. What does that presuppose about the price, the debt, and the person who owes it?

What I want from you: reconstruct the view of education, of fairness, and of the person that these regimes commit you to. Education as public investment, as a private good you buy, as a meritocratic sorting device, as a citizenship thing, as self-ownership and adult responsibility, or nothing at all -- just path dependence? Sort out which of those the practice strictly needs from the ones it merely happens to fit. Say where its underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same fees and loans. Then tell me what would actually have to change on the ground if you swapped the presupposed view for the rival one -- the loans, the grants, admissions, how the debt gets discharged, whatever.

And be upfront about the weak spots: tell me what would change your mind, what evidence or argument would push you to a different reading of what the practice presupposes, and where youre guessing about how these countries actually work rather than reasoning from what you know.
