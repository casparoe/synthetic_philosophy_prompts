Title: Echoes and Bearers

Hofstadter's later writing on the self is usually summarized as the claim that the I is a strange loop: not a thing but a pattern, a self-referential, self-representing weave of symbols that a brain spins about itself. The striking corollary is that a pattern is the sort of thing that can be instantiated more than once, and since we spend our lives building rich models of the people we love, something of them is instantiated in us. He seems to allow that this comes in degrees: the more richly, and in the more minds, a person's pattern is reproduced, the more of that person persists. Call this, unkindly, a calculus of soul-percentage. On such a view the death of a much-loved person is not the annihilation of a thing but the truncation of a pattern, and being forgotten is a genuine diminishment — arguably the real second death.

I think this conflates two relations, and that only one of them can do the metaphysical work Hofstadter wants.

The first relation is echoing: my friend carries a model of me. The second is bearing: part of my cognitive life actually runs in her. She holds my appointments, she remembers the name of my dentist, she reminds me of the resolution I made in January. This is the transactive-memory picture, in which a couple functions as a single distributed store and each partner serves as the other's index. It is reinforced by the memory-conformity findings, where a couple's recollection of a shared event drifts toward a jointly authored version that neither would have produced alone, so that the record of the event is stored across two brains in a form that neither brain contains.

Echoing, I argue, cannot be survival. A representation is not identical with what it represents, and — this is the important part — it can be wrong. My friend's model of me is heavily shaped by her needs; it is a portrait partly painted by the sitter's admirer, and its distortions are features of her, not of me. If we counted echoes as persistence, fame would be a form of immortality and a caricature would count for as much as a portrait, since all that would matter is that some pattern is instantiated somewhere. Hofstadter has an obvious reply: the brain's own self-model is a representation too, so if the original I is a representation, why object to representation as a medium of survival? My answer is that the self-model is causally locked to its subject in a way that nobody else's model is; the loop closes at home. That asymmetry is what echoing lacks.

Bearing, by contrast, is real, but it is local, mundane, and unevenly distributed — it tracks the division of cognitive labor and who can be relied on, not love or renown. And it faces the indexicality objection: whatever is spread, the experiencing is not. A pattern can be in two heads, but a here cannot. There is always one locus of experience (or, in the contested split-brain and multiple-occupancy cases, more than one — but never a fraction of one). So the distributed self buys persistence at the price of no longer being about the thing we cared about. Unless the loop is not where experience happens but what experience is a level of description of; but then two heads is not half of me in you, it is a second instance of the same loop, which is a different and much stranger claim, and it raises the fission problem in a new costume: two instances, one pattern, and no fact of the matter about which one is me.

So the I is distributable in a thin sense — patterns are repeatable — and in a thick sense that is real but small. Hofstadter's picture lives off the ambiguity between them. The question I want to end on is whether anything can be had in between.
