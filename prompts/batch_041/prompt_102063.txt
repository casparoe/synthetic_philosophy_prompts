I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I keep running into a pedagogical problem that I would like you to solve by building a case to order. The standard illustrations of historical entitlement — the athlete whose salary a million fans voluntarily pay, and its cousins — make the theory look as though it licenses whatever a chain of voluntary transactions happens to produce, so my students leave believing that the historical/end-state distinction simply *is* the verdict: entitlement theory says hands off, pattern theories say redistribute. I want a scenario that breaks that equation. Please construct a case, realistic and no longer than a paragraph — an ordinary workplace, a land parcel, a pension, a firm's ownership history, something a person could actually encounter, with no fictional island, no invented country, and none of the famous textbook examples — in which a historical-entitlement theorist in the tradition of Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, who accepts the three principles of acquisition (proviso and all), transfer, and rectification, and a pattern or end-state theorist of your choosing (a utilitarian or a Rawlsian — say which, and why that one suits the case) both conclude that the very same, specific redistribution is required, but for reasons that have nothing to do with one another. The entitlement theorist must arrive at that conclusion through rectification of a genuine past wrong, not by covertly importing a pattern; the pattern theorist must arrive at it through the pattern alone.

Then, in a second section, run the checks that a case of this kind demands. State what each theorist says and why, and identify precisely which feature of the scenario is load-bearing — that is, the feature whose removal would make the two theories peel apart. Remove it, and show that they do peel apart, telling me which way each one then goes. I also want an honest note on how a defender of the entitlement theory would try to resist the case: the natural move, as I understand it, is to argue either that the supposed past wrong was not really a rights violation on the theory's own terms, or that the remedy the theory actually mandates is not the remedy the pattern theorist wants, so that the agreement is a coincidence of arithmetic rather than a meeting of principles. Say whether that move succeeds here and on what it turns. Finally, because my students will press the point, tell me whether the case survives the disappearance of the wrongdoer — suppose the person or body responsible for the original wrong is long dead and its holdings have been dispersed — and if the rectification claim does survive that, say who the theory holds owes the remedy and on what basis.
