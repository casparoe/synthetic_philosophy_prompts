I teach an undergraduate seminar on free will, and I want to build a session around Kubrick's A Clockwork Orange and the Frankfurt cases. Students tend to leave the film feeling that it settles the matter: Alex is conditioned by the Ludovico technique, he can no longer choose violence, and the prison chaplain protests that goodness must be chosen and that a man who cannot choose has ceased to be a man. That sounds like a ringing endorsement of the principle of alternative possibilities, and I want a reading that takes the temptation seriously and then interrogates it.

Here is the difficulty I want you to confront head-on. Frankfurt's counterexamples are meant to show that someone can be morally responsible even though he could not have done otherwise, because the actual sequence of his action ran through his own deliberative machinery, with the intervener waiting in the wings and never having to step in. But the Ludovico technique does not wait in the wings. It rewires Alex's responses so that the violent option is not merely blocked in some counterfactual scenario; it is unavailable in the actual sequence, and the nausea is triggered by the very thought of violence. So the film may not be a Frankfurt case at all. It may be closer to the sort of direct manipulation or engineered aversion that even many defenders of Frankfurt-style compatibilism concede is responsibility-undermining. I want you to say whether the film's argument survives that disanalogy, and precisely where it does or does not.

Please make the reading do several things. First, reconstruct the film's implicit thesis about freedom, and separate what the film shows from what the chaplain says; the chaplain may be a spokesman for the film or a foil, and I want a defended view on which he is. Second, lay out the structure of a Frankfurt case in plain terms and explain exactly why the Ludovico case diverges from it — then ask whether the divergence is fatal to the film's point or whether the film is making a different and still defensible point, perhaps about the value of the capacity to choose rather than about the conditions of moral responsibility. Third, address the scenes that complicate the moral ledger: Alex's genuine relish for violence before the treatment and the question of whether he was responsible then; the behavior of the state, the chaplain, and the writer, each of whom wants something from Alex's freedom; and the closing suggestion that the cure has been undone. Fourth, say what the film contributes that the philosophical literature does not — the emotional and political texture of a society willing to trade a man's capacity for choice for a reduction in crime, which is not what Frankfurt's tidy counterexamples are about.

Do not treat the film as a mere illustration of a thesis, and do not distort the philosophy to fit the film. If the honest conclusion is that the film runs together two distinct threats to freedom — determinism and manipulation — say so and explain what follows. Assume no one in the room has the novel, the screenplay, or the philosophical essays in front of them, so work from what is common knowledge about the film and from the argument itself rather than asking me to check any lines or page numbers.
