After a long and increasingly heated argument over the weekend, my closest collaborator and I have agreed to do the thing our reading group keeps telling us to do and write our dispute up as an adversarial collaboration. She is a frequentist of the error-statistical persuasion: for her the replication crisis is a crisis of researcher behavior rather than of the machinery, and the p-value remains a serviceable tool once the sampling plan is fixed in advance, the test has been shown capable of detecting the effect if it is there, and the garden of forking paths has been closed off by pre-registration and full disclosure. I have drifted to the Bayesian side of the room: I think the p-value was never a measure of evidence in the first place, that sorting results into significant and non-significant is not the distinction we should be drawing, and that what we owe a reader is a posterior or a Bayes factor under a prior we are willing to defend. What unsettles me is that we keep discovering we disagree about things that sound like the same thing, and agree about things each of us assumed the other denied, and I would like the document to make both of those visible.

Please draft the joint statement we could both sign. I want, first, a numbered list of claims we can both endorse: about what the large replication projects in psychology and medicine did and did not establish; about what the formal objects of each framework do and do not mean (for instance, that a 95% confidence interval and a 95% credible interval computed on the same normal-model data can coincide numerically while answering entirely different questions, or that a Bayes factor and a posterior probability are not interchangeable); and about what a reader is entitled to infer from each kind of number a paper might report. Then a disagreements section, laid out as a table, giving for each live dispute the precise claim each of us would put our name to, in wording that side would accept rather than a caricature of the other's position. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that if either of us conceded one, our position would actually have to move.

I already suspect where some of the cruxes lie. One is the stopping rule: whether it is legitimate to analyze data as though the sample size had been fixed in advance when it was not, and whether a procedure that ignores the stopping rule, as the likelihood principle seems to license, can still control error rates over the long run. Another is the case where a result clears the conventional significance threshold while the Bayes factor under a sceptical default prior favours the null, the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox, which neither of us can decide whether to treat as a defect of one framework or of the other. For each crux, give the test, argument, or concrete case that we would both accept as bearing on it, and record each of our predictions about how it comes out, written down before we run anything. Finally, I want the document to take seriously and either adopt or explicitly reject a proposal of mine: that we stop treating the p-value and a default-prior Bayes factor as rivals, report both as a matter of course, and treat the cases where they disagree as flagged anomalies rather than as findings. I want to know whether she and I can both sign that, and if not, which of the cruxes that disagreement turns on. And if it turns out that part of what we have been arguing about is purely verbal, two words for one thing or one word for two, say so plainly and identify which part.
