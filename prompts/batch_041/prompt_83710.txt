For the past fourteen months I have been writing a novel about a woman who does not know what is happening in her own house, and I had assumed I understood the mechanics of that kind of not-knowing until my sister handed me a live version of it and I discovered that I understand nothing at all. My sister is fifty-one and has run a small commercial printing business with the same partner for eleven years; for the last three months she has been sitting in rooms with auditors and a lawyer because that partner was moving money out of the company — not cleverly, as these things go, paying invoices to a supplier that did not exist. What interests me is not the fraud but the arrangement of her life around it. She signed every check. She kept a drawer in her desk that she called the Later Drawer, into which went anything she did not want to think about that day, and several of the documents the auditors eventually built their case from had sat in that drawer for two years, still in their envelopes. In the spring of the year before last she made herself a rule that she would not open the accounts after nine in the evening, because that is when she panics, and so for eighteen months she looked at the company's books almost exclusively in the mornings, when she was calm and the numbers were easiest to reconcile. And I remember, at a dinner in that same period, her asking me whether it was normal for a partner in a small firm to keep a private credit card for business expenses — a question that only makes sense if she had already seen something. When I remind her of it she says she does not remember asking. She says that the woman who signed those papers was not her.

She insists she never knew. She is not defending herself well: she has told the story the same way to me, to our brother, and to the lawyer, with the same pauses in the same places, and twice she has said that she would have left him in a tone that suggests she is describing someone else's marriage. My difficulty is that I am in no position to be certain about her, because I also noticed things — an odd remark from the partner at a Christmas lunch, a letter that arrived at the house addressed to the company — and I said nothing to her for two years, and I am still not sure why. So when I try to say what my sister is doing, I find I have three options and no way to choose among them: that she genuinely did not know, and that I am retrofitting a pattern onto ordinary inattention; that she is lying to me now; or that she is doing something in between, for which I have no name, in which she in some sense knew and in some sense did not, and which a friend of mine who teaches philosophy insists does not exist — he says the middle case is a story people tell in order to be forgiven, and that anyone who claims to have lived in it is either mistaken about their own past or lying about it.

What I want from you is not a character sketch of my sister but an account of whether that third condition is a real thing a person can be in, and if it is, what it looks like from the inside and how one could ever tell it apart from the other two from the outside — what I should be watching for in what she says and does, and what I should say to her. I would also like to know whether the answer changes what I owe her, because if it does not, I would rather stop trying to work it out and go back to the book. And if you think the middle case is incoherent, say so plainly, and tell me the most plausible replacement story for what has been going on in that office for eleven years. Do not send me to the library: I do not need names, dates, or what anyone has written. I need to know how to think about her.
