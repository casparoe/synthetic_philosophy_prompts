I'm a novelist, third book, and this one's set in a near-future where we've actually gone and done it — engineered the suffering out of people, and out of the animals too. I've been re-reading The Giver (Lois Lowry) as research and I keep feeling she got there first, and got something right that I can't put my finger on, and also something wrong. So what I want from you is a proper reading of the novel through the hedonistic imperative — David Pearce's abolitionist project, the biotech plan to phase out suffering everywhere and swap it for what he called "information-sensitive gradients of bliss".

Two things I'm chewing on. First, Jonas's community seems to have abolished suffering but kept the hardware: the people still feel, they've just had the range flattened — no colour, no real love — and the whole thing runs on a daily pill plus one poor sod, the Receiver, holding all the pain on everyone else's behalf. Is that a failed version of the imperative, or is that what the imperative actually looks like once you build it? And if it's the second, what does that say about the "who pays" question, because Pearce's stuff always sounds suspiciously costless to me. Second, the novel's obvious counterpunch is that you can't have love or wisdom without the pain, and the memory of it. Is Lowry making a conceptual point there — that suffering is constitutively necessary for the good stuff — or just a contingent one about how her particular society botched the trade? A Pearce defender would say contingent: fix the neurochemistry and you keep the depth. I want you to say who wins that one, and not fudge it.

In the weeds is fine, in fact wanted — the pain/suffering distinction, cases where people feel pain but don't suffer from it, whether the community abolished the signal or the experience, all of it. I'd rather you didn't reduce the book to an illustration of a thesis; tell me where it resists the reading as well, and finish on what the novel sees about this that the philosophy definately doesn't. No reading list or citations please, I'm not going to chase papers, and keep it in British English — my editor's in London.
