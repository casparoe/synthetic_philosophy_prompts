I chair the collections committee of a university art museum, and I have to circulate a written opinion before we meet on Thursday. The director has said she will be bound by whatever we recommend, so the document has to carry the weight of a real decision.

The estate of a sculptor whose work we have courted for years has sent us a bequest whose terms our counsel calls unfortunate. Clause 4 leaves to the sculptor's longtime studio assistant the unglazed clay figure in the north studio, together with the whole of the material of which it is composed. Clause 5 leaves to us the last work described in the catalogue raisonné, in whatever state of completion. The catalogue raisonné's last entry describes that same unglazed figure. The assistant's lawyer has written, in the alternative, that the clauses cannot both be performed, since the figure simply is its material, so that either we take the figure and the assistant takes a description, or he takes the figure and we take nothing; and that if we insist there are two things here, then the whole of the material names no particular thing, because a portion of clay is only one among indefinitely many overlapping parcels, and the gift to his client fails for indeterminacy. The executor has asked us to state our position by the end of next month, after which he will distribute the estate.

There is a complication of our own making. Our acquisitions policy contains a clause drafted two years ago, when we were accessioning a work with a detachable plinth, which we have never had occasion to interpret: where a single portion of matter simultaneously constitutes more than one object, the museum shall treat the object whose persistence conditions are those of the material as the object of record. The object of record is what we enter in the register, insure, and lend. I have a sinking feeling that the clause, read as written, hands the assistant everything we are asking for. Counsel replies, unhelpfully, that the clause governs only what we write in our own register and not what the testator wrote in his will.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- accept, decline, accept on stated conditions, or propose a specific division or settlement -- with a deadline; state how many objects there are and defend the count; say whether the policy clause settles the case, misfires, or should be read down, and supply replacement wording if you think it should be amended; say what we should enter in the register and insure, one item or two; and put the strongest case for the position you reject, then answer it rather than waving it away. Two further points will be raised at the meeting and should be dealt with: whether it matters that the clay would survive being broken up and reworked while the figure would not, and whether the cracked foot our conservator wants to fill with a reversible consolidant means we would be accessioning something other than the object the will names. Assume the will is valid and that its language is not decisive in law; I need the committee's reasoning, not a prediction about how a court would rule.
