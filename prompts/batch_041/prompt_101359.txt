I maintain the review queue for a shared data platform on which teams define collections. A collection is introduced by supplying a membership rule: an object belongs to it exactly when it satisfies a condition the author writes. Three features of the platform matter here. Authors may write conditions that mention other collections, including the collection being defined, and that quantify over all collections without restriction. The platform also provides an operation that returns the collection of all subcollections of a given collection. And it provides a second, recursive style of definition, in which a collection is declared to be the least collection closed under a given rule. Last quarter a reviewer accepted a proposal defining the collection of all collections that do not contain themselves, and, in the same batch, a proposal defining the collection of all collections; downstream jobs that combined those two with the subcollections operation produced contradictory results, and we spent a week tracing the fault. I do not want to forbid the ordinary comprehension style, since nearly all our useful definitions use it, but I need a procedure my reviewers can run without any training in logic.

Write that procedure. It should be an ordered sequence of questions, each with a decision attached: what the reviewer must inspect, what would count as an adequate answer, where the procedure returns a definite rejection, and where it must halt and escalate to a person because what remains is a design or policy choice rather than a fact about the proposal. Each step should be justified by what the paradoxes actually establish rather than by a general suspicion of self-reference, so that a reviewer can explain to an author why the step is there. Keep it runnable in about fifteen minutes per proposal, and mark plainly which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: a collection defined as the collection of all collections that do not contain themselves; a collection defined as the collection of all collections that contain themselves; and a collection introduced in the recursive, least-closed-collection style, defined as the collection of all objects reachable in finitely many steps from a given seed under a given operation. Include at least one proposal that the procedure rejects although it is harmless and useful, so that I can see what the procedure costs us, and say what a reviewer should do when that happens.

Close with what the procedure cannot settle, which failures would show it is wrong, and which single step is doing most of the work.

For calibration: roughly forty proposals a week, the reviewers are data engineers with no logic background, a bad acceptance can silently corrupt datasets that other teams treat as ground truth, and any change to the platform's rules has to be signed off by me and the platform architect.
