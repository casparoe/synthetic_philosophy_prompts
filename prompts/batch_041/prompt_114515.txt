I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on an essay about why very old puzzles of naming, predication, and reference keep returning in contemporary debates about language and mind. A philosopher friend, knowing I was trying to explain the School of Names to a general readership, sent me the following exchange from the White Horse Dialogue, in a translation she had at hand, and I've been unable to give a clean account of what the Advocate is actually entitled to conclude:

> "Can 'a white horse is not a horse' be asserted?"
> [Advocate] "It can."
> [Objector] "How so?"
> [Advocate] "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape. Therefore I say: a white horse is not a horse."
> [Objector] "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. If one cannot say there is no horse, does that not mean there is a horse? Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, how can the white [one] not be a horse?"

I can see the surface move: horse picks out shape, white picks out color, and a color-designator is not a shape-designator. But I cannot see how that licenses the conclusion that a white horse is not a horse, rather than merely the conclusion that white is not horse or that a white horse is not just a horse. The Objector's reply seems to be making the obvious point that white horses are horses, yet the Advocate treats it as a confusion. What I'd like is help understanding the passage on its own terms: what distinction is the Advocate drawing between the two terms, how does the Objector's counterargument misunderstand or shift that distinction, and what should I take away about the difference between identity, predication, and class membership in this kind of classical Chinese argument? I don't need a reading list or citations; I need a clear, step-by-step reconstruction that I can use to explain the exchange to a general reader without flattening it into a mere word game.
