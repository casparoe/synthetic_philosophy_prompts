I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of art seminar, and I've been asked to write a short memo adjudicating an argument that two of my classmates had during a session on AI image generators. I have my own leanings, but I'd rather hand in something that reasons through the exchange rather than simply picks a winner, so I'd like you to do the adjudication: go move by move, say what each of them gets right, what each overstates, and where the disagreement is really located. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Maya argued, first, that a system trained to predict the next fragment of an image is doing interpolation within a learned distribution, and that interpolation is not creativity: creativity requires that the maker could have done otherwise and held herself to a standard she might have missed, so that she could have been wrong. The model, she said, has no stake in the outcome and no standard it could fail, so it can produce novel and even beautiful images, but "creative" is misapplied to the process, and the products inherit the misapplication. Second, she conceded that the human prompter exercises real judgment — choosing, discarding, refining — but said that relocating the creativity to the prompter concedes her point: a person using a tool is creative, the tool is not. And since the prompter cannot foresee the specific features of the output, she cannot be said to have authored them; she is closer to a client who commissions a portrait and is surprised by it. Third, she added a ceiling claim: however sophisticated the prompting, the system's originality is bounded by its training distribution, so it can at best explore a space it did not choose, never redraw the space itself.

Dev replied, first, that this is a romantic picture of creativity. We attribute creativity to products relative to a domain and a history — something is creative when it is new and it matters — and most human making is also recombination of what came before. If the test is "a standard you might have missed," he said, you will find that most human makers are following a groove too, and the AI case is being used to smuggle in a demand that human creators do not actually meet. Second, the prompter is genuinely in the loop and the skill is real and learnable; iterating on prompts, judging and discarding results, is more like a photographer framing a shot than like pulling a lever. Third, unforeseen does not mean unowned: painters exploit accidents, composers have used chance procedures precisely so as not to foresee the result and we still count the result as theirs, and there is a long tradition of artworks specified by instructions and executed by others, so authorship can be detached from execution. He also denied that the space is fixed: the human's choices plus the system's sampling generate combinations outside the training set, and the line between exploring a space and transforming it is not as sharp as Maya needs it to be.

Maya came back on that last point: even granting that some outputs fall outside the training set, "outside the set" is not the same as "chosen against a standard," and a system that cannot represent what it is failing at cannot be credited with getting it right. Dev's chance-procedure example, she said, actually supports her, because the composer chose the procedure against a live alternative and understood the space he was refusing; the prompter is not in that position with respect to the model's internals. Dev answered that understanding the internals is not required for authorship — a painter need not understand the chemistry of drying oil — and that the prompter does choose against alternatives, namely the prompts she doesn't send and the images she throws away. They stopped there, and the seminar never settled it.

What I want from you is a genuine audit rather than a verdict on the vibe. For each of the roughly six moves above, tell me whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — flagging especially any place where one of them is quietly shifting between creativity as a property of a process, of a product, and of a person, or where a point that is true of the model is being transferred to the human or vice versa. Then tell me what both of them are missing: is there a distinction that would let the exchange be resolved rather than just continued, or is the dispute at bottom a disagreement about what the word is for? Finally, if you had to say which of them has the stronger overall position, say so and say how confident you are, and note anything that would change your mind.
