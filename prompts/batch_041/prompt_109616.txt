I am not a philosopher and have no academic training. I have simply been trying to think through a question on my own, and I have gotten stuck on an old text.

The question is what we mean when we say that some part of a living thing is "for" something. As far as I can tell, there are two quite different ideas that people run together. The first is the job a part does: a heart is for pumping because pumping is the work it performs, and one can establish that simply by watching it. The second is the reason the part is there at all: a heart is for pumping because the ancestors that pumped are the ones that left descendants, which is why hearts of this kind are present rather than absent. The first is a fact about what a thing does now; the second is a fact about why it exists. It seems to me that a part could satisfy one of these without the other, and that a great deal of confusion comes from not saying which one is meant.

I have been reading an old English translation of Aristotle's Physics, Book II (the translation is by Hardie and Gaye), and I cannot make out which of these two ideas, if either, the passage is using. Here is the part that stops me:

"A difficulty presents itself: why should not nature work, not for the sake of something, nor because it is better so, but just as the sky rains, not in order to make the corn grow, but of necessity? What is drawn up must cool, and what has been cooled must become water and descend, the result of this being that the corn grows. Similarly if a man's crop is spoiled on the threshing-floor, the rain did not fall for the sake of this — in order that the crop might be spoiled — but that result just followed. Why then should it not be the same with the parts in nature, e.g. that our teeth should come up of necessity — the front teeth sharp, fitted for tearing, the molars broad and useful for grinding down the food — since they did not arise for this end, but it was merely a coincident result; and so with all other parts in which we suppose that there is purpose? Wherever then all the parts came about just what they would have been if they had come to be for an end, such things survived, being organized spontaneously in a fitting way; whereas those which grew otherwise perished and continue to perish, as Empedocles says his 'man-faced ox-progeny' did. Such are the arguments (and others of the kind) which may cause difficulty on this point. Yet it is impossible that this should be the true view. For teeth and all other natural things either invariably or normally come about in a given way; but of not one of the results of chance or spontaneity is this true. We do not ascribe to chance or mere coincidence the frequency of rain in winter, but frequent rain in summer we do; nor heat in the dog-days, but only if we have it in winter. If then, it is agreed that things are either the result of coincidence or for an end, and these cannot be the result of coincidence or spontaneity, it follows that they must be for an end."

To make my two ideas concrete: a scar on my knee is there because of a fall, but nobody would say it is for anything. Goosebumps, by contrast, I understand once raised the fur of our ancestors, though they do nothing for me now. My two ideas pull apart on a case like that, and I want to know which idea the passage is working with.
