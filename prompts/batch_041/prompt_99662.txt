I have been trying to state, for myself, when a word that only one person could ever apply is a genuine word and when it is merely a noise that person makes in private, and I want to know what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts. The private language argument is the obvious place to look, but my intuitions do not line up with the standard moral of that story, so I would rather work from cases.

1. The diarist. A man resolves to write S in a notebook whenever a certain sensation strikes him, and otherwise to say nothing. He never compares one occurrence with another, never uses S to anticipate anything, and never finds himself wondering whether this time it really was an S. My verdict: S is not a word here. He is performing a ceremony, not saying something that could be true or false.

2. The taster. A wine taster uses flinty for a taste he cannot show anyone. Others use the word as well, disagree with him, and over the years correct him; he is expected to defer to the judgment of the trade about which wines are flinty. Verdict: plainly a real word.

3. The aura. A migraine sufferer records aura for a visual disturbance only she experiences. It is private, but it reliably precedes a headache that others can partly observe, and her neurologist has told her she has been misusing the word for a different visual event that is not an aura. Verdict: a real word.

4. The stroke patient. After a stroke a woman has a sensation no one else has ever reported, and she coins brim for it. She cannot check the word against anyone, and no instrument can detect the sensation. But brim turns out to anticipate, several minutes in advance, a numbness in her left hand that anyone can see; when the numbness fails to arrive she marks the entry as a false alarm, and she has taught herself to distinguish brim from a similar sensation that does not predict the numbness. Verdict: a real word, and I am fairly confident of this.

5. The itch. A man coins gloop for a private itch. He uses it only when alone, never acts on it, never distinguishes it from nearby itches, and would not notice if he applied it differently tomorrow. Verdict: not a word, the same as the diarist.

6. The machine. A system is built whose internal states include one that no external instrument can yet read, though the system itself can. It coins a token for that state and uses the token to route its own processing, slowing down when the state is present, and it revises the token's use when the routing misfires. Verdict: I want to say the token is meaningful, but I am noticeably less sure here than in case 4, and I cannot say why.

7. The inversion. A man's colour experience is inverted relative to everyone else's, so that what he sees when looking at grass is what the rest of us see when looking at blood. He uses green exactly as we do and is corrected by us exactly as we would be. Verdict: whatever is going on, his words are meaningful and mean what ours mean; the inversion, if it exists, is not something his language can express.

What troubles me is this. Cases 1 and 4 look alike in every feature I can name — a private sensation, a self-issued name, no second party able to check it — yet I give them opposite verdicts. Cases 3 and 4 both lean on a public event downstream of the sensation, which suggests the principle is that a private word is real only if it answers to something public. But then case 6 should be no word at all, and I resist that. Case 2 suggests instead that a private word is real only if other people can correct you, which would sink case 4, where no one can. And case 7 suggests that the privacy of the sensation is irrelevant, that only the public practice around the word matters — which would make case 1 a word after all if the man merely kept his notebook in public.

I would like the principle, or a small set of rival principles, that best captures these verdicts, with a note for each clause saying which case forces it. Then apply the winning principle to two cases I have not given you: (a) a woman who invents a word for a recurring mood, uses it to decide whether to go out, never corrects it against anything, and turns out to be right about the mood's arrival more often than chance; and (b) a pair of people who share a sensation no one else has, use a word for it, and correct each other. Finally, tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot keep, and whether you would give that verdict up or add a clause to save it.
