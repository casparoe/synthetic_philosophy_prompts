I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and I need to produce a short research proposal — roughly 1,500 words — for my department's work-in-progress workshop. The audience will be mostly philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who work on consciousness but not on personal identity or the metaphysics of implementation, so the proposal has to stand on its own without my being in the room to fill gaps. I want to use the slot to attack an argument I keep running into and half-believe, in roughly this form: take a person and replace her neurons one at a time with silicon devices that do exactly the same thing; behaviour is preserved at every stage, it would be absurd to think that experience flickers off and on as each chip is installed, so experience must be preserved throughout, and hence what matters is the program rather than the tissue. A friend of mine who works in machine learning thinks this is obviously right and that only sentimentalists resist it, which is part of why I want to get it straight. What I cannot get to fit together are three worries, and I suspect that once they are separated the argument either proves much less than advertised or turns on a premise nobody has actually stated.

The first is a decomposition worry. "The device that does exactly the same thing" presupposes that a component has an input–output profile specifiable independently of the rest of the system; in a densely recurrent system — which is what cortex looks like — a unit's contribution is fixed only relative to a way of carving the system up, and if we are permitted to specify the replacement's output as whatever the original unit would have produced given the current global state, we have not specified a component at all but a placeholder for the whole. The second is a limit worry. Grant that some notion of component-equivalence is well defined at each finite stage; the specification may nonetheless be anchored in the surviving biological tissue, since each chip's job is characterized by reference to what the biological unit would have done. As the biological remainder shrinks, that characterization loses its anchor, and equivalence at stage n need not survive to the limit, so the conclusion about the fully silicon system does not follow from premises about the intermediate stages. The third is a hidden-state worry. If biological units carry internal states not recoverable from their input–output behaviour over any finite interval — adaptation, fatigue, continuous dynamics — then sameness of inputs and outputs is only ever equivalence over a window, and a replacement matched on that window is not functionally equivalent. The premise then either becomes unverifiable in principle or must be weakened to behavioural equivalence, which is precisely what a sceptic about the silicon brain already grants without conceding anything about experience.

Please turn this into a proposal with a single precise question at its centre, one that has a determinate answer rather than being a theme, and explain why the existing positions on the replacement argument leave that question open, which of my three worries is doing real work and which is a red herring, and what approach would actually settle it: what I would do to the argument and in what order — reconstruct it formally, build a toy model of a replacement sequence, test it against a worked case — laid out week by week over one semester, since I have ten weeks and no empirical work of my own. I would like the proposal to say what a successful outcome looks like (a repaired version of the argument, a demonstration that it fails at a specific premise, or a diagnosis of why the question is ill-posed) and what would count as the project failing, including the real possibility that I end up having only clarified the terms. Please do not include a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I will add the references myself, and what I need from you is the framing and the plan, together with an honest assessment of whether this is a semester-sized project or a dissertation-sized one.
