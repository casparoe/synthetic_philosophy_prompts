I was at a lab dinner last week and two of my friends went at each other for most of it, and I've been chewing on it since. I'm a grad student in a bio department; Dan is in the same department and works on insect behaviour, Priya is a philosopher who hangs around us because she does philosophy of biology. Here's how it went.

Round 1. Dan is describing this beetle he works on. There's a wing vein that in the ancestral species just stiffens the wing. In his species the males scrape it against a ridge on the thorax and it makes a little drumming noise, and females use the noise to find them. Knock out the gene and the wing is totally fine, but the drumming is gone and the males basically never mate. Dan says: so the drumming is now the function of that vein, and the stiffening is a leftover, because selection is currently maintaining the vein for drumming.

Round 2. Priya says that's a story, not a fact. "Currently maintaining" is doing all the work, she says, and you can't observe it, you're inferring it from the very thing you're trying to explain -- the vein does drumming, therefore drumming is its function, therefore selection maintains it for drumming. She says the cleaner way to talk is that a function is just a role some part plays in some capacity of the system that you're interested in explaining. If you're explaining wing stiffness the vein's function is stiffening; if you're explaining mate-finding its function is drumming. Both true, no history needed, no fact of the matter about which one it "really" is.

Round 3. Dan says fine, then "the function of the heart is to make noise" is true whenever I feel like studying acoustics, and the whole point of saying a stopped heart is malfunctioning goes up in smoke. He says normativity is the test case: a malformed heart is a broken heart, not just a heart that isn't doing the thing I happen to care about, and the only way to get a broken heart is to have something the heart was supposed to do. Priya says the supposed-to is smuggled in from your explanatory interest -- you picked pumping as the capacity and then acted like the picking was done for you. She also says his view has a worse consequence: a brand new trait, arose by drift a hundred generations ago, happens to be keeping the population alive right now, has no function yet on his view, which she thinks is absurd. Dan says on her view a rock in a stream has the function of channeling water as soon as somebody's interested in erosion, and that can't be right either.

Round 4. Dan says the drift trait has a function once selection has acted on it, and "not yet" isn't absurd, it's just the truth about a trait that hasn't been tested. He says the real absurdity is on her side, where a sponge's spicules and the noise my car engine makes and the drumming vein are all functions on a par, differing only in what somebody wants to explain. Priya says that's exactly right and not absurd at all, and adds that Dan can't even state his own view without her notion: to say the vein's function is drumming you first have to identify the vein as a trait, and the only way to carve a beetle into traits is by reference to roles things play in capacities we care about, so his account presupposes hers. Dan says trait individuation is a real problem but it's a problem for everybody, including her, because she needs to know what counts as a "capacity" and that isn't given by nature either.

Can you go through this move by move -- each round, and within each round each separate claim they make, including the sub-claims -- and say who's right about what? Rate each move the way a referee would, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification. Then the big question: is any of this just two people using one word for two things? If so, which part is verbal and which part is a real disagreement, and does the verbal part being verbal mean one of them should just shut up? And what are they both missing -- is there a position that keeps the normativity Dan wants without the history Priya is suspicious of, or one that keeps Priya's interest-relativity without making the heart-makes-noise answer sound as good as the pumping answer? I dont want a winner declared and left at that, I want the scorecard, including the bits where they're both being wierd.
