I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading my way into AI-safety theory on my own time. I keep circling back to an idealization that goes by the name HCH, as I've seen it presented: you take an ordinary human, and for any question you let them break it into subquestions, hand each subquestion to another copy of themselves with the same ability (so the copy can itself recurse in the same way), and then stitch the sub-answers into an answer to the original question. The intended reading, as I understand it, is that HCH is what a person could in principle arrive at given unlimited time and unlimited copies of themselves, with each copy still finite and fallible in the ordinary human way. It is usually paired with a claim sometimes called the factored cognition hypothesis, roughly that any task a sufficiently capable system can perform can be split into subtasks small enough for a bounded agent to handle, with the overall result assembled from the parts. As someone who writes recursive functions for a living, this reads to me like a recursion whose base case is an unassisted human judgement and whose return values are natural-language summaries passed between parent and child. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of mind who also knows this literature well, think carefully before answering, and explain to me as intuitively as you can, without formalism, what the central conceptual problem is with treating HCH as an idealization of deliberation.

What I'm after is not a survey of every objection but a judgement about which one is load-bearing. The candidate worries I keep running into are these: that the recursion has to bottom out in the unamplified human, so the idealization silently inherits whatever the base human can and cannot do; that the aggregation step, where a parent combines its children's answers, is itself a cognitive task of the same kind, so it's unclear whether the idealization is circular or merely regresses; that because a child has to compress its findings into a summary of bounded length, the idealization presupposes something like a good-enough compression of thought, which may be exactly the hard part; and that different ways of carving up the same question may yield different answers, in which case what the human would conclude after unlimited consultation may not be a determinate fact at all, and the idealization would have no well-defined limit to converge on. Please tell me which of these, if any, is the real difficulty and why the others are comparatively superficial, and help me see whether the problem is an artifact of how the setup happens to be described or would afflict any attempt to idealize deliberation in this style — the way an ideal observer or a fully rational agent is used in ethics, or the way a logically omniscient reasoner is used in epistemology. If the worry does turn out to be serious, I'd also like your view on whether HCH can still do useful work as a scaffold or a target even if it isn't a coherent ideal, or whether that is just wishful thinking.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. You may think on the page at the start if that helps you sort out which worries are genuinely distinct before committing to a view, but I'd rather have your considered judgement than an exhaustive catalogue.
