I have spent the past year making the decisions about my mother's care, and my brother, who is finishing a doctorate in ethics, keeps pressing me to think about it from what he calls a fairness perspective — as though my attachment to her were one consideration among others to be weighed. When I said that Bernard Williams had something to say about exactly this, he told me I had it from a popularization that makes the objection cruder than it is. The popularization runs roughly as follows: Williams asks us to imagine a man who must choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger from drowning; the utilitarian says the man may save his wife, but only once he has seen that her being his wife is one consideration among many, and that saving her is permitted because the general welfare is served by people being attached to their own families; Williams replies that this is one thought too many, and that the man's motivating thought should simply be that this is his wife. The author concludes that utilitarianism requires the agent to be impartial at the moment of action, that it therefore cannot be lived and is refuted, and that Williams's integrity objection — so called because the theory splits the agent's feelings from his reasons and destroys his psychological wholeness — generalizes to every moral theory that asks us to adopt an impartial point of view, all of which are thereby shown to be alienating.

I would like an accuracy audit I can act on: which parts a careful reader of Williams should accept, which are standard simplifications, and which Williams himself would have denied; and whether the author's concluding claims — refutation, and the generalization to all impartial theories — survive once the misreadings are corrected. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion, and in the fifth item say what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would make you reassess the summary, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
