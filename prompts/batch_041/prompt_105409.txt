I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist before retiring, and I have spent the years since reading philosophy on my own, without any institutional guidance, so I know the biology of animal signalling far better than I know the philosophy of it and would rather you tell me plainly where I have hold of it badly than flatter the framing I bring. I now chair the selection panel of a foundation that funds high-risk, long-horizon research. We meet twice a year and award roughly ten fellowships from a pool of two hundred applicants; the decisions shape careers; my reviewers come from several disciplines and none of them can competently assess every candidate's technical work; and we have one day in which to reach defensible conclusions. What I want from you is a decision procedure — a sequence of tests a reviewer can actually run on a file — for judging whether the costly things a candidate puts forward are honest evidence of the capacity we care about, namely the ability to sustain self-directed, risky work over years, rather than merely costly noise.

Let me set out what I have collected, because the translation is the hard part and I may be holding it badly. The handicap principle, as I learned it in behavioural ecology — the peacock's tail, the gazelle that stots in full view of the lion — says a signal can be believed because it is costly. But everything I have read since insists that cost is not the operative thing: what does the work is that the cost be borne differentially, falling harder on the low-quality signaller than on the high-quality one, since a cost that everyone in the pool can equally afford transmits nothing. I have also met a distinction between a signal that is honest because it wastes resources (a handicap) and one that is honest because it is causally bound to the quality it indicates (an index), and it seems the two require different tests. I am further told that at evolutionary equilibrium a handicap need involve no actual waste at all, which would make looking for the waste precisely the wrong instruction, and that where interests align, or where the trait is directly measurable, quite cheap signals can be perfectly honest, while in some settings the strongest candidates signal least.

Turn this into something my panel can run. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure must stop and hand the file to a person because what remains is a judgement about which trait we value rather than a fact about signalling. Decide for me which of the distinctions above must survive the simplification and which I can drop without betraying the theory, and be candid about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three files: a candidate whose decade of remote fieldwork I confess I find moving and whom I expect the procedure to embarrass me by declining to reward; a candidate whose expensive credential is held by nearly everyone in the pool; and one you construct yourself in order to expose a failure of the procedure. Close with what the procedure cannot do.

Keep what is settled among people who study signalling apart from what is genuinely contested — I do not want a contested reading handed to me as the consensus. And open with the single test that decides the largest share of our files, in one sentence, before laying out the rest; no preamble, and no restating of my question.
