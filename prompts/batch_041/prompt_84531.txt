I chair the standing classification committee of a national health agency. Each year we receive roughly fifteen petitions to add a condition to the schedule of recognized conditions; inclusion obliges the agency to develop a clinical guideline and to fund treatment, and our decisions are published and routinely contested. The committee has nine members — clinicians, an epidemiologist, a health economist, a patient representative, and me — and we sit for a single afternoon per batch of petitions. None of us is trained in the philosophy of medicine, and we have begun to contradict ourselves: we have admitted conditions on the strength of a biomarker and refused others diagnosed by reported symptoms alone, without any account of why the two were treated differently.

I want a decision procedure we can actually run, as a checklist we can keep in front of us. Give me the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops with a refusal; and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person, because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should say what to do when the evidence is missing or contested, which is most of the time, and whether the burden of proof runs against inclusion or in its favor. Ground it in what the philosophy of medicine actually knows about the concepts of health and disease, in particular the dispute between those who hold that disease is a value-free matter of biological malfunction and those who hold that harm is constitutive of it, so that our recurring disagreements are located in the procedure rather than left to whoever speaks last. One member has proposed that we ask, of every petition, whether the condition would be a misfortune for the person under any social arrangement; tell me whether that test does any work, where it fails, and what should replace it. The agency's chief medical officer ratifies our decisions and may send a case back once, so the procedure should be defensible to her as well as to the petitioners.

Then run it on three petitions: a genetic variant that produces a measurable abnormality but is currently asymptomatic and has no treatment shown to alter outcomes; a chronic condition diagnosed by reported symptoms alone, with no identified lesion or biomarker, whose petitioners describe severe impairment; and a condition whose impairment, on the petitioners' own account, exists only relative to a particular social arrangement, so that the disability lies in the environment rather than in the body. Include the case whose verdict will be unwelcome to us — we are under budget pressure and the chair is skeptical of expanding the schedule — and tell me why we should accept it anyway. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do, and which of the three it cannot settle.
