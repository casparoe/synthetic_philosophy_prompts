I'm in a second-year metaphysics course and I've been trying to make sence of the lecture we had last week on Black's spheres. The lecturer is good but she compresses hard, and I know from the essays she's marked that she rewards people for cutting to the chase, so before I build an essay on this I want to check how much of what I wrote down I should actually trust. Here's my reconstruction, more or less in her words. Leibniz's Law comes in two halves: if two things are identical they have all their properties in common, and if they have all their properties in common they're identical, and the second half — the identity of indiscernibles — is the one Black is after. His argument asks you to imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, two miles apart, hanging in otherwise empty space; they match in every respect, same size, same mass, same colour, same composition, so nothing distinguishes them, and yet there are obviously two of them, so the principle is false. The only defence left to a Leibnizian is to say the spheres differ relationally, since each is two miles from the other, and that fails because a relation that runs both ways can't distinguish anything. Black's conclusion is that the distinctness of the two spheres is really a matter of how we describe them, that there's no fact of the matter about which one is which; and since the two-sphere world has no reason to be arranged one way rather than its mirror image, the example takes down the principle of sufficient reason along with the identity of indiscernibles. She added that the whole puzzle evaporates if you're a relationalist about space, because then the spheres aren't at different places in the first place, and that the modern concensus is that Black settled the matter and Leibniz was simply wrong.
