I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, midway through a chapter on scientific realism, and over the break I've been reading a popular trade-press book on scientific method — the kind of overview my undergraduates actually finish — to see how the debates I work on get packaged for a general audience. One paragraph in the chapter on scientific progress has been bothering me, and I'd like a careful audit of it. The author's line runs roughly as follows. Popper's great contribution, he says, was to replace the vague idea that science aims at truth with an exact one: since most of our best theories are strictly false, Popper proposed to rank theories by their verisimilitude — roughly, the stock of true consequences a theory delivers, set against the stock of false ones — and to identify scientific progress with the replacement of one theory by a rival of higher verisimilitude. The programme then collapsed, the author reports, when Tichý and Miller showed in the 1970s that Popper's definitions have a fatal consequence: on those definitions no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other, so the very notion of getting nearer the truth, on which Popper's philosophy of science ultimately rests, is incoherent. Popper, we're told, conceded the technical point immediately but never abandoned the underlying idea, and the author's verdict is that nothing has been repaired in the half-century since: later attempts to define truthlikeness either run into the same difficulty or quietly reduce a theory's closeness to the truth to its degree of confirmation, which amounts to saying that the theory for which we have the most evidence is by definition the one nearest the truth. As an aside he adds that for quantitative theories the idea is straightforward anyway — a theory is closer to the truth the closer its predicted values come to the true ones — which, he says, is all that anyone has ever meant by calling a mature theory approximately true. The paragraph closes with the suggestion that the realist's appeal to the approximate truth of mature theories was always a metaphor, and that the verisimilitude episode shows it was never anything more.

Is that a fair summary?
