"Benacerraf's dilemma comes from his 1973 paper 'Mathematical Truth.' The setup is that a satisfactory account of mathematics has to do two things at once. It has to give a semantics on which mathematical sentences mean what they seem to mean — 'there are infinitely many primes' has to be a genuine statement about a subject matter, true or false in the ordinary Tarskian way, not a disguised rule or a useful fiction. And it has to give an epistemology on which we can actually know such sentences. The tension is that the most natural semantics makes mathematical sentences refer to abstract objects, namely numbers, that exist independently of us, while the most natural epistemology of reference is causal: you know about a thing because it causally impinges on you, the way the rocks in my garden send light into my eyes. Abstract objects are outside space and time and stand in no causal relations, so on a causal theory of knowledge we could never be in epistemic contact with them. So we face a choice: keep the standard semantics and lose the epistemology, or keep the causal epistemology and saddle mathematics with a non-standard semantics on which its sentences don't really state truths about objects at all, which sells out what mathematicians take themselves to be doing. That's the dilemma — the semantic horn and the epistemological horn.

Benacerraf's own way out was structuralism. In his earlier work he points out that you can reduce the natural numbers to sets in at least two ways — Zermelo's and von Neumann's — which agree on the Peano axioms but disagree about which set each number is. Since both are equally good, there's no fact of the matter about which objects the numbers are, and so arithmetic cannot be about any particular objects; it is about the structure that all the candidate reductions share. This is supposed to defuse the dilemma at the epistemological horn, because if mathematical statements are about structure rather than about a realm of abstract objects, then the causal constraint simply doesn't get a grip: we don't need a causal channel to a structure, since a structure is specified by its axioms and is known by deduction from them.

The obvious objection is that this only relocates the problem. Structures are themselves abstract objects, so if the causal theory blocks knowledge of abstract objects it blocks knowledge of structures too, and nothing has been gained. The structuralist can reply that knowledge of a structure just is knowledge of the relations the axioms specify, which is not the sort of thing that needs a causal channel. There's also the indispensability argument, which Benacerraf gives as a further reason to think numbers exist, since we can't do our best science without quantifying over them."
