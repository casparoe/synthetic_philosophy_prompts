I teach a 300-level seminar on inference and justification, eighteen students, and I have one 90-minute session to give to the regress in Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." I want the students to run into the regress with their hands rather than hear it described: they should spend twenty minutes trying to earn a conclusion, fail, and only afterwards be told that a Victorian logician staged exactly their failure. Design the session. They should not hear Carroll's name, or the name of the puzzle, until the debrief.

The activity I have in mind, which you should feel free to rebuild if you can make it cleaner, is a licensing office. Split the room into houses of four. Each house must get a single target proposition entered in a ledger by a Clerk. Use trivial, sensorily checkable targets, so that nobody can doubt the content and all attention goes to the form; something like "the lamp is on" plus "if the lamp is on, the switch is up," with the conclusion to be entered. One student per house plays the Clerk from a script; you circulate and play Clerk yourself for any house that loses its actor.

Round one, the ordinary exchange: the house hands over its two premises, the Clerk enters the conclusion without a question, and the house banks its tokens. Round two, the audit: fresh target, same form, but now the Clerk enters a proposition only if the house designates it as an asserted premise, or spends a token to exhibit a licensed step, and whenever the house asks for the conclusion the Clerk asks one scripted question, "What requires me to enter it?" The house gets eight blank premise cards and a hard cap of eight minutes. When they offer the conditional as an extra premise, the Clerk enters it, thanks them, and asks again. Round three: the house may do anything except assert a further premise, and has three tokens; the only thing that scores is getting the conclusion entered. The moves that work are the ones that change what the Clerk does rather than what the Clerk believes — asking the Clerk to perform the step rather than justify it, pointing out that the Clerk did exactly this in round one without being asked, handing over a rule of action rather than a proposition. If a house tries a disguised premise, the Clerk enters it and asks again.

Give me the materials, the Clerk's script with the two or three lines that matter and a note on how to deliver them flatly rather than smugly, a payoff scheme that needs no money, and a timing breakdown that leaves at least 25 minutes for the debrief.

The debrief is where this either lands or doesn't. Map the moments onto the parts of the puzzle: premise cards onto propositions, the conditionals the house keeps adding onto the tortoise's willingness to grant each new hypothetical, the Clerk's repeated question onto the demand for a premise that forces the step, round one onto the rule that was already in force before anyone asked it to justify itself, and the winning move in round three onto the difference between a conditional and an inference. Then lay out the live options rather than a moral: Russell's distinction between implication and inference, the formal-system reply that treats the rule as a rule of the system rather than a premise within it, the Wittgensteinian line that learning to infer is learning to do something, and the deflationary reply that a creature who accepts the conditional but will not detach it has not really accepted it. Be explicit that the activity has staged the problem, not solved it.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I expect at least these: houses that burn all eight cards on conditionals and feel cheated rather than instructed; a house that tries to win by rhetorical pressure and a Clerk who either caves or turns pedantic, when the Clerk should stay boring and scrupulous; a house that declares the game rigged and stops, which is diagnostically useful and should be mined in the debrief rather than smoothed over; someone who reaches for truth tables or a formal system and finds that this too is entered as one more premise; and time loss, since the regress is absorbing and houses will not want to stop.

Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot do. The impasse here is with a scripted partner, so it makes the regress look like an interpersonal standoff, when the philosophical worry concerns a single reasoner with no interlocutor at all. The token budget makes it look like a resource problem, which it is not. And the activity cannot show that the regress is vicious rather than merely tedious, or that the tortoise is unreasonable, or that any of the available resolutions is correct; those I have to argue for myself, on the board, after the ledgers are closed.
