I'm putting together a short essay for a workshop on suffering-focused ethics, and I'd like your critical comments before I commit to the argument. The topic is suffering risks (s-risks): outcomes in which the future contains an enormous amount of suffering, whether or not humanity survives them. I haven't drafted the essay in full; below are the notes I plan to write from, followed by what I'd like you to comment on.

Thesis. The usual definition of an s-risk — an event that makes the long-term future substantially worse in terms of suffering — is implicitly comparative. But every natural comparison class makes the concept either collapse into an existential risk or turn into a claim about our own predictive uncertainty rather than about the world. I want to argue that s-risks are better defined as a feature of the whole distribution of possible futures, specifically the probability mass in the extreme upper tail of the suffering distribution.

1. The baseline problem. Worse than what? If the baseline is the future as we currently expect it to go, then whether something counts as an s-risk depends on our forecasts, and two people with different forecasts will disagree about whether the same event is an s-risk at all — which seems like the wrong kind of dependence for a category of outcome.

2. The collapse into x-risk. If the baseline is "no intelligent life ever," then an s-risk is just an existential risk seen from the other side — the future failing to go well rather than failing to exist — and the two categories stop doing separate work. If the baseline is "the best achievable future," then almost everything is an s-risk and the concept loses its grip.

3. The tail proposal. Define the s-risk of an outcome as the probability mass it places above some very high suffering threshold (equivalently, the integral of suffering over the far tail), rather than as a shift in the mean. On this view s-risks are about the shape of the risk distribution rather than its average, and "reducing s-risk" becomes a claim about risk-aversion with respect to suffering.

4. The uncomfortable tradeoff. If s-risk reduction is a tail property while ordinary optimization targets the mean, the two come apart: one intervention can lower the tail while raising expected suffering, and another can do the reverse. So "maximize expected value" does not entail "reduce s-risks," and in some cases the two actively conflict — which bears on how s-risk and x-risk work should be prioritized against each other. (I have a candidate example, but I want to know whether you think the general point is sound first.)

What I'd like from you: comments on (a) whether the "collapse" argument in 2 is a real problem or just a verbal trick with baselines; (b) whether the tail proposal in 3 is well-defined, or merely renames a familiar idea such as risk-aversion without adding anything; (c) whether the tradeoff in 4 is correct as stated; and (d) whether the essay, if it lands, would strike people working on this as interesting or as obvious.

Please be blunt. If the whole framing is confused — for instance, if "s-risk" is doing no work that a generic concern for tail risk doesn't already do — say so plainly rather than helping me polish it.
