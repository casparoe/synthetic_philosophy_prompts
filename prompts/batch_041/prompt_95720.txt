I am a novelist, most of the way through a book whose protagonist is a cognitive neuroscientist who, after a stroke, develops a rare condition in which she feels pain but is not troubled by it. Much of the novel's argument — she is the kind of scientist who turns her own case into a thesis — leans on a passage from a trade book on the neuroscience of emotion, the sort that opens with a case history and then generalizes from it. Before I build another hundred pages on that foundation, I want to know whether I have been leaning on a fair summary. The passage runs roughly as follows: "Pain asymbolia is a rare condition, usually caused by damage to the parietal or insular cortex, or by a surgical cut to the cingulate, in which patients lose the ability to feel pain — or rather, lose the ability to feel it as pain. What is remarkable is that the sensory machinery survives intact: these patients can still localize a pinprick and say which of two is sharper, and they still use the word 'pain' to describe what they feel. They are simply not distressed by it. Since the sensation is intact while the suffering is gone, the condition shows that pain and the badness of pain are two separable things: what we call the badness of pain is an emotional coloring that the brain adds on top of a neutral bodily signal, and once that coloring is stripped away there is nothing left in the sensation for anyone to object to. This is why the condition is widely taken to refute any view on which pain is intrinsically bad, and to support the view that suffering is a matter of how we interpret what our nerves report."

I would like an audit of that passage rather than a lecture on pain: go clause by clause and tell me which parts a philosopher of mind or a pain researcher would accept as fair, which are the simplifications a competent popularizer makes and a specialist would wince at, and which are simply wrong — and, above all, whether the last two sentences, about intrinsic badness and about interpretation, follow from anything true in the earlier ones. Three further things bear on this. My partner is a palliative-care nurse and objects that the standard clinical definition of pain builds unpleasantness into the definition, so that by the book these patients cannot be in pain at all; I cannot tell whether that is a substantive objection or a verbal quibble about how a word is used. A friend in the philosophy department mentioned that there is supposed to be a mirror-image phenomenon, suffering with no localizable pain, which he says would clinch the separability claim; I do not know what to make of that. And my protagonist's arc turns on a single inference — that if a creature could feel pain without minding it, it would have no reason to avoid the pain — which I now suspect may be doing more work than the evidence can bear. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict and single out the one point about which you are least sure; where specialists genuinely disagree, tell me what the disagreement turns on rather than collapsing it into a verdict, and if any part of my passage turns out to be a defensible reading rather than an error, say so.
