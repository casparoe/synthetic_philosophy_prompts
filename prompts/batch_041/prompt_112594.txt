I chair the exhibitions committee of a mid-sized municipal museum. We are signing off on a touring exhibition about global inequality that will open in four countries over the next two years, and I need a short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting.

The lead curator's design brief proposes that every quantitative panel in the exhibition use a single system of repeated pictograms, in the style of the pictorial statistics that Otto Neurath developed for the social and economic museum in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s and that later took the name Isotype: a fixed quantity is represented by one identical figure, repeated, rather than by a longer bar or a larger figure. The brief asks us to go further than Neurath in two respects. First, it would strip the panels of words, numerals, legends and keys, on the ground that a genuinely self-explanatory picture language needs no translation and can therefore travel unchanged between host countries. Second, it presents the pictogram set as culturally and politically neutral — the brief's own phrase is no words, no ideology — and uses that claim to justify a single uniform design across four very different national contexts, one of which has a live public controversy over how poverty statistics are presented.

Three objections are on the table. Our education team says the panels will be unreadable to anyone who cannot hold a stated unit value in mind, and that Neurath himself described his method as a language-like technique rather than a full language, one always shown alongside verbal elements — so the brief is not really Neurathian. A community advisory group says the generic human figure reads as belonging to a particular kind of person, and that an icon standing for one million people in poverty quietly settles who counts. The host venues, meanwhile, want the right to caption the panels locally and, in one case, to substitute local figures and clothing.

Draft the committee's written opinion: a clear recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with reasoning a curator could actually work with, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, namely the curator's case that a picture language which needs captions, keys and numerals in every venue has abandoned the one thing that made it worth using. Say what we should require of the design brief, who is accountable for meeting those requirements, and by when; the exhibition opens in fourteen months and the committee can convene again in three weeks. Argue from the material above; no sources or citations are needed.

End with a final line in exactly one of these forms: Verdict: APPROVE, Verdict: APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, or Verdict: REJECT.
