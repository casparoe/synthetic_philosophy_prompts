I run a small reading group on personal identity and material constitution, and the next session is on the ship of Theseus — specifically the version where someone keeps the discarded planks and rebuilds from them, so that two candidates for the original are standing side by side. I want to open with a poem that makes the group feel the difficulty in the reading rather than hear it summarized, and I want the poem's form, not its commentary, to carry the argument.

Write a poem whose text is rebuilt as it goes. Open with a short stanza; then let each following stanza be the previous stanza with exactly one word replaced — same syntax, same line breaks, one plank swapped at a time — so that someone reading straight through can watch the whole thing change and could, if they kept the record, reconstruct the first version from what was taken out. Let one line recur as a refrain, word-for-word identical each time, never altered by a character; it is the single part that is never replaced. Keep the stanzas short (two to four lines) and the poem long enough — a dozen or so stanzas — that most of the text has been swapped by the end. Free verse is fine; the replacement rule is the only constraint that matters.

Two things must do structural work. First, the refrain's wording never changes, but the surrounding planks must be arranged so that its sense is forced to shift as they change: sameness of form, difference of content. Second, at some point the poem should notice that its discarded words are still lying around and that a second ship could be built from them — two candidates, each with a claim — and it should let the difficulty of that moment stand.

Do not let the poem settle into 'it's the same because we call it the same' or 'there is no fact of the matter.' If it takes a side, let it take a specific one — that 'same' is always relative to a kind, so 'same ship,' 'same planks,' and 'same history' are different questions; or that the survivor is whichever candidate continues the original's history most closely rather than the one that shares its matter; or that the thing cannot survive the loss of any part at all — and let the reader feel what that costs. Render the ideas; name no philosophers, theories, or texts, and cite nothing.

Then, in a short prose note after the poem, tell me where the verse takes liberties with the ideas. Sort what is settled among people who work on the problem — that this is a question about criteria of identity over time, and the difference between being the same one and being exactly alike — from what is genuinely contested: whether the question has a determinate answer, whether identity is relative to a sortal, whether a thing survives the replacement of all its parts, and which of the two candidates is the original. Say which contested reading the poem leans on, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
