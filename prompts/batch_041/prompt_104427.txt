My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a piece of nineteenth-century mathematics, and only at the end did we realize that we had been circling the rule-following problem without ever naming it. I would like you to take the episode seriously and run it through the theory, in both directions.

Here is the episode as I understand it, going from memory. Euler's formula — vertices minus edges plus faces equals two — was long treated as a general truth about polyhedra. In the early nineteenth century, after Cauchy's attempted proof, geometers began turning up solids for which it fails: a torus-shaped "picture frame", a pair of nested cubes joined by a tunnel, and other solids with holes. What is striking is what the mathematical community did next. Rather than conclude that the formula was false, it kept the formula and narrowed the concept, adding conditions such as convexity or simple connectivity and redefining "polyhedron" so that the offending solids no longer counted. Imre Lakatos later made this history the centerpiece of his account of how mathematical concepts are patched up under the pressure of proof and refutation, and gave one of the moves involved a memorable name (monster-barring). If I have any of the history wrong, correct it silently and proceed.

Now the theory. Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, sometimes called Kripkenstein, begins from a skeptical paradox: any finite run of past uses is compatible with indefinitely many rules. Someone who has only ever added numbers below 57 has, in their past behaviour, no fact about them that distinguishes ordinary addition from a deviant rule — call it quus — that agrees with addition on everything they have ever done but returns 5 the moment either input reaches 57. There is no fact of the matter, on this view, that fixes which rule they were following. Kripke's Wittgenstein does not refute the paradox; he offers a skeptical solution, on which correctness is grounded not in a private grasp of a rule but in the agreement, correction, and shared dispositions of a community. That is the theory I want you to bring to bear.

I would like four things, and I care more about the discipline of the mapping than about completeness.

First, map the episode onto the paradox. Is "polyhedron", before the counterexamples, like "plus" before 57 — a rule whose future application was underdetermined by everything the community had done with it? When the community narrowed the definition, was it following the rule it had held all along, changing the rule, or is that question ill-formed on Kripkenstein's own terms?

Second, test the skeptical solution against the case. The solution says communal agreement is what fixes correctness. But here the community's verdict looks like legislation undertaken precisely to save a favoured result. Does the case vindicate the skeptical solution, embarrass it, or show that communal agreement is doing something the solution cannot describe — tracking a fact that was there all along, say?

Third, turn it around, because I think the episode cuts against the theory in a way that is easy to miss. The counterexamples did not stay monsters. The solids with holes were eventually absorbed into a new subject: the Euler characteristic became a genuine topological invariant, and the "failures" of the formula became informative features of surfaces of different genus. If the redefinition had merely been agreement, why should it have opened onto anything as robust as that? Say what this does to the skeptical solution, and whether it is a real anomaly or something the solution can absorb.

Fourth, tell me where the theory is silent. Do not make it speak where it has nothing to say: the case concerns the definition of a kind of object rather than a function on numbers, and the community's agreement is entangled with an appeal to mathematical necessity that the skeptical solution may have no right to. I would rather have the silences than a forced fit.

I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the analysis itself, and an honest verdict on whether the episode confirms the theory, refutes it, or is a case it never anticipated.
