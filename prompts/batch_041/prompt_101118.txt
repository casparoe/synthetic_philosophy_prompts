I'm a backend dev with no real philosophy or history training, but my friend Rosa is finishing a PhD in ancient history and I keep asking her weather legends are history. Here's what she's told me so far. Troy: she thinks there was probably a real war or at least a real destruction at the site people call Troy, and maybe some Hittite records line up, so she calls the war historical in outline, but the Iliad itself is mostly invented. King Arthur: she says maybe a real warleader or two got merged into the legend, but the Arthur we actually have is a magic-and-romance figure with no near-contemporary evidence, so she calls him a literary figure, not a historical one. Exodus: she says the Exodus as described didn't happen, no archaeological trace of a mass migration of that size at the traditional time, and the miracle stuff is central, so it's founding myth, not history, though she'll allow some smaller group of people escaped Egypt and the story grew. She also says the Trojan Horse is pure story even though she thinks there was a war.

Now a new one. A museum I volunteer at just got a bunch of tablets from a dig on a made-up island called Veyra. The story on them: about 900 years ago a king named Oren ruled a rich island city. A rival king attacked, the city burned, Oren was killed, and his daughter escaped by sea. The story also says a sea serpent rose up and destroyed the enemy fleet as punishment from the gods. The earliest version we have is a poem written about 400 years after the events it describes. Archaeologists found a burned palace layer from roughly the right period, bronze ingots, and signs of a violent destruction. No other written source mentions Oren or the war. Some local people still tell a version of the story.

What will Rosa say about whether Oren or the war is historical? I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule she seems to be running. I can see at least three rules that fit her earlier calls: one, independent evidence, if archaeology or a near-contemporary text backs the core, call it historical; two, source distance, if the earliest source is centuries later, it's legend, not history; three, no miracles, if the core requires gods or magic, it's myth. Which of these is she actually using, or is it some combo? What does each rule predict for Oren? Which of her earlier calls is the closest analog? Give me your best guess and the reasons for it. And tell me what other case would separate the rules, like a legend with a near-contemporary source but no archaeology, or archaeology but no plausible human core. Also, if her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would she be adopting with each choice?
