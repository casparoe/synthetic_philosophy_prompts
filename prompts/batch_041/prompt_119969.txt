I am a journalist who covers decision theory and artificial intelligence for a general-interest science magazine. I have been asked to run a 90-minute participatory session at an ideas festival for about 25 attendees, most of them educated laypeople with no formal background in decision theory. The session is meant to make them feel the force of two puzzles about choice under uncertainty, and I want the game to do the teaching before I say a word of theory. By the end, they should be able to state the two ways of reasoning in their own words and feel the pull of both, even if they cannot say which is right.

The first puzzle is this. A perfectly reliable predictor flips a fair coin. If the coin lands heads, it asks you to pay $10. If the coin lands tails, it gives you $1,000—but only if you would have paid in the heads case. You know all of this before the coin is flipped, and you know the predictor is never wrong. Should you pay when it asks? The second puzzle is this. The same predictor sends you a message if and only if either your house is infested with termites or you will pay it $1,000, but not both. A termite infestation would cost you $1,000,000. The predictor is never wrong. Should you pay? In both cases, one way of reasoning says your choice cannot causally affect what already happened or what the predictor has already done, so paying is pointless. Another way of reasoning says your choice is evidence about which world you are in, and paying is the better bet. I want participants to run into that clash themselves, not hear me describe it.

Design the activity. Give me the rules, the roles, the materials, the payoff scheme (no real money), and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. The game should put participants in a position where they have to choose, live with the consequence, and then discover that a different way of reasoning would have led them the other way. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the two puzzles and onto the difference between asking what an action causes and asking what it reveals. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, be honest about which feature of these puzzles the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief. Do not assume the participants know any decision theory terminology; the game should carry the weight.
