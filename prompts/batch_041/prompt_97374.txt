I write a fortnightly column on language and public life, and I'm in the middle of a piece about that familiar but slippery class of statements which are, strictly speaking, true yet are plainly meant to convey something the speaker can later disavow. One researcher I interviewed argued that these cases amount to a distinct communicative phenomenon rather than a mere lapse in honesty, and she drew a contrast with Grice. Her claim, roughly, is that Grice's machinery for conversational implicature presupposes a broadly cooperative speaker who intends his audience to recognize the intention behind the utterance, whereas the disavowable statement is engineered so that only part of the audience will take the intended point — or so that the point can be denied on the ground that it was never actually asserted. I'd like you to help me think this through before I commit to a line.

Please compare, as carefully as you can, Grice's account of speaker meaning and conversational implicature with the phenomenon of the covert or deniable signal as it has come to be discussed in the philosophical literature on dogwhistles. In what respects is the latter simply a special case of the former, and in what respects does it strain or exceed Grice's framework? I'm especially interested in three pressure points: whether the speaker must intend that his hearer recognize his intention (Grice's reflexive-intention condition); whether the message can be calculated from the conversational maxims in the way Grice supposed; and whether it can be cancelled without contradiction. Finally, tell me which of the two notions — Gricean speaker meaning or the deniable signal — you would treat as the more fundamental for explaining how such statements actually function in public life, and why.
