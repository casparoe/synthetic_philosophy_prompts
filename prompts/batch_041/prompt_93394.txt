I was up late argueing with my roommate about religion and politics -- she grew up in Germany, I grew up in Texas, and we kept using the same words for totally different things. I'm taking a course on church and state and I keep having reactions that don't seem to fit one theory. Here are my gut verdicts. A country having an established church, like England with the Church of England: I don't think that's inherently wrong, though I'd rather not have one. A church tax like Germany's, where you pay a percentage unless you formally leave: I think that's actually fine, maybe even good, because you can opt out and it funds hospitals and stuff. France banning religious symbols in public schools: I think the ban on headscarves is wrong, but I also think it's okay for public schools to teach about religion in a neutral way. The US motto about God on coins: I definately don't care, it seems harmless. A public school teacher leading a prayer: not fine, even if no one is forced to join. A town putting up a Nativity scene alone at city hall: not fine, but if it's one display among many, including a menorah and a secular thing, I think that's fine. A religious hospital getting government money to provide secular medical care: fine. A church getting a tax exemption for its sanctuary: fine. A church getting a tax exemption for a commercial parking lot it runs: not fine. And here's the one I keep going back and forth on: a state that funds religious schools, including ones that teach creationism in science class, as long as parents choose it and the schools meet some baseline secular curriculum. My gut says that's wrong, but I can't say why it's different from the hospital case.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? And another: a public university that has a state-funded chaplain available to students of all faiths and none, who mostly does counseling and grief support. Answer in American English, and don't just tell me what some famous philosopher said; I want the principle itself and how it handles the cases.
