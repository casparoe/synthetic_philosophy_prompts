I'm a journalist working on a long feature about whether moral dilemmas are real — the strong sense, where you're in the wrong no matter what you do and there's no move that gets you clean, not just "tough call." I've been going case by case on my gut and I keep makeing calls I can't reconcile, so I want you to tell me what principle is actually doing the work, and which of my calls it makes me drop.

Here's the pile.

A source hands a reporter a tape that would clear a man on death row. Turning it over gets the source killed. My gut: genuine dilemma. Whichever way she goes she's destroyed something she can't rebuild, and even after she picks, she still owes the other one — she should carry it forever.

An ER doc during a surge has one ventilator and two patients, a 19-year-old and a 60-year-old. My gut: not a dilemma. There's a right answer — triage by expected life-years, or whatever the protocol says — it just feels like hell. So the horror isn't the test.

Now put the doc's own daughter on one bed and a stranger on the other, same prognosis, and she's the only one on shift. My gut: genuine dilemma, full stop — and yeah, I know I just said triage has a right answer, so this one eats my rule.

A guy swore to his dying mother he'd never sell the family shop. His kid needs surgery only the sale can pay for. My gut: not a dilemma — the kid's health wins, do the surgery, apologize to a ghost. But I don't feel as clean about that as I feel about the vent case, and I can't say why.

The reporter one, by the way, collapses if it turns out she can publish the tape with the source relocated. Not a dilemma anymore, and I don't think that's cheating — if there was a door she didn't see, it was never a dilemma, she just hadn't looked. My editor says that's cheap, that there's almost always some third path you didn't think of, so nothing's ever a dilemma. I half believe him and half think he's missing the point.

The principle I keep reaching for is: it's a genuine dilemma when the two things in front of you are both unfixable and both land on a person — not expensive, unfixable. But that doesn't sort the vent case from the daughter case, does it, since both cost a life. And a friend says the whole thing is really just about whether "you ought to do A" and "you ought to do B" adds up to "you ought to do both" — that if it doesn't, dilemmas are possible and if it does, they're not. I don't know if that's the same question I'm asking or a different one, and I'd like that untangled too.

So: what's the principle, stated tight enough that I could put it in the piece? For each clause, tell me which of my cases put it there. Where does my "unfixable and lands on a person" formula need a patch — a clause about whose person it is, or what? Give me its verdicts on a case I haven't listed, and tell me straight which of my gut calls it makes me throw out.
