I run a small monthly salon on philosophy of mind — twelve or so regulars in the back room of a pub, mostly working scientists and engineers, no philosophy training, no reading in advance, one whiteboard, fifty minutes. Next month is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I want you to plan the session rather than write it. As I intend to present it: experiences are real and we really do have them, but they do not have the phenomenal properties that introspection reports them as having — the felt quality, the what-it-is-like, is itself a representation manufactured by introspection, and a misrepresentation at that. I don't want the usual tour of philosophical zombies, bats and Mary, and I don't want the hour spent adjudicating whether the view is true; I want the room to leave able to say exactly what the view claims and exactly what it does not. So: the dependency order (what must already be in place before the next piece can land, and why the order can't be shuffled); the single demonstration or case that carries the whole session and what it must have and lack — it has to be something they can run on themselves in the room, since nobody has read anything, and I want to know why the obvious candidates can't do the job; the misconceptions they will predictably form, with the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off; and the one question I ask at the end that tells me whether it landed.

Two things to build into the plan. First, the distinction between illusionism as a claim about the metaphysics of consciousness and illusionism as a claim about our concept of phenomenal consciousness — that the concept is confused, or that the term names nothing over and above the functional and access facts — and where in the sequence that distinction belongs, or whether it should be left out at this level and openly declared out of scope. Second, one of my regulars is a long-time meditator who will insist that if you look closely enough at experience you find bare awareness; I need to know where that objection arrives in the arc and how the sequence should have absorbed it before it does.

Assume they arrive believing that illusionism denies that anyone experiences anything, and that "illusion" here means something like a mirage, a mistake about the world; and assume that by the end the self-defeat worry (if introspection misrepresents, the evidence that it misrepresents is itself introspective) and the who-is-having-the-illusion worry will surface. Treat my list as a floor, not a ceiling: if this audience will form misconceptions I haven't anticipated, and those block comprehension, they belong in the plan — I would rather have the ones that actually block comprehension than the famous ones. Be committed: choose the order, choose the example, say why the rejected alternatives are worse, and don't hand me three options to pick from. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
