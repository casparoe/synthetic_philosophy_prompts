I've been reading about Carnap's politics lately—weird, I know—because I'm on the board of our housing co-op and we're about to vote on whether to let the planning committee use a new allocation algorithm. I have to give the opening pitch at the meeting next week, and I want to defend a Carnapian line: the algorithm can't tell us what counts as a fair allocation; that's a practical decision, not a theoretical one. But once we decide that, the algorithm can tell us how to get there. I'm presenting this to a room that's half data people (one of them keeps saying "just maximize utility, it's obvious") and half old-school co-op activists who are suspicious of anything that smells like technocracy. There are also a couple of people who remember the socialist calculation debate and will bring up Mises and Hayek. Prep me for the Q&A: what will they object to, in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging, with a two-minute reply for each, where I should concede, where I should hold, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. My sketch of the argument is: (1) Carnap thought theoretical questions are about truth, but practical decisions about what to do are not true or false—they're volitional; (2) choosing a planning framework (what counts as a cost, what counts as a benefit, whose preferences get aggregated) is a practical decision, not a theoretical one; (3) but that doesn't make it arbitrary—once the framework is chosen, there are objective facts about whether a plan achieves it, and we can rationally discuss means and consequences; (4) so the co-op should adopt a two-stage process: first a democratic vote on the framework, then expert planning within it. I want the prep sheet to take the audience's actual commitments seriously, not just give me generic philosophy-defense talking points.
