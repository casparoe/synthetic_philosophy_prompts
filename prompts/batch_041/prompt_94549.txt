I'm in a pretty informal game theory reading group with two friends, Priya and Marcus, and we've been stuck on the bargaining problem for about three weeks — two people who can produce something together if they agree on how to split it, each with a fallback if they don't. Priya is a Nash person: pick the split that maximizes the product of the two people's gains over their fallbacks. Marcus is a Kalai–Smorodinsky person: draw a straight line from the fallback point to the utopia point (each person's best possible payoff, given the other gets at least their fallback) and take where that line crosses the edge of what's actually achievable, so each person ends up the same fraction of the way from their fallback to their dream outcome. On a straight frontier they agree — if every extra dollar for me costs you exactly a dollar, both rules land on the same split — but as soon as the achievable set is curved they come apart. Take a case where the only undominated deals are (100,0), (50,75) and (0,100) plus all their mixtures, with (0,0) as the fallback: Priya's rule picks (50,75) and Marcus's picks (60,60). We have been yelling about that example for two hours and I'd like to see the arguement actually laid out.

So: a dialogue between the two of them, going at it properly. Priya's case is that hers is the only rule that's efficient and symmetric and doesn't let irrelevent alternatives matter — add a deal neither of them would ever take and the answer shouldn't budge, and if it does, something's broken. Marcus says that's the vice and not the virtue: his rule cares about the whole set because if I'm giving up a fantastic option in order to cooperate with you, that ought to show up in the split, and a rule that's blind to how much better either of us could have done is the arbitrary one. Priya's counter is that under his rule you can improve your share by dreaming up options you'd never use, and Marcus's counter is that under hers you can improve your share by wrecking the other person's outside options. I want them to actually test each other's objections on the example instead of trading slogans — I suspect some of the complaints stick to both rules, or to neither.

Beyond the example, get them into what each rule is really assuming about fairness — whether the fair thing is equal gains measured from where we'd land without a deal, or equal sacrifice measured against what we each could have had — and whether either rule needs us to compare how much utility one person gets with how much another gets, which a lot of people think is meaningless, and what it means that Marcus's benchmark is a point neither of them can actually reach. They should be equals: Marcus shouldn't be a mushy relativist and Priya shouldn't be a caricature of an axioms robot, and each should answer the other's actual point rather than the one they wish the other had made. It's fine if they don't settle it — I mostly want to know where the disagreement really lives. No citations or reading lists needed, I just want the argument.
