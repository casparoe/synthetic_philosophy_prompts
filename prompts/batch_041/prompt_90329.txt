Last term the reading group I sit in on discussed a paper arguing that the Ubuntu conception of personhood — the conception captured by the Nguni maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, usually translated "a person is a person through other persons" — sits badly with any robust notion of individual dissent. Two members of the group, both of whom I respect and neither of whom I felt competent to referee, spent the rest of the session arguing about it. What follows is my reconstruction of the exchange; I have tried to keep both of them at their best.

Nadia began: The maxim is not a piece of warm sentiment; it is a constitutive claim. It says that the relations make someone a person, rather than being something a person happens to have. So when a young woman in a village publicly rejects the council's decision about how the communal land is to be used, the community is not merely disagreeing with her. On this view she is failing at being what a person is. Her standing to object is not prior to the community's claim on her; it is downstream of it. That is why appeals to individual rights sound foreign here — they presuppose a kind of person the view denies.

Ben replied: You are collapsing at least three claims into one. The first is metaphysical: relations constitute persons, so a solitary person is not merely unfortunate but incoherent. The second is developmental: no one acquires the capacities of a person — language, reason, self-understanding — except through others, so other people are causally necessary to becoming a person. The third is normative: personhood is an achievement, a lifelong project of ethical conduct that one can succeed or fail at. Only the first does the work you want, and it is the least plausible of the three. On the developmental reading, others are necessary for acquiring the capacities, but once acquired the capacities are hers, and nothing follows about who decides what she may say. On the achievement reading, "she is not a person" is not a metaphysical verdict; it is a claim about what she is failing to become — and it applies to the council as much as to her. A community that silences its members may be failing to provide the very relations through which persons are made.

Nadia: The achievement reading is the one that is actually doing the work in the tradition, and it makes my point rather than yours. If personhood is an achievement, then someone has to judge the achievement, and the judge is the community. You have made the community the arbiter of who is a person and then tried to use the community to limit the community. And when you say a community can fail at personhood, you have quietly imported a standard from outside — a liberal standard of respect for dissent — that the view itself does not supply. You cannot convict a tradition of betraying its own values by measuring it with yours.

Ben: The standard is not imported if the relations are reciprocal. The maxim runs in both directions: if my personhood depends on your recognition, yours depends on mine. A council that refuses to hear a member's objection is not only wronging her; it is withdrawing the recognition on which its own standing as a community of persons depends. That is an internal criticism, not an external one. And notice the asymmetry your position requires: you want the community's judgment to bind her, but not hers to bind the community. Where does the direction come from?

Nadia: From the fact that the relations that made her are the ones that actually shaped her — the language, the practices, the ancestors, a whole larger than any one member. Her objection is one input among many; the tradition is what gave her the capacity to object in the first place. And "reciprocity" is doing a great deal of work for you. It slides between mutual dependence, which is perfectly compatible with a council deciding for everyone; mutual recognition, which is not; and mutual respect, which is a thicker and more modern notion still. Pick one and I will tell you whether the tradition endorses it.

Ben: Then I pick recognition, and put a question back to you. On your account the community's judgment binds because the community is the many and she is the one. But personhood is not a voting procedure; it is a status, and majorities can be wrong about statuses. If you say they cannot, you have stopped describing a relational conception of personhood and started describing a conception in which the group simply is the person and individuals are its parts — a much stronger thesis, and one that has trouble accounting for the very thing this view is usually praised for, namely that it makes everyone's personhood depend on everyone else's.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on the whole. For each move above, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and why; then say who is closer to right overall. I suspect the two of them are partly talking past each other about what kind of claim the maxim is, and partly operating with different tacit pictures of what a community is, so I would value your saying where each of them is missing something the other sees. Please state how confident you are in each verdict, identify the verdict you are least sure of and why, and tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to the other side, and where in your own reasoning the weakest link lies.
