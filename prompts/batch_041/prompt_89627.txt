I'm a PhD student working on Jain epistemology and I'm sick of anekāntavāda getting boiled down to the blind men and the elephant. Write me a short story, 1200-1800 words, close third or first person, any setting you want, where the many-sidedness doctrine actually does work in the plot.

To be clear about what I mean, cause alot of people get the lazy "everyone has their own truth" version: the claim is that a single thing can be truly called F from one naya, i.e. a deliberately partial standpoint, like looking at it as what persists through change (dravyārthika) versus as a stream of changing modes (paryāyārthika), and truly called not-F from another. The seven-fold predication (in some sense it is; in some sense it isn't; in some sense both; then the three inexpressible combinations, ending with in some sense both and inexpressible) is a way of stating a claim while marking the standpoint it's stated from. Its meant to be rigorous, not a shrug.

So I want a concrete dispute with real stakes — a water rights fight, a will, a diagnosis, a land claim, whatever you like — where two or more characters are each saying something true, and where the resolution comes from someone actually doing the qualifying work: taking a claim that was asserted flat, restricting it to a standpoint, and showing it survives the restriction. And I want the cost of skipping that work to be visible, somebody should lose something because a character hears "it depends on your perspective" as "nothing can be decided." Please don't end with a wise elder explaining the doctrine to everyone, thats a cop out, and don't name-drop texts or scholars. Just do the thing.
