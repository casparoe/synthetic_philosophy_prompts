I spent thirty-five years as an industrial chemist and, since retiring, have been reading philosophy in a fairly undisciplined way. For the past several months I have been stuck on the Liar paradox. I have worked through the familiar family of responses — declare the offending sentence neither true nor false, declare it both, stratify the truth predicate into levels, or treat truth as whatever a revision procedure eventually assigns — and I can see the motivation behind each. What I cannot see is whether any of them addresses the problem rather than its most celebrated instance. I have constructed a variant that I believe moves the difficulty off truth entirely, and I would like you to argue for the conclusion I have reached: that no treatment of the word true can repair this case, and that what must be surrendered is an assumption about confidence rather than an assumption about truth.

The variant is the sentence: "I am less than half confident that this sentence is true." Let c be my confidence that it is true. If c is below one half, then what the sentence says is correct, so the sentence is true; and since I know my own confidence and understand what the sentence says, my confidence that it is true ought to be 1, not something below one half. So c cannot be below one half. If c is one half or above, then what the sentence says is incorrect, so the sentence is false; my confidence that it is true ought then to be 0, which again contradicts c being one half or above. No value of c survives. It appears to me that nothing in this reasoning depended on whether the sentence is true, false, both, or neither, but only on the relation between my confidence and what the sentence says; I would like you to argue that this appearance is not misleading.

So: argue that the fault lies on the side of confidence and not on the side of truth. Identify which assumption about confidence ought to be given up — that a thinker's degrees of confidence are always determinate, that they are always known to the thinker who has them, or that one's confidence in a sentence is always the confidence one has in the proposition that sentence expresses — and say why that assumption rather than the others. Then tell me what becomes of the thesis that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the axioms of probability: is it refuted by the case, or merely narrowed in scope? Close by stating the strongest objection to your conclusion and answering it. Work from the case itself; I have no interest in what authorities are supposed to have said about it.
