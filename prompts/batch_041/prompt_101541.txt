Construct a thought experiment to specifications. The purpose of the exercise is to determine whether epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are genuinely separable positions, or whether each can simply absorb the other's paradigm cases by redescription. You may take the following characterization as given. Epistemic contextualism holds that the truth value of a knowledge ascription varies with features of the ascriber's conversational context — paradigmatically, with which error possibilities are salient or which standard of evidence is in play. Pragmatic encroachment holds that whether a subject knows varies with the subject's own practical situation, in particular with the stakes of acting on the belief. The two positions are normally illustrated with different vignettes; I want a single case engineered so that they must disagree about the same ascription.

Design the case to meet every one of the following specifications.

1. Realistic setting. A workplace, a household, a clinic, a construction site, or the like. No lottery cases, no fake barns, no brains in vats, no evil demons, and not the familiar bank-queue cases.

2. One subject, one proposition, one body of evidence. The subject's evidence must be adequate by any ordinary standard, so that the case does not turn on the subject's being careless, hasty, or epistemically irresponsible, and nothing in the case may be a coin flip.

3. Two versions differing in exactly one respect. In the first version, hold the ascriber's context fixed and vary only the subject's practical stakes. In the second version, hold the subject's stakes fixed and vary only the ascriber's context, by changing which error possibilities have been raised in conversation rather than by changing anyone's interests.

4. The ascriber must be a third party whose own interests and conversational context are stipulated to be unaffected by the subject's stakes; otherwise the two variables are not independent and the design proves nothing. If you judge that stipulation unrealistic in any setting that satisfies specification 1, say so and state what it costs.

5. The verdicts must be generated, not stipulated. You may not simply assert that the contextualist says one thing and the encroacher another; each verdict has to follow from the stated commitments of the position.

6. The subject's stakes must be concrete costs — money, time, injury, an irreversible commitment — not merely the word important.

After the scenario, provide the following.

- A verdict table. For each version, state the ascriber's context (salient alternatives, standard of evidence), the subject's stakes, and the verdict of standard contextualism and of pragmatic encroachment on whether the subject knows.

- The isolating feature. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the two positions come apart, and explain why the case would collapse into agreement if that feature were removed. Construct the collapsed version explicitly and show that both positions then return the same verdict.

- The rebuttals. Explain how a contextualist would try to redescribe the first version as really a change in the ascriber's context, and how an encroacher would try to redescribe the second version as really a change in the subject's stakes. Assess whether either redescription succeeds; if one does, revise the case until it does not.

- A boundary note. State what a relevant-alternatives contextualist and a subject-sensitive invariantist would each say about your case, and whether either can be distinguished from the two positions above by your design.

Keep each version of the scenario itself to no more than a paragraph; the analysis may be longer.
