I am a PhD student in philosophy, and in two weeks I have to run a fifty-minute session on the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies, for a mixed group of second- and third-year undergraduates in an interdisciplinary course on death and dying — nursing and social-work students alongside a few philosophy majors. Do not explain the argument to me; plan how I should explain it.

Start with the order of ideas and the dependencies between them. My working assumption is that the requirement that a harm needs a subject who exists to bear it must come first, that the timing problem — that there seems to be no moment at which the dead person is worse off — must come before the deprivation account can even be assessed, and that the question whether the conclusion needs a notion of harm that is not simply being worse off than one otherwise would have been has to be raised late, if at all. Correct this if it is wrong, and tell me what genuinely depends on what. (By the deprivation account I mean the view that death is bad because it deprives the person of goods they would otherwise have had.)

Then the case that should carry the whole session. I have been weighing a pair: someone instantaneously and painlessly annihilated at forty, at the peak of a flourishing life, with no one left to grieve; and someone who at forty irreversibly loses consciousness while the body lives on for decades. Say whether to build on one of these, on both, or on neither, what the carrying case must have and must not have if the Epicurean point is to land — in particular it must not smuggle in the badness of dying or the badness of the survivors' loss — and whether the obvious alternative, the symmetry between the time before one's birth and the time after one's death, should carry it instead.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface, with the point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I expect they will conflate the badness of dying with the badness of being dead; read the conclusion as the claim that death is good, or as consolation rather than as a thesis about welfare; assume the argument requires hedonism; and treat the deprivation account as plain common sense rather than as one contested option among several — which is precisely what the session has to unsettle. Tell me which of these is load-bearing and which I should let go if the hour runs short.

Finish with a single question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the argument as an argument rather than absorbed a mood, and with what I should openly decline to cover at this level and announce that I am declining.
