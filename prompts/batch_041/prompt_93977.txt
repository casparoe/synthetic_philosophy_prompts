I am writing a seminar paper on Hume's essay on suicide, and I have put his case into my own words rather than reproducing his; the reconstruction below is therefore mine, and any clumsiness in it is not his. The essay divides the claim that suicide is a crime according to the three duties it might violate — toward God, toward our neighbours, and toward ourselves — and my trouble lies with the first. Here is my reconstruction of that part.

(1) The Almighty governs the world by general and immutable laws, established from the beginning of time and never set aside.
(2) Every event, whatever its author, proceeds from the powers with which the Deity has endowed his creatures: a house that falls of its own weight is no less the work of his providence than a house pulled down by human hands, and a death by fever is no less an instance of the course of nature than a death by one's own hand.
(3) The objection that suicide is a crime against God can therefore amount only to this: that a man, by a single act, steps outside that course of nature — that he disturbs the divine government rather than merely occupying a place within it.
(4) But nothing a creature does can fall outside the order of things.
(5) Therefore no act is a disturbance of the course of nature or a rebellion against the Deity.
(6) Therefore suicide is not a transgression of any duty to God.

My referee says the argument proves too much, and I think he is right. Step (5) is not a claim about suicide; it is a claim about everything. If it holds, then perjury and murder are no more offences against God than suicide is, since each is equally an instance of the same general laws, and the essay will have answered the religious objection by emptying the very notion of an offence against God. I would like to keep (1) and (2), which I take to be no more than theism together with a doctrine of general providence, and I would like to keep (6), which I believe. That leaves (3) through (5), and I cannot see where the trouble is: the move from (4) to (5) looks airtight to me, and (3) reads like nothing but a restatement of the objection I am trying to answer.

Tell me which step or transition fails, and why it looked acceptable. Then price the repair. If the honest fix is to give up (1) and allow that God sometimes acts outside the general laws, say what becomes of (2) and of the rest of the essay. If instead the argument is sound and (5) must simply be accepted, say what remains of the essay's case once no act can offend God, and whether its arguments from our duties to our neighbours and to ourselves can carry that weight on their own.
