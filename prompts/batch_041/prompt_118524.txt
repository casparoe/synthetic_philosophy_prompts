For thirty-four years I did physical chemistry, most of it on the kinetics of heterogeneous catalysis, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of probability with more enthusiasm than system. I have reached the stage where I can follow an argument line by line and still not feel it, which I take to be a failure of imagination rather than of intellect. I would like you to help me by writing a story.

Please write a short story — two or three thousand words is ample — organised around the Dutch book argument, in the following sense. The argument holds that a person's degrees of belief answer to the ordinary rules of probability, because if they do not, then anyone who knows the prices at which that person will take either side of a wager can offer them a collection of bets, each of which they will accept, which together leave them worse off no matter how the world turns out. The story should be about a person in that position, or about someone in the business of putting others in it, and it should be a story rather than an illustration: the philosophy carried by the plot and the talk, the mechanism of the collection of bets made plain enough that a reader meeting the argument for the first time can see what is being done to whom, and no narrator's summing up at the end.

What I am after, more precisely, is a story that takes seriously the several joints at which I think the argument can be pressed, and lets them surface as events and choices rather than as objections.

The first is that the theorem is stated about prices, not about beliefs. A person may hold a degree of belief without being willing to trade on it on either side at any stake, and it is not obvious that such a person is touched by the argument at all. Someone in the story should refuse that equivalence, and refuse it well.

The second is that the guaranteed loss is a loss of money. A guaranteed loss of money is a guaranteed loss of welfare only if the person's valuation of money is near enough to linear over the range at issue; and the size of the extractable loss depends on how large a stake the person will accept, so a person grossly inconsistent who only ever plays for pennies may be unexploitable in any way that matters to them. I would like a scene in which a character knowingly takes a combination of wagers that leaves them poorer in every possible outcome, and has a reason for doing so that the reader can feel the weight of.

The third is the difference between a book closed all at once and a book closed over time. If the bookmaker lays one bet before a piece of evidence arrives and another afterwards, counting on the customer to have revised their beliefs in a particular way in between, then everything turns on whether the customer's later self is bound by the earlier self's plan, and on whether the earlier self's confidence about the later self was itself a belief that might have been wrong. I would like the story to turn on that hinge.

The fourth is that no one is compelled to bet. I keep wanting to ask whether the argument shows that an incoherent person will be exploited, or only that their state of mind is internally defective — a fault in the map rather than in the wallet — and the distance between those two conclusions seems to me to be the whole of the practical question.

There is a fifth difficulty, subtler than these, of which I would like at least a shadow in the story, though I can see that it may be hard to stage. When the inconsistency concerns an infinite family of mutually exclusive possibilities, the collection of bets that guarantees a loss is itself infinite; no finite part of it guarantees anything, and the loss arrives only if the whole infinite sequence is somehow completed. That seems to me the most disturbing of the difficulties, because it suggests the guarantee is not quite a guarantee. A lottery, or a chain of contracts that never quite closes, might carry it.

Set the story wherever it suits you — a betting shop, an insurance office, a village fair, a trading floor — and keep the people ordinary. What I want to finish reading with is a sense of why the argument is powerful and why a clever person might nevertheless walk away from it intact; I do not need to be told which of them is right.
