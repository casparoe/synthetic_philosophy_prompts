We have been reading Hobsbawm on invented tradition and Anderson on imagined communities in my course on nations and nationalism, and I have to write a response paper. The argument I drafted for it now strikes me as proving far too much, and I cannot tell where to cut. Here it is, in my own reconstruction.

(1) A practice is an invented tradition when it presents itself as the continuation of an old way of doing things, when its present form is the product of deliberate construction by identifiable people within recent memory, and when it thereby attaches people to a present institution.
(2) The standard cases fit: the kilt in the form we know it, the ceremonial of the coronation, a national day fixed by statute in the 1880s rather than inherited.
(3) So do cases that nobody calls invented. The festival in my grandparents' town, which everyone there describes as immemorial, was reconstituted in 1971 by a committee of local shopkeepers, with costumes designed by a schoolteacher, and it does precisely the work the third clause describes. The form of the liturgy that people call traditional was standardized by decree in the sixteenth century.
(4) In general, for any inherited practice one can find a moment of deliberate formalization and a claim of continuity, and for nearly any practice one can find a group that is attached to something by it.
(5) So the category contains nearly everything that anyone would call a tradition, including the practices it was introduced to contrast with.
(6) So it draws no line and explains nothing, and the charge that a nation's traditions are invented has no more force than the same charge brought against a family's holiday dinner.
(7) But the charge does have force, and it has more force against a state that manufactures a past for its citizens than against a family repeating its own dinner.

I would rather keep (7) than keep (5). Which step should I surrender, why did it look acceptable when I wrote it, and what do I lose by surrendering it? If more than one step is defensible to give up, give me the price of each, since I have to defend the choice in class and cannot simply drop the whole thing. I am not looking for the general remark that social constructions are real, or that invented traditions are still traditions; I need to know which step fails and what it costs to give it up.
