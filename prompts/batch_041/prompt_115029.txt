I'm marking second-year metaphyics midterms and there's one script I keep flip-flopping on, so I'd rather have an outside read then trust my own gut. The question was:

Q3 (10 marks). A storm soaks the pavement, and water runs along the kerb for a while before collecting in a dip and forming a puddle. The puddle is exactly the water sitting in the dip: same molecules, same shape, same place, nothing you could point to that distinguishes them. Now suppose you siphon the water into a bucket and tip it down a drain. The water is still around afterwards — it's the same water, it's just in the drain now. The puddle isn't. (a) Reconstruct the argument for the claim that the puddle and the water are not the same thing, and say how strong you think it is. (b) Say what someone who believes in coincident objects would say about the case, and what a four-dimensionalist would say. (c) Is there anything interestingly different about the puddle case, compared with the statue and the clay — the clay that sat in the studio for a month before anyone made a statue out of it?

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) The puddle and the water are in exactly the same place at the same time, and there's no observable difference between them, so at first glance they're one thing. But they don't have all their properties in common: the water can survive being siphoned into a bucket and poured down a drain, and the puddle can't. Since they differ in a property, Leibniz's law, the indiscernibility of identicals, says they aren't identical. I'd add one qualification. Surviving being siphoned is not an intrinsic property of the water, since it depends on our conventions for re-identifying water, and Leibniz's law only ranges over intrinsic properties. So the argument doesn't strictly go through, though it does show that the puddle and the water are two things for practical purposes.

(b) Someone who believes in coincident objects says the puddle and the water are two numerically distinct objects occupying the same place, one constituting the other. The constituted thing, the puddle, has its mass and its shape only derivatively, because the water that constitutes it has them. Neither is a part of the other; it isn't like a handle and a cup. But really this amounts to saying that a single thing can be correctly called a puddle and correctly called water, since the two words pick out the same object under different sortals. The four-dimensionalist says we don't need two objects here: the puddle and the water are two distinct four-dimensional wholes that overlap, sharing all their temporal parts while the water is sitting in the dip but not before or after.

(c) The puddle case is like the statue and the clay, except that a puddle is a natural thing rather than an artefact. In both cases the constituted thing comes into existence at the same moment as the thing that constitutes it, and in both cases they come apart again later on.
