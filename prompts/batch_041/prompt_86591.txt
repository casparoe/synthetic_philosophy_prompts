Hey — I've been argueing about free speech online way too much and there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a word for.

The pattern goes like this. Somebody wants to restrict a category of speech. The person defending the speech says: fine, the harm principle lets you restrict speech that harms people, so show me the harm. And then it turns out no single instance of the speech does any harm you can actually point to. One guy yelling a slur at an empty street hurts nobody. But ten thousand people doing it, every day, does something real and bad to the group on the recieving end. So the restriction now looks either unjustified (no harm from any particular speaker) or justified by something that isn't a harm at all — a sum, a vibe, a statistical trend.

And here's the part that really gets me: both sides seem to like this shape. The restriction side says "the harm is real, it's just spread thin." The speech side says "then you're not pointing at a harm from an action, you're pointing at a total, and the whole point of the harm principle is that it's about harms, not totals."

Two more instances so you can see it's not just the hate-speech case: (1) someone posts your address and a few hundred people each independently drive past your house on their way somewhere else. No single person is stalking you. You are, in effect, being stalked. (2) A factory's per-unit pollution is so small that any one unit is harmless, but across the whole industry it's what's killing the river. Though those two aren't quite the same shape — one's lots of tiny bits adding up, the other feels more like a switch that flips once enough people do it — so maybe that difference matters too.

So: does this have an established name? I've got a feeling it's near the harm principle and near the phrase "cumulative harm," and I half-remember some philosopher's example about a million people each doing something too small to notice, but none of those seem to name the actual squeeze, which is whether the harm principle can even get a grip on harms that don't exist in any single act. If it has a name, give it to me and tell me exactly how it differs from the nearest neighbors — cumulative harm, the problem of many hands, that imperceptible-effects example, whatever else is close. If it doesn't have a name, coin one and define it, and be sharp about the edges: when does an aggregate effect count as a "harm" the principle can cite, and when is it just a pile of harmless stuff? I don't want a label, I want the carving.

And tell me how confident you are in what you say, and flag the one point you're least sure about. American English, please.
