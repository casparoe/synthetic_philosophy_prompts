I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and I'd like help with an argument that broke out at our department colloquium last week. It wasn't my argument. I was in the audience, and both of the people involved are friends of mine, so I have been trying to referee it privately and getting nowhere.

The facts, as neutrally as I can state them. Our department has a norm, unwritten but well understood, that you don't use a question period to interrogate a graduate student's paper in a way that leaves the speaker no room to answer: you may press as hard as you like on the argument, but you leave a way to respond. During the discussion, a professor, P, used his turn to reprimand a postdoc, Q, for something Q had done to a graduate student at a conference the month before. Q had spent the entire question period dismantling the student's paper while cutting off every attempt at a reply. That happened; Q does not deny it. What everyone in the room knows is that P does this constantly, and did it to a visiting graduate student in that same room last term. Q's postdoctoral renewal is in P's hands. P delivered the reprimand in front of the whole department and did not let Q finish a sentence.

Afterward, two other students, Maya and Dev, argued about whether P had any business saying it.

Maya began: P's reprimand carries no weight at all. Someone who denounces a practice while quietly indulging in it is not criticizing, she said; he is performing. The point of telling someone 'you shouldn't have done that' is that you put yourself behind the norm, and P has not.

Dev replied that this confuses the messenger with the message. Whether Q's conduct was wrong has nothing to do with who says so; Q has the same reasons to refrain either way. If only the spotless may criticize, he said, the norm will be enforced exclusively by people who, by hypothesis, have never had to learn anything — and worse, the more often you offend, the less anyone may call you on it, so the norm ends up in the custody of its worst violators. He added that the people who best understand how a norm gets bent are usually the ones who have bent it, so we should want them to speak.

Maya said she would grant the content: Q did wrong, and P identified a real wrong. Her point concerned the act of saying it. Blame is an address — it asks the other person to answer for what she did, and it presupposes that the two of them stand under the norm together. P's address is false in precisely that respect: it presents him as entitled to demand compliance while exempting himself, and Q, who cannot answer back without risking her job, is being asked to accept a demand from a man who has made himself the exception. Maya added that she would feel differently if P had said, in the same breath, that he does it too and has no right to lecture; the acknowledgment would have changed what the address was. She also noted that P's reprimand was itself delivered in the style the norm forbids — public, and with no room to answer — so he violated both the norm he was invoking and the norm governing how it should be invoked, in a single act.

Dev said this proves too much. Maya did the thing herself — she cut off a speaker at the graduate conference two years ago — so by her own standard she has no standing to criticize P, and neither does anyone else in the room who has done it. And her distinction does her no good: if P may still register the fault, then what he cannot do is merely feel entitled, and whether P is entitled to his self-image is P's problem, not a defect in what Q hears. He said the same about the second-order point: fine, P was a hypocrite about the manner as well, but Q still has reasons not to do what she did.

Maya answered that she was distinguishing two things blame does. It can attribute a fault, and it can demand an answer. P can do the first. Her claim was that he cannot do the second, and that what happened in the colloquium was unambiguously the second.

I have been going back and forth on this all week and still do not know what to think. Please take the exchange turn by turn and tell me, for each, whether the move is sound, half-sound, or mistaken, and why — I want the specific step that fails, not a general verdict about hypocrisy. Then: what conception of blame must each of them be assuming for their moves to work, and does the disagreement survive or dissolve once that is made explicit? Is Q's inability to answer back a separate issue from P's record, or the same issue described a second way? Can Maya's position be restated so that it does not produce Dev's result — say, standing lost only with respect to the particular norm, only while the violation goes unacknowledged, and restored by acknowledgment — and is that a principled restriction or an ad hoc patch? If P's reprimand is defective, is the defect a wrong done to Q, or only a flaw in how the department conducts itself, and does anything follow for Q about whether to comply? What are both of them missing? And finally, who has the better overall position, and how much of it survives?

I am not asking for a survey of the literature on hypocrisy; I want these moves assessed on their merits.
