I am preparing a handout for a graduate unit on constructive mathematics and want a decision tree rather than a list of schools. The tree should start from a reader who uses classical logic and full classical set theory without qualms, and lead through questions that can each be answered without knowing which position they lead to; every leaf should name a position and add one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

Build the branches from commitments, not from historical labels. The nodes should include at least: whether a proof of a disjunction must yield a proof of one of the disjuncts; whether a proof of an existential claim must yield a witness; whether, from the premise that a computation does not fail to halt, one may infer that it halts; whether double negation elimination is valid; whether a continuous function on the unit interval that takes both a negative and a positive value must vanish somewhere; whether one may quantify over all sets or only over sets definable by some specified procedure; whether the axiom of choice is acceptable when it yields a selector with no recipe; and whether a single logic governs all of mathematics or classical reasoning remains legitimate wherever only consistency, not constructive content, is wanted.

Include as leaves at least: Brouwerian intuitionism, with the continuum built from free choice sequences; Bishop-style constructive analysis; a computability-first view that identifies existence with computability and accepts the halting principle above; predicativism; Hilbertian finitism; type-theoretic constructivism in the Martin-Löf style; and the view that intuitionistic logic is the correct logic of what can be verified rather than a restriction adopted for convenience. Mark any combination your tree leaves unoccupied, and say whether that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and name the question that would separate them.
