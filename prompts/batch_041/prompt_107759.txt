I teach in a Great Books program at a maximum-security prison, and I have been asked to give the commencement address at this year's graduation. The graduates have just finished a semester on Dostoevsky; they know the Grand Inquisitor passage well, some of them better than I do. The audience will also include their families, prison staff, and donors, most of whom have not read it. I want to give a speech that takes the Inquisitor's challenge seriously rather than treating it as a rhetorical foil. The Inquisitor's claim, as I understand it, is not merely that freedom is hard, but that Christ's gift of freedom was a mistake: that ordinary people cannot bear the burden of free choice, that they will always trade it for security, mystery, and someone to tell them what to do, and that the Church's correction of Christ's work was therefore an act of mercy. That claim is difficult to face in a room full of people who have had their freedom taken away by the state and who are now being given an education that is supposed to be about freedom. I have spent enough time in rationalist-adjacent forums to know the standard move: steelman the Inquisitor, then explain why he is wrong. I do not want to do that. I want the steelman to be the speech — to let the Inquisitor's argument shape what I can and cannot say, to make the speech honest rather than inspirational, and to leave the graduates with something they can actually use. I also want to avoid the easy move of casting the graduates as the few who can bear freedom; that seems both patronizing and false. Please write the speech. It should be about ten minutes long, and it should be something I could deliver without embarrassment.
