I'm a science journalist — I write a column on mind and evolution, mostly for people who like their metaphysics with their biology — and I've been carrying around an argument a friend handed me after a long dinner, and it's been nagging at me for weeks. The setting is Dennett's careenium: the giant spherical pinball machine full of little magnetized balls (simmballs) bouncing around, in which a stable higher-level pattern — a 'memory register' — shows up in the way the balls clump and jostle. Nobody designed the register; it's just a regularity in the swarm, and the swarm is supposed to show how a memory could be real without being anything over and above the balls. My friend's argument goes like this. (1) At any moment, every simmball's motion is fully fixed by the local magnetic forces on it, and those are fixed by where its neighbors happen to be. (2) Nothing about the higher-level 'memory' — nothing about what the register 'says' — enters into the determination of any individual ball's motion; the local facts already settle everything. (3) A fact counts as a cause only if it makes a difference to some event over and above the facts that already determine that event. (4) So the memory register is not a cause of anything; it's a pattern we notice, not a force that acts. (5) But the careenium is supposed to be a model of us — neurons instead of magnets — so the same reasoning says our beliefs and meanings never cause anything we do. (6) So no one has ever walked to the kitchen because they believed there was coffee there. That last step is obviously false, and I'd rather keep it false than keep (3). But then he springs the trap: (7) if you save the memory register by saying it's a real cause because it's a real, predictive pattern, then my own gerrymandered pattern — a disjunctive, ugly-predicate 'shadow register' I can define that predicts the ball positions just as well but corresponds to no memory at all — is a real cause too. (8) So causal efficacy gets handed out to arbitrary gerrymandered patterns, which is absurd.

I can't tell whether the flaw is in (2) (some equivocation about what 'enters into the determination' of a ball's motion), in (3) (the difference-making test quietly assuming there's one privileged level of description at which causes are real), in the slide from (4) to (5), or in the shadow-register step at (7). I'd like you to name the step you'd reject, explain why it looked fine until you pushed on it, and price what rejecting it costs — including whether I can keep the memory register real without letting in every grue-ish pattern anyone cares to define, and whether the careenium's answer even transfers to brains. If the whole setup is confused — if 'do symbols push the substrate around?' is already a bad question because causation isn't a single-level affair — say so plainly rather than humoring me; I'd rather be told my question is malformed than get a tidy answer to the wrong one.
