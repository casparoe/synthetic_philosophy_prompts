I'm in a second-year ethics seminar that's basically turned into a running fight about whether anyone is ever really a saint, and I have to hand in a short position statement next week and then defend it in front of the whole room, so I'd rather find the cracks in my own view now than have someone else find them for me in public. Here's where I've landed, roughly in the order I'd defend it. (1) Supererogation is real — there are acts that are morally permissible and yet morally better than the permissible minimum; the person who goes back into the burning building for the neighbour's kid, or who gives away 40% of their income to people they'll never meet, is doing something you couldn't be blamed for skipping. (2) I don't think the saint/hero thing is a deep structural divide in the moral landscape — it's a difference in the kind of cost the agent carries (her own good versus her own skin), not a difference in the moral category, and I get suspicious whenever people try to make heroism its own special department. (3) I'm a hard-liner about "ought implies can" in the sense that I don't think morality can require of us anything ordinary people genuinely can't live up to — a morality that demands the impossible, or the merely unlivable, is either false or self-defeating — and that's also why I think the demandingness objection is basically fatal to act-consequentialism: a theory on which buying a coffee instead of donating the money is wrongdoing is a reductio of the theory. (4) I'm a reasons-monist. I don't think there's a special "moral ought" with its own logic sitting alongside the ordinary ought of reasons; there's one ought, and it's fixed by the balance of reasons. If there's more reason for you to do A than for anything else, then you ought to do A, and "you ought to" just means it's required. (5) Praise and blame track requirements: you don't get credit for doing what was required anyway, and you can't be blamed for skipping what was optional. (6) I also think there's a category on the other side that nobody talks about enough — the suberogatory, acts you're allowed to do but that are still genuinely objectionable, like not swapping seats on a plane with a parent and a screaming toddler, or telling a friend you're out when you're not. Not sure that's even the standard name for it, but you know the thing I mean.
