I am the head of investigations for a state occupational safety agency. My investigators are engineers with no philosophical training; between us we produce about forty findings reports a year, each due within thirty days, or seventy-two hours when the injury is serious, and each report names the causes of the incident, which in turn fixes the citations we issue, the remediation the employer must pay for, and what becomes an exhibit in the administrative hearing and in the civil suit that follows. I sign the report and defend it before a review panel with a lawyer on it, and the employer's representatives, and sometimes my own staff, want a single root cause naming one worker's slip. I need a procedure rather than a theory, and I need no citations or reading list, just something I can hand to an engineer.

Two accounts of causation are on the table. On the first, a factor caused an outcome if the outcome would not have happened without it, judged by imagining the actual history with that one factor removed and everything else left as it was. On the second, a factor caused an outcome if the outcome is sensitive to an idealized intervention on it, that is, a manipulation that changes that factor and nothing else upstream of it and then lets the change propagate through the system's own connections, so that the test is what happens downstream when you wiggle this, not what would have happened in a world where the rest of the situation had adjusted along with it. Taken literally, the first makes the presence of oxygen and the fact that the shift started on time the causes of every fire we investigate, and it implies that when two independent faults are each sufficient for the failure, neither is a cause, which is an absurd finding to sign. The second is the one that tells my investigators what to wiggle, but I am not sure whether it implies that factors nobody could ever manipulate, such as the age of the equipment or the fact that the process was designed with a known dead leg in 1963, are not causes. I think that would be a mistake, and I want the procedure to say so out loud rather than leave an engineer to guess. Both accounts also depend on how finely I describe the outcome, and the outcome is described in whatever words I choose: a release, a release of four hundred pounds, a release of four hundred pounds at 3:14 a.m. from the north header. Fix the level of description, or give me the rule for setting it, because the answer to the first test changes with the words.

Run the procedure on three cases. First, a pressure vessel that ruptured through two relief paths, each blocked independently and each sufficient on its own to allow the overpressure, one by a plastic sheet a contractor left in place that morning and one by a valve that had been seized for years and reported by no one; everyone wants me to name just one of them. Second, a conveyor with no guard at the nip point, which ran that way for twenty years before a new worker's sleeve was caught; the employer says the absence of a guard cannot be a cause because nothing happened for twenty years, and my investigators cannot say what to wiggle when the factor is an absence. Third, a process with a design flaw known since the 1960s that collects residue in a dead leg; every serious incident at the site traces back to it, no site manager can change it, the employer will call it a background condition rather than a cause, and it is nonetheless the largest lever anyone has. That third one is the answer I expect not to like, and I would rather hear it now.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, written in American English. The first sets out the questions in the order an investigator should ask them. The second says what counts as a sufficient answer to each question and the point at which the procedure stops and records that a factor is not a cause. The third says how the procedure resolves the conflicts between the two accounts and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question has become one of policy or values rather than of fact. The fourth runs the procedure on the first two cases. The fifth runs it on the third case and closes with what the procedure cannot do.
