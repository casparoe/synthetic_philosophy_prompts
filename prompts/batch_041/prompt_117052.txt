Write a pantoum that stages the liberal paradox, and let the form carry the argument rather than any commentary from the poem itself.

The paradox, in outline: no rule for aggregating individual preferences into a social ordering can satisfy all three of the following at once — that it accept any profile of individual rankings (unrestricted domain); that if every individual strictly prefers one alternative to another, the social ordering does too (weak Pareto); and that at least two people are each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives (minimal liberalism). Each condition is independently mild. Together they are unsatisfiable, and the failure surfaces as a cycle rather than a stalemate.

Build the poem on this case. Two colleagues, Vera and Sam, have a paper accepted at a conference and must settle who, if anyone, presents it. Three states are on offer: Vera presents (a), Sam presents (b), neither presents (c). Vera ranks them c, then a, then b — she would rather withdraw than watch Sam misstate their argument, but would rather present it herself than withdraw. Sam ranks them a, then b, then c — he wants Vera to present, would do it himself if she declined, and would rather withdraw than let the paper die. Grant Vera authority over whether she herself presents, and Sam authority over whether he does; those are the two decisive pairs, (a, c) and (b, c), and they meet at the shared option c. Then the verdicts fall out one by one: Vera's authority puts c above a; Sam's puts b above c; and because both rank a above b, the Pareto principle puts a above b. Follow the relations around and they close on themselves: b above c above a above b.

I want that closed loop to be the poem. A pantoum's second and fourth lines return as the first and third lines of the next stanza, and its final stanza is bound back to its first, so the form, if the rule is kept, must end where it began — which is just what the social preference does here, and just why the three conditions cannot be held together. Keep the rule. Let the recurring lines carry the preference relations themselves (this option beating that one), so that a reader tracing the repetitions around the loop feels it tighten rather than hears it described. A reader who knows the paradox should be able to point to the line that voices Vera's authority, the line that voices Sam's, and the line that voices the Pareto verdict, and see that no ordering of the three escapes the circle. Rhyme is optional; keep the meter steady, since the whole point is that a rigid rule yields something that cannot be completed without contradiction.

After the poem, in no more than three sentences, name the single condition you had to bend in order to close it, and say what the poem would have become had you sacrificed a different one instead.
