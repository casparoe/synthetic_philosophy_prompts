I go to a little reading group at the library — we're all amateurs, nobody there has a philosophy degree, we just take turns picking something short and weird to chew on. Last month I picked a piece of an old Chinese text called the White Horse Dialogue, by a guy named Gongsun Long, who's usually lumped in with the so-called School of Names, Warring States era I think. The whole thing is two people arguing about one sentence: that a white horse isn't a horse. That's the entire text. That's the claim.

Two of the group, Dev and Marcus, went at each other for about an hour afterward, and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Here's roughly how it went, I'm probably mangling it.

Dev said the argument is a shell game. He says "horse" names the shape and "white" names the color, and since naming a color isn't naming a shape, "white horse" isn't "horse." Then there's the bit about asking for a horse — if you want a horse, someone can bring you a black one, but if you want a white horse, a black one won't do. Dev says all of that is just the words "is" and "not" being asked to do two jobs at once. When I say a white horse is a horse, I'm not saying the two phrases mean the same thing, I'm saying the animal belongs to the class of horses. The old man answers a question about what the animal is with a point about what the words mean, and the "not" slides between "isn't the same as" and "isn't one of." Fix the meaning and it's either obviously false or so boring nobody would bother writing it down.

Marcus said no, you're the one importing modern logic. He's not talking about the animal at all, he's talking about names. A name names one thing. "White horse" names two things, whiteness and horseness, so it can't name the same thing "horse" names. And the asking-for-a-horse bit is the proof: the two expressions aren't interchangeable, you can't swap them into a sentence and keep the truth, and that's a real difference, not a verbal one. He also said the guy in the text who keeps dragging it back to actual animals is the one being refuted, not the one arguing.

Dev: your swap test only shows they pick out different groups — white horses are a smaller bunch sitting inside the horses. Everyone already knew that. If the big headline claim just means the group of white horses isn't the group of horses, fine, but that's arithmetic, not a paradox. If it's about the actual animal, it's just wrong. Pick one.

Marcus: here's what I think you're missing. "White" adds something to "horse." So a white horse is horse and something besides. Whatever is horse and something besides is more than horse. And what's more than horse isn't horse. That's the argument, and it never mentions sets.

Dev: then it proves way too much. A tall man is man and something besides, so a tall man isn't a man? A red apple isn't an apple? Also you keep sliding on "more" — more words in the name isn't more stuff in the animal. And by your own line, if a white horse is horse plus white, then it's got horse in it, and if it's got horse in it you'd think it counts as a horse. So your principle eats itself.

Marcus said the principle isn't supposed to generalise, and that tallness is a comparison while whiteness is just a feature you can see sitting on the thing itself. He said there's another piece by the same guy about hardness and whiteness, something about whether one stone can be both at once, and that it's the same puzzle. Then he said Dev keeps assuming a word has to be a label for a bunch of things, when the whole point of this school is that a name picks a thing out by pointing at one feature or another, so a name that points at two features can't be a name that points at one.

Neither would budge and they're both pretty sharp about other stuff, so I can't just assume the confident one is right.

What I want is for you to go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins on points, the actual steps. Where does Dev's equivocation charge land, and where does it miss? Is Marcus's "more than horse" thing a real argument or is he making it up? Is there any reading where the old man's claim comes out neither trivially true nor obviously false, because that's the part that bugs me. And what are they both missing? If the real text is doing something cleverer than the version we hashed out, feel free to say so — but I'm not after a history lesson or a reading list, just the argument.
