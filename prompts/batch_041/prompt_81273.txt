I am a second-year undergraduate taking a course on rational choice and its critics, and I am also the coordinator of a twelve-person student housing cooperative. We have just finished the unit on Parfit's hitchhiker — the stranded traveller who can get a lift only by promising to send money afterwards, knowing that once he is safe he will have no reason to send it, so that the only way to get the ride is to be the sort of person who would send it anyway. Our tutor's phrase for the lesson is rational irrationality. I can state it and I still do not know how to apply it, and I have realised that my house has been running that case as an institution for years without understanding it.

Nothing we agree to is legally enforceable and we do not want it to be; enforcement is the thing we are trying to live without. Our undertakings fall into three kinds. First, a housemate borrows money from the house kitty and undertakes to repay it after graduation, when the house will have no leverage over them and half the current members will have moved out. Second, a housemate undertakes to take over someone else's kitchen shift in a week in which they will have an excellent reason to be somewhere else. Third, the house as a whole pledges something to an outside party — a sum to a local charity, a commitment to a landlord — where no individual member is obliged to do anything and each of us could quietly do less than our share. Membership turns over almost completely every twelve months, so whatever makes a promise credible here cannot simply be someone's standing character, since the someone will be gone.

I want to put this to a house meeting. Give me a decision procedure I can actually run in twenty minutes, with eleven people who have not read any of the theory, on a decision that comes round roughly monthly, over sums between about twenty and four hundred pounds. It has to be explicit about the level at which it operates — the individual act, the policy, or the character of the person promising — because I keep losing track of that, and I suspect the confusion is where our mistakes come from. It has to say what makes a promise credible to the person relying on it, and what to do when I can tell, while I am making a promise, that I will not want to keep it; I have done that twice and both times the other person was standing right in front of me. It has to say something usable about the third kind of case, where the promise belongs to the house and no single person's disposition seems to be doing the work. And it has to tell me where to stop and hand the decision to the people in the room.

Please give the response in exactly these sections:

1. "The claim in plain terms" — under 250 words. What the hitchhiker case does and does not establish, phrased so that I can read it aloud to people who have read no philosophy at all.

2. "The procedure" — around 800 words. The steps in the order we should run them, each with the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether that step is a question of fact or a question of values.

3. "Worked cases" — around 600 words. Run the procedure on the three cases above: the departing member's debt, the swapped shift, and the house's pledge to the charity.

4. "The case I will not want to hear" — around 200 words. One application where the procedure tells me to do something I will resist, with an honest account of whether my resistance is legitimate.

5. "What the procedure cannot do" — around 250 words. Where it must misfire, which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and what it cannot settle at all.
