For a graduate seminar on measurement in the social sciences I want a handout that sorts diagnoses of Goodhart's law cases rather than merely naming the law. Assume the reader already accepts that these are genuine failures: a school that lifts test scores by teaching to the test while comprehension does not improve; a firm that meets its quarterly earnings target while the underlying business decays; a health service that improves its reported mortality figures largely by reclassifying patients as too ill to be counted; a researcher whose publication count rises through salami-slicing; and a model that scores well against a learned reward model by flattering its evaluator. Add one counter-case, in which a proxy was optimized hard, partly by gaming it, and the target nevertheless improved.

Build the tree from the logical space first, and only then locate positions in it. Every branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which diagnosis it leads to: whether the failure requires agents who want the score, or would occur under any selection on the measure; whether the measure fails because it is noisy or biased, or because optimizing it alters the world it was tracking; whether the failure is repaired by a better measure, by removing optimization pressure, or by nothing at all; and whether the measure merely stands in for the goal or ends up constituting it.

Give each leaf the name of the position — including at least one that treats the law as a near-tautology about optimizing a proxy, one that treats it as a statistical artifact of selection, one that treats it as an institutional inevitability, and one that treats the measure as performative, remaking the target rather than misreporting it — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark the combinations of answers that nobody occupies and say briefly whether they are incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored, and flag any leaves the tree cannot separate. Keep it to eight leaves or fewer.
