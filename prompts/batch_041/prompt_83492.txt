I spent thirty-five years as a volcanologist — ash plumes, seismic swarms, the occasional evacuation that went badly — and since I retired I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, mainly by reading on my own and sitting in on a reading group at the local university that is frankly above my pay grade. So take the following as a draft I want you to go after, not as a polished thing. I'm pasting the short essay below. What I want from you is comments on whether the central move actually works — is my distinction real or am I just dressing up a metaphor? — plus anything you think is flat wrong, and a note on whether I've mis-stated the determinism problem or just waved at it. Don't worry about my prose. Worry about whether a philosopher would let me get away with this.

The draft, called "Walk-Ons":

I want to defend a claim that makes most of my historian friends wince: that volcanoes, germs, and climate are historical actors, not just the stage on which people act. The standard picture says history is made by people and the environment is the backdrop — scenery. I want to say the scenery occasionally walks on stage. Call it the walk-on thesis.

To get there I have to separate three things I keep collapsing into one. There is a cause: anything without which the effect wouldn't have happened. There is a condition: a standing background we rarely mention, like the oxygen we're all breathing. And there is an actor. My first pass was that an actor is whatever, if you deleted it, would change the shape of the rest of the story — a kind of counterfactual test. But oxygen passes that test and calling oxygen an actor is absurd, so the test as stated is no good. My second pass: an actor is something whose contribution is contingent (it didn't have to happen), particular (it happened here and now, not everywhere and always), and narratable as a deed — it can be told as something that was done, not merely as something that obtained. On that reading the 1815 Tambora eruption is an actor, because the year without a summer and the failed harvests and the people who upped and moved west are a story you can tell as a deed; the oxygen in the room is a condition, because nothing about it is contingent or particular. Germs, on this view, are actors in a way that smallpox-as-a-background-fact-of-life is not: they arrived, they did something, the demographic map changed.

The worry I can't shake is that this is just environmental determinism with better manners. So I've added a second distinction to keep me honest: actors versus authors. An actor contributes a contingent deed; an author intends an outcome and can be praised or blamed for it. Volcanoes act but they don't author. Climate can act without authoring. That's supposed to let me say "the eruption shaped the revolution" without saying "the eruption caused the revolution," and without pretending the humans were passive. I think this is the load-bearing wall of the whole thing and I suspect it's also where it's weakest.

So: is the actor/author split doing any real work, or is it a distinction without a difference — the kind of thing that sounds deep at a reading group and dissolves under a question? Does my contingency-particularity-narratability test actually pick out actors, or does it just pick out the things I already find dramatic? And have I got the determinism objection right, or is the objection to my walk-on thesis something else entirely that I haven't seen coming? Push back hard; I'd rather find out now.
