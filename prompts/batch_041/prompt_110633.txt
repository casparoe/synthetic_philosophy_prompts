A is a wage-series economic historian. B works on Qing fiscal institutions and reads a lot of philosphy of science for fun. Here's the exchange:

A: "'Why Britain and not China?' As asked, that's not a real question, it's a 19th-century hangover. It assumes 'Britain' and 'China' are the right units, and it assumes China was the default and Britain the deviation. Neither survives contact with the numbers. Compare England to the Yangzi delta and the striking thing is that they're in roughly the same league on consumption and market development into the 18th century; the difference is that England had cheap coal sitting next to the people who needed it, plus a hemisphere of new land and silver to draw on. And the timing of the divergence moves depending on whether you use silver wages, grain wages or output per head. If you can't even fix the explanadum, then 'and not China' isn't part of the question — it's a promissory note on an answer."

B: "That's a philosophy-of-science complaint wearing a historian's hat. Every why-question is contrastive; that's the only shape explanation has. van Fraassen made the point decades ago — asking 'why P?' is really asking 'why P rather than Q?', and which Q you pick is fixed by what the asker wants to know. So the question is fine, you just have to say which contrast you mean. Why did the breakthrough happen in England rather than in the most commercially advanced region of the most advanced Asian economy? Why did it happen in England rather than nowhere? Different questions, different answers, both legitimate. You can't dodge contrasts by refusing to state one, you just end up with an unstated one. And the units complaint cuts both ways — 'England' is as much a construction as 'China'. What you want is transparency, not abolition."

A: "But the contrast isn't neutral, it does causal work. Making China the counterfactual tells everyone where to go hunting for the cause: go find what China lacked — a science that got 'blocked', an exam system, an inward-turning state — and you end up with a laundry list of British virtues. It's asymmetric, too. Nobody asked why China didn't industrialize first until after Britain did; the question is generated by its own answer. And 'why not' questions are their own species. The non-event has no mechanism to inspect, so you're free to nominate any difference you like as the cause. That's how you get cultural explanations that can't be falsified."

B: "Now you're attacking bad answers, not the question. The virtue of the Needham question is that it forces you to state a baseline — what did you think was normal? — and no causal claim comes without one. Take your preferred version: why did sustained growth appear in England? Same structure, hidden comparison. And non-events do have mechanisms. Absences get explained by the absence of the conditions the thing requires, which is ordinary counterfactual reasoning — why didn't the Yangzi delta burn its coal? Transport costs. That's a why-not question with a perfectly good answer. Your argument throws out most of historical explanation, including the parts you like."
