I write about science and ideas for a general-readership outlet, and one evening a week I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of science for final-year undergraduates and beginning master's students. I am now drafting the final examination, and I want a single essay question on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook summary — the hard core, the protective belt, the positive and negative heuristic, and the contrast between progressive and degenerating problem shifts. My students can all reproduce that vocabulary on demand; what I want to find out is whether they can actually use it.

To force that, I have invented a case for them to analyse, and I want the question built around it. Please present the case roughly as follows, in your own words but keeping the details. The case concerns a research programme, call it the recruitment programme, whose hard core asserts that a whole class of phenomena, cascades, are generated by a single mechanism, recruitment; its protective belt carries auxiliary assumptions about thresholds, noise, and boundary conditions. Four episodes are described. First, an anomaly — cascades that lack the expected threshold behaviour — is handled by adding the auxiliary assumption that cascades may be seeded by a hidden trigger; the programme then predicts that removing the trigger will suppress cascades, and an experiment confirms this. Second, a later anomaly is handled by introducing a free parameter tuned case by case, with no new prediction issued. Third, a further anomaly is handled by redefining the core term recruitment so that the awkward cases no longer count as cascades at all. Fourth, the tuned parameter from the second episode is later reinterpreted as a genuine quantity, opening a new line of inquiry and yielding a prediction that is then confirmed.

The question should ask the students to classify each episode and, more importantly, to say what that classification presupposes about the unit and the timing of appraisal: whether degenerating is a verdict we can pass on an episode as it occurs or only in hindsight, and whether a research programme can be appraised without knowing its future. I would like one further part that asks whether the apparatus requires the hard core to make contact with prediction at all, and what that implies for a programme whose core is a normative or interpretive commitment rather than an empirical hypothesis.

The examination is closed-book and the students will have about fifty minutes for this question, so keep it to a single essay prompt. Please supply three things: the essay question itself; a model answer of about six hundred words, written at a level a strong student could plausibly reach in the time; and a marking scheme worth twenty marks in total, itemised into the specific moves a top answer must make. The marking scheme should state plainly what separates an A answer from a B answer, and should add a one-line note, for each assessed move, on the mistake candidates typically make there. Assume the students have the Lakatosian vocabulary but not the analysis; do not ask them for citations or reading, and there is no need for you to supply any.
