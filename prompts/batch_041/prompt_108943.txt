I am a high school student writing a long essay on capital punishment, and I have been persuaded by the case Camus makes against it. The version I want assessed, though, is my own reconstruction rather than an interpretation of his text, so please treat it as a position rather than as exegesis. The case has three legs. First, irreversibility: judicial killing is the one penalty that cannot be corrected, so a state whose judgments are made by fallible people may never impose it, and the proper maximum is life at hard labor — arguably harsher, but at least reversible. Second, deterrence: executions are now carried out out of sight, which forfeits the exemplary function that might once have justified them, and in any case the threat of death adds little when death is the fate everyone already shares and most killings are not premeditated. Third, standing: no one is absolutely innocent or holds absolute authority to judge, so no one is entitled to pronounce the final sentence, and a state that kills in the name of the value of life contradicts itself and is really exacting revenge.

Tell me what would count against this. Rank the defeaters from those that would sink the position to those that would merely be awkward for it, and say which ones someone who holds the view would concede rather than only which ones critics would press. In particular: is the second leg purely empirical, so that the whole position is hostage to a deterrence finding and would have to collapse if one arrived — and if so, does the view concede that the penalty would be justified if it worked? Is the first leg really about death, or does it apply equally to a life sentence, in which case why is the remedy abolition rather than a higher standard of proof? Does the third leg prove too much, since the thought that none of us is innocent enough to judge would disable every punishment, imprisonment included, and since the state may need authority rather than innocence? And does the concession that the practice may have been coherent in a believing society make this a claim not about the penalty itself but about our epistemic situation — in which case, what would have to change for it to apply again? I would find most decisive a case where the punishment is plainly deserved by my own lights and the practice still seems unjustified, because that is the case I cannot currently handle.
