I'm a graduate researcher in a small environmental-testing group, and part of my job is to keep our reporting internally consistent. My supervisor, who trained as a Bayesian and reviews every borderline batch with us, has over the past few months stated what she takes the correct posterior to be in a run of cases, and I've begun to suspect she is running a rule she has never actually articulated. The background model we all accept is this: for a given batch, let H be the proposition that the batch is unsafe to ship, and let E be the proposition that the target compound is present in detectable quantity; H implies E. Our standing prior is P(H) = 0.2, P(E but not H) = 0.3, and P(no compound) = 0.5. Her stated verdicts so far: (i) a reference assay for the compound, known to be error-free, comes back positive, and she puts the posterior for H at 0.4; (ii) a cheap alarm with known false-positive and false-negative rates fires, and she puts the posterior at 0.28, which she derives by conditioning on the alarm's having fired; (iii) a soft confidence instrument whose dial reads 0.8, and which is known to be compound-specific, i.e. it tells you nothing about severity beyond what it tells you about whether the compound is present, and she puts the posterior at 0.32, explaining that the dial reading just is your new probability for E and that you should hold your conditional probabilities fixed while you move the marginal; (iv) the same kind of compound-specific instrument reads 0.6, and she puts the posterior at 0.24. Every instrument she has ever been shown has been compound-specific, and I suspect she has not noticed that the tidy pattern in (iii) and (iv) holds only because of that.

Next week's case is the first one from our new chromatograph, which is not compound-specific: its score tracks the contaminant's severity as well as its presence. Per the calibration table printed on the instrument, on an unsafe batch it reads high with probability 0.625, on a compound-positive-but-safe batch with probability 0.25, and on a clean batch with probability 0.1. The batch before us has just read high, and the instrument's display, which converts its score into a confidence for the compound's presence, shows 0.8, the same number the soft instrument in case (iii) showed. I want you, acting as a philosopher of science who works on Bayesian confirmation and uncertain evidence, to reason this through carefully rather than pattern-match. First lay out the candidate rules that her four verdicts are jointly consistent with, and I expect there to be more than one. Then say what each candidate predicts for the chromatograph case and compute those posteriors. Then give your best guess at what she will in fact say and the reasons for preferring it. Finally, name one further case that would separate the candidates if her answer to the chromatograph case turns out to be ambiguous between them. Throughout, keep in mind that I care far less about the exact number than about which rule she is implicitly running, and about what would force her to choose.

End your reply with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{posterior | rule}, for example \answer{0.50 | condition on the total evidence}, giving your predicted posterior for H and a short label for the rule you take her to be running.
