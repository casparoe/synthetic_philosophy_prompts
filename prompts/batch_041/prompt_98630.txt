For a couple of years now I've been working my way, without any philosophical training, through a fat anthology of political thought, and one sentence has been stuck in my head ever since I first underlined it: "some people are slaves by nature." I originally met it as a slogan in an online argument, where a man used it to defend an extremely rigid workplace — his line being that some employees are simply built to take orders and do best when somebody else does the deciding, so there is nothing unjust about a firm being run that way. Since then I've watched the same phrase do quite different work in different mouths. One friend treats it as a sober factual claim about who can plan and govern their own life; another treats it as a claim about who is better off being directed, even if they could in principle manage alone; a third treats it as a flat assertion that nature has already settled who belongs on top and who below. I've also noticed people quietly sliding between "this is how some people are," "this is what suits them," and "this is what they deserve," and I can't tell whether that slide is innocent or the whole trick.

I'd like you to lay out, as a menu I can keep and bring back to these arguments, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from the merely definitional or trivial through to the substantive — and for each one tell me whether it is an empirical claim, a claim about what is good for someone, or a claim about justice, and whether it is defensible on its own terms, contentious, or simply false. I suspect a lot of the confusion comes from running together what is natural with what is beneficial and what is just, so please separate those strands explicitly and say which of them the original workplace use actually needed. Please also say something about the difference between being a slave by nature and being a slave by law or convention, and whether the slogan is even the sort of thing that could be an empirical hypothesis rather than an essentialist verdict — because if it's the former I want to know what evidence would settle it, and if it's the latter I want to know what work "nature" is doing. For each reading, finish by telling me whether it would really support the conclusion the man in the argument drew, and if not, what further premise he'd need to add. Please write your answer in British English.
