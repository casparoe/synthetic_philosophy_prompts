An effective-altruist friend of mine settles arguments by telling me to "shut up and multiply." His most recent use of it: I suggested that a modest donation to anti-malarial bednets is the safer bet, and he answered that the same sum given to research on a speculative technology that may matter only decades from now has the higher expected value, and that my hesitation is merely a failure to do the arithmetic. I would like the slogan taken apart. What distinct, precisely statable theses could "shut up and multiply" be expressing, running from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical? For each, say whether it is defensible on its own terms and whether it actually delivers his conclusion. I am chiefly interested in where ordinary expected-value maximization ends and expected-value fanaticism begins — the claim that a sufficiently tiny probability of an enormous or infinite payoff outweighs every finite consideration — and in whether the "shut up" contributes anything beyond the "multiply," or instead asserts something stronger than the milder maxim of doing the math and then going with your gut. Close by saying which reading he most likely had in mind.
