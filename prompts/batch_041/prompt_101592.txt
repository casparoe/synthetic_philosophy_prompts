I'm a graduate student in philosophy of science, and after a seminar on confirmation theory two classmates of mine, Dana and Marcus, spent the better part of an hour re-litigating the raven paradox without ever landing anywhere. I've been asked to write a neutral summary of the exchange for our reading group, and I'd rather produce an actual adjudication than another gloss on Hempel, so I want you to referee their argument as it was actually made. Here is how it went. Dana opened by saying the whole thing is a pseudo-paradox: since 'all ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' and since the green apple on the table is a non-black non-raven, the apple is a positive instance of the contrapositive and therefore confirms the original hypothesis. This, she said, is nothing more than the equivalence condition — if two hypotheses are logically equivalent, whatever confirms one confirms the other — which she called about as uncontroversial as anything in the field. Marcus replied that this proves too much, because the identical contrapositive reasoning also makes the green apple confirm 'all swans are white'; and if an observation confirms each of two hypotheses then it confirms their conjunction, so the apple would end up confirming the joint hypothesis that all ravens are black and all swans are white. Since that is plainly absurd, he said, something must be wrong with treating the apple as an instance at all: genuine confirmation has to be selective, in the sense that the observation must be one that could have come out otherwise had the hypothesis been false. Dana answered that the tacking move only shows the confirmation is minuscule, not that it is nonexistent — the apple raises the probability of the raven hypothesis by a hair, and by that same hair it raises the conjunction — and that a tiny probability boost is not an absurdity. Marcus then pressed that even the 'tiny boost' is an artifact of treating the apple as a random draw from the totality of objects: the probability that a random object is a non-black non-raven is essentially the same whether or not all ravens are black, because non-black non-ravens swamp ravens in number, so the likelihood ratio is one and the boost is really zero. Dana countered that this is a claim about the size of the effect under one particular sampling model, not about the logic of confirmation, and that if the paradox truly dissolves into a point about which observations are 'relevant' or were genuinely 'tests,' then stating that point requires a theory of relevance that nobody has supplied without circularity — which is exactly why the puzzle keeps coming back. Marcus granted the equivalence condition but insisted the culprit is the word 'instance,' not the logic. They left it there, each convinced the other had changed the subject.

Then say who has the better of the overall dispute. Most importantly, I want you to identify what both of them are missing: the distinction, if there is one, that would let each of them see where the other is right. Please don't hand me the standard textbook resolution of the ravens; I want the specific points on which Dana and Marcus are each right and each wrong as they actually argued, and I want you to flag any place where one of them is relying on an assumption they never stated.
