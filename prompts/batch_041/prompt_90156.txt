Although my training is in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I keep circling back to the quantum-suicide thought experiment and the immortality conclusion that is sometimes drawn from it within the Everettian or many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics. I grasp the argument well enough at the level of slogan — that a subject who arranges a fatal measurement will, in some branch of the wavefunction, always find himself alive, and that this is supposed to license a first-person expectation of survival — but I cannot get the physical vocabulary I actually think in to line up with the vocabulary the argument seems to require, and I increasingly suspect that the mismatch is the entire story rather than a mere expository nuisance.

I would therefore like you to restate the argument, and the principal objections to it, as faithfully as you can in the language I genuinely use: unitary evolution and the Schrödinger equation; decoherence and the emergence of a preferred basis; reduced density matrices and the tracing-out of the environment; records and correlations among subsystems; the Born rule together with the various attempts to derive or at least motivate it; and above all the measure problem — how one assigns weights to branches, and whether those weights are probabilities in any sense I would recognize from statistical mechanics. Treat an observer as I would treat it, namely as a subsystem whose memory and records have become correlated with a measurement outcome. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the quantum-immortality argument survive that translation intact, which survive only after being quietly altered, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all — in particular, what becomes of the "I" that is supposed to persist across branches, and of the claim that this persisting thing should expect to survive?

I would ask you to be genuinely self-critical rather than diplomatic: where the translation forces a choice, say which choice you made and why. And please close by telling me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you from the verdict that the residue is a defect of my physical vocabulary (a sign that the first-person claim is not a physical claim at all, or is not well-formed) to the verdict that it is instead a real gap in how physics represents observers, or the reverse.
