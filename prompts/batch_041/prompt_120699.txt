I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts about when one person is better or worse off than another, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The verdicts are mine and I hold them with the confidence noted; I care about the principle, not about what the cases are called.

1. Two patients with identical injuries, one dose of the only analgesic available, which will abolish one patient's pain and only halve the other's. I give it to the one in greater pain. This seems to me to need no argument.

2. Two people each lose a thousand dollars; one is a millionaire, the other has nothing else in the world. I say the second has lost more. I do not mean this as a metaphor. I mean that he has been made worse off by more, and that this is a straightforward comparison.

3. I can give one person a large improvement, or give two people the same improvement each, at the same cost. I choose the two, and I would choose the hundred. But if the choice is one person's large improvement against a barely detectable improvement for a great many people, I choose the one, without hesitation.

4. I would not make two people equal by making one of them worse off, even if no third party were affected. Equality seems to me to have no value of its own.

5. Suppose there is a being who gains more from consuming others' resources than they lose — enormously more, and without limit. I would still stop him. Whatever the sum of welfare is, I do not think it settles the case.

6. Two people report the same degree of satisfaction with their lives; one is healthy, one has a painful, progressive illness. I think the healthy one is better off, and that the ill one's report reflects a narrowed horizon as much as a genuine comparison. So I do not take self-report as the last word.

7. If two lives differ by a hair in every respect I can name, I do not think there is a fact about which is better. The comparison becomes meaningful only as the gap grows; below some resolution, the question lapses.

8. This is the one that worries me. I think I can rank any two people by how well off they are — that seems to me simply true. But I cannot say that the gap between the best-off and the middle is larger than the gap between the middle and the worst-off. The idea of measuring those gaps, or of one person's improvement being twice another's, seems to me to presuppose something I do not believe in.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is three principles in a trench coat: rank people by level; count a difference between two people as real only when it is large relative to the lives being compared; and when two such differences are real, favour the worse off unless the gain to others is much larger.

I would like you to test this against the list. Is the combination in 8 even coherent — can one have level comparisons without difference comparisons, and if so, exactly what does it forbid and what does it permit? If the loss in 2 is a real comparison of magnitudes, why is the gap comparison in 8 not equally real, and if it is not real, what is 2 doing? Which of my verdicts must a single principle give up, and which principle would give up the fewest? State the principle, or the small set of candidates, precisely, tie each clause to the case that forces it, and tell me honestly what it cannot accommodate. Then say what it implies for cases I have not listed: comparing the welfare of a person with dementia to that of the person he was ten years ago, comparing a dog's welfare to a child's, and a policy that helps the worst-off a little at great cost to the better-off.
