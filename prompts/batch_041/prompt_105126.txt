Construct a thought experiment as an isolation test: a single case in which strong longtermism and a moderate, present-prioritizing consequentialism return opposite verdicts, with the disagreement traceable to exactly one feature.

The case: a person with a fixed budget of money and effort must commit it to one of two projects. The first delivers a certain, modest, near-term benefit to identifiable people now living. The second delivers no near-term benefit at all; its entire effect is a small increase in the probability that civilization avoids a permanent, value-destroying collapse at some point in the coming century, and hence a small expected gain in the very long-run future. Set the magnitudes so the far-future gain is not astronomical — close enough to the near-term gain in expected value that the case cannot be settled by the sheer size of the future — so that the only thing pushing toward the second project is a commitment to time-neutrality plus expected-value maximization. Keep it realistic (an ordinary philanthropic or career decision, not a trolley), no science fiction, and no longer than a paragraph.

Then do the check: state the verdict strong longtermism gives and derive it from its commitments (time-neutral totalism, impartiality across times, expected-value maximization); state the verdict the moderate view gives and derive it from its commitments (a threshold or lexical priority for present people, or a small pure time discount); and show that the two verdicts hinge on that single axiological difference rather than on any empirical assumption about the future. Identify precisely which feature does the work; say what happens if you remove it (for instance, add a small pure time discount, or make the far-future effect so fragile that the expected-value calculation becomes unstable); and explain how a defender of the view that fares worse would attack the case.

Begin directly with the scenario itself, with no preface, commentary, or restatement of this request.
