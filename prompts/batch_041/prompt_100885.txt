I am trying to state, in a single principle, my views about how two parties should divide the gains from cooperating. My intuitions keep splitting into two camps, and I would like you to find the principle — or the smallest set of principles — that best fits the verdicts below, and to say plainly which of them it cannot keep.

Each case involves two parties, A and B. If they reach an agreement they divide a pot; if they do not, each receives a stated fallback. Where I give a split, it is the split I consider fair, not the one I expect them to reach.

Case 1. The pot is $100 and there are no fallbacks: if talks fail, each gets $0, and any division of the $100 is available. My verdict: $50 each.

Case 2. The pot is $100, and if talks fail A gets $60 while B gets $0. My verdict: $80 to A and $20 to B. Cooperation adds $40 to what the two would receive anyway, and I think that $40 should be divided equally.

Case 3. Exactly as in Case 2, with the fallbacks reversed: if talks fail A gets $0 and B gets $60. My verdict: $20 to A and $80 to B.

Case 4. The pot is $100 and there are no fallbacks, but the parties differ in how much of the pot they can absorb: A cannot end up with more than $30, B can absorb up to $100, and the two together cannot divide more than $100. My verdict: about $23 to A and about $77 to B. Here I do not want to give A its full $30. I want each party to fall short of its maximum by the same proportion: A is short by about $7 of $30, and B is short by about $23 of $100, and those are the same proportion.

Case 5. This is the case I cannot settle. The pot is $100, and if talks fail A gets $40 while B gets $0; neither party has a ceiling. I keep wavering between giving A $70 and giving A about $63, with B receiving the remainder.

Cases 2 and 3 pull me toward dividing the surplus over the fallbacks equally, while Case 4 pulls me toward making each party fall short of its maximum by the same fraction. I suspect these are two different ideas and that no single principle can honor both. Is there one — perhaps a principle I have not stated — that delivers my four settled verdicts and also decides Case 5? If there is, state it precisely, explain why it is not merely the union of my two hunches, and say what it implies for a case I have not described. If there is not, tell me which verdicts I must abandon and what the surviving principle is.
