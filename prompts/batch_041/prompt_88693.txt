Two of my coworkers, Ana and Ben, spent most of a lunch break arguing about a third coworker, Marcus, and I would like your help in sorting out who is right about what. Marcus has refused to contribute to a group gift for a departing employee whom he believes treated him dismissively, and he insists this is a matter of principle rather than personal pique: he says he will not lend his name to honoring someone who treated people badly. Ana says this is simply a bias — Marcus is holding a grudge, and the principle is a rationalization manufactured after the fact. Ben says it is a legitimate moral stance, or at least a legitimate preference, and that Ana is smuggling her own moral view into the supposedly neutral label "bias." Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Ana proposed a test: a reaction counts as a bias, she said, when the person holding it cannot give a reason that would be accepted by someone who was not already on their side; if the only defense is "it just feels wrong to me," we are dealing with a bias. By that standard Marcus is biased, since the only reason he can offer is his own reaction to how he was treated.

(2) Ben replied that this test proves far too much. Plenty of settled moral convictions — that it is wrong to betray a friend, say — cannot be justified to someone who does not already share them, yet we do not call those biases. If the test is "can you convince a skeptic," then every controversial moral view is a bias and only the uncontroversial ones qualify as genuine moral views, which just makes "bias" mean "moral view I happen not to hold."

(3) Ana said that was not her point. The test is not whether you can convert a skeptic but whether the reason you give is doing independent work or is merely a restatement of the feeling — and she thinks Marcus's reason is a restatement, because he would feel the same way regardless of what the coworker had actually done, so long as he had been slighted.

(4) Ben said the same could be said of loyalty, or of someone who refuses to shake the hand of a person who insulted their mother: there the reason and the feeling arrive together, and to demand that they come apart is to demand that moral commitments be motivationally inert, which is not a neutral standard but a substantive one. He then offered his own test: a moral stance is legitimate if the person would still hold it with no personal stake in the matter, and he claimed Marcus passes, since Marcus objects to anyone being honored after being dismissive, not merely to this coworker.

(5) Ana shot back that this is a counterfactual no one can check and precisely the kind of test we are all too skilled at passing in our own imagination; she added that if we cannot distinguish biases from preferences in principle, then "bias" becomes a mere word for views we dislike, and we lose the ability to call anything — racism, for instance — a bias rather than a taste. Ben answered that we draw the distinction the way we draw any other: by arguing about the reasons, case by case, without a master formula, and that the absence of a formula does not make the distinction arbitrary.

I could not referee this myself. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, adjudicating the exchange one move at a time — the first point on move (1), the second on move (2), and so on through move (5). For each move, say who has the better of it and why. If there is something neither of them has noticed, fold it into whichever point it most affects. Write in American English.
