So I'm in this seminar on science and religion — the kind where we read the classic texts — and last week it was Galileo's letters on scripture, the short one to Castelli from 1613 and the longer one to the Grand Duchess Christina from 1615. I came out of it more confused than when I went in. The Galileo in those letters is careful: scripture is written so that ordinary people can get the point, its business is salvation rather than astronomy (he keeps borrowing Cardinal Baronio's line about teaching us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go), and where a natural conclusion is genuinely demonstrated it's scripture that gets reinterpreted, not nature. But he also insists he's a loyal son of the church, he hedges constantly, and the thing he thought was a knock-down demonstration of the earth's motion — the tides — wasn't one. A friend of mine in the seminar keeps saying that if he'd lived today Galileo would have been a Dawkins-style firebrand, and I can't square that with the letters at all, but I also can't say exactly why not.

What I want from you is a prediction exercise, done with some discipline. Take Galileo as he actually comes across in those letters and tell me what he'd say about things that only turned up later: young-earth creationism and flood geology; the intelligent-design crowd who want their critique of evolution taught in public school biology; the modern biblical scholars who treat Genesis as a composite ancient text with older Near Eastern cousins rather than as a report; the rule that science should proceed by methodological naturalism — as if nature were all there is, as a matter of method rather than metaphysics; and the 19th-century "warfare" historians who made him the mascot of a war between science and religion. Would he sign onto the non-overlapping magisteria line, that science and religion answer different questions and should stop fighting? And if you had to drop him into that standard four-way typology from science-and-religion courses — conflict, independence, dialogue, integration — which box would he actually land in, as opposed to the one people keep putting him in?

Don't just give me bullet points of verdicts. Lay the reasoning out so I can see where it's load-bearing: which of his stated principles really force a prediction, which parts are just me extrapolating, and where his own situation makes the extrapolation shaky — he had no demonstration of heliocentrism in his day, he had an Inquisition to live under, and the church eventually came around on the very thing he was condemnded for, so I don't know how much of his caution was conviction and how much was circumstance. Tell me what about the modern world would plausibly have changed his mind rather than just handing him new ammunition. And finish with a bottom line: where he'd land, how confident you are, and the single strongest reason to think the whole exercise is a category error — I'd rather hear that than have you paper over it.
