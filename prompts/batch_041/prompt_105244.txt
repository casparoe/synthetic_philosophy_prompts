I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in the Dutch Republic in the 1670s, whose central figure is the lens-grinder who published the Theologico-Political Treatise without his name on it and never afterwards let himself be drawn out. I have been reading the book slowly, and I have arrived at a difficulty I cannot settle by rereading, so I would rather argue it out with the man himself than with my own notes. Please play Spinoza for me. Speak in the first person, reason only from the concepts and vocabulary available to you in the 1670s and from what you have actually written, and stay in the role until I say stop — do not step outside it to comment on the state of the question or on how later readers have taken you. Concede a point if I make one, but do not surrender your position merely to be agreeable; if you need some fact about my century, or about the novel I am writing, that you could not know, ask for it as you would ask it. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than two hundred words, so that this feels like two people talking across a table and not a treatise.

Here is my opening, and I mean it as a real objection. You end the Treatise by praising democracy as the most natural form of state and the one most consonant with liberty, and I can see why a book in defence of the freedom to philosophise should finish on that note. But the argument that carried you there seems to cut against the conclusion. You have told me that right extends exactly as far as power, so that whoever has the power has the right; that the multitude is moved by passion rather than by reason, and that no one is made free by being commanded, since command reaches only the outward act and not the mind; and that the sovereign's authority over the public exercise of religion must be absolute, or the commonwealth dissolves. Put those three together and democracy begins to look like nothing more than a state in which the strongest passions happen to be shared by the greatest number — no freer than a monarchy that keeps the crowd in order, only louder and more crowded. So tell me plainly: in what sense is the democratic commonwealth more natural, or more free, than the monarchy you allow to be workable? Or is the praise of democracy a flourish you permitted yourself at the close of a book whose real business was obedience?
