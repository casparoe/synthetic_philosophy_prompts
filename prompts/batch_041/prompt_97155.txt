I'm a doctoral student in the history and philosophy of science, and something about the way the sciences present themselves has been nagging at me. Pick up almost any introductory textbook or museum exhibit, or look at how research divisions and funding lines are organized, and you find the same picture: a ladder of levels running from particles or fields up through atoms, molecules, cells, organisms, and on to minds and societies, with the lower rungs described as more fundamental and the higher ones as resting on them. The vocabulary is everywhere. Work is called more fundamental the further down the ladder it sits; higher-level phenomena are said to be nothing over and above the lower-level goings-on; fundamental physics is treated as the deepest inquiry there is. And yet the very same institutions fund and celebrate research on emergent, higher-level phenomena as though those were just as real and just as worth understanding. I want to know what view of grounding and fundamentality this whole way of speaking and organizing presupposes. Does the practice commit anyone to the claim that lower levels really do ground or generate the higher ones, in the metaphysician's sense of a non-causal, asymmetric dependence relation, or is it merely a convenient heuristic for inquiry that leaves the underlying metaphysics open? When a level is called lower, is that a claim about composition, about dependence, or both at once, and does the practice notice when those come apart? And is the word fundamental here doing the technical work of tracking what depends on what, or is it quietly smuggling in most important or smallest?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, then tell me which parts the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is underdetermined, that is, where several rival accounts of grounding and fundamentality would license exactly the same textbooks, funding lines, and rhetoric. Bring in at least one rival that would rearrange the picture, such as the view that the whole is prior to its parts, and say what would actually have to change in the practice, not just in the philosophy, if we adopted it.

Please be direct with me. If my puzzle rests on a confusion, for instance if I'm sliding between fundamentality, scale, and explanatory importance in a way that makes the question ill-posed, say so plainly rather than reconstructing a tidy theory to fit a muddle.
