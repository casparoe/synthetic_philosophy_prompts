I taught physical chemistry for 34 years and retired in 2022. Since then I've been sitting in on the third-year theory of knowledge course at my old university, mainly because I kept turning up and putting my hand up, and eventually the convener gave in and asked me to write one of the questions for the final. So what I need from you is: the exam question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. It's a two-hour paper with four questions, so this one has to be answerable in about 40 minutes; the students are third-years, they'll have done the standard material on belief, justification and knowledge, but they're not specialists.

Here's the angle, which is the thing that's been bugging me since I started reading this stuff. The lottery paradox and the preface paradox get taught as two faces of one problem, and the textbook escape from the lottery one is always something like: you can't rationally believe your ticket will lose, because you've got no evidence at all about that particular ticket — all you know is that the odds are long, and long odds in a symmetric setup don't single out any ticket. I think that move works. What I don't see is what it does for the preface. When I was active I published a bit over 150 papers, and I'd have defended every claim in every one of them on the evidence I had; and I knew perfectly well then, and know now, that some of those claims are wrong. I keep thinking these are two seperate problems. In the preface case I had specific evidence for each individual claim, whereas in the lottery I had nothing about the individual ticket except the odds.

So the question has to make them do two things. First, say exactly which assumption has to be given up to avoid the contradiction in each case — I know there are three or four candidates on the usual menu, and I want them to pick one and say why, not just recite the menu. Second, say whether the escape that works for the lottery transfers to the preface, and if it doesn't, what that shows. My own hunch is that the honest conclusion is that ordinary all-or-nothing believing, together with the rule that if you believe two things you believe them both together, can't be made coherent, and that you need graded credences instead — but write the question so that a student who argues the other way can still recieve full marks if they do it properly.

Two more things I want them to have to engage with, because they're where I get stuck:

(a) I invented an unfair version of the lottery for the class and the paradox seemed to fizzle. 1000 tickets, one of them with a 90% chance of winning, the other 999 splitting the remaining 10%. Does that show the whole thing was really about symmetry and the absence of evidence rather than about high probability, or did I set it up wrong? I honestly don't know, so the model answer should take a definite position rather than hedge.

(b) If you accept that a rational person can hold beliefs she knows can't all be true, what happens to the rule that you should only assert what you believe? She can't just stop asserting anything. I'd like a definite answer here too.

Please keep the marking scheme free of name-dropping: if a candidate attributes a view to someone without saying what the view is, that earns nothing, and nothing in the question should depend on the students remembering who said what, since they can't look anything up.

For the marking scheme: 20 marks total, five must-hit points, the marks for each, and one line per point on how candidates typically miss it. The top band should be reserved for answers that commit to a position on the lottery/preface asymmetry and defend it against the obvious objection, rather than answers that list the four ways out and sit on the fence.
