I'm a philosopher of the social sciences, and I've been drafted onto the steering committee of a small interdisciplinary working group on democratic backsliding: four political scientists, a constitutional lawyer, two historians, and me, with a two-year postdoc line and a modest workshop budget to allocate. Our regional coverage is uneven in a way that matters — two people know Hungary and Poland well, one works on Turkey, one on the United States, and none of us reads the others' primary sources in the original — and the committee has already spent two meetings on the reflex that the field seems to share, which is to argue about the definition and then immediately about measurement. Before we commit the postdoc to a topic, I want a defensible agenda rather than a taxonomy, and I would rather find out now which disagreements are substantive and which are just the ordinary politics of the people doing the arguing.

To be concrete about the dispute we keep re-litigating. I have been pushing a criterion I call the reversibility test: a polity has backslid, on this view, to the extent that the cost of returning to some earlier constitutional equilibrium has risen relative to what it would have cost to preserve or restore that equilibrium at the earlier moment. The political scientists object that this smuggles in a normative baseline — which equilibrium, chosen by whom, and on whose authority — and that it makes backsliding a matter of continuous degree in a way that lets the cases we most care about come out looking like ordinary constitutional churn. The lawyer says it collapses into a claim about entrenchment and adds nothing; the historians say it is just consolidation with the sign flipped, and that it is precisely the kind of state-centered, court-centered concept that cannot see how backsliding looks from below, in schools, local government, or the press. Meanwhile we cannot even agree on the comparison set: one colleague insists the United States belongs in it and another thinks including it is the clearest sign that the whole research program has become a vehicle for partisan sentiment. I would like you to treat both the reversibility test and the labels the field has generated over the past two decades — competitive authoritarianism, autocratic legalism, constitutional retrogression, executive aggrandizement, democratic deconsolidation — as candidate organizing ideas rather than settled ones.

So: which open questions about democratic backsliding should this group put its two years and its postdoc on, and in what order? I care much less about the list than about the reasoning. For each question you propose, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for a group with exactly our mix of competences and no ability to run surveys or field experiments, and how neglected it is relative to the questions that already attract funding. Tell me whether the worry that the concept is little more than a partisan label is itself a research question worth the postdoc's time or a distraction we should refuse; tell me who, if anyone, is entitled to adjudicate what counts as backsliding, and whether that is a philosophical question or an empirical one; and tell me which of the criteria I have just named — importance, tractability, neglectedness, upstreamness — you are actually using and where they conflict, since I suspect my own ordering of them is unstable. Finally, say what evidence or argument over the next year would move a question up or down the list, and be specific enough that we could notice it happening. Please structure the response as follows: a section titled Framing (roughly 200 words) on what is genuinely in dispute and whether the concept's contestedness is a research problem or a political one; a section titled The candidate questions (roughly 500 words) laying out the shortlist, each with a one-sentence statement; a section titled The ranking (roughly 800 words) giving the defended priority order, with the criteria made explicit and the trade-offs between them acknowledged; a section titled What would change the ranking (roughly 300 words); and a closing section titled Bottom line (no more than 120 words) naming the single thing you would fund first and the single thing you would refuse to fund at all.
