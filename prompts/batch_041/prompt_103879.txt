I've been drafting a short paper on collective intentionality, and a friend who works on social ontology handed me an argument he says shows the whole topic is incoherent. It came up because I'd been using a jazz duo as an example — a saxophonist and a bassist each listening and adjusting, producing a line neither could have played alone — and asking what makes that one action rather than two actions that merely happen to fit. Here is the argument as he set it out. (1) A joint action is an action done by two or more agents together, and it is done together only if it is guided by a shared intention. (2) A shared intention is nothing over and above the participants' individual intentions; it is realized in those intentions standing in the right relation — each participant intends to do his or her part, and each does so in a way that is responsive to what the others intend, so that each intends to do his or her part on condition that the others do theirs. (3) For A's intention to be responsive to B's in the way (2) demands, A must form her intention with reference to B's intention, and B must form his with reference to A's; so A's intention depends on B's, and B's on A's. (4) Where two things each depend on the other, neither can be complete until the other is; each must await the other. (5) A relation of dependence that can be discharged only by an endless awaiting is never discharged. (6) So neither musician ever arrives at the intention that (1) requires. (7) So the duo is not performing a joint action — and, generalized, there is no such thing.

What stops me is not doubt about (7) but how much rejecting it seems to cost. The same reasoning would show that two people who exchange promises never form the intentions their promises require, that a conversation is never a single exchange of meaning, and that no committee ever decides anything together. I would rather keep (7) false than keep any one of the premises, but I don't want to pay more than I must. So tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it doesn't, which premise is least costly to give up. Be concrete about the price. If I drop (4), what does that commit me to about how individual intentions are individuated — can two intentions be mutually dependent without either waiting on the other, and if so, in what sense is the dependence real? And does dropping (4) leave any room for the shared intention to be more than a tidy summary of what each person privately intends, or does it quietly collapse the collective back into the individual? If instead I give up (2) and admit the shared intention as a genuine further item, what account do I owe that an individualist about intentions won't simply wave away as a reified fiction? I'm less interested in being told the regress is "benign" than in being shown exactly which step trades on a reading the surrounding premises don't support.
