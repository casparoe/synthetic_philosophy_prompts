I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a philosopher drawn unmistakably from the later Wittgenstein — the voice, the method, the refusal of theory. In the chapter I am drafting he is brought, by a device I need not trouble you with, into a present-day department, where a doctoral student presents him with the study most often credited with launching experimental philosophy: Joshua Knobe's questionnaire of 2003, in which the head of a company is told that a new programme will raise profits and damage the environment, replies that he does not care in the least about the damage, and proceeds. Some four respondents in five say that he damaged the environment intentionally; only about a quarter say the same of a counterpart who knowingly brings about an improvement to which he is equally indifferent. The student's own project sits inside a larger enterprise — questionnaires put to ordinary speakers, samples drawn across cultures, worries about replication, and the claim that questions of this kind are to be settled by discovering what people are inclined to say — and she has added a variant of her own in which the side effect is neither good nor bad, expecting that respondents will then see no intention at all, since she takes the asymmetry to track evaluation rather than knowledge.

My difficulty is that I must not make him a ventriloquist's dummy for my own opinions, nor put into his mouth anything that a reader who knows the texts would find false. He died in 1951, half a century before any of this existed, so nothing can be recovered from anything he wrote on the subject; whatever I give him must be inferred from what he did write and from the manner of his thinking. What I want from you is the most defensible prediction the evidence allows of what he would have made of all this, together with a frank account of where the evidence runs out. In particular:

1. Which of his actual commitments bear on the case — his treatment of intention and of first-person avowals, his insistence that meaning is use, his suspicion of philosophy conducted as a quasi-science, his habit of attending to the circumstances and the language-game rather than to an inner state, his diagnosis of how perplexity arises from language itself. Separate what is genuinely in the texts from what is merely his reputation.
2. What, if anything, he would concede. Is there a version of the finding he might find interesting, or even a confirmation of something he already held?
3. What he would refuse, and on what grounds: the questionnaire, the aggregation of answers into a result, the assumption that a survey could settle a philosophical question, the treatment of a concept as something whose contours may be mapped by varying one factor at a time. If he would not answer the student's question but reframe it, show how.
4. Where his texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, what the best guess turns on, and what would tip it.
5. The anachronism traps — the places where I am most likely to give him a speech that sounds like him but is in truth a projection of present-day disputes, such as having him defend ordinary language in a vocabulary that postdates him.
6. Finally, the first move he would make in the scene: not a summary of a position, but the opening thing he says, something an actor could deliver.

Please write in British English, and do not hedge everything into mush: where the prediction is firm, be firm; where it is not, say plainly what it would take to settle the matter.
