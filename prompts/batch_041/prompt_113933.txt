Consider the familiar complaint that the standard possible-worlds semantics of knowledge makes every knower logically omniscient: on that semantics an agent knows every logical truth, knows whatever is entailed by what she knows, and knows that she knows. Write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words arguing to a verdict on what this complaint shows.

Three readings of the complaint are available, and I want them kept apart. On the first, the omniscience result is an idealization of the same kind as a frictionless plane: real knowers are resource-bounded, the semantics abstracts from that, and the abstraction is legitimate insofar as the phenomena we care about are robust under relaxing it. On the second, the result is an artifact of representing propositions as sets of worlds and knowledge as an accessibility relation, so that a change of representation — awareness sets, impossible worlds, syntactic or procedural models — removes it. On the third, the result exposes something about the concept rather than the model: there is no coherent notion of a body of knowledge that is not closed under entailment, and the appearance of a problem comes from conflating knowledge with belief, with what is explicitly represented, or with what can be extracted in time.

In reaching your verdict, do at least the following. Treat the closure principles separately — knowledge of all validities, closure under known entailment, closure under conjunction, positive introspection — and say of each whether you take it to be constitutive of knowledge, an artifact of the formalism, or a substantive but revisable thesis. Assess the standard repairs and say whether they repair the semantics or merely rename the problem; in particular, say what the awareness-based approaches owe us in the way of an account of awareness itself, and whether impossible worlds address the problem of knowing too many logical truths or only the problem of tolerating inconsistency. Test the idealization reading against a hard case: an agent whose entailment relation is undecidable and who has no guarantee that any given inference will terminate, or an agent who knows each of infinitely many instances of a schema but cannot grasp the generalization. Finally, ask what would discriminate between the three readings, and whether part of the disagreement is verbal — that is, whether the technical term in the semantics answers to the vernacular concept at all.

I want a defended conclusion, not a survey. If you conclude that the problem is genuine only under one reading, say which one, and say what the other two readings were really tracking. Do not quote or cite the literature and do not supply a reading list; reconstruct the positions in your own words, and let the argument carry the weight.
