For most of a long career in experimental chemistry I treated it as the most innocent of truisms that the future would resemble the past — that next month's reagents would behave as last month's had — and it never once occurred to me to ask what that sentence actually asserts. Having lately taken up philosophy, I sat in on a seminar on induction where a participant closed off a discussion of Goodman's grue puzzle with the remark that we need not trouble ourselves, since nature is uniform. I could not tell whether I had just heard a deep truth, a tautology, or a way of changing the subject, and I would like to be able to say which.

Please treat the slogan 'nature is uniform' — together with its near relative, 'the future will resemble the past' — as a piece of language to be disassembled, and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making. I would like the readings sorted along the axes that seem to matter: what exactly is claimed to be uniform (the course of events, the laws, the kinds, the projectibility of our predicates, the reliability of inductive method); whether the claim is about the world, about our evidence, or about a rule we adopt; and whether it is offered as an empirical discovery, an a priori truth, or a methodological stipulation. For each reading, give me a verdict — trivially true, substantive and defensible, false, or not really truth-evaluable at all — and tell me whether, if it held, it would genuinely license the inference from green-so-far to green-next in a way that survives the grue construction, or whether it only relocates the difficulty. I am especially interested in which single reading the seminar participant must have had in mind for his remark to do the work he wanted it to do, and in whether that reading is one a working scientist can honestly hold. Lay the options out as a menu I can keep, rather than settling on the one you favour.
