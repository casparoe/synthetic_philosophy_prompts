I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I also run a small graduate seminar on self-trust and epistemic circularity, for which I'm now writing the take-home final. I need a single essay question that a well-read student cannot dispatch by reciting the familiar bootstrapping problem — the inference from particular beliefs to the reliability of the faculty that produced them — or the standard charge that reliabilism lets us know our faculties are reliable too easily. I want the question to turn on a specific proposal, so that answering it demands argument rather than recall.

The proposal, floated by a colleague and not to be presupposed either right or wrong by the question, is what she calls an independence-of-source constraint: a process may be used to license a belief about its own reliability only when the licensing evidence comes from a source other than that process. I have built a small case to hang the question on. A man checks a weather app each morning and finds its forecasts right most days over the course of a year. The app also displays a reliability figure — that it has been 92 percent accurate over the past twelve months — and when asked why he trusts it, he cites that figure. The figure is computed by the app from its own forecast history, and nothing outside the app corroborates it. I want students to decide whether his trust is bootstrapping of the objectionable kind, and whether the proposed constraint resolves that worry or merely relabels it.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 650 to 750 words, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth twenty marks in total, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically go wrong. Pitch it so that a strong answer must separate the order-of-knowing worry from a bare circularity charge, and must say whether the app case is or is not helped by the fact that the man also observes the outcomes himself. Keep the question answerable in about seventy minutes.
