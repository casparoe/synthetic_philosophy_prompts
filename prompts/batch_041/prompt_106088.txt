I'm a novelist — three years into a book set on a made-up island city-state that is obviously a stand-in for Singapore, which is how I've ended up reading a stupid ammount of political science and journalism for a novel that has maybe nine pages of politics in it. Anyway, this has left me with a question I can't shake.

Here's the pattern. When you ask people in a place like that to defend the arrangement — not the ones on the payroll, just ordinary educated people who've done fine under it — they almost never say "it's just." They say "look at what we've got." Which is fair enough. But when you push, "look what we've got" splits into two versions that are doing very different work. One is comparative: look where we were in 1965, look at the neighbours, look at what everyone else did with their independence. The other is about the slope: it keeps getting better, each generation does better than the last, the trains go further every year, and the same thing shows up in the China arguments where "we lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty" is meant to close the conversation.

The second version is the one I keep chewing on, because it seems to have a built-in flaw. If what justifies the whole setup is that it keeps improving things, then it can never arrive. Arriving is the same as failing. A plateau at a very high standard of living would read as a crisis — and it would read as a crisis even if nobody's life actually got worse, purely because the line went flat. I've noticed the same shape in how companies talk about themselves, and, honestly, in my own marriage.

Is there a name for this? I know people say "performance legitimacy," and I've seen "output legitimacy" used in contrast to something else (input?), but as far as I can tell those are about where a justification comes from, not about this treadmill quality — that the justification requires the improvement to continue and so can't be satisfied by any level of performance. Am I wrong? And is the treadmill just the same as that thing I half-remember about people getting angrier as things get better? That feels like a different animal — that's about what people feel, and I'm asking about the logic of the claim itself.

Don't give me a menu. I can get a list of candidate terms from anywhere and I'm not going to read a reading list either. Tell me which one you actually think fits, what it fails to cover, and if nothing covers it, name the leftover yourself and give me the definition and where the edges are. If you think I've got it wrong and this is just ordinary politics — every incumbent anywhere gets judged on the trend, nothing special about these regimes — then say that and tell me what would show I'm wrong. A definite answer I can argue with beats a survey.
