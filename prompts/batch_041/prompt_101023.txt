For a philosophy club presentation I have been asked to set out my settled views on mental content so that the other members can press me on them, and I would like a consistency check before I defend the package in public. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Content is natural and teleological. What a representational state represents is fixed by its biological function, meaning the job it was selected to perform: the conditions under which tokens of that state were selected to be produced and to be consumed by the systems that use them.

(2) Misrepresentation is possible. A state can be about some feature of the world even when that feature is absent, and can be flatly wrong, and this is part of what separates a representation from a mere reliable indicator.

(3) Content is determinate. For any given representational state there is one best answer to the question of what it is about, rather than a spread of answers that are all equally correct.

(4) Human beings routinely have representations with content that no ancestor of ours was ever selected to produce: thoughts about electrons, about prime numbers, about the outcome of a particular election. I take these to have real content, and I want to say that this is not an exception to (1) but an extension of it. Selection can be cultural as well as biological, since we inherit representational practices much as we inherit anything else, and a state can acquire its job from what it was selected to do in a cultural lineage rather than a genetic one.

(5) A swampman, meaning a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person assembled by chance with no evolutionary or cultural history behind it, has no mental content at all, however convincingly it behaves.

I do not believe I have smuggled in anything inconsistent, but I am aware that the package is doing a great deal of work at once, and I would rather discover a fault now than in front of an audience. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Please rank the real tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad; for each one, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me; and then tell me which single change would resolve the most while giving up the least.
