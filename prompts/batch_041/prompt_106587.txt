Last night a friend and I spent most of a bottle of wine on the question of whether anything we think or want ever actually makes anything happen, and we ended on opposite sides of the table: she holds that the exclusion argument is a genuine reductio and that everything short of plain physicalism is wishful thinking, while I want to keep the mental in the causal order without surrendering the causal closure of physics. Rather than another essay, I would like you to settle the shape of the dispute for me with a tier list.

Place the following candidate replies to the exclusion problem into tiers, ordered from the one that handles it best to the one that handles it worst, and give each a score out of ten for how well it preserves the three commitments I list below:

- type identity theory, with mental properties simply being physical properties;
- token identity combined with irreducibly non-physical mental properties, the standard non-reductive physicalist package;
- epiphenomenalism, embraced rather than avoided;
- emergentism with genuine downward causation;
- interactionist substance dualism;
- the reply that mental and physical descriptions are not in competition for one and the same causal role, so exclusion is an artefact of asking which of two descriptions is the cause;
- the reply that the exclusion argument is only as strong as the Humean, production-style theory of causation it presupposes, and that it dissolves once causation is understood counterfactually or interventionistically;
- the reply that the mental is no worse off than any other higher-level kind, since the special sciences generally have this problem and nobody concludes that biology or economics is epiphenomenal;
- and eliminativism or illusionism about the mental, which buys a clean physical story by giving up on mental causation altogether.

The three commitments I am not willing to negotiate are these: mental properties are not identical with physical properties; mental properties are causally efficacious, not merely correlated with or realised by what is efficacious; and physics is causally closed. My suspicion, which I would like you either to confirm or to correct, is that no entry on that list keeps all three, so that the ranking is really a ranking of which commitment each view quietly drops.

Then answer the following as a single number: what fraction of my credence should go to the view that the whole problem is an artefact of a theory of causation we ought to abandon rather than a fact about minds? Give me the number and defend it.

Finally, tell me which entry on the list my friend has actually committed herself to, whether she realises it or not, and then take a definite position of your own: name the one entry you would bet on, state which of my three commitments you are giving up in order to hold it, and give your reasons. I do not want to be told that it depends on which theory of causation I favour, and I do not want the options laid out for me a second time.
