I've been kicking around an idea for a short paper in accuracy-first epistemology and I want your take before I try to write it up properly.

Background so you know what I'm after: the accuracy-first crowd says the only thing that makes a credence good, epistemically speaking, is how accurate it is, i.e. how close it comes to the truth values of the propositions it's about. You pick a scoring rule (Brier, log, whatever), you argue that a rational credence function is one that isn't accuracy-dominated, and out pops probabilism, and then with extra machinery you get conditionalization and the Principal Principle and so on. The scoring rule has to be strictly proper, otherwise the honest credence isn't the one that maximizes expected accuracy.

Now the idea, which is where I get unsure. All of this treats accuracy as distance from the truth value: if p is true, a credence of 1 in p scores perfectly and 0.9 scores well, but there's no credit for being "almost right" in the sense of being close to the actual world. So what if I score a credence against a similarity-weighted cloud of worlds instead of the single actual world? Utility of credence function c at world w is the sum over worlds w' of sim(w,w') times S(c, v(w')), where sim is some similarity measure and S is a standard proper scoring rule. Intuition: a credence that puts its mass on worlds near the actual one should count as more accurate than one that puts its mass on far-away worlds, even if both are equally wrong about the truth values.

Here's what I think happens and I'd like you to check it. Once you blur the scoring target, the resulting rule isn't proper anymore with respect to the original algebra of propositions. An agent maximizing expected blurred accuracy is being scored against a smeared distribution, and the thing she'll end up reporting looks a lot like a credence function over a coarser-grained proposition space. Worse, the smeared target is itself a function of her own credences, so the scoring looks circular. So either (a) the blur just changes the subject, you're now doing accuracy about coarse propositions and nothing deep has happened, or (b) if you insist the propositions stay fine-grained, you lose propriety and the whole dominance argument for probabilism falls apart, since a non-proper rule can be gamed by someone who reports something other than her true credences. I lean toward (a), i.e. the truthlikeness-flavoured version of accuracy-first is just ordinary accuracy-first on a coarse algebra in disguise, but I can't tell whether that's a real result or whether I've just built the blur into the setup in a way that begs the question.

Second thing: even if the formalism works out, I'm not sure the motivation survives. If what you care about is getting close to the truth in the "almost right" sense, isn't that a different epistemic good than accuracy, and doesn't accuracy-first just deny it rather than absorb it? I want to know if there's a version of the proposal that keeps accuracy as the sole good while still giving partial credit for near-misses, or whether that's a contradiction in terms.

Can you give me comments on whether the core argument is sound and whether the paper is worth writing? Dont worry about style, I'll deal with that later. I mostly want to know if the collapse-into-coarse-grained-accuracy move is right, and if not, where it breaks.

Lead with your overall verdict on the idea in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, and skip any preamble or restating of what I've said.
