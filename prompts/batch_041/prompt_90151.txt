Okay, here's a view I'm pretty much sold on, and I want you to try and talk me out of it — or at least show me where it's exposed. It's the coherence argument for expected utility maximization: an agent who doesn't maximize expected utility isn't merely suboptimal, she's incoherent, because her own preferences can be turned against her. The classic money pump drains someone with a cycle in her preferences down to a sure loss, and the Dutch book does the same kind of thing to inconsistent credences. So rationality requires maximizing expected utility, full stop.

What I want to know is what would count against that. Not "people are bad at probability" — real defeaters. Which assumptions is the argument actually resting on (completeness of preferences? transitivity? that the trades are costless? that the agent keeps honoring the same preferences over time instead of wising up or committing to a plan), and for each one, what would have to be true of the agent or the world for it to fail? Are there agents who genuinely violate expected utility maximization but can't actually be pumped — cases where the pump only runs because we rigged the offers or assumed the agent never learns? And what about the small-world problem, where nobody's preferences are even defined over all the grand-world acts the theory seems to need?

Then tell me honestly which of these the defenders of the coherence argument themselves treat as real costs and which they just shrug off. Rank the defeaters from the ones that would actually sink the thesis to the ones that are just an itch. I'd much rather you draw a hard line between what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff than paper over it. American English, please.
