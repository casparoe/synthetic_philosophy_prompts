I am a third-year PhD student in value theory, and I need a short research proposal — three pages, for a competitive internal summer grant — on a problem I have been circling for months without being able to state it cleanly. I would like you to help me state it and then plan the work.

The view I am trying to defend is what I have been calling lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: the position that below some level of welfare the relief of suffering takes lexical priority over any quantity of pleasure — no amount of mild enjoyment compensates for one episode of agony — while above that level ordinary trade-offs between pleasure and pain are permitted. The standing objection to any view of this shape is that it presupposes the commensurability of pleasure and pain: to place a threshold on a scale, the objection runs, you must already have a single scale, and once you have it the lexical priority looks arbitrary rather than principled.

My half-formed idea is that this objection equivocates between two things I want to pull apart. Call the first unit commensurability: pleasure and pain are measured in a common cardinal unit, so they can be added and any amount of one can in principle be exchanged for a fixed amount of the other at a constant rate. Call the second threshold comparability: there is a well-defined level at which the lexical priority switches off. My suspicion is that lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism needs only the second, that the standard objection refutes only the first, and that the real work lies in characterizing the second without smuggling the first back in. The difficulty, which I take to be the actual project, is that the threshold will be vague in the cases that matter. I have been toying with a case in which a fixed number of people can each be given a modest pleasure, or one person can be spared an agony, and the number of pleasure-recipients is adjustable: the view seems to say there is some number at which we should switch from sparing the agony to distributing the pleasure, yet nothing in the view appears to fix it, and I cannot tell whether that indeterminacy is a defect of the view or an artifact of asking the threshold to do unit-commensurability work.

Turn this into a proposal with the following parts. First, a statement of the central question sharp enough that it has a determinate answer, not a gesture at a topic. Second, an account of why the existing positions in this area leave that question open, framed in terms of the arguments and commitments at stake rather than the literature — do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, since I will supply those myself. Third, the method: what I would actually do across a summer, month by month, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternative of simply stipulating a threshold and defending the stipulation. Fourth, what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing. Fifth, an honest assessment of whether this is too much for one summer and, if so, which part to cut. The angle throughout should be the unit/threshold distinction and the vagueness problem, not a standard defense or rejection of negative utilitarianism.

Begin with the proposal itself, opening with a single sentence that states the project's central claim, then the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question.
