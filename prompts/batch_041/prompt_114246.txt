I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am to give the toast at the ninetieth-birthday dinner of my oldest friend. She has spent her working life inside Latin poetry; she has lately lost most of her sight and most of her walking, and she will sit at the head of the table listening with the exacting ear she has always had. The room will be about half family, who will be lost if I begin naming books, and half a small cluster of classicists who have read everything she has read and will notice the instant I sentimentalize. I would like a speech of roughly six hundred words — warm, occasionally funny, and not evasive.

I want it built on the old Ciceronian defense of old age, and then to withhold part of that defense. In the dialogue, Cato is made to answer four standard complaints — that age removes one from public life, that it ruins the body, that it takes away pleasure, that it brings death close — and he answers them with a serenity that belongs to a vigorous eighty-four-year-old invented by a man of sixty-three who had not yet grown old himself. Let the toast take up those four complaints in order and concede the third and the fourth rather than answer them. I am not going to tell a woman who can no longer read that she has been freed from wanting, and I am not going to console her with the immortality of the soul, which she has never believed. Whatever consolation the speech finds must come from somewhere else.

One image of Cato's I want to keep and turn: that nature, having composed the earlier acts of a life so carefully, cannot have botched the last one like a lazy playwright. I would like the speech to arrive there at the end and to make that image stand for people who no longer trust the playwright. Nothing should depend on quotation or citation; I will have no text in front of me.
