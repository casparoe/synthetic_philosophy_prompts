I'm a novelist — third book, and it's mostly about people and their jobs — and there's a move in arguments about work that I keep running into and don't have a word for. It's driving me a little nuts. Let me describe it, because I don't want to just get pointed at the Protestant work ethic and sent on my way.

Here's the shape. Somebody works insane hours and asks for more money. The defence is: this is labour, it's a burden, it wrecks your body, obviously it should be compensated. Same person, a few years later, asks to go down to four days. Now the same voices say: but you love this, it's your calling, people would kill to do what you do — you're not really working, so what are you complaining about? The description of the identical activity flips depending on which conclusion is wanted. Burden when the claim is pay, blessing when the claim is time.

Second instance, and this is the one that shows up in my own life. Rest only counts as legitimate when it's secretly instrumental. A nap is okay because it's recovery for tomorrow. A walk is okay because you get ideas. A hobby is okay if it might become a side hustle. But rest that has no output at all — the genuine kind, lying in a hammock doing nothing — gets treated as slightly shameful, and the person taking it gets called lazy, while the person who never takes it gets called a martyr, or in the case I keep reading about in Japan, just drops dead and everyone calls it karoshi and moves on. Meanwhile France legislates a 35-hour week and half the commentary treats the extra day as a theft from someone.

So it's two things tangled up, I think: the flip where the same work is re-described as burden or blessing to suit the argument, and the rule that leisure is only respectable if it's actually work in disguise. I'm fairly sure "Protestant work ethic" isn't the specific thing — that's about a duty to labour, not about this convenient re-description — and "hustle culture" is just a vibe, not a mechanism.
