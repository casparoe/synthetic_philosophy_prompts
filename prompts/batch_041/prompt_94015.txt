I am a senior at a public high school taking a film-and-philosophy elective, and for my final paper I have been asked to read the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men — the adaptation of Cormac McCarthy's novel, whose title is borrowed from a Yeats poem about old age and the body's decay — through the problem of moral luck that Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel opened up in the 1970s. Our teacher introduced the problem with the two lorry drivers who both doze off at the wheel, only one of whom happens to run over a child, and with Nagel's inventory of the ways luck reaches into what we are and do: into the results of our actions, into the situations we happen to face, and into the character and temperament we never chose. The control principle — the thought that we can be assessed only for what was up to us — strikes me as obviously true, and yet the film's world seems built to make it impossible to apply, which makes me suspect the film is doing something more interesting than illustrating a paradox. What I want from you is a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously; I will use it to organize my own essay rather than to substitute for it.

Specifically, I want to know which kind of luck each of the film's central moments dramatizes, and where the film complicates the taxonomy rather than fitting it. Moss's decision to go back at night with the water is a choice whose entire moral significance is hostage to what he cannot see coming; his death happens offstage and is caused by someone else, so that we are denied even the consolation of watching him meet it; Chigurh's coin tosses, first at the gas station and then with Carla Jean, hand the outcome of a life over to chance, and she refuses to let the coin speak for her, which seems to me the most philosophically loaded moment in the film; and Bell's two dreams and his retirement amount to a man trying to stop being the sort of creature who holds others to account at all. Does the film care whether anyone deserves blame, or is it more interested in Williams's thought that a person's relation to what she has done is not settled by whether others are justified in blaming her — a regret that attaches to the deed itself rather than merely to the guilt? And does Chigurh's coin really illustrate luck, or is it a device for refusing to be an agent, a way of converting the fact that outcomes are not up to him into a rule he obeys so that he never has to be the one who decides? If the latter, is there something the philosophical debate underplays — namely, that the interesting question is not whether luck undermines responsibility but what people do with the discovery that luck governs outcomes? I also want you to say where the film resists this whole reading. It may be trading on fate or necessity rather than on luck, which are not the same thing, and the Yeats allusion plus Chigurh's air of inevitability make that confusion tempting. My teacher warned us against treating the film as a morality play with a thesis, so if its attitude is genuinely unresolved, tell me so and explain what the unresolvedness is doing.

A few constraints. I have no internet access while I work on this, and I do not need page numbers, line references, or a bibliography — describe scenes and characters rather than quoting dialogue, and correct me if I have misremembered a scene. Please do not reduce the film to an illustration of a philosophical position, and do not bend the position to fit the film; I would rather have an honest verdict that a particular scene supports the theory, complicates it, or simply sits outside it. Finally, close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not — whether it dramatizes a case the literature has not adequately described, or exposes a cost of taking moral luck seriously that the essays leave out.
