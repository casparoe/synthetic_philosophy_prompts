So my girlfriend's friends are all gay guys and they call each other the f-word constantly — affectionately, the way you'd say "girl." I knew this, it's not new to me. But my old buddy Dan, who's doing an MA in linguistics, was over at her place last week, somebody used it, Dan did a little wince, and it turned into a full argument that I had to sit through. Now I honestly don't know what to think, and there's also a practical thing for me at the end, because I'm not in that group and I still have to decide whether I'm allowed to say it.

Heres the fight, roughly.

Dan said the insult isn't a message you tack onto the word, it's part of what the word means, full stop. You can't use it and then go "no offense, I mean it nicely" any more than you can call someone a bachelor and add "but he's married" — tacking the caveat on just sounds broken. That's the tell, he said: the derogatory part can't be cancelled, so it's not a side-comment, it's in the content.

Marco (her friend) said that can't be right, because look at us — if the insult were baked into what the word means, then everything we say to each other is either false or incoherent, and it obviously isn't, nobody in that room was insulting anybody. So the contempt has to be something the word *does* rather than something it *says*, like an eyeroll or a tone. Same word, different act.

Dan: fine, but then who gets to perform the non-insulting act, and why? He said the second you make the exemption depend on the speaker you owe a rule for who's licensed, and every rule he tried was circular — it's not derogatory when it's not derogatory. He also raised reported speech: if one of them repeats the word to a third person while recounting an insult, the insult seems to travel with the quote no matter whose mouth it's in.

Marco: that's because the report is about the insulting use. And he had his own cases — a dictionary entry listing the word isn't insulting anyone, a character in a novel says it and we don't blame the author, someone quoting a bigot isn't endorsing the bigot. Then he floated a picture I actually kind of liked: the word comes with a price. Using it costs you something, and the price depends on who's paying, so it comes out of his mouth cheap and out of Dan's mouth expensive, and that's not because it means two different things.

Dan said the price thing is just relabeling the same problem — a price you knowingly pay is still you buying the thing — and it doesn't explain why the word seems to add the same flavor to a sentence regardless of who's talking.

Then I stupidly asked whether it matters if everyone forgets. Like, suppose some community uses a word only as a compliment for 300 years and nobody remembers it started as a slur. Marco said then it just isn't a slur anymore. Dan said then a slur is whatever the current majority says it is, which he thinks is exactly what's wrong with Marco's whole view.

Can you walk through this and say who's right at each step — the cancellability point, the same-word-different-act point, the who's-licensed problem, the reported speech point, and whether the price idea actually buys anything or is just a redescription. Not just who wins overall, I want the moves. And then the part I actually care about: is there a principled answer to whether someone like me can use it, or does that just fall out of whichever of them turns out to be right?
