Two people in my dept reading group got into it last week over David Lewis's little paper on penal substitution and I got drafted as referee, which is a problem because i honestly can't tell who's right. Maybe you can.

Here's the setup. Lewis's move, as I understand it, is this: everyone says the doctrine of penal substitution is conceptually confused -- how can someone be punished for something he didn't do? -- but we already accept penal substitution in mundane life. Someone volunteers to serve a friend's sentence, or pays the friend's fine, and nobody says the concept has broken. So the doctrine isn't unintelligible on its face. Whatever else is wrong with it, it isn't that.

Then A said: look closely at the mundane cases though. When you pay your kid's speeding fine, you aren't being punished, you're bearing a cost. And we don't let a volunteer go to prison for your murder conviction; if a court did allow it we'd think the institution had gone wrong, not that we'd discovered a hidden practice of penal substitution. So the cases only show that we let people bear each other's costs, which nobody ever doubted.

B said that's false, substitute imprisonment is a real historical practice, and conscription substitutes, bail, debt -- one person undergoes the very thing that was imposed on another and we use penal language without flinching. And Lewis isn't claiming we have a worked-out theory, he's claiming the alleged conceptual impossibility doesn't show up in practice, which is evidence that there isn't one.

A: but that's the equivocation. In the fine case the state only ever wanted money; in the conscription case it only wanted a body. Those are demands satisfiable by anyone, so no punishment is being transferred at all. Where it does matter who suffers -- retribution, condemnation, the expressive side of punishment -- we don't allow substitution, or we do and we call the institution corrupt. Lewis picked the cases where substitution is allowed precisely because punishment isn't doing any work in them.

B: Lewis has an answer to that. Even granting the expressivist view that punishment is essentially the expression of condemnation, you can still make sense of the substitute undergoing something that would have counted as punishment if the guilty party had undergone it, and that's all the doctrine needs. Penal doesn't mean the substitute is literally condemned, it means he bears the penalty.

A: that's a retreat with extra steps. If the substitute isn't being punished, what's penal about it? And if all we mean is that Christ bore consequences that would have been punishment for us, then you've given up the thing that made the doctrine distinctive -- now it's just a generic substitutionary atonement, somebody else carrying a burden, and you can get that from any number of theories. The justice-satisfying story loses its grip too, because justice wasn't satisfied, a cost was just covered.

B: you're conflating the metaphysics with the vocabulary. And anyway Lewis isn't arguing that the doctrine is true or even plausible, he's clearing away one objection. So A's complaint that the mundane cases don't establish the theology is aimed at a claim Lewis never made.

A: then the paper is much less interesting than everyone treats it as, because it gets cited as if it settles the thing.

Please just write it out in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, the way you'd explain it to me in an email.
