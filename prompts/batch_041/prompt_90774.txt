I'm in the middle of a graduate seminar on scientific realism and truthlikeness, and I've hit a wall with the problem of verisimilitude. I understand the motivation well enough: we want to say that Einstein's theory is closer to the truth than Newton's even though both are, strictly speaking, false, and the notion of verisimilitude was introduced to make that comparison rigorous rather than merely intuitive. What I can't do is see whether the machinery actually delivers on that promise in a real case, as opposed to the toy examples in the readings. So I'd like you to take one concrete episode — the replacement of Newtonian gravitation by Einstein's general relativity, from the Mercury perihelion anomaly through the 1919 eclipse confirmation and onward — and run it carefully through the concept, treating it as a test of the concept as much as of the episode.

I don't want a history of physics or a scorecard of who turned out to be right; I want the mapping done step by step. What exactly is the truth against which both theories are being measured, and does that comparison even have a determinate sense when neither theory is true? Newton's law comes back as a limiting case of the Einstein field equations in the weak-field, slow-motion regime — does that correspondence relation by itself establish greater verisimilitude, or is it compatible with the successor being further from the truth in some respects (about the nature of spacetime, say, or about what gravity fundamentally is)? Then bring in the formal trouble: as I understand it, an objection pressed independently by two philosophers in the mid-1970s showed that the original comparative definition of verisimilitude has an embarrassing consequence — roughly, that among false theories it cannot rank one as closer to the truth than another, which would make the whole apparatus useless for precisely the cases it was invented to handle. Given that, tell me what the Newton-to-Einstein case shows about the viability of verisimilitude: does it confirm that we need a better formal definition rather than abandoning the idea, does it suggest that the intuitive notion is doing work no formalization can capture, or does it show that the real explanatory labor is being done by something else — approximate truth, unification, empirical success — that verisimilitude was only shadowing?

Finally, tell me what would move your assessment. What would a satisfactory definition of verisimilitude have to satisfy for this case to count as a success for it, and is there a competing episode — phlogiston to oxygen, or caloric to kinetic theory — that would be a harder or fairer test of whether the concept earns its keep?
