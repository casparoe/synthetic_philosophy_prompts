I write about philosophy for a general-readership science and ideas desk, and I am at work on a piece about a question that keeps surfacing in interviews with metaphysicians: what is going on when we say that some particular person might have been different from how they in fact are. The piece has to explain the dispute between the standard picture of transworld identity and David Lewis's counterpart theory, and rather than reconstruct the debate from primary sources I have been carrying around a summary that a philosopher gave me over coffee. I would like an audit of that summary before I file, because the last time I paraphrased a metaphysical dispute from memory I ended up printing a correction, and I would rather not repeat the experience. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need to know which sentences I can stand behind and which ones would make a specialist wince.

Here is the summary as I have reconstructed it. David Lewis's counterpart theory is the chief rival to the standard Kripkean picture of transworld identity. On the standard picture, when we say that Kripke might have been a banker, we are talking about the very same individual, Kripke himself, as he appears in another possible world in which he took a different job. Counterpart theory denies that any individual exists in more than one world. Instead, when we say that Kripke might have been a banker, we are saying that in some other world there is a distinct individual who is his counterpart, and the existence of that counterpart is what makes the modal claim true. The counterpart relation is a relation of overall similarity between you and individuals in other worlds; it is not identity, so it need not be transitive, and context can tilt the weighting toward some respects of similarity rather than others. The main motivation for the view is economy: by refusing to populate other worlds with our own selves, counterpart theory lets us make sense of modal claims without paying the metaphysical price of believing in possible worlds at all. The famous objection, due to Kripke, is the Humphrey objection. If all we have in the other world is a counterpart, then when Humphrey says that he could have been a banker, he is not talking about himself at all but about somebody else, which appears to change the subject. Lewis's reply was that the objection rests on a misunderstanding: the modal claim is still about Humphrey, because it is Humphrey who has the property of having a counterpart who is a banker, and the counterpart relation is simply the relation that does the work that identity was supposed to do for the transworld identity theorist.
