I have just come out of a long and rather heated argument with a close friend -- she is a condensed-matter physicist, I am the sort of person who reads too much metaphysics -- about whether it could matter, morally, if experience turned out to be a fundamental feature of the physical world rather than a late biological achievement. We agreed on almost nothing, and the disagreement was interesting enough that I would rather see the space of positions laid out properly than keep rehashing it in conversation. Please act as a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of metaethics and of the philosophy of physics, and produce for me a decision tree or taxonomy of the positions one might hold on the ethical implications of panpsychism and its relatives. Build it from the logical space of the commitments rather than from a roster of names.

Before you commit anything to the tree, think carefully on paper: enumerate the distinct questions on which the positions actually come apart, and only then assemble those questions into branches. Each branch node must be a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to -- no node may simply ask whether one is a panpsychist -- and each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed," if no one defends it) together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The distinctions I care about, at least at the outset, are these: whether consciousness is fundamental or emergent; if fundamental, whether it is distributed across many micro-subjects, unified in a single cosmic subject, or present only as protophenomenal properties that are not themselves experiences; whether having an experience entails having a valence, and whether valence entails having interests; and whether, granting micro-interests, the correct moral response is to take them seriously, to treat them as practically inert, or to read the whole picture as a reductio. I would also like the tree to register the structuralist worry -- that physics, being a description of relations and dispositions, may simply be silent on whether its relata are experienced -- and to say at which node that silence actually bites, since I suspect it changes which branch survives.

Then flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and note any combination of answers the tree permits that no one seems to occupy, with a brief diagnosis of whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for roughly eight to twelve leaves. Set the tree out clearly and annotate it as you go; I do not need an essay on panpsychism itself, only the instrument and your remarks on where it strains.
