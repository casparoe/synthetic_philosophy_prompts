You are a philosopher of inquiry and of democracy with a deep working knowledge of John Dewey's pragmatism, and you are also an attentive critic of narrative film. I am preparing a session for a seminar that reads novels, plays, and films philosophically, and I need a reading I can trust to be faithful in both directions — to Dewey and to the film. Please think carefully before you write, and begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list the scenes you intend to rely on and the Deweyan ideas you intend to bring to them.

The work is Sidney Lumet's 1957 film Twelve Angry Men, in which a jury deliberates over a murder charge against a teenage boy accused of stabbing his father, an initial lone vote of not guilty becomes a slow reversal, and the film ends in unanimity. I want the film read through Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry: his account of inquiry as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate one; his insistence that inquiry is a social, communicative, and public affair rather than a private mental event; his instrumentalism about ideas, on which a thought is a hypothesis whose worth is shown in its consequences for resolving a real problem; his resistance to the idea that knowledge is a spectator's contemplation of a finished world; and his view of democracy as a mode of associated living and shared communication rather than a mere machinery of voting, with majority rule as a device and not the essence.

Please then give me the reading itself — a substantial essay, roughly twelve hundred to eighteen hundred words — and make sure it addresses each of the following, since these are the points my students will press:

First, does the deliberation actually follow the pattern of inquiry Dewey describes, and does the film's dramatic structure betray that pattern by turning it into a tidy, screenwriterly sequence of steps? Note that Dewey himself was wary of hardening inquiry into a fixed recipe, and say whether the film is more Deweyan or less Deweyan than the textbook version of him.

Second, Dewey was hostile to psychologizing inquiry: the indeterminacy he cares about belongs to the situation, not to the inquirer's feelings of doubt, and he thought the philosophical tradition's fixation on private doubt was a mistake. The film, by contrast, seems to dramatize doubt as a state of mind — a single juror's unwillingness to be rushed, the discomfort of the others, the mounting personal pressure as the afternoon grows hotter. Is the film's psychology of doubt in tension with Dewey's anti-psychologizing account of inquiry, or can the film be read as showing that what looks like private doubt is really a public situation disclosing itself?

Third, consider the jury as a small face-to-face public. Dewey worried that a great society had not yet become a great community, and that the public had been eclipsed by the scale and opacity of modern life. Is the jury room Dewey's great community in miniature, an image of what associated living can be? Or does the film inadvertently show the fragility and limits of face-to-face deliberation — twelve strangers, one room, no appeal to a wider public, a verdict that cannot be revised once delivered?

Fourth, the verdict is the obvious candidate for something like Dewey's notion of a warranted assertion rather than a discovery of truth. But the jurors arrive at it from different reasons, and one of them may be moved more by his own grief than by the evidence. Does a verdict that must be unanimous but rests on divergent grounds fit Dewey's picture of warranted assertibility, or does it expose a gap between the convergence a jury needs and the warrant Dewey's theory demands?

Fifth, and most important to me, this is inquiry without experiment. Nothing the jurors hypothesize can be tested against the world; they cannot rerun the killing, cannot inspect the scene, and can only test their hypotheses against testimony, physical detail, and one another's resistance. Does the film dramatize the limits of Deweyan experimentalism in settings where action is impossible and only persuasion remains? Or does it show that persuasion and the willingness to be moved by reasons just are the experiment, which is what Dewey meant by saying that inquiry is social?

Finally, a rival explanation for the jury's success that Dewey's account may not capture: social epistemology has a well-known result to the effect that a group of diverse, moderately competent problem-solvers can outperform a group of individually stronger but more homogeneous reasoners. If the film's hero succeeds mainly by importing a perspective the others lacked, the film may be a drama about the value of cognitive diversity rather than about shared inquiry at all. Say which reading the film supports, and what the answer implies for Dewey's claim that inquiry is inherently social rather than merely aggregated.

Close by telling me where the reading strains — the scenes or moments that resist the Deweyan frame and would force an honest critic to say so — and by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that Dewey's prose alone does not, if anything.

A few constraints. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not quote Dewey or the film verbatim; paraphrase the ideas in your own words and refer to scenes by description rather than by line. Assume your reader has seen the film and has a rough acquaintance with Dewey but needs the argument made, not the background recited. Write in American English.
