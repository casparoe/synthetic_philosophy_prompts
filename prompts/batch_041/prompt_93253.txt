I am assembling a specification for an account of vagueness, and I want the requirements before the theories: the phenomena an adequate account must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it must not violate, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. The account is meant to do double duty, as a semantics for vague predicates and as a guide to reasoning and deciding with them, so adequacy is to be judged against both jobs at once.

Treat the following as raw material to be converted into requirements, and add whatever the list plainly needs:

1. The sorites in its ordinary form — a heap, a bald head, a colour sample — a long run of individually innocent increments ending somewhere no one will accept.
2. The same paradox in pairwise or comparative form: any two items differing by an imperceptible amount are alike in the relevant respect, so by the transitivity of likeness no two items differ in it at all.
3. Higher-order vagueness: the region of borderline cases appears to have no boundary of its own, and the vocabulary we use to describe vagueness — definitely, borderline, clearly, arguably — is itself vague.
4. Stipulated precision: some predicates have official cut-offs, such as a statutory age of majority, a diagnostic threshold, or a statistical definition of unemployment, and yet speakers still hedge about cases near the line. Say what the account owes us here: whether the hedge is vagueness or mere uncertainty, and whether stipulation creates precision or merely relocates it.
5. An invented contrast: a community trained to answer only definitely-yes or definitely-no for a given predicate, whose usage converges on a stable cut-off, set against a community that refuses the training and keeps hedging. Does the first community make the predicate precise? If so, what does that show about where vagueness lives — in the word, in the users, or in the subject matter?
6. Vagueness outside language: a coastline drawn on a low-resolution map, a blurred photograph, a memory indeterminate in its details, a cloud or a mountain that seems to have no definite boundary. Is there vagueness in the world or only in our representations of it, and what has to change in the account to accommodate either answer?
7. The practical value of vagueness: we keep bald, heap, and tall rather than replacing them with numbers; say what a theory must explain about why that is rational, and what precision costs us.
8. The assertoric data: we assert vague claims, call them true, claim to know some of them, contradict one another with them, and reason from them; an account that makes all of this systematically mistaken has to pay for that.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Identify any subset of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which member of the subset has to give; this is the part I most want to see. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. Then, briefly, how epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree-theoretic treatments, contextualist treatments, and the view that there is no vagueness at all fare against the list, noting for each the first requirement it fails — keep this part short, since the list is the deliverable and the scorecard is not. One further question to close on: if the metalinguistic vocabulary must itself be precise for the theory to be stateable, does that reinstate a sharp boundary and so violate the higher-order vagueness item? Argue from the cases rather than from literature, and do not cite sources or supply a reading list.
