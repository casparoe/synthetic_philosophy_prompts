I'm in a small reading group on effective altruism, and last week we discussed moral trade: the practice of two people who care about different things each doing something the other cares about more than they do, so that both come out ahead by their own moral lights. One member then brought up moral offsetting — paying someone else to do good, or buying up emissions reductions, in order to keep doing something one takes to be permissible but costly — and insisted that this is nothing but moral trade with money counted among the traded goods, so that anyone who accepts the first has no principled ground for objecting to the second. I suspect he is wrong, but I keep failing to say exactly why. My own half-formed thought is that a trade is action for action, whereas an offset is action for resource, and that this matters because a resource carries no moral content of its own until someone spends it; I cannot tell, however, whether that is a genuine structural difference or merely bookkeeping about who performs which act.

Please compare the two ideas as sharply as you can, and give me a verdict on whether offsetting is a species of moral trade or a distinct practice. If you think the distinction is principled, state the criterion as precisely as you can and then test it against two cases: a person who pays to offset the emissions of a flight taken for pleasure, and a pair of people who each pay the other to license conduct they would otherwise count as harmful, so that the payments cancel out and no good is produced. If you think the distinction collapses, tell me what illegitimate work my action-for-action criterion was doing. I care less about whether such arrangements are psychologically realistic than about whether the two structures differ in a way that any adequate theory of permissible cooperation must track.
