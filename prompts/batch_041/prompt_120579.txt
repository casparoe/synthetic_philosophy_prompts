I am assembling a framework for adjudicating cases that involve cognitive artifacts — paper notebooks, smartphones, cloud-stored files, shared calendars, wearable and implanted devices, and the software assistants that increasingly hold what people used to remember — and I need the standard of adequacy before I can evaluate any particular theory. Write the desiderata that a theory of cognitive extension must satisfy if it is to be usable for that purpose: if it is to tell a court, a hospital, or a regulator when an external resource is genuinely part of a person's mind, and when it is merely something the person uses.

Present the requirements as a numbered list. For each one, state the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either non-negotiable or a commitment of one camp that a rival need not accept. Among the requirements you should consider, organized as you see fit:

(a) The cases the theory must get right: the ordinary notebook case and its variants; a resource consulted deliberately, against a resource whose contents arrive unbidden; a resource shared with another person; a resource that has been destroyed; a resource that its manufacturer can disable or repossess at a distance; a device that is implanted rather than carried.

(b) The distinctions it may not collapse: a resource contributing causally to a cognitive process, against its constituting part of that process; extension of cognition, against extension of the self or the person; a claim about how we ought to theorize, against a claim about what exists; extension that is momentary and deliberate, against extension that is long-standing and automatic; extension that is graded, against extension that is all-or-nothing.

(c) The findings it must not contradict: the division of memory labor across people and their external stores; the evidence that offloading to an external store degrades unaided recall; the incorporation of tools into the body schema; the routine use of external props in expert performance; the clinical and legal treatment of implanted devices.

(d) The constraints on the shape of the theory itself: it must not stipulate its conclusion by defining cognition as what happens inside the skull, and it must not make extension so cheap that the library and the people standing nearby qualify.

Then, and this is the part I care about most: identify any pair or larger subset of your requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise plausible theories. Finally, in a few sentences each, say how the parity-based functionalist account, the internalist or embedded rejoinder, the scaffolding-and-integration approach from evolutionary cognitive science, and the extended-self approach fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and on their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary. Note as well which of your requirements are really demands of my normative purpose rather than demands of the metaphysics, since I want to know which parts of the list a purely descriptive theory could refuse.
