ok so I'm in third year and my Science, Technology & Society module has an AI safety unit, and the final assignment is to write a satirical / ironic / parodic piece "related to a concept" — about 600 words, any genre, as long as the humour comes from getting the ideas right rather than from doing a bit about killer robots. I want to use the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax, and I'd rather you drafted it than me because I keep sliding into just explaining them.

Here's the two ideas as I'd explain them to my flatmate, so you know what I've got:

The alignment tax — whatever you give up (capability, speed, money, staff) by making a system safer, more interpretable, more controllable or more honest than you strictly had to. It gets discussed like it's a real number that could be audited, and nobody agrees whether it's huge, tiny, negative, or the wrong frame altogether.

The competitiveness constraint — if you're one actor among several and you pay that tax while the others don't, you fall behind, and (so the argument goes) falling behind costs you exactly the influence you'd need for things to go well, while the capability gets built anyway by someone less careful. Strong version: paying is self-defeating, so the only stable outcome is that nobody pays, and everyone can point at everyone else as the reason. Wierd bit: I've also seen the same constraint used the other way round — the careful labs have to stay in front so that they're the ones who get there first — and I honestly can't tell if that's an answer to the first argument or the same fact pointed the other direction. If the piece can hold both of those without resolving them, great.

What I want written: a frontier lab's glossy annual Safety Report. CEO letter, a couple of sections of prose where charts would be, and a part where the alignment tax is reported like a line item in the accounts, with notes to the accounts underneath. Sincere, slightly proud, no winking at the reader. The thing I'm aiming at is the move where the constraint gets used to close a conversation rather than open one — "we'd love to, but competitiveness" — and the way a document like this can report not paying a tax in a tone that suggests it paid one. The funny should come from the machinery: what the tax is actually paid in, who assesses it and on what basis, why this year's figure is so small, what qualifies as a deduction, what a credit looks like, and what the note says about the scenario where you pay it and nobody else does. The competitiveness constraint should be doing real work in the document — it should be the reason behind every number — and someone who's actually in this debate should be able to read it and think the writer knows what they're talking about.

Then three bits after it, in this order and roughly these lengths:

- The report — the piece itself, title plus ~600 words
- Where I've taken liberties — ~120 words on the places the satire leans on a contested reading or exaggerates for effect, so I don't quote it in the seminar as if it were established
- Settled vs. contested — ~150 words seperating what people who actually work on this agree on from what's genuinely in dispute, and if anything in my two bullets above is wrong, or is a position rather than a fact, say so plainly

Don't hedge the piece itself though — the report should be confident. It's the notes where I want the honesty.
