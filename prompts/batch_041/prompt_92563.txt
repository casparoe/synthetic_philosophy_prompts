I've been reading about cognitive penetration and I keep seeing the wine study trotted out as the killer example, but I think the standard telling is a mess. Here's the actual setup, as best I can tell: in a 2001 study by Morrot, Brochet, and Dubourdieu, they took a white wine, dyed it red with an odorless dye, and gave it to a panel of 54 enology students (undergrads, not master sommeliers). The week before, the same students had tasted a real red and a real white and generated descriptors. Then they came back and were given two glasses: one white wine, one the same white wine dyed red. They were handed their own descriptor lists from the previous week and asked to assign each descriptor to whichever of the two glasses it fit best. Most of the red-wine descriptors got assigned to the red-colored glass. That's it. The pop version says experts couldn't tell red from white, which is not what happened.

What I want is a careful analysis of this episode through the cognitive penetration debate. Not the usual look, expectation shapes perception take. I want the mapping: what exactly is the perceptual state here, what is the cognitive state (the belief that it's red wine, the visual experience of redness, the concept red wine), and what is the evidence that one penetrated the other. Then run it through the main positions. What would the encapsulation view (Fodor, Pylyshyn) say is actually going on? Probably something like response bias or judgment, not penetration. What would a pro-penetration view (Siegel, maybe others) say? Can this study even distinguish between penetration of olfaction and a post-perceptual decision bias? And if it can't, what would a better experiment look like? I know there's a critque literature (Firestone and Scholl and others) that says most allegded cases are actually memory, response bias, or task demands. Does the wine case survive that critique?

Then flip it: what does the case show about the theory of cognitive penetration? Is it an anomaly, a confirmation, or a case the theory never anticipated? Does the fact that the effect shows up in a forced-choice task with experts-ish tasters tell us anything about the boundary between perception and cognition, or does it just show that people use color as a cue when they're asked to sort descriptors? Be honest about where the theory is silent and where the case is just underdescribed. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the analysis.
