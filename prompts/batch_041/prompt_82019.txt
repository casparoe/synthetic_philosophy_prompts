I'm a postdoc in philosophy of mind and I've somehow been roped into giving a work-in-progress talk at a pain research centre next month, and I want to walk in ready for the question period rather than improvising. The room is roughly half pain neuroscientists who work on the sensory-discriminative side — they treat nociception and unpleasantness as seperable systems and cite pain asymbolia as the dissociation that proves the nasty part lives in the affective/motivational machinery — and half clinical psychologists plus a couple of palliative care people who care mostly about suffering and get prickly about anything that sounds like it's telling patients their pain isn't really bad. There's one senior figure in the room who basically built the affective-separability story and is not going to enjoy me. Stakes are modest but real: this is the rehearsal for a paper I want to submit, and I'd rather be embarrassed now than in review.

The line I'm defending is this. Asymbolia doesn't show that the badness of pain lives in the affective system rather than the sensory one; it shows that badness isn't a component of the pain state at all — it's an attitude, a kind of caring-about-the-pain, that can come apart from the painfulness while the painfulness is fully intact. So asymbolics aren't people whose pain is missing its nasty bit; they're people who are in pain and simply don't mind it. I want to draw a distinction between the sensation's badness-making feature and the subject's stake in the sensation, and claim it's the second that's absent in asymbolia while the first is untouched. Ordinary pain, on this view, is just the case where the stake is non-optional — where you can't help caring — and I think that non-optionality, not the sensation, is what makes badness feel intrinsic, which is the actually interesting bit rather than the exotic cases. I'll be honest that the uncomfortable consequence is that a lot of what we call pain relief is attitude-management, and that a treatment leaving the sensation untouched while dissolving the caring could in principle be as good as one that kills the sensation.

So: give me my Q&A prep sheet. The eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room — phrased the way a sensory neuroscientist or a palliative clinician would put them, not the way a philosopher would — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, plus what I should concede up front and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole thing to 900 words max, I have to read it on the train. Write it in British English (I'm at a UK centre, house style is -ise). And please, if my thesis is just a relabelled version of a view that already has a name, or if the "caring" I keep invoking is doing no work that "affective component" wasn't already doing, say so plainly instead of humouring me — I'd genuinely rather find that out from you than from a reviewer.
