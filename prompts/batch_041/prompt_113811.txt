So I do internal comms at a mid-size healthcare company — about 4,000 people, we just finished a rebrand, and our CEO has started listening to philosophy podcasts on his commute, which is how we ended up here — and our Chief Equity Officer has asked me to polish up her draft announcement of something called the Epistemic Equity Initiative. The draft is... a lot. The centerpiece is a quarterly Testimonial Credibility Audit: meeting transcripts get run through a model that scores how much airtime each person got, and anyone landing below their role's baseline gets flagged for a Credibility Rebalancing Protocol, with default multipliers by seniority in an appendix table. There's a dropdown where employees can log that an experience was "hermeneutically obscured", an escalation path for "unresolved credibility disputes", and an FAQ line saying that testimonial smothering is out of scope because the company can't compel anyone to speak. There's also a paragraph renaming something called willful hermeneutical ignorance as "non-malicious knowledge deferral", wich I don't think the CEO read all the way through.

I want you to write the actual memo, 700-800 words, deadpan corporate register — subject line, a "why this matters" section, "what's changing", a short appendix table, the usual. The humor should come from getting the theory right, not from mocking the idea that people get disbelieved. Things I need you to be faithful to, because she really did read the papers and will notice: testimonial injustice is a credibility deficit that tracks identity rather than evidence, and credibility excess is its mirror image, so the multipliers gag has to cut both ways and not just make execs the villains; hermeneutical injustice is a gap in the shared interpretive resources rather than individual stupidity, which is exactly why "we added a dropdown option" is the wrong kind of fix — and the memo should never realize this; and there's a real difference between a speaker who isn't even taken as a knower and a speaker who can tell she'll be dismissed and so stops talking, and the memo should treat both as scheduling and facilitation issues. Aim the satire at an institution that turns a critique of how credibility gets distributed into a credibility-scoring system, keep it legible to my CFO who thinks Foucault is a brand of olive oil, and let a philosopher laugh instead of wince. Nobody in the memo should be a villain or notice the irony — the officer should be sincerely proud of this, and if you include one employee quote, make it someone complaining that the audit form has no field for complaining about the audit form.
