You are a philosopher of mind and epistemologist who has written on expertise, cognitive offloading, and the division of epistemic labour. I want a careful conceptual explanation, not a survey — so begin with a short burst of brainstorming in which you lay out the candidate distinctions people usually reach for, and only then commit to an answer.

Here is the issue I keep circling. We all distinguish between offloading a cognitive task to an AI and atrophying the capacity to do it yourself. The first looks benign, even virtuous; the second looks like a real loss. But I have never seen a clean account of what the difference consists in. Is it a counterfactual difference — you could still do it, you just don't? A difference in whether you can tell when the output is wrong? A difference in whether the skill is load-bearing for some further capacity you care about? Or does the distinction dissolve once you press on it hard enough?

Explain it as precisely as you can. Give your best account of what separates reliance that costs you nothing from reliance that hollows you out, state that account in a form that could actually be falsified, and then give the strongest objection to it. Say also whether the line falls in the same place for different cognitive goods — recall, judgment, taste — and whether the real story is about individual minds at all, or about the epistemic division of labour in a community. Be concrete where concreteness earns its keep, but do not pad.
