My best friend -- call him Dev -- is getting married in three weeks and I'm the best man, so the toast is on me. Dev is finishing a dissertation on Bradley's regress, which for anyone lucky enough not to know is the problem that keeps coming up whenever you try to say what holds some things together into one thing: if you say the two things plus a relation between them are one thing, you need something further to hold that relation to those two things, and then something further for that, and off it goes; and he thinks the same thing happens when you ask what makes a proposition one thing rather than a heap of its parts, or a sentence one thing rather than a list of words. He gave me exactly one rule for the toast, and he gave it to me at his own bachelor party, which honestly tells you everything: I'm not allowed to say that love, or commitment, or the bond between them is the thing that makes two people into one, because a bond is precisely the thing the regress is about, and he'd rather I said something that could survive that than something that sounds lovely for ten seconds and then collapses. His fiancee Maeve is a pediatric nurse; her family is huge and mostly farmers and would rather hear about the time he got stuck on a roof than about relations; my side of the room is basically a metaphysics reading group plus Dev's advisor, who will be grading this silently; his mother cries at car commercials, so the emotional bar is low but the bar for not lecturing anyone is very high. I keep going back and forth on whether the honest move is to say the unity was never a further ingredient in the first place, or to say the two of them are already caught up in something (a shared practice, a running argument, a house with a leaky radiator) that you'd have to be inside of to even ask the question -- but I don't want to hand you my answer, I want the toast to find one.

So: write me the toast. Four or five minutes out loud, built so that the regress is doing real structural work rather than being a cute reference -- the constraint has to shape what I can and can't claim, and it has to be the reason the thing ends where it ends. It needs to be genuinely funny at least once (the roof story is available, and so is the fact that he proposed in the parking lot of a hardware store because he'd been rehearsing something more elaborate for months), it should not explain the regress for two minutes to people who came for the cake, and it must not fall back on any of the standard wedding-toast moves -- no dictionary definition of love, no I knew from the moment I saw them together, no clever thing about glasses being raised and also empty. End on a line I can actually say with a straight face and then drink to.
