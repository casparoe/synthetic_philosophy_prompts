My dad turned 71 last month, and over his birthday dinner he mentioned, in the way he mentions everything, that he'd been to see a cardiologist about a heart valve that's been slowly narrowing for a few years (aortic stenosis, I think -- he calls it "the valve" and waves a hand). The doctor told him that for people in his situation there's roughly a 1 in 20 chance per year of something serious happening -- fainting, chest pain, that kind of thing -- and that when it gets bad enough the fix is replacing the valve, which is a serious operation at his age, so for now they're weighing "wait and see" against doing it soon. My dad, who is the most easygoing man alive, said 5%, that's not so bad, and moved on to the cake. My sister's husband, who prices policies for an insurance company and is not easygoing, went quiet for a minute and then announced that the number is basically made up. His argument: "people in your situation" could mean anything -- every man over 65 with any narrowing at all, or men exactly your age with exactly your degree of narrowing plus your blood pressure plus your other conditions -- and if you slice it narrowly enough you land on four people in the world, or zero, and if you slice it broadly the number describes nobody in particular. So the doctor drew a circle and the 5% is a fact about his choice of circle, not about my dad.

Where it gets more interesting: my dad's narrowing was found by accident, during a scan for something else, whereas the doctor said the number comes from a big registry of patients at specialist heart centres. My brother-in-law pounced on that -- those people got sent to a specialist because some doctor already thought they were sick, so the registry is stuffed with the worst cases, and my dad, whom nobody would have referred anywhere, isn't in that group at all. That seems like a real objection to me, definately. But if you take it seriously it seems to eat every statistic anyone has ever handed you, including the ones the insurance company sets premiums from, and the doctor clearly wasn't an idiot -- he meant something by it.

So: is my brother-in-law right that the 5% is meaningless? If he isn't, what exactly is that number a fact about, and what should the cardiologist have said instead? And here's the part I actually care about -- is there a principled way to decide how wide the circle should be drawn, some rule that says which of my dad's features belong in the class and which (his postcode, his surname, the day of the week he was born) obviously don't? Or is the whole thing a judgement call dressed up in arithmetic? I did some philosophy of science at uni years ago and I have a nagging feeling there's a name for this and a small literature on it, but I can't for the life of me remember what it's called, and I'd rather have the substance anyway: what the puzzle actually is, what the standard moves are, whether any of them survives the accident-of-discovery point, and whether any of it gets my dad any closer to knowing what to do.
