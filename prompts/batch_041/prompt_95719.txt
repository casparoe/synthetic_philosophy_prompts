ok so I want an essay on the Frege-Geach problem, and I need you to actually land somewhere, not pull the "both sides have a point" move at the end.

Quick recap so we're on the same page, because I dont want this to turn into a naming contest: expressivists think a moral sentence like "lying is wrong" isn't describing a fact, it's expressing a non-cognitive attitude -- disapproval, a plan, a bit of motivational oomph, take your pick. Frege-Geach is the worry that the same sentence also shows up unasserted, e.g. in the antecedent of a conditional ("if lying is wrong, then lying to your landlord about the rent is wrong"). There it looks like it's doing ordinary truth-apt work, and the inference looks valid, so either "wrong" quietly changes meaning the moment it's embedded (which seems insane, the word didn't change), or it's attitude-expressing all the way down and the validity of the inference becomes a mystery. The standard escapes treat the embedded occurrence as a different kind of attitude, or as a higher-order attitude aimed at some combination.

Here's the bit I actually want you to chew on. A freind of mine has a view they call proposal-ism. On it, a moral utterance isn't an attitude at all -- it's a proposal: let's from here on treat a certain plan or norm as settled. Embeddings are then just proposals at one remove; the conditional proposes a conditional commitment, and the logic in play isn't truth-functional at all, it's the practical logic of plans. So there's no hidden descriptive content to recover, because on their view there never was any to begin with.

Is that a real solution, or does it just relocate the whole problem one level up? Write the essay so that it:

1. says whether proposal-ism actually dodges Frege-Geach, or whether the same pressures (meaning-invariance, validity, negation, disagreement) just reappear for proposals;
2. stress-tests it on embeddings people usually skip: moral predicates under "probably" ("it's probably wrong to..."), under "because" (offering the wrongness as the reason for something), and inside noun phrases ("the wrongness of lying", "her belief that lying is wrong", "doubting that lying is wrong");
3. and then commits. Is expressivism -- proposal-ism included -- dead, alive, or alive only with a big pile of epicycles? Pick one and defend it.

Try not to just recite the standard expressivist playbook, I already know it, I want your own reasoning about whether the machinery actually holds up. And please, no hedging at the end.
