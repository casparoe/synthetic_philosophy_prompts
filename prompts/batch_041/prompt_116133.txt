I am a professor of philosophy, and I have lately been retained as an adviser by a small team building an argument-analysis tool. The tool ingests written arguments and, for each inferential step, returns a verdict: licensed or unlicensed. The team's ambition is that the tool be able to justify, on demand, every inference it accepts. Their proposed mechanism is what they call rule expansion: when a user challenges a step, the tool answers with the principle that licenses it; if the user then challenges that principle, the tool appends a further principle to its rule set and carries on. They take this procedure to make the system self-justifying.

I have told them that the difficulty here is the one Lewis Carroll's tortoise put to Achilles, but they deserve a proper account rather than a slogan. Please set out the most important implications of the tortoise's regress for how a system of this kind can be built and, more to the point, for what it can honestly claim to be doing. I want the implications that would actually change the design or the public description of the product: what the regress shows about the difference between a rule of inference and a further premise; whether any rule set can be certified from within; what a tool's base of accepted rules can and cannot be defended by appeal to; whether the regress is a defect of the tool or a feature of inference as such; and how the tool should handle a user who behaves like the tortoise, granting every principle it is offered and still declining to draw the conclusion. Where an implication that people commonly draw is in fact a misreading, say so and give the correct reading.

Please present the implications in prose, ordered by how much they should change the team's plans, with your reasons for the ordering. No bibliography is needed; the team needs the argument, not the literature.
