I've been working through some formal epistemology for a seminar, and I keep running into a methodological move that I want to stress-test rather than simply absorb. The move goes roughly like this: credences are treated as if they were acts available to an agent, the accuracy of a credence — how close it comes to the truth — is treated as if it were the payoff of that act, and epistemic rationality is then reconstructed as ordinary decision theory with accuracy in the place of utility. Maximize expected accuracy, the story goes, and the familiar norms (that your credences should be probabilities, that you should update by conditionalization, that you should be indifferent among possibilities you have no evidence to distinguish, and so on) fall out as theorems rather than as deliverances of intuition. Its proponents present it as a clarifying analogy: epistemic norms, they say, are just the norms of rational choice applied to a special domain, so anyone who already accepts expected-utility reasoning about action should accept the parallel reasoning about belief. I find the parallel seductive, and I also suspect it is carrying more weight than its proponents admit.

What I'd like from you is an audit of the analogy itself, not a survey of the surrounding literature. Lay the correspondence out explicitly — what plays the role of the agent, the acts, the states of the world, the utility function, the expectation, and the constraints on what counts as an admissible option — and then tell me where the mapping strains or outright breaks. I am especially interested in the disanalogies that would matter for the conclusion the analogy is used to support. For instance: can the truth-value of a proposition really occupy the role that a state of the world occupies in practical decision theory, or is the fit only verbal? Does the fact that the "act" simply is the credence introduce a reflexivity with no counterpart in practical choice? Does importing a single scalar payoff smuggle in the veritist assumption that accuracy is the sole epistemic good, so that the whole reconstruction presupposes what it was meant to justify? And does the decision-theoretic frame presuppose that credences are up to us in the way actions are, which many would deny?

Please separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the reconstruction of epistemic norms from the ones that are merely picturesque. Then say whether the conclusion — that epistemic rationality is nothing but expected-utility reasoning about accuracy — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, sketch what a better analogy would be. Throughout, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and flag the single point you are least sure about.
