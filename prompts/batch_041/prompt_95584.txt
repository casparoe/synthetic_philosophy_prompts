I am the returning officer for a learned society's annual prize. Twelve members vote by ranking the shortlisted candidates; I must produce one winner to announce at the AGM, and the outcome is challenged often enough that I want a procedure I can defend in writing to the membership. I have no training in social choice theory and the panel changes every year, so it must be something a volunteer can run in an hour with pen and paper.

Write that procedure. It should be a sequence of tests and decisions, grounded in what is actually established about aggregating rankings, including the impossibility results, and it must say at each step which desirable property I am sacrificing and what that sacrifice costs me. Where a step cannot be settled by a rule, say so and say who decides and on what basis. Where the rule will predictably be gamed, say whether ballots or tallies should be published, and require that the elimination order, the tie-break and the choice of rule be fixed in advance of seeing the ballots. Then run the procedure on both ballot profiles below, giving the result at each stage alongside the result under the obvious rival rules, and flagging where the procedure and my own preference come apart.

Profile 1 (nine voters, three candidates A, B, C): four voters rank A then B then C; three rank B then C then A; two rank C then B then A. My own preference is C.

Profile 2 (twelve voters, four candidates A, B, C, D): four rank A then C then B then D; three rank B then A then C then D; two rank C then B then A then D; three rank D then C then A then B. My own preference is D.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can tell me that the choice of rule is a matter of value rather than of correctness, and whether it can protect the outcome from a panel that has already decided whom it wants.

Please write the answer in British English.
