For about a year now, a few friends and I have been carrying on a running argument about whether Norway or Sweden is the more liberal country, and it has gradually become clear to me that we will never settle it — and, more interestingly, that the failure is not for want of facts. We agree on most of the facts. Norway collects its oil and gas revenue into a fund the state owns and spends it according to a fixed rule; Sweden has nothing really comparable and has instead built its welfare state on broad, high taxes on ordinary income. Norway sits outside the European Union and outside the euro, and kept an established national church long after Sweden disestablished its own; Sweden joined the Union but kept its own currency, and was for a long stretch conspicuously more permissive on immigration. On gender, Norway required companies to put women on their boards before Sweden did, while Sweden's parental leave is the more generous and the more heavily taken up by fathers. And here is the crux: when one of my friends says "liberal," he means roughly the freedom to live as one likes without the state or the neighbours interfering; when another says it, she means the state actively securing people against the market and against misfortune. Each of them can point to a column of facts that supports the verdict, and neither column is wrong. It has begun to seem to me that the question "which of these two is the more liberal?" is not merely hard to answer but malformed — that it presupposes a common scale on which the two countries can be laid side by side, and that no such scale exists, because "liberal" is not one thing but a bundle of things that can trade off against one another.

What I would like from you is an example of this conceptual issue: an example of a comparison between two real jurisdictions — countries, states, cities, whatever you think serves best — where the facts are not in dispute, where each side can cite a genuine body of facts in its favour, and where the verdict flips depending on which of two internally defensible readings of the key term you adopt. Please work the example out concretely rather than gesturing at it. Name the pair, set out the specific divergences, say which reading of the term favours which side, and then say what, if anything, could settle the comparison — whether that would be a further fact nobody has yet collected, a reasoned decision about which reading to privilege, or a demonstration that the single question ought to be replaced by several narrower ones. I would rather you build your own example than hand me back the one above, and I would rather it not be one of the well-worn chestnuts — I have already heard about hot dogs and sandwiches, about Pluto and planets, about whether a tomato is a fruit — because those seem to turn on a different problem (where to draw a boundary) than the one I am chasing (how to weigh two things that do not share a scale).

As you work the example out, please also make clear what the issue is *not*: how it differs from the ordinary vagueness of a word, from the plain fact that people disagree about values, and from the case in which one party is simply mistaken about the facts. If it happens that there is already an established name or body of writing for exactly this situation, tell me what it is and how it is usually distinguished from those nearby things — but the example is the main thing I am after, and I would rather have a well-drawn one than a label.
