Before I read any more theories of collective mentality, I want the desiderata for such a theory. The central case is the ant colony: Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, the colony that behaves as a unified conversational partner in the Ant Fugue while each ant follows local rules and none represents the colony's goals. The question is whether a colony can have a mind—whether it can be a subject of mental predicates—and what any theory that answers yes or no must deliver. I want the specification any adequate theory of colony mentality must meet: what it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which empirical and conceptual results it must be consistent with, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular contenders. You may close with a brief assessment of the superorganism view, the intentional-stance view, and the distributed-cognition view against the list, but keep the weight on the desiderata themselves. Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I want the criteria, not citations.

The cases I want the theory to handle include at least: the ant colony as a persistent agent across ant turnover; the individual ant as a finite-state device with no colony-level representation; the brain as a system whose neurons are not conscious; the immune system as an adaptive, memory-bearing system without a unified subject; the termite mound built by stigmergy with no blueprint; the slime mold that solves a maze without neurons; the corporation as a legal person with distributed decision-making; the flock of starlings as a murmuration with no central control; the split-brain patient as a test of unity; a colony split into two viable halves; a colony whose ants are replaced one by one; a colony whose decision threshold is hijacked by a parasite; and a colony engineered to signal a binary choice by quorum, where the signal tracks a hidden food source. The distinctions I care about include mere aggregate versus integrated system, representation versus mere information, derived versus original intentionality, cognition versus consciousness, agency versus adaptive behavior, functional unity versus spatial contiguity, realizer versus realized, and subject versus process. The results the theory must not contradict include that ants follow local rules, that colony-level behavior can be adaptive without any ant understanding the problem, that pheromone fields can implement a kind of global workspace, that natural selection can produce adaptive behavior without a mind, and that the hard problem of consciousness and the combination problem remain unresolved.
