I took early retirement from a plant physiology department six years ago and have spent most of it doing something I always meant to do, which is read philosophy — mostly philosophy of biology, which honestly I should have been reading since my twenties, because it turns out that a lot of the arguments I used to have with my own students about whether you're allowed to say a structure is 'for' something were being had in print by people with much better manners. I've fallen in with a fortnightly reading group of six or seven people, and one of them, Ruth, taught field ecology for thirty years before going back and doing a philosophy degree at seventy-four, for fun. She's a menace in discussion because she won't tell you which theory she's running. She just gives verdicts, usually one word, and when I press her she says, 'I don't have a theory, I have a nose.' Which is charming but useless to me, because I'm definately trying to work out what rule her nose is applying. The group argues about this alot, and the fight, as far as I can make it out, is over whether the history matters — whether something has to have been selected for a job before we're allowed to call that job its function — or whether a function is just the part's contribution to the working of the whole, history or no history.

Here's her record, as best I can reconstruct it. Healthy human heart: pumping blood is its function, obviously. The thumping noise the same heart makes, and the fact that it takes up the middle of the chest: not functions, she says, just things it does. A baby born in the spring with a heart so malformed it never pumped a drop of blood and who died on the fourth day: Ruth says the function of that heart was still to pump blood — it's a heart that failed, she said, not a heart with some other function. And when our friend Pieter asked her whether the function of a river is to carry water to the sea, she was quite sharp about it: rivers have courses, she said, not functions; only living things have functions, and everything else just has uses somebody found for it.

Now the new case, which is the reason I'm writing. Last April a single plant came up in my vegetable bed — a bittercress of some kind, tiny, nothing like the ones in the hedge — and it's covered in fine stiff hairs, and the flea beetles that have been shredding everything else in that bed won't go near it. I sent a leaf to a friend in the botany department and she says the hairs aren't in any of the local populations; as far as she can tell this is one plant, one mutation, appearing this year, and nothing has had a chance to select for anything — the plant hasn't been through a winter, there's no second generation, the hairs might turn out to be a dead end or a disaster. So: does Ruth say those hairs have a function, and is it to stop the beetles? What I really want isn't just the prediction but the rule or rules that fit her earlier verdicts, what each of them says about my bittercress, which one you'd bet on and why, and above all which case I should put to her next to settle which rule she's been running all along — because the cases we've hit so far all seem to be ones where the rival stories happen to agree, and she'll answer anything, so it can be as strange as you like. If one of the rules has a name, tell me what it is; Pieter keeps saying words at me and I'd like to know which ones are the real ones.
