I teach a seminar organized around prediction markets, and there is a puzzle about them I have never resolved to my satisfaction. Firms and forecasters run markets in which people stake real money on future events — whether a product will ship on time, who will win an election — and then treat the resulting prices as authoritative forecasts. The more ambitious version of the idea lets the market's verdict actually settle the question: participants bet on which of several proposals best advances some measurable objective, where the objective itself has been fixed in advance. I would like you to reconstruct what this arrangement presupposes. What must it assume about the nature of value — does it require a single scalar objective against which competing goods can be traded off? What must it assume about the relation between facts and values, given that ends are settled by voting and means by betting? Why should a decision count as legitimate merely because prices track the truth about means, and what does the scheme assume about who counts, given that influence is proportional to willingness and ability to stake money? Tell me which of these commitments the arrangement strictly presupposes and which it merely fits well with; where it is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that rival theories of value, democracy, or knowledge would license it just as well; and whether any of its parts sit uneasily together — for instance, when the bets help determine the very outcome they are meant to forecast. Finally, suppose I replace the implicit theory with a serious rival, say value pluralism or a deliberative rather than epistemic conception of legitimacy: what in the design would have to change?
