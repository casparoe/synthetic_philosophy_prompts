I am in the middle of a long-running argument with a friend about how to weigh the British Raj, and we have each ended up leaning on a historian who suits us: he keeps invoking Ferguson's revisionist case that British rule in India was, on balance, a modernising and developmental force, while I keep invoking Tharoor's counter that the ledger only looks favourable if you ignore what was extracted and who was doing the counting. Before I go further I want to understand the primary source that both of them are implicitly fighting over, so I have gone back to Dadabhai Naoroji's Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, in the 1901 Swan Sonnenschein edition, and I am working from my own notes rather than from the page images. There is a stretch in the middle of the book where he develops the drain argument, and I can follow the bookkeeping half of it: a continuous one-way transfer of wealth out of India, through remittances, pensions, home charges, and the cost of an army officered largely by foreigners, impoverished the country even while it was being governed for its own good. What I cannot get a grip on is the second half of the claim, where the drain stops being a matter of money and becomes something more like a drain of capacity. He seems to say that the same machinery which moves the wealth out also moves out, or prevents the formation of, the ability to generate wealth in the first place, so that India is kept not merely poorer but structurally dependent and the deficit reproduces itself. I want to know how that inference is supposed to work. Is it an economic claim with a specifiable mechanism, such as underinvestment, the absence of a native capitalist class, the displacement of handicrafts, or the crowding out of Indian enterprise by British firms and state guarantees? Or is it a political claim about what a subject population is permitted to do, with the economics as a symptom rather than a cause? And when he calls the rule un-British, is that a substantive argument or merely a rhetorical one: does he mean that British conduct in India violates principles the British themselves profess, and if so, does that framing strengthen his case by meeting the rulers on their own ground, or weaken it by conceding that the standard of judgement is British?

What I would like from you is help reading the passage rather than a summary of the drain theory in general. Reconstruct the argument as a chain of premises, and tell me which step is actually load-bearing and which steps are decoration. Tell me what would have to be true about the Indian economy of the period for the capacity half of the claim to go through, and what evidence would falsify it. Explain the force and the cost of the un-British framing, including whether a historian in Ferguson's or Tharoor's position could accept that framing while rejecting Naoroji's conclusion, and what work the framing does that a straightforwardly economic indictment would not.

Finally, tell me where a reader today is most likely to over-read the passage. I am conscious that I may be importing concepts he did not have, such as dependency theory, underdevelopment, or extractive institutions, and that these might make him look either more theoretically sophisticated or more anachronistic than he is. If my paraphrase above has already flattened a distinction he is careful to draw, or has attributed to him a mechanism he would have rejected, say where and how, and tell me what in the passage itself would settle the question.
