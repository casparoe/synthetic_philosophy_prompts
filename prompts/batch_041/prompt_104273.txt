Hi — I sit on the board of a mid-size community credit union (we're a co-op, around 40k members, lots of low- and moderate-income folks, alot of them ex-payday-loan customers). Our innovation team keeps pitching a "goal lock" account and I have to circulate a written opinion before next Tuesday's board meeting, so I'm asking you to draft it. The design: a member names a savings goal and a target date, parks money in the account, and in exchange the money is illiquid until the date — except she can pull it out early by paying a fee, and the fee doesn't come back to us, it goes to a charity the member herself nominates as one she'd hate to be funding (yes, they lifted that straight from the commitment-contract apps). The team wants to launch it to everyone with the default being the hard lock, on the grounds that it's opt-in so nobody's coerced. Two board members are uneasy. One says we'd be monetizing members' self-control problems, and that the person most likely to sign up — someone who already knows she'll cave and raid the account — is precisely the person we'd be charging for rescuing her from herself. The other says it's soft paternalism either way: if she later changes her mind, locking her out means we're letting a past self boss around her present self, and the past self may just have been wrong about what she'd want. In the same meeting somebody called this "hyperbolic discounting" and somebody else called it "weakness of will," and I dont actually know whether those are the same thing or two different things that get smuggled into one another, and I suspect the answer matters for what we should do.

Please write the memo. I need a concrete recommendation — launch it, kill it, or launch it with specific design changes — with a real time frame (pilot or full rollout, a review date, whatever you can defend), and reasons the innovation team could push back on without me just intoning "paternalism" at them. And take the strongest objection seriously instead of dissolving it: if there's a real sense in which offering this exploits a member's weakness rather than helping her, say so, and say what follows for the design. For what it's worth I don't think the right answer is the same for a member who's naive about her future self-control as for one who knows exactly what she's doing when she signs up — I'm not sure our team has thought about that distinction, and honestly I'm not sure the board has either. Keep it short enough that I can read it aloud in ten minutes.
