Next Tuesday our health system's Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee votes on whether veloxipine keeps its place on formulary for hospitalized adults with severe community-acquired pneumonia; its temporary approval lapses at the end of the quarter, so this is the vote that decides it. I am the member assigned to write the review, and it has to be in the board packet two days before the meeting. What I need from you is that memo: a clear recommendation (keep, restrict to a defined population, or drop, with any conditions and a date on which we would revisit it), the reasoning set out in plain language for members who do not do statistics, and an honest statement of the strongest case for the conclusion I end up rejecting. That last part matters more than usual, because the person who will make that case sits on the committee and will circulate a rebuttal no matter what I write; a memo that caricatures his position will lose the room. It should also say what evidence would change our minds.

Here is what the two-year registry shows. Veloxipine, 600 patients: 79 deaths, 13.2 percent. Standard care, 600 patients: 52 deaths, 8.7 percent. On those numbers the drug looks worse. The registry also records a baseline severity band, assigned on admission before anyone chose a treatment. Veloxipine: low band 3 deaths out of 100, intermediate 16 of 200, high 60 of 300. Standard care: low 20 of 400, intermediate 18 of 150, high 14 of 50. Every band favors veloxipine, and the advantage grows the sicker the patient. The second analysis, prepared by our clinical pharmacist, stratifies by something the registry records at 24 hours, namely whether the patient went to the ICU. On that split veloxipine is worse in both strata: ICU 45 of 300 against 15 of 120, no ICU 34 of 300 against 37 of 480. His table agrees with the crude numbers, which is precisely why he trusts it. He also notes, correctly, that veloxipine carries a labeled risk of transient hypotension and that our protocol places some patients on ICU-level monitoring for the first 24 hours, which is why so many veloxipine patients appear in the ICU column to begin with. His argument is that the severity band is an unvalidated composite that no one in this room could defend, whereas ICU admission is the real-world marker of how sick someone actually was. Two other members have begun calling the whole business a Simpson's reversal and concluding that the data can be made to say anything; the pharmacist says it is just confounding and there is nothing paradoxical here at all. I cannot tell whether those two claims disagree about the arithmetic or about something else, and I would rather the memo stay out of it unless it bears on the vote.

Two further questions the memo should settle. First, whether approval should be limited to the high-severity band, where the absolute benefit is largest, even though the drug still looks better in the low band and restricting it there would deny it to patients who appear to benefit. Second, how much weight to give cost, roughly 1,400 dollars per course against a fixed drug budget the committee has already overspent twice this year. Please keep it to two pages, no citations are needed, and write it so that the recommendation can survive the pharmacist's rebuttal rather than ignore it.
