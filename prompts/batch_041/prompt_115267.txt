My friend's argument, and honestly every premise looks fine to me:

(1) Any inference from an object to a past belief or institution has to run through a general linking principle of the form "people who make or do X under conditions C think or believe Y."
(2) A linking principle is only as good as the evidence for it, and the only cases where we can check the link directly are cases where we can ask the people — living people, or people whose writing we can read.
(3) So every well-supported linking principle is supported only for people like us, or for people in the documented present.
(4) To use such a principle on the deep past you must assume the people there were relevantly like the people you checked — same kind of mind, same basic responses to similar situations.
(5) But that uniformity assumption is itself a claim about the past, and we can't establish it by asking anyone. The only route is inference from the material record — the very thing we're trying to justify.
(6) So every inference from object to past belief is either circular or rests on a uniformity assumption nobody has ever argued for.
(7) Therefore we can't know anything about what past people beleived or how their institutions worked.
(8) But we obviously do know some such things — nobody seriously thinks the ochre pit was an accident, and we're not wrong to say some buildings were temples, some burials were intentional, some tablets record taxes. So one of (1)-(6) has to go.
