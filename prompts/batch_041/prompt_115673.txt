Six months ago I joined the research council of a small institute that funds quantitative macrohistory, mostly because I have spent fifteen years writing history that resists being quantified and I wanted to see the other side from the inside. What I did not anticipate was spending the autumn refereeing the same argument between our two resident fellows: one a committed cliodynamician working in the Turchin tradition of secular cycles, demographic-structural strain and elite overproduction, the other a historian convinced that the whole cycle is an artifact of how the underlying data are coded and aggregated. They have been talking past each other since March, and in January I have to put something in front of the board that both of them can sign, with my name on it as the person who convened them.

Write that document. I want the claims they can genuinely both endorse: about what a cycle would have to be for the term to earn its keep, about what the coded historical databases can and cannot support, about which empirical patterns are not actually in dispute between them, and about what does and does not follow if a pattern is real but turns out to have no predictive content. Then I want the disagreements set out in wording that each of them would accept as a fair statement of his own position rather than a caricature. I expect at least three: whether the cycle is robust across datasets and coding decisions, whether the proposed mechanisms can be identified at all from evidence of this kind, and whether retrodicting past collapses carries any weight for forecasting future ones. Then the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move one of them, and for each crux the test, case or argument that both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep this concrete, use the four things already on the table between them. First, a null-history test: generate synthetic polities from a process with no internal cycle, calibrated to the variance of the real series, run the standard detection pipeline over them, and report what comes out. Second, a recoding test: redo the same histories under a different convention for where a polity begins and ends and for what counts as a collapse, and see whether the cycle survives. Third, a sealed forecast: a named set of countries and a horizon of one or two decades, predictions fixed now, scored against a naive baseline. Fourth, a mechanism probe: whether any within-country measure of elite competition leads instability once the ordinary business cycle is controlled for. If you judge one of these tests to be rigged in one camp's favour, say so and say why.

If some part of their quarrel turns out to be about words rather than about the world, identify it and explain why it dissolves. No citations and no reading lists, please; I know what each of them would put in those, and it would only restart the argument. Write it as continuous prose in ordinary paragraphs, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, so that it reads like a letter the two of them could send together.
