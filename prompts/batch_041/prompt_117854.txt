Act as a philosopher of technology and a conceptual engineer. I am preparing a seminar on technological determinism, and before we assess any particular account I want a specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the political content of artifacts must meet. The target is a theory that can say when and how a technical object, system, standard, or design expresses, enforces, or stabilizes a political order, and what makes that politics rather than mere distribution, economics, or aesthetics. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the requirements themselves and the cases behind them.

Start with a brief brainstorm of candidate requirements, then settle on a list. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I am especially interested in the following cases, which should be treated as constraints on the theory rather than as illustrations to be waved at: a parkway overpass with clearance too low for buses, which sorts who can reach a public beach; a technical standard that is formally open but in practice requires a proprietary tool to implement, so it decides who can enter a market; a platform interface whose default settings make some users’ work visible and others’ invisible without anyone deleting anything; a building code or accessibility rule that is satisfied on paper by an entrance at the back, so compliance and inclusion come apart; a risk-management device that reduces one harm while shifting it onto people who did not choose the tradeoff; an audit trail that is technically tamper-evident but practically unreadable without specialized expertise; a metric used for hiring, lending, or policing that is race-blind or gender-blind in its inputs but inherits historical patterns in its training data; and a maintenance regime that quietly decides which infrastructures keep working and which are allowed to decay.

I have been sorting the political registers of artifacts into four rough categories, and I want you to test whether these are the right cuts or whether the list should be redrawn: first, artifacts as settlements, where a design encodes the outcome of a past political conflict; second, artifacts as constraints, where a design changes the feasible set of actions for different agents; third, artifacts as scripts, where a design assigns roles, competencies, and obligations to users and non-users; fourth, artifacts as infrastructures, where a system distributes visibility, legibility, and maintainability. For each register, say what a theory would have to explain and what counterexample would force revision.

The theory must also respect distinctions such as designer intent versus artifact function versus downstream effect; artifact-level versus system-level politics; politics in design versus politics in use versus politics in maintenance and decay; necessary versus contingent political consequences; an artifact being political versus an artifact being used for politics; and politics that is visible to participants versus politics that operates by shaping what is thinkable. Decide which of these are genuine requirements and which are optional or camp-specific.

Flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what that impossibility would show. For instance, a theory that requires political content to be traceable to human intention may be unable to accommodate cases where the politics emerges only at the level of a system or a maintenance regime; a theory that requires political content to be observable in use may struggle with artifacts whose politics consists in preventing certain uses from ever being considered. If you see a sharper tension, give that instead. Also identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts.

Finally, give a brief verdict on whether any single family of accounts—designer-intent, functionalist, use-centered, affordance-based, or structural-power—can satisfy the non-negotiable list, and where each most likely fails. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria are the deliverable. At the end, state what argument or consideration would change your mind about the most contestable requirement on your list, and identify the point in your own reasoning where you are most likely to be wrong.
