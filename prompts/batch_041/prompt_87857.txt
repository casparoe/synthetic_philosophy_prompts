For the past few months a friend and I have been circling the same argument: she maintains that Norway is plainly the more liberal of the two countries, I keep coming back with Sweden, and we have begun to suspect that we are not disagreeing about the countries at all but about what the word 'liberal' is doing in the sentence. I run a fortnightly political theory discussion group for final-year undergraduates, and I should like to turn our stalemate into a handout for it — not a comparison of the two countries, but a classification instrument that maps the ways of answering the question. Please build me a taxonomy of the positions on what would settle which of two neighbouring countries is the more liberal, laid out as a decision tree: each branch node should be a question that separates the views beneath it, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, and ideally without any political theory behind them, only opinions about the countries; each leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed' where nobody defends it), the criterion it uses for 'more liberal', the kind of evidence that would settle the comparison on that reading, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions about what is being compared, what is being measured, how the measurements are aggregated, and who the subject of the comparison is — and only then place the actual positions within it. I want the leaves to include at least the following: the aggregate-index reading; the limited-government or negative-liberty reading; the republican reading on which freedom consists in not being dominated; the permissiveness reading that looks to drugs, alcohol, speech, religion and the regulation of private life; the law-in-action reading that looks at how rules are enforced rather than at what they say; the subject-relative reading on which the answer depends on whether we ask about citizens, residents, minorities or would-be immigrants; the reading on which the comparison is a partial order, so that the two countries are simply incomparable without a stipulated weighting; and the reading on which the question has no determinate answer because the criteria for applying the term are themselves contested. I would far rather have eight to twelve leaves that are genuinely distinct than twenty that blur into one another.

The tree has to accommodate a few cases I have found awkward, so please make sure each of them can be located somewhere on it: a country whose statute book is liberal but whose police and courts quietly ignore it; a country that is markedly liberal for its own citizens and markedly restrictive towards those who wish to become citizens; a country in which a stable majority would happily vote away a minority's rights but a constitutional court will not let it; a country whose laws are liberal but whose prevailing social pressure makes the liberties nearly worthless in practice; and a country whose record with an indigenous minority, such as the Sámi, cuts against the rest of its record. Where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, say so and diagnose why, distinguishing between a view that is incoherent, one that is merely unattractive, and one that is simply unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly ask, flag the pair rather than inventing a question that pretends to separate them, and tell me whether the difference between them is one of substance or merely of emphasis. Finally, add a short paragraph on how a reader of the handout should use the tree, including what to do when their answers land them in two leaves at once and whether the tree ever issues a verdict rather than a conditional one. I am not after a reading list or references — I want the structure itself — and I should like the whole thing written in British English.
