I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I sit as the lay member on the ethics advisory committee of a fertility clinic. The clinic is in a country where only expenses-only surrogacy is lawful and donor compensation is capped, and the committee decides which requests the clinic will take on or help facilitate. We have now decided six cases, and next month I vote on a seventh. I have been treating the six as data points and trying to fit a rule to them, because the minutes recite the same boilerplate every time — voluntariness, counseling, commercial element — without ever saying which of the three did the work.

Approved: (1) gestational surrogacy carried by the intended mother's sister, expenses only, both women independently counseled; (2) a woman donating eggs to a close friend, uncompensated, the donor identifiable to any resulting child, both counseled; (3) surrogacy carried by a friend who ran her own business and was comfortably better off than the intended parents, expenses only, both counseled.

Rejected: (4) a request to refer a couple to a foreign agency whose surrogate would be paid a fee, recruited by the agency, a first-time surrogate in debt, with no contact with the intended parents and a contract containing a penalty clause; (5) our own proposal to recruit egg donors for a per-cycle fee, advertised at universities, above the legal cap, without independent counseling; (6) a donation from a twenty-two-year-old who lived with the intended parents and depended on them financially, where no fee would be paid but the parents would cover her rent and tuition for three years, with no independent counseling for her.

Each of the six, as it happens, is defensible on at least three different grounds, which is why I am stuck rather than reassured.

The seventh case: a same-sex male couple in another country, where paid surrogacy is lawful, wants to commission a gestational surrogacy through a local agency using a compensated egg donor, and has asked whether our clinic will cooperate. The surrogate would be a repeat surrogate, has her own income, is not in debt, has her own lawyer and her own counselor, is paid in stages out of escrow, and could withdraw at any point; the couple is not wealthy relative to her.

Act as a bioethicist who works on reproductive markets and comparative policy. Start by brainstorming the candidate decision rules that fit our record, then predict how we will decide, say which rule you think we are really running and why you prefer it to the others that fit equally well, and name the further case that would best distinguish those rules from one another. If six decisions cannot support picking a rule, or if the honest reading is that we are managing legal exposure and reputation rather than applying a principle, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a rule for me.
