I'm in a seminar on rational choice and idealization this term -- we've done the ideal-advisor / ideal-observer stuff and then some of the less academic material on coherent extrapolated volition, the Yudkowsky idea where you take someone's values and extrapolate them as they'd be if the person knew more, thought faster, and had become more the sort of person they wished they were. For some reason the whole time we've been doing this I keep thinking about Groundhog Day. It seems to me the film is basically a machine for idealizing a person: Phil Connors is stuck repeating Feb 2, he can't permanently die, he can't opt out, and across what's implied to be thousands of loops he goes from a cynical jerk to a guy who learns piano, ice-sculpts, catches the kid falling out of the tree, and ends up genuinely kind -- and only then does the loop release him. So read the film as an argument about coherent extrapolated volition.

Here's the thing I can't get past. CEV is supposed to hold your values fixed and idealize your cognition -- you give the person more information and better reasoning, but you don't change what they care about, because if you change that you're not discovering their volition anymore, you're inventing a new agent and calling it theirs. But Groundhog Day does roughly the opposite: it's Phil's values that get rebuilt, while his cognition is basically intact the whole time (he understands the situation almost immediately, he's not confused or misinformed). So is this a story about a true self being revealed under pressure, or about one self being replaced by a better one that just happens to wear the same face? And notice that CEV's own formula quietly smuggles in a transformation clause -- "more the person they wished they were" -- which is exactly the kind of move the film seems to dramatize. So does Groundhog Day actually refute CEV by showing there's no antecedent coherent volition to extrapolate, only a process that manufactures one, or can the theory be patched? If we say the loop is just an unusually vivid epistemic input that lets Phil finally see the consequences of his existing values, does that rescue it or is it cheating?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the idealization literature seriously and doesn't just say "Phil gets good, so idealization works." Point me at the specific beats that decide it -- the phase where he exploits the loop and manipulates people, the suicidal phase, the day he engineers everything perfectly to win Rita and it still doesn't take, the last day where he isn't performing for anyone. Where does the film dramatize the idealization-vs-transformation distinction and where does it blur it? And tell me honestly which side the film comes down on, or whether it's ambivalent. This is for a term paper so I'd rather have the hard version of the objection than a tidy answer.
