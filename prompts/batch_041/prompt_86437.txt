I've been going back and forth with a couple of people in a reading group about the intentional stance and AI systems, and I've ended up defending a position I'm not sure I can defend properly, so I want you to attack it. Here's the view: attributing beliefs to a language model is literally apt, not just a useful fiction or a metaphor. When a model reliably gets the right answers across paraphrases, pushes back when a premise is false, updates in the way a believer would update, and does this about some subject matter over many different phrasings, there is nothing more to "the model believes P" than that. That's the same standard I use for my dog, for a toddler, and honestly for most people I know -- I can't inspect anyone's inner representations, I just see a pattern in what they do and say, and if the pattern is robust and counterfactual-supporting, I call it a belief. The intentional stance isn't a ladder you climb on the way to something more real, it's the whole game. Dennett basically said this decades ago (I think he even called himself a mild realist about the patterns, though I may be mangling the label), and I don't see what changes when the system is made of silicon instead of meat. What I keep getting told is either that this is trivially true or that it's obviously false, which usually means nobody has actually tried to say what would count against it.

So: what would count against it? I don't want a survey of the literature, I want the defeaters themselves, sorted by how much they should actually move me. Some candidates I've thought of and don't know what to do with: (1) if the pattern turns out to be gerrymandered -- the model gives contradictory answers depending on phrasing, and there's no grain of description at which a stable belief shows up, just a smear; (2) the "it's really just roleplay" rival -- the model is simulating a character who has beliefs, and that hypothesis predicts all the same behavior, so maybe my view loses on parsimony; (3) the point that whatever intentionality the thing has is inherited from us and from the training data, which I've never understood as anything other than a genetic fallacy, but maybe I'm missing something; (4) that there's no persisting subject there at all, no occurrent states between forward passes, so at best you get dispositional belief-talk with nobody home; (5) that self-reports from the model about its own "beliefs" are confabulated and prompt-sensitive, which I take to be a problem for using reports as evidence but not obviously for the attribution itself; (6) the whole design-stance point -- if the mechanistic story predicts the outputs just as well, does my intentional description earn anything, or is it a free-riding redescription? I'd be most moved by things that are empirical and measurable (an experiment I could in principle run that would come out wrong for me), and least moved by arguments that amount to "it's not a real believer because it's not made of the right stuff," but tell me if that's just my bias protecting the view.

Give it to me in this shape: first a section called "What I'm actually committed to" (~150 words) where you pin down the position precisely and say which nearby views I'm not committed to, since I suspect I'm being attacked for things I never said. Then "Defeaters, ranked" (~700 words), working from the ones that would be fatal down to the merely awkward, and for each one say what would have to be true, what it would show, and whether anyone actually has evidence for it or whether it's just a possible scenario. Then "What my own side should concede" (~300 words) -- not what critics say, what a careful defender of my view should admit counts against them. Then "How exposed am I really?" (~300 words) on whether the position risks anything at all or whether the move to "interest-relative, no fact of the matter" has insulated it into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a defect for a view of this kind or just what you sign up for when the thing you're analyzing is a pattern rather than an object. Finish with "Bottom line" (~100 words): is this position live, and what single thing would most change my mind.
