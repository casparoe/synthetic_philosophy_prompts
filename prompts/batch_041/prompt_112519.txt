I write about science and tech for a business daily, so a lot of my job is stories that boil down to: company installs new system, workers push back, everybody says the tool made them do it. And there's a shape to these stories that I keep running into and don't have a name for.

Here it is. A place adopts something new -- a shift-scheduling system, a new publishing platform, whatever -- and the same pecking order as before reassembles itself inside it. Same bottlenecks, same two people who get to decide, different interface. Fine, thats office politics, nobody's shocked. But then the second half. Everyone, me included, explains the arrangement by pointing at the tool: the software requires approvals in this order, the algorithm can't take that input, the format won't let you file it any other way. Then the tool gets swapped out, and the arrangement survives intact. Same approvals, same missing input, same filing, with nothing in the software demanding any of it. Which makes me think the appeal to what the machine can't do was doing justificatory work, not mechanical work -- people reached for the tool as the reason things had to be the way they were, and the tool was never the reason. And I get the mirror image too: when a format genuinely cannot represent something, when a field simply doesn't exist in the schema, sources shrug it off as "thats just politics, somebody chose it."

A colleague told me this is technological determinism, but I don't think thats quite it. I'm not asking whether technology drives history. I'm asking about a specific move: invoking what a tool can and can't do as the grounds for a human arrangement having to look the way it does. Is there an established term for that move? If not, coin one and define it, but either way I don't want a label, I want the joints. Where's the line between this and (a) the tech actually foreclosing something, (b) designers deliberately building a politics into an artifact, and (c) ordinary rationalization, where any excuse would do? Does it matter whether the person saying it believes it? And whats the test -- how do I tell, in a given case, whether I'm looking at a real constraint or an alibi wearing one? No reading list please, I won't chase citations, I just want the thing carved up well enough that I can use it in a story.
