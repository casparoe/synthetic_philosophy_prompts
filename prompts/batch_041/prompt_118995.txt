I'm trying to work through the typogenetics material in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach alongside the opening sections of Gödel's 1931 paper, and the pairing has produced a tension I can't resolve on my own. I'd like help understanding a difficult stretch of the texts, and then a judgment about whether the comparison Hofstadter draws is deep or merely suggestive.

First, some background, so that your answer doesn't depend on my reader having the book in front of them. Typogenetics is a toy formal system whose objects are strands: strings over a small alphabet. A mechanical translation procedure reads a strand and yields a set of enzymes; each enzyme is itself a short string, and each of its letters corresponds to a fixed typographical operation (cutting, deleting, inserting, moving along a strand, and so on). An enzyme acts on a strand at a site determined by its own letters. Strands are therefore both data and recipes, enzymes are programs that were themselves manufactured from recipes, and a strand is self-replicating when the enzymes it encodes, applied in sequence to it, produce a copy of it.

The passage I'm stuck on is a paraphrase, not a quotation; I've compressed two texts into one paragraph, so please treat it as a reconstruction of the argument rather than as anyone's exact words.

Gödel observes, in a footnote to the 1931 paper, that the self-referential sentence is a device for exhibiting incompleteness rather than its source. The source, he suggests, is that a formal system makes available only countably many levels of type while the hierarchy of types can be continued into the transfinite; the undecidable sentences he constructs become decidable as soon as a suitable higher type is adjoined, and something analogous holds for set theory. Hofstadter, in the typogenetics material, treats the relation between Gödel's proof and molecular biology as an isomorphism: a strand is at once a string of symbols and a set of instructions, the enzymes that copy it are themselves encoded by strands, and a strand that produces the enzymes which copy it is doing in a causal-typographical register what a formal system does when it proves a sentence about its own proofs. On this view self-reference and self-replication are two faces of one fact, and the toy genetics is designed so that the search for a self-replicating strand can succeed.

The tension is this. If Gödel is right that self-reference is inessential to incompleteness, then typogenetics, which is a machine for producing self-replication, may be illustrating a contingent feature of one particular proof rather than the deep structure of the phenomenon. If Hofstadter is right that self-replication just is self-reference under a code, then Gödel's footnote must be either wrong or misread. I'd like to know which way to go.

Specifically:

1. What is Gödel's footnote actually claiming? I want the distinction between the claim that self-reference is not the only route to an undecidable sentence and the stronger claim that self-reference is not the reason such sentences exist. Does the remark about higher types commit him to anything about whether a self-referential construction is dispensable, or is it a remark about where the truth of the undecidable sentence gets decided?

2. I have been distinguishing what I call designation fixed points from production fixed points. A Gödel sentence is a fixed point of a definable predicate: a sentence that is equivalent to the assertion that no number codes a proof of it. A self-replicating strand is a fixed point of a computable dynamical map: a string that the operations induced by its own translation carry to a copy of itself. Are these instances of one fixed-point phenomenon in different categories, or are they two different things that share a name? If the latter, which one deserves to be called the diagonal construction?

3. Does typogenetics contain anything that plays the role of an undecidable sentence? Concretely: is there a strand that no sequence of enzyme actions can produce from a given starting strand, but which we can nonetheless recognize as the one the system was, so to speak, aiming at? If the question is not well posed as it stands, say what would have to be added — negation, a notion of well-formed strand, a provability-like predicate, a specification of which strands count as axioms — before it becomes well posed. And tell me whether the answer would be a fact about the toy system or a fact about our description of it.

4. A test case I invented, which may or may not be well formed. Add a blocking rule to typogenetics: an enzyme, once bound at a site, prevents other enzymes from acting on overlapping sites, and releases the site after one operation. Does self-replication survive the restriction? Does the question in (3) change character? I want to know whether the isomorphism is robust to changes in the operational rules or whether it depends on the absence of such constraints. Relatedly: what happens if enzymes are allowed to make errors, so that copying is imperfect? DNA's fidelity is enforced by machinery rather than guaranteed by the code, whereas Gödel numbering's fidelity is stipulated by us. Does that asymmetry matter for the comparison, or is it a difference in implementation rather than in structure?

5. Finally, where does the isomorphism break, and is the break shallow or deep? My own suspicion is that it breaks over the difference between a code whose decoding is causally enforced by machinery and a code whose decoding is stipulated, and over the difference between truth and replication as the semantic level of the respective systems. Tell me whether that is the right place to put the break, or whether there is a deeper one. And if the comparison is more than a pun, say what typogenetics shows about incompleteness that a proof of the diagonal lemma does not.
