I sit on the governance committee of a midsize grantmaking foundation: roughly forty trustees, an endowment in the low nine figures, and a long habit of deciding grants by majority vote after open discussion. A trustee has formally proposed a bylaws amendment that would replace one-trustee-one-vote with a weighted scheme. Each trustee's vote would be multiplied by a factor between 0.5 and 3, set annually from a calibration exercise in which trustees forecast observable outcomes about our own portfolio — which grantees will miss milestones, which programs will hit their targets, where the next funding gap opens — scored with a proper scoring rule, with the multiplier rising smoothly in the score. Trustees who skip the exercise default to the 0.5 floor. Weights would be public, and trustees could delegate their weight to another trustee, revocably. The amendment also carries a renewal clause: it runs for a three-year trial, and the vote on whether to renew it would itself be conducted under the weighted scheme.

Our committee has to settle its position before Thursday's board meeting, so please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt with specified modifications — with a timeline, and it should give the strongest argument on the losing side a real hearing rather than a ritual one. Two things I keep going back and forth on and want the memo to address squarely: whether a body whose members have demonstrably different track records is being irresponsible in counting those records equally, and whether a scheme that governs the vote on its own renewal can be legitimate even if it produces better decisions. I have read enough of the aggregation literature to know that when errors are correlated, adding heads does not help, so I would rather the memo reason from first principles than gesture at the wisdom of crowds. I am also aware that once weights hang on a score, the score becomes something people will manage.
