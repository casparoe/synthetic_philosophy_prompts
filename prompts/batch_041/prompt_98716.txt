I recently found myself refereeing an argument between two friends over a small thought experiment I had devised, and what I actually want from you is a careful comparison of the concepts they were deploying, not a ruling on the scenario itself. The setup is this. Two people disagree, sincerely and after long reflection, about which cause most deserves their money and attention: one is convinced that the gravest ongoing wrong is the industrial farming of animals, the other that it is a distant, slow-onset humanitarian crisis that almost no one is funding. Neither has moved the other, and neither believes the other to be wicked or confused. They nevertheless reach an arrangement: over the coming year, each directs a fixed share of their giving to the other's preferred cause, and each also takes on one concrete personal burden that the other regards as important and that they themselves regard as merely irksome. Because their priorities are ordered differently, each is giving up something they rank comparatively low in exchange for something they rank comparatively high, and both walk away describing themselves as having done more good than they otherwise would have, with neither feeling that a principle has been betrayed. My first friend calls this a moral trade and treats it as straightforwardly commendable. My second friend insists it is really an ordinary compromise of the familiar kind, merely dressed up in the vocabulary of mutual gain.

Please compare the two concepts that are genuinely at issue here, namely the concept of a moral trade and the concept of a moral compromise, rather than the two friends' opinions about the case. Begin by setting out what each concept is at its core and what work it is meant to do, then work through what separates them and where the separation begins to collapse. I want you to press especially on three points. First, does a moral trade really leave both parties better off by their own moral lights, or does it merely distribute the losses differently than a compromise would, so that the apparent mutual gain is an artifact of how the exchange is described? Second, does the trade presuppose something contestable, namely that the goods at stake are commensurable enough to be swapped at some rate, or can it survive a firm insistence that the two parties' values are incommensurable and admit of no exchange rate at all? Third, does the trade require each participant to treat their own moral commitments as things they may spend or bank, and if so, has too much already been conceded? Close with a defended verdict about which of the two concepts better captures what is happening in the scenario, and state what would have to be true for the other concept to be the better description.
