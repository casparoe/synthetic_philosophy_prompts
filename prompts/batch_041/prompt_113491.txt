I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and for a seminar on the epistemology of formal reasoning I have been using the MU-puzzle, as Hofstadter presents it, as my running example of the difference between working inside a system and reasoning about one. While drafting that section I built the following argument, and I have come to think it proves far too much. I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

The background, as I understand it. The MIU-system begins from the string MI and has four rules: append U to any string ending in I; double everything after the initial M; replace any occurrence of III by U; delete any occurrence of UU. The puzzle is to derive MU, and the answer is that it cannot be done. The usual reason is an invariant: every theorem of the system has a number of I's that is not divisible by three, whereas MU has no I's at all, and zero is divisible by three. But that invariant is not itself a rule of the system; it is a fact about the system, established by standing outside it.

The argument:

(1) Any claim about which strings a formal system derives is either derivable within that system or established from outside it.
(2) The claim that MU is underivable in the MIU-system is not derivable within the MIU-system — the rules speak about strings, not about what the system can and cannot derive — so it is established from outside.
(3) Whatever is established from outside a system, and is not licensed by its rules, is in effect an addition to that system: what the rules do not supply the system does not contain, and a principle fixing which strings are to count as theorems plays the same role an extra rule would play.
(4) So the invariant argument tells us nothing about the MIU-system as such; it describes a different system — the MIU-system with the invariant principle attached — and proves something about that.
(5) Moving to that enlarged system is a free stipulation, and so is the move to a system in which MU is simply taken as an axiom. Both are unforced choices made from outside, and neither is answerable to anything except our convenience.
(6) So there is no fact of the matter about whether MU is derivable, only facts about which stipulations we find congenial. Generalizing, no formal system has determinate limits, and the difference between proving a theorem and positing one disappears.
(7) But there is a fact of the matter about MU, and the invariant argument is a proof rather than a stipulation. So some step above is wrong.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (7) than (6), though I suspect I am also attached to (2), so I would like to know whether that attachment is part of the problem. I would also like to know what becomes of the thought that made the example interesting to me in the first place: that the invariant is not obtainable from the rules by any routine procedure, and that this is why the puzzle is a decent model of mathematical discovery. If the repair makes the invariant look mechanical after all, say so, and say what is left of the analogy.

End your answer with a single final line of exactly the form Verdict: (n) — <a label of at most eight words>, naming the step you reject.
