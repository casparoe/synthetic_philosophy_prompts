So I got into this long, stupid argument with my friend Dev last week — he's doing a psych PhD and he's read all the Milgram and the Darley & Batson good-Samaritan stuff and the Mischel person–situation literature, and his line is basically Harman and Doris: there's no such thing as character, there are only "local" traits — helpful-in-a-hurry, honest-when-someone's-watching — and virtue ethics is a nice pre-scientific fantasy that the experiments quietly killed. And I kept saying that's ridiculous, I know people with real character, and he kept saying "name one," and I couldn't think of one fast enough, and then a few days later I was rereading The Remains of the Day (we'd just watched the Merchant Ivory film with Hopkins and Thompson, which is why it was on my mind) and I thought: Stevens. Obviously Stevens. A man whose entire life is one trait — dignity — held under enormous pressure, at the cost of his father, the woman he loves, and his own moral judgment, and he never cracks. And then I thought, wait, or does the book actually prove Dev right? Which is what I want you to sort out.

I want a reading of the novel aimed squarely at the virtue-ethics-versus-situationism fight — not the usual "the butler is repressed" essay. Some things I'd like you to actually take seriously. (a) Stevens's dignity looks like exactly the thing Doris says doesn't exist: a trait that holds across situations. But look at what it really is — a professional role, drilled in, exercised only inside Darlington Hall and only in front of staff and guests; and when the Hall changes hands and Farraday turns up, the trait has to be rebuilt from scratch (the whole bantering project at the end), which is weirdly good news for the situationist. (b) Miss Kenton is the closest thing to a controlled experiment in the book: same house, same years, same pressures, and she objects, she threatens to resign over the two housemaids being sacked for being Jewish, she says what she feels — two people in the same situation behaving differently, which is the sort of datum the situationist has to explain away; except she also backs down and stays, so it cuts both ways. (c) Which scenes should I actually be pointing my reading group at, and what does each one show — the maids, the night she tells him about the proposal and he stays on duty, the secret meeting with Ribbentrop, the bench on the seafront at the end where he admits he can't even claim his own mistakes as his? I want the reading faithful in both directions: don't turn Stevens into a moral lesson about repression, and don't soften the philosophy to fit him.

One more thing I'm curious about, and I don't think anyone says it well: a novel is a machine for producing characters, and "character" in the moral sense might be a byproduct of the form — the novel needs Stevens to be one thing for 250 pages, so of course he looks like a robust trait, and maybe that's precisely the illusion the situationists are warning about. Or maybe it's the opposite: the novel can show the inside of a trait, the years of self-policing and the sheer labor of it, which no experiment can, because an experiment only ever sees a person for an afternoon. Does that get the situationist anywhere, or is it just an excuse I'm making for my team? And where does the novel resist the whole reading — is there something in it that neither side's vocabulary quite catches? I'd like you to end by saying how confident you are in what you've claimed and singling out the one point you're least sure about; I'd much rather you flag the shaky bit than smooth it over.
