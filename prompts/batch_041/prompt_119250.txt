You are a comparative macrohistorian with deep knowledge both of cliodynamics — specifically the structural-demographic theory and secular-cycle framework developed by Peter Turchin and colleagues out of Jack Goldstone's work on revolutions — and of Tokugawa-period Japanese social history. I want a disciplined case analysis, not a summary of the theory, and I want you to brainstorm the mapping before you commit to it.

Take Tokugawa Japan from the early seventeenth century to the Meiji Restoration of 1868 and run it through structural-demographic theory. Assume the following salient facts: after the wars of unification the warrior class was frozen into a hereditary status comprising some five to eight percent of the population and drawing stipends in rice; the alternate-attendance system obliged daimyo households to maintain expensive dual residences in Edo; population growth stalled by the early eighteenth century while commercialization, urban markets, and a money economy expanded; the fiscal position of the shogunate and of many domains deteriorated; famines and urban riots punctuated the period; the Kyoho, Kansei, and Tempo reform programs tried and largely failed to restore finances; growing numbers of samurai slid into debt, sold or mortgaged their rank, or became masterless ronin; and the regime ended not in peasant revolution or general collapse but in a civil war and a rapid, elite-led reconstruction of the state, triggered by the external shock of Perry's arrival in 1853.

Do four things. First, name the theory's variables — population pressure, elite overproduction, popular immiseration, state fiscal distress, and the integrative/disintegrative phase structure — and say what value each takes here, with the specific evidence you would want to settle it. Second, say where the fit is good and where it is bad, and in particular consider whether the immiseration doing the causal work was mass immiseration at all, or rather the downward mobility of a hereditary elite whose size and status could not adjust. Third, treat the closed, hereditary elite as a test of the theory: ask whether structural-demographic theory implicitly presupposes an open recruitment channel — credential inflation, patronage, upward mobility — through which elite overproduction can be expressed, and what becomes of the mechanism when elite numbers are fixed by law and only relative status and wealth can decay. If it helps, introduce a distinction between an aspirant counter-elite excluded from office and an entrenched incumbent elite, and say whether that distinction does work the theory's single elite variable cannot. Fourth, close with what the case shows about the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or boundary condition. Does the fact that the disintegrative phase was resolved by a segment of the lower-ranking elite capturing and rebuilding the state, rather than by demographic collapse and reconstitution from below, count against the theory, or can the theory absorb it without amendment?

Keep the analysis tightly bound to the case, and where the theory is silent — about ideology, about the content of the reform programs, about why this coalition of domains rather than another — say so rather than forcing it to speak. No reading lists or citations; I want the argument.
