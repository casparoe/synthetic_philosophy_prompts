You are a philosopher of public memory and an expert in the ethics of commemoration, advising a municipal commission that must decide whether to remove, retain, relocate, or recontextualize monuments and honorific street names. You have no access to external sources; treat the following record as your entire evidence base. Think carefully, and before you recommend a verdict, brainstorm at least three candidate decision rules that fit the Commission’s past rulings equally well. Then answer the question at the end.

The Public Memory Commission of the city of Marlow has issued the following rulings over the past fifteen years. Its enabling ordinance directs it to consider, among other things, whether a monument’s continued presence exacts an ongoing expressive cost from a living constituency that contextualization cannot adequately repair — a clause the local press calls the living-constituency standard — but the Commission has never defined that standard precisely.

1. The Kettle statue. A statue of General Amos Kettle, a minor officer in a failed separatist rebellion, was erected in 1912 by a heritage society at the main entrance to Marlow Park, during the city’s era of legally enforced racial segregation. The Commission voted 5–2 to remove the statue. The majority wrote that the statue’s original dedication was part of a campaign to intimidate the city’s Black residents, and that its prominent public site continued to send that message.

2. The Vale statue. A statue of Thaddeus Vale, a merchant who enslaved people and whose donation of land made possible the city’s first public library, was erected in 1840 by a citizens’ committee. The Commission voted 6–1 to retain the statue in front of the library, with a new plaque that names the people Vale enslaved and explains how the library’s land came from that wealth. The majority wrote that the statue commemorated civic benefaction, that contextualization could correct the record, and that removal would erase a complicated history.

3. The Renn statue. A statue of Dr. Silas Renn, a physician who founded the city’s first tuberculosis sanatorium and also advocated compulsory sterilization of people he deemed unfit, was erected in 1925 by the county medical society in front of the hospital. The Commission voted 4–3 to relocate the statue to the city museum, with an exhibit on the history of eugenics in medicine. The majority wrote that the hospital entrance was a place of healing, and that leaving the statue there falsely suggested institutional endorsement of Renn’s eugenics; but the statue had historical value and should be preserved in a critical setting.

4. The Drayton statue. A statue of Mayor Celia Drayton, a civil rights leader who was later convicted of taking bribes and steering city contracts to allies, was erected in 1982 by a coalition of her supporters while she was still in office. The Commission voted 5–2 to remove the statue from the plaza in front of City Hall. The majority wrote that the statue was a tribute by beneficiaries of her patronage, that its site conflated her civil rights legacy with her corruption, and that a plaque could not neutralize what they called its honorific residue.

5. The Kessler street name. A street was named in 1901 for August Kessler, an industrialist whose factories used child labor and who later endowed the city orphanage. The name is now used for a neighborhood, a bus route, and a school district. The Commission voted 6–1 to retain the name, with an interpretive plaque at the neighborhood library. The majority wrote that the name had become a generic geographic identifier, that removing it would disrupt residents and erase the neighborhood’s identity, and that contextualization was sufficient.

The new case: the Voss statue. Dr. Elara Voss was a surgeon who developed a technique that saved thousands of lives, but between 1948 and 1955 she performed experimental surgeries on institutionalized patients, including children with disabilities, without consent; some patients died and others were permanently injured. The statue was erected in 1960 by the hospital’s board of trustees at the main entrance of Marlow General Hospital. Its plaque identifies her as a healer, innovator, and benefactor, and says nothing about the experiments. A coalition of disability rights advocates and descendants of the patients has petitioned for removal. The hospital historical society proposes keeping the statue, adding a plaque that describes the experiments, and commissioning a companion memorial to the victims on the hospital grounds.

The Commission must now decide what to recommend. What should it decide if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents? Identify the candidate rules that fit the earlier rulings, say what each predicts for the Voss statue, give your best recommendation and the reasons for preferring it, name the closest analog among the five earlier cases, and — if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome — state what principle the Commission would be adopting with each available choice. Finally, name a further case that would best discriminate between the candidate rules.
