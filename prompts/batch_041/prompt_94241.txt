I'm in a political theory seminar that's mostly Rawls and Habermas, and I keep getting stuck on Pascal because his politics seem to come from a totally different planet. What I take from him (correct me if this is a misreading, and I'd rather you flag it than quietly smooth it over): justice on its own can't accomplish anything because there are always people who won't comply, force on its own is just tyranny, so in practice the two get soldered together — the law is obeyed partly because it's backed and partly because people believe it's right. And he seems to think the belief does more work than we admit, since what counts as just in a given place is largely what has been established there; it's dangerous to disabuse ordinary people of the idea that their laws are just, because the alternative is civil war, which he treats as close to the worst thing that can happen to a polity. He also clearly thinks there's a real justice that isn't the established one, but locates it in God rather than in any earthly tribunal. So he's got this odd combination: deep skepticism about the justice of any actual legal order, plus a strong practical demand that you not destabilize it, plus a sense that the magistrate's authority is a kind of borrowed dignity resting on custom rather than merit.
