I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and last term I sat in on the social choice course that my department cross-lists with the economics faculty — long enough to have absorbed the statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, not long enough to feel confident adjudicating it. A colleague in my reading group recently circulated a long essay on democracy and expertise, the sort of piece that gets passed around and cited approvingly in seminars, and its treatment of the theorem has been nagging at me ever since. The author is a political theorist rather than an economist, and my suspicion is that the essay extracts more from the theorem than the theorem can bear; but I cannot locate the slippage precisely, and I would rather be told I am wrong than go on half-suspecting. For what it is worth, I am inclined to think the result really is an embarrassment for anyone who talks about elections revealing the will of the people, so I have no stake in rescuing the essay's target.

Here is the relevant passage, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it: 'Gibbard and Satterthwaite showed in the 1970s that every voting rule — any procedure that takes the voters' rankings of the candidates and produces a winner — can be manipulated: whatever the rule, there is some voter, in some configuration of preferences, who can do better by reporting a ranking other than her true one. The usual way of putting this is that the only strategy-proof voting rules are dictatorships, meaning rules in which one person's preferences simply determine the outcome, as in an autocracy. This is really the same result as Arrow's impossibility theorem, transposed from the language of aggregation into the language of incentives; both say that no procedure can be built that reliably registers what people actually want. The assumptions are minimal — you need at least two candidates, and voters who can rank them — so there is nothing to design around. The upshot is that honest voting is impossible in principle. When a ranked-choice or approval ballot asks citizens to state their preferences, what it collects is not a preference but a strategic guess about how everyone else will vote; the ballot is a fiction, and the more sophisticated the method, the more elaborate the fiction. Critics sometimes object that the theorem assumes voters are calculating maximizers who care only about the outcome, and that real people often vote expressively or out of a sense of duty; but this is wishful thinking, since anyone who cares about the outcome at all has an incentive to misreport, and the theorem's assumptions are too thin to be dodged.' The essay then concludes that because no voting rule can elicit the public's genuine preferences, the case for democratic procedures over expert or technocratic decision-making is weaker than democrats like to admit.

What I want is an audit, not a verdict on the essay as a whole. Take the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a social choice theorist would accept as stated, which are the sort of compression that is forgivable in a popular piece but would need qualification in a seminar, and which are simply wrong; I would especially like to know which hypotheses of the theorem the summary quietly drops, and how much of the essay's rhetorical force depends on those omissions. Then tell me whether the closing inference survives — granted everything true in the summary, does anything follow about technocracy versus democracy? — and what a defender of ranked-choice voting or of Condorcet methods should concede and where she should push back. Finally, if the objection the essay mentions is a standard one, say whether it is a refutation or merely a limitation on how the theorem may be read, and whether the essay's reply to it is any good.
