I build multi-agent coordination systems for a living: my working vocabulary is shared state, message passing, consensus protocols, common knowledge, eventual consistency, commitments as explicit objects in a protocol, and norms that are enforced rather than merely desired. I have little formal training in philosophy, but I understand that philosophers of collective intentionality dispute whether two or more agents can literally intend and act together, and whether the resulting we is anything over and above facts about the individuals.

Restate the main positions in that debate, one by one and as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use, and then give me a residue report for each: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all. The positions I want covered are: the view that we-intending is a primitive, biologically basic mode of intending that individual minds possess, and is not assembled out of individual intentions plus beliefs about one another; the view that a shared intention is simply a suitable interlocking structure of individual intentions, plans, and common knowledge, with no additional ingredient; the view that genuinely shared action requires a joint commitment that constitutes a new normative subject — a plural subject — bearing obligations and entitlements of its own; the view that the important contrast is between acting in a we-mode and acting in an I-mode, with group reasons that are not merely the members' reasons aggregated; and the view that a group can itself be an agent, with attitudes and responsibilities, rather than only a collection of individuals.

The residue report is the part I care about most, so keep it concrete. For each position, tell me which of its claims map onto machinery I already build (common knowledge, commitments as explicit protocol objects, shared goals, norms as enforceable constraints), which map only after a fudge, and which fall outside my vocabulary entirely. I want a verdict on three specific questions. First, the bindingness of a joint commitment — that a participant is answerable, and not merely predictably cooperative — does it have any counterpart in my world, or do I only ever model incentives and equilibria and leave the ought stranded? Second, the first-person plural perspective, what it is like to be one of a we that is the subject of an action: can that be expressed in terms of agents and messages at all, or is it pure residue? Third, when a position resists translation, is that because my vocabulary is genuinely missing a primitive, or because the philosophical claim was doing less work than it sounded like — for instance, quietly presupposing an unexplained we in its premises?

To close: if I wanted to add the missing primitive to my framework as honestly as I could, what would it be, and what would it cost me — what would break in my protocol design or my verification story if I took joint commitment as basic rather than derived? Treat the residue as the interesting part, not as noise to be explained away.
