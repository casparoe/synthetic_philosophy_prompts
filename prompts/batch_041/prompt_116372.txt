I am a science and ideas journalist at work on a feature about cooperation that requires no communication, and I have spent the past few weeks inside a research program that has circulated under two names: it was first presented as multiverse-wide superrationality, and it is now usually called evidential cooperation in large worlds. As I understand the claim, it runs roughly like this. In a universe large enough to contain many agents, among them agents who are relevantly like me in how they deliberate but unlike me in what they care about, my own deliberation is evidence about how those other agents will choose, because agents who reason alike about alike problems tend to arrive at alike answers. Because my choice and theirs are correlated in that evidential way, I can benefit from their choices even though nothing I do can causally reach them, and they can benefit from mine, so I should adopt the policy that fares best on the assumption that my adopting it is evidence about what they adopt. On this view the payoff is not a causal exchange but the evidential coupling itself, and the largeness of the world matters because there must be enough such agents for the coupling to be worth anything at all.

The idea I want that set against is the older and more general one that Douglas Hofstadter christened superrationality. In its usual telling, two agents who know themselves to be the same kind of reasoner, facing the same problem, cannot treat their choices as independent: whatever line of reasoning would lead me to defect would lead my counterpart to defect too, so I should evaluate the available strategies as though everyone in that position must land on the same one. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma this is supposed to yield cooperation rather than defection.

Please compare the two, and in doing so tell me what, if anything, the newer idea adds beyond the older one, and where the older one demands more than the newer one can supply. I would like the comparison to be specific on four points. First, superrationality is standardly stated for agents whose reasoning is identical and whose payoffs coincide, so that the symmetry is exact and the sameness is known to them; does evidential cooperation need anything that strong, or does it survive with agents who merely correlate with me probabilistically, or who reason by a different method altogether? Second, the large-world version is motivated precisely by the prospect of cooperating with agents whose values are not mine, since that is where the gains from trade are supposed to arise, whereas the classic superrational story concerns agents with a common payoff structure; what does the divergence in values change — only the size of the prize, or the decision rule itself? Third, what work is done by the largeness, the sheer multiplicity of agents and regions, as opposed to the correlation, and could the same logic run with exactly two agents, or does it need the many to have any force? Fourth, does either idea depend on a particular decision theory, evidential as against causal, or are both available to anyone who accepts the correlations the argument assumes?

Then give me your verdict on which of the two the people working on this are really running. And work both ideas through the case I keep returning to, because it seems to me to be the one that separates them cleanly: a counterpart in a causally disconnected region who is my equal in reasoning power but whose values are the exact inversion of mine, so that what it wants is what I most want to avoid and the reverse. Does superrationality say anything determinate about such a pair, does evidential cooperation, and do the two agree? If they come apart, tell me the case that shows it most cleanly, and tell me which idea, in the end, deserves to be called the more general of the two.
