I'm a graduate student in philosophy of language, and I've been asked to run the first meeting of a reading group on vagueness for students who can state the sorites paradox and have a rough sense of what epistemicism and supervaluationism say, but who have never had to commit themselves on the underlying questions. I want to open the session with a decision tree that takes them from a starting point almost everyone accepts -- that in a sorites series from a clear heap to a clear non-heap, or from a clearly tall person to a clearly short one, there is no adjacent pair that seems different enough to mark a boundary -- to a named position. Please write that tree. The branch nodes should be diagnostic questions about concrete cases and claims, phrased so that a student can answer without knowing which label the answer leads to. They should cover, at least: whether there is a fact of the matter about the first borderline case; whether the relevant sentence is true, false, or neither at that case; whether the failure of bivalence, if there is one, belongs to language, thought, or the world; whether borderline is itself vague and whether that generates higher-order vagueness; whether the predicate's extension shifts with context or with a speaker's practical purposes; whether borderline applies to sentences or to objects; and whether the sorites argument is best diagnosed as invalid, unsound, or valid but with a conclusion we are not forced to accept.

The leaves should include epistemicism, supervaluationism, subvaluationism or other glutty/paraconsistent views, degree-theoretic or fuzzy views, contextualism, relativism about truth, ontic indeterminacy, and the view that vague predicates are defective or meaningless. If your questions cannot separate two of these, flag that rather than pretending they do. I also want the tree to mark any combinations of answers that are logically available but apparently unoccupied, with a sentence on whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or neglect. To keep it from being just the standard heap-and-bald-man exercise, please build in at least one case from institutional life -- a rule requiring a thesis to be substantially complete before a defense, or a library policy barring large bags -- and say what that case reveals that the standard cases do not. Finally, because the group will spend most of its time on epistemicism and supervaluationism, make sure the tree shows exactly where those two views part company: which single answer, if any, forces a student from one to the other, and whether there are plausible hybrid positions the tree cannot separate.
