I run a ninety-minute seminar for graduate students in law and philosophy who have no formal logic, and next month's topic is self-amending rules: systems whose rules govern how the rules themselves may be changed, the thing the game Nomic turns into play. I want the session built around a miniature legal order I've drafted rather than a real constitution, so that the students can hold the whole thing in their heads. Mine has four articles: any member may propose an amendment; an amendment passes by a simple majority of those present; this Article may be amended only by the unanimous consent of all members; Article 3 may be amended by the procedure set out in Article 2. Plan the teaching rather than explaining the topic to me. What has to come first for the pull between Articles 3 and 4 to arrive as a discovery rather than a trick? I take it the students must first see the difference between a rule that tells you what to do and a rule that licenses changing a rule, and that a rule's authority is conferred by other rules rather than built into it, but tell me if that order is wrong. Say whether my four articles are the right carrying example, what it must contain and what it must leave out (I am tempted to add a fifth article voiding any amendment that would render the set inconsistent; say whether that sharpens the lesson or muddies it), which misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to head each one off, I predict 'the text settles it, someone just has to read it carefully' and 'this is only the liar paradox in legal dress,' and the closing question that would show me they have grasped what makes a rule about rule-change different from a rule. Tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level.
