OK so something's been bugging me about the mech interp scene, which I follow pretty closely. The standard move goes like this: you train a linear probe on some layer, or find a direction, and if the probe decodes "this is a sentence about a country's capital" at 94%, or if adding a Golden Gate Bridge direction makes the model shoehorn the bridge into every answer, everyone says the model *represents* that concept, has a "feature" for it, and that the sparse autoencoders (the things people use to chop activations into features) are getting closer to the model's actual ontology — what it "really thinks in". Then the same people, when pressed, go all deflationary: obviously it's just matrices, there's no little homunculus in there, "representation" is a useful fiction, features aren't real, we're doing engineering not psychology. Meanwhile someone in the replies always points out you can train a probe on random labels and still get high accuracy, which sure seems like it should matter, and that you can rotate the residual stream without changing the function at all, so which basis is "the" features?

So what does the practice actually presuppose about representation? Which views does it quietly rule out? Is there a coherent realism about features that survives the arbitrariness point and the control-task point, or is the day-to-day work really committed to something deflationary that nobody wants to say out loud? Don't give me a survey — take a side. Tell me what the workflow strictly needs vs. what it merely fits nicely with, say where it's underdetermined, and what would brake if you swapped the presupposed view out. Give me your confidence on your own verdict too.
