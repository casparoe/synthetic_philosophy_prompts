Please explain the conceptual problem that arises for theories locating an agent's normative reasons in the outcome of idealized deliberation — the family of views, sometimes gathered under the heading of indirect normativity, on which what you have reason to do is fixed by what a suitably idealized counterpart of you would endorse. I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and my suspicion is that an analogy borrowed from my own field is doing more work in these theories than is usually acknowledged, so I would like the explanation organized around it.

In the physical sciences, idealization takes at least two quite different forms. Sometimes one takes a limit: a parameter is driven toward a value it never attains — the number of particles to infinity, a friction coefficient to zero — and one treats the limiting case as the real object, with the finite cases understood as approximations to it. Sometimes one instead corrects an instrument: one knows that a measurement is distorted in a specific, independently characterizable way, and one subtracts the distortion, expecting the corrected value to be what the quantity was all along. Both procedures are called idealization, but they license different inferences, and only the second presupposes a standard fixed independently of the procedure itself.

My question is which of these the idealized deliberator is supposed to be. The deliberator is stipulated to be fully informed, fully coherent, unlimited in time, and free of bias and of distorting passion; the actual agent is then told that her real reasons are whatever that counterpart would endorse. Read this as a limit, and the target of the theory may be an object that no finite agent ever occupies — as with the thermodynamic limit, the ideal point can fail to be realized by any actual case while still being the thing the finite cases approximate. Read it as error-correction, and the account seems to require a principled distinction between those of the actual agent's attitudes that are distortion and those that are data; but any such distinction looks like an independent normative standard, in which case the idealization is not generating the normativity it promised to generate.

Please explain, in terms a newcomer can follow without the technical issue being flattened: (a) what the idealized-deliberation approach is trying to secure, and why the normativity is called indirect rather than direct; (b) whether the limit reading and the error-correction reading are genuinely distinct, or whether one collapses into the other under pressure; (c) the specific difficulty of specifying what the idealization is licensed to correct for, with attention to cases in which the attitude that would be idealized away — a particular attachment, a partiality toward one's own, a revulsion — is a serious candidate for being a genuine source of reasons rather than noise; and (d) whether the idealization is meant as an analysis of what our normative concepts already mean or as a substantive proposal about what they ought to mean, and what difference that makes to whether an actual agent has any reason to defer to her idealized counterpart.

I would also like the regress stated plainly: if the idealized deliberator is idealized about her own deliberation — if her idealization conditions are themselves open to deliberative revision — what prevents the account from either never reaching a fixed point or reaching one only by holding some feature of the actual agent fixed by stipulation? And is there a version of the view on which the idealization is not a target to be approached at all, but merely a device for making explicit commitments the actual agent already has? If so, does that remain an account of normativity, or has the deliberative apparatus become decorative? I am after the structure of the problem rather than a survey of who has said what.
