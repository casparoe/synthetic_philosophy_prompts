Last night my flatmate (an economist, which is relevant) and I spent two and a half hours arguing about David Lewis on convention — the gist being that a convention is a regularity everyone follows because they expect everyone else to follow it, and everyone knows the others expect it, so it keeps rolling along without anyone ever having agreed to anything — and somewhere in hour two I agreed to run next Thursday's session at my reading group. Mostly economists and two lawyers, no philosophers, ninety minutes, whiteboard, and they've all read the chapter so I can't just talk for an hour.

Don't explain convention to me. Plan the explanation.

- The order the pieces have to go in, and why each depends on the last. My instinct is coordination problem, then regularity, then expectation, then common knowledge, then the arbitrariness-yet-stability bit — tell me where that's wrong and what I'd be sneaking in too early.

- The one example that carries the whole thing. Can it be the one from the argument? Our lot have drifted over about two years from always sitting at the front of the cafe to always heading straight for the back room. Nobody decided it, the guy who started it moved away a year ago, and new people pick it up within a week without being told. I'd love to use it, but I'm worried it's secretly two examples, or that it's a habit rather than a convention and I won't be able to tell in front of a room. If it can't carry the session, say what the carrying example needs and why the obvious ones (which side of the road, the two people trying to find each other in a city) are the wrong choice here.

- The misconceptions in the order they'll actually surface, and exactly where in my sequence to defuse each. I'm expecting: that a convention is an agreement or a deal; that if it's arbitrary we could all just decide to switch tomorrow; that it's the same thing as a custom or a habit; and that arbitrariness means it isn't really binding. What am I missing? And where do I head off the economists' instinct that this is nothing but equilibrium selection, and the lawyers' that with no agreement there's no obligation?

- The ending. I want to land on the norm that outlives the interest that's supposed to be holding it up — everyone follows it, everyone expects everyone to, nobody would pick it from scratch, and people keep to it even when it costs them. I can never remember which preference condition Lewis actually needs, the one about preferring to conform given that others do or the stronger one about wanting everyone to conform, so sort that out too. And work in the drift: which parts of the apparatus survive a convention changing under people's feet without anyone noticing.

- One question I can put to the room at the end that tells me they've got it, rather than just nodding along to a definition.

- And what I should announce up front that I'm not going to settle in ninety minutes. I'm thinking the formalism of common knowledge and anything to do with language, but you tell me.
