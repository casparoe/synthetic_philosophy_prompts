I chair the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI developer. Our charter requires the board to apply what it calls the competitiveness constraint: no safety requirement may be imposed on a release unless it would remain defensible in a world where competitors are free to do less. Over three meetings this has hardened into a conversation-stopper — whoever invokes the constraint wins — and we have approved two releases the board was uneasy about.

The case before us concerns our next frontier model. Its training pipeline includes a harm-refusal intervention that measurably degrades the model on a suite of agentic planning and long-horizon coding evaluations (roughly 8 to 12 percent on our internal composite) and adds about six weeks to the release schedule. Two competitors have already shipped comparable-scale models without any equivalent intervention. Our commercial team argues that shipping without the intervention is the only defensible choice under the charter, and that our restraint simply moves users, and revenue, to models that are no safer. What the board has taken to calling the alignment tax is, on their reading, a tax we would be paying unilaterally for nothing.

Two colleagues have asked the board to settle whether the charter's test, as written, is even coherent. One proposes a release-parity rule: we match the safety posture of the least cautious plausible competitor, on the reasoning that anything stricter is pure self-harm. The other proposes a marginal-risk test: a restraint is warranted only if we can name the mechanism by which our adopting it lowers total risk in the world rather than only our own share of it, and only if we can specify what would have to change for that mechanism to fail.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this release — ship as is, ship with the intervention, or ship on a stated trigger condition — with a time frame, and it should say plainly which of the two proposed tests the board should adopt, if either, and why. Treat the competitiveness constraint as the serious objection it is rather than dismissing it; if you conclude it defeats the intervention, say so. The commercial team and the two dissenters will all read this document, so it should give each of them something they can actually respond to in the meeting.
