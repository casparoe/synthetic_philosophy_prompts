For about a year now a reading group I sit in has been going over the Lucas–Penrose argument, and it has the texture of a dispute that cannot move. The anti-mechanist case is put like this: take any consistent formal system a mechanist might offer as a model of a mathematician's arithmetical reasoning; it has a Gödel sentence it cannot prove; the mathematician, reflecting on the system, can see that the sentence is true; so the mathematician is not that system, and since the manoeuvre can be repeated for any system on offer, no system models the mathematician. The mechanist replies with what look like the same three moves every time: that seeing the truth of the Gödel sentence presupposes the soundness — or at minimum the consistency — of the system reflected on, which in the mathematician's own case is exactly the point in dispute; that if the mathematician is a machine then there is a Gödel sentence for her too, and her failure to prove it contradicts nothing; and that the argument shows at most that no single formal system captures the whole of mathematical reasoning, which is compatible with a machine that is a succession of systems, each extending the last. Lucas's 1961 paper and Penrose's two books of 1989 and 1994 are on the table and are not the difficulty; everyone has read them. What no one can say is what the disagreement is about.

I want a diagnosis rather than another round of the same exchanges. Is the core of this a formal matter on which one side is simply wrong about what the incompleteness theorems do and do not yield? Is it an idealization dispute, where the mechanist means what a finite physical system could be brought to output in principle and the anti-mechanist means what a thinker can be entitled to, so that the two are answering different questions under one word, 'proves'? Is it a claim about quantifier order — the observation that there is a true arithmetical sentence no machine can prove is nearly vacuous, since every true sentence is provable by some machine, and whatever content the anti-mechanist has must live in the claim that a single thinker can do this for every sound system she can recognize? Or is the impasse a sign that the anti-mechanist thesis is not the sort of thing that could be confirmed or refuted from inside any system, including ours, and that this is a defect on one reading and the whole point on another? My own hypothesis, offered to be knocked down, is the quantifier-order one, combined with the thought that the mechanist's succession-of-systems reply is doing far more work than either side admits. Test it against what each side actually says rather than against the textbook summaries.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, an empirical finding — or argue that none should be expected, and say which. State how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
