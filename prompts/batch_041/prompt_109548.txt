I'm a policy analyst on an interagency working group revising our central guidance for regulatory cost-benefit analysis, and I keep getting stuck on what the guidance we've been using actually commits us to. It tells analysts to discount future costs and benefits using a declining schedule: 3 percent for the first 30 years, 2 percent for years 31 through 75, 1 percent for years 76 through 200, and 0 percent beyond that. It also carves out two exceptions: irreversible or catastrophic outcomes may be discounted at 0 percent regardless of when they occur, and purely hedonic goods -- things like recreational enjoyment, as opposed to health or income -- are to be discounted at a flat 4 percent. Meanwhile, the value assigned to a statistical life is projected to grow with per-capita income, so a life saved in 2100 is worth far more in today's dollars than a life saved in 2030. The preamble insists that the discount schedule is a positive description of how people actually trade off consumption over time, not a moral judgment about how much future people's welfare counts.

What philosophical commitments does this package presuppose? I want you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: does it commit the agency to a positive pure time preference, or can the whole schedule be reconstructed as diminishing marginal utility plus uncertainty about future growth plus the opportunity cost of capital? Does the 0 percent catastrophic exception presuppose that some future losses are lexically prior or incommensurable with ordinary consumption, or is it just an insurance premium? Is the income-growing value of life consistent with the rest of the schedule, or does it smuggle in a different view about what makes future welfare comparable? Does the practice presuppose that future people's welfare is commensurable with ours in a single currency, and which rival theories -- zero pure time preference, a prioritarian weighting of future people, a rights-based refusal to aggregate across generations, or something else -- would license the same practice, and which would require the guidance to change? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, and without pointing me to sources, since I'm working offline and need the reconstruction itself.
