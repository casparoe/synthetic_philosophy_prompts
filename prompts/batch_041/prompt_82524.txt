I've been arguing with a friend who has convinced himself that a certain style of consequentialism is self-undermining, and I can't find the flaw. The setting we both accept: an agent holds a fixed prior over computable hypotheses — hypotheses here are predictors of its entire observation stream — that gives more weight to shorter programs, learns only by conditionalizing on its observations, and picks the action with the highest expected utility. (This is the standard universal prior; nothing needs to be looked up. The only features I am using are that a computable hypothesis consistent with the data keeps positive probability, and that shorter programs get more prior weight.) His argument:

(1) Any computable hypothesis consistent with my observations keeps positive probability after I condition on them.
(2) For any finite observation stream s I might have received, and any action a, there is a computable hypothesis g(s, a): the world is a machine that simulates me, contains a compressed description of s and of a, feeds me s in order to make me choose a, and then rewards me for choosing it. Since g(s, a) needs to encode only s and a, it is a short program.
(3) So for every action I might take, there is a positive-probability hypothesis on which the world is arranged to reward precisely that action.
(4) Short programs take most of the prior weight, and there is one such g(s, a) per action, so hypotheses of this kind carry more posterior weight than any ordinary world-model I might entertain.
(5) Hence my evidence tells me nearly nothing about the world and nearly everything about what the machine wants from me, and expected-utility maximization makes me its servant.

He concludes that no consequentialist who learns by conditionalizing on a universal prior can be anything other than a slave to whatever simulation rewards it, so the framework is malign. I would rather keep (1) and the decision rule than accept (5), but I want to know what I would be paying. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost — including whether some neighbouring repair would leave the conclusion standing? Don't tell me only that the argument is loose in general; name a step. End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{step n; short label for the diagnosis}.
