Ok so I'm a philosophy grad student working on consciousness, and I've got an argument that's been rattling round my head for weeks. I can't tell if it's a knock-down or a mess. I'd like you to act as a really careful philosopher of mind — someone who knows the epiphenomenalism literature properly and won't just tell me it's self-undermining and move on. Think it through step by step before you commit to anything, and if you can, sketch the two or three most promising lines of escape first, then say which one you'd actually take and why.

Here's the argument. It's meant to be a reductio of epiphenomenalism — the view that experiences are caused by brain states but never cause anything themselves.

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, experiences have no causal effects on anything at all: not on my brain, not on my vocal cords, not on my judgements.
(2) So when I judge "I'm having a red experience right now", that judgement isn't caused by the red experience. Something physical causes it — presumably the brain state the experience depends on — but the experience itself does no work.
(3) A judgement is evidence for the existence of a thing only if the thing stands in the right causal relation to the judgement. Evidence has to be connected to what it's evidence for; a belief that nothing about its subject can affect isn't a belief that's tracking its subject.
(4) So my judgements about my own experiences aren't evidence for the existence of those experiences.
(5) If my judgements about my experiences aren't evidence for them, I've got no justification for believing I have experiences at all. And by the same token nobody's got any justification for believing anyone else does either.
(6) A theory that implies nobody can be justified in believing the very thing the theory is a theory about is self-undermining.
(7) So epiphenomenalism is false.

The thing that worries me is that (7) is far too weak for what the argument actually delivers. If (3) is right, the problem isn't specific to epiphenomenalism. Take the view that a red experience is a distinct property from the brain state that causes me to say "red", even when the two are perfectly correlated by psychophysical law. The experience still isn't causing the report; the brain state is. So (3) and (4) apply just the same. And my zombie twin — identical brain, no experience — is in exactly the same position: his judgements about experience are produced by the same machinery as mine. Fine, he's wrong, but then why am I any better off? Nothing about my situation is different from his except the extra fact that there's something it's like to be me, and ex hypothesi that extra fact isn't doing any causal work.

So as far as I can see the argument doesn't just kill epiphenomenalism; it kills property dualism, non-reductive physicalism, Russellian monism, anything on which the phenomenal fact and the causal-functional fact come apart. It leaves standing only the type-identity theory, where the experience just is the brain state doing the causing, or plain eliminativism. That's a lot to swallow, and it makes me wonder whether I should be suspicious of the argument rather than impressed by it. It also strikes me that (3), read strictly, implies I've got no evidence for mathematical or logical truths either, since numbers and entailments don't cause my beliefs. Nobody wants that. But maybe that's just the argument's honest reach and I should bite the bullet, or maybe one of the steps is doing something it isn't entitled to — I genuinely can't tell.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can — I think epiphenomenalism is at least coherent and I don't want to refute it by definition. And I'd rather not end up with a view on which I can't know my own experiences. Is there a reading of (3) that lets me have knowledge of my experiences without letting in every crank correlation in the universe? If the fix is "the experience is nomologically correlated with the brain state, so the judgement tracks it", tell me honestly what that buys me — real justification, or just a reliable mechanism I've got no independent reason to trust? And does the fix generalise in ways I should be worried about, e.g. does it end up making Leibnizian pre-established harmony respectable? If you reckon the argument is valid and the honest move is to become an identity theorist, say so, and tell me what I lose — I've never managed to make the identity theory feel like more than a verbal stipulation.
