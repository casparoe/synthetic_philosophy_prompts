I spent thirty-five years as a field entomologist, most of it on the anatomy and behavior of desert insects, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology on my own. I have run into a disagreement among colleagues that I suspect is philosophical in nature, and I would rather have help thinking it through than be handed a definition and sent on my way. Please take the role of a philosopher of biology who works on the concept of biological function, and reason with me carefully.

A former student of mine is preparing a species description of a beetle she has been studying. In this species the hind wings, which in its flying relatives are thin and beat in flight, are stiffened, folded flat over the abdomen, and permanently immobile; the beetle cannot fly. She has documented, both in the field and in the lab, that the beetle reliably uses these stiffened wings as a seal over its abdominal spiracles, and that individuals whose wings are experimentally held open lose water far faster and die sooner in dry air. Two colleagues are now arguing about how the description should characterize the wings. One says they are vestigial and should be called functionless, since they no longer do the job wings are for. The other says it is absurd to call them functionless when the beetle plainly depends on them to survive.

My own hunch is that the two are using the word function in different senses, one having to do with why the structure is there at all and the other with what it currently contributes, but I cannot tell whether this is a mere quarrel over a word or a real disagreement about the biology. Before you answer, please brainstorm the several distinct ways biologists and philosophers have understood what it is for a trait to have a function, then use them to diagnose this dispute. Tell me whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal, and whether one of the senses should be treated as the genuinely biological one. Then consider two variations. First, suppose the spiracle-sealing role is a lucky side effect that has never been favored by selection. Second, suppose it has been favored by selection only in the last few thousand years. Does your verdict change? Finally, tell my student what she should actually write in the species description, and warn me where a careless phrasing would commit her to a claim she cannot defend.
