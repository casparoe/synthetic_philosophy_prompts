I would like an audit of an analogy that is doing real work in a dispute I keep having, and I want it explicit enough that I can use it as a procedure rather than as a talking point.

The analogy is this: a degree of belief is a price. To have credence p in a proposition is, on this picture, to hold fair betting odds of p — to be willing to buy or sell a bet that pays a fixed sum if the proposition is true, on terms that make the bet's expected value zero. The rest of the traditional argument for probabilism then falls out: an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms holds prices that are internally misaligned, and a bookmaker who knows them can assemble a package of bets that leaves the agent poorer whatever happens — a Dutch book. Coherence stands to credence as no-arbitrage stands to a system of prices.

What the analogy is being used to establish in my context: a friend treats it as the whole story about why our credences must obey the probability calculus, and concludes that the more recent programme which defends those same axioms by appeal to accuracy — credences scored for closeness to the truth, with incoherent ones dominated because they are further from the truth in every possible world — must be either the betting argument restated in fancier dress or an unacknowledged borrowing from it. I suspect that is wrong, but I cannot say precisely where the mistake lies.

What I want from you:

- the correspondence table, made fully explicit: what in the target (credence, coherence, epistemic rationality, the badness of incoherence) is played by what in the source (price, arbitrage, profit and loss, the bookmaker);
- where the mapping breaks, with the breaks sorted according to whether they matter for the conclusion the analogy is being asked to carry — in particular, whether the source's reliance on an adversary, a counterparty, and a stake changes the kind of badness at issue;
- what the analogy quietly imports that the target may not contain: an assumption about how degrees of belief are to be measured, about the agent's attitude to risk, about which propositions can be bet on at all. On the last point, say what the price picture implies for a credence in a proposition no one could collect on — an event that leaves no trace, or one whose truth would be altered by the act of betting — and whether an agent with no disposition to gamble, no counterparty and no money has no credences, which would look like a reductio;
- whether the conclusion that our credences should be probabilities survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and by which route, if any;
- and, if the betting picture is the wrong vehicle for the job, a better analogy, with its own failure modes flagged.

Treat the two justifications as genuinely distinct options, and tell me what, if anything, one of them can establish that the other cannot. Do not simply assert that they come to the same thing in substance; if they do, exhibit the translation, and if they do not, say what turns on the difference.
