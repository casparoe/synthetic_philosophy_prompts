I'm a software engineer with no training in philosophy, and I've been having the same argument with a friend for years about religion and the state, so I'd like my own view written down properly. What follows are gut verdicts on a set of arrangements, not arguments. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that generates them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking again, along with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save. Don't send me to the literature; a reading list is no use to me.

Take England as I understand it. The monarch is supreme governor of the established church, its bishops sit in the legislature by right, and a wedding in one of its churches is a legal marriage with no registrar in the room. It mostly funds itself from historic endowments and its members' giving, and nobody has to belong or attend. I think this is wrong, and I can't point to anyone it harms.

Then Germany: the tax office collects a surcharge on my income tax bill for whichever registered church I'm on the books of, and I leave by making a formal declaration to a civil authority, which has social costs in the community. I think this is fine. The state is acting as a billing agent for money that is mine and that I can stop paying whenever I like.

Denmark has both at once — the same sort of tax, collected by the state, for a church whose affairs are run by a government ministry — and my verdicts split: collection fine, ministry not, even though there they are a single arrangement.

A state that pays the salaries of clergy of all the recognized religions out of general revenue, in proportion to how many members each has, seems to me plainly wrong; treating everyone alike doesn't rescue it.

A state that funds religious schools on exactly the same terms as secular ones — standard curriculum, open admissions, a secular ethics option for anyone who wants it — seems to me fine. And even-handedness isn't doing the work there, because a state that funded only one denomination's schools, open to all and teaching the standard curriculum, wouldn't bother me much either.

France: schoolchildren barred from conspicuous religious symbols, wrong. Public employees barred from displaying religious affiliation while on duty, fine.

The American arrangement — a national motto invoking God on the currency, a prayer before the legislature, publicly funded chaplains for the armed forces and the prisons, churches exempt from property tax — is mostly fine by me. The chaplains especially, since the state is the one who locked those people up.

My own guess is that it all turns on whether the state is paying for religion or merely passing money through, but that can't be the whole story: the English case costs nobody anything and still bothers me, and the Danish one does both at once and I want to split it.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a state that lets religious bodies run their own arbitration panels for family disputes, binding on members who signed up in advance, with the ordinary courts enforcing the result the way they would enforce any other arbitration award. Half of me says that is just freedom of contract; the other half says a piece of the state's own authority has been handed to a body the state doesn't supervise.

So: what principle, if any, generates these verdicts, and which ones do I have to give up? For each clause of it, tell me which of my cases forces that clause. Then apply it to two cases I haven't raised — a state that lets religious bodies opt out of a generally applicable anti-discrimination law when hiring their own staff, and a state that keeps the church tax but divides the proceeds among all religions and secular humanist organizations by declared membership. Where the principle is silent, or gives an answer I'd hate, say so rather than patching it quietly.

Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a letter.
