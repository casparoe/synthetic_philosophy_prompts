My department's formal epistemology reading group has been circling the same dispute about the principle of indifference for two years, and I would like a diagnosis of why it refuses to resolve rather than another defence or rejection of the principle itself.

Let me state the positions as I understand them, so that the diagnosis has something to bite on. One camp treats credences as personal and the principle as bookkeeping: on this view Bertrand's chord problem — the three answers 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, each generated by a defensible way of picking a chord at random — shows only that the phrase "a random chord" is an incomplete description, not that any norm has been violated. A second camp thinks the principle can be repaired by tying indifference to invariance, so that a prior is licensed only when the problem supplies a symmetry group under which the question is invariant. A third holds that probability statements detached from a chance mechanism are a category error, so the paradox is a pseudo-problem generated by asking a question with no truth conditions. A fourth, which I find the most interesting, concedes that the prior is genuinely underdetermined and concludes that the right representation of our state is a set of credences rather than a single one.

My working hypothesis — held loosely — is that this is a partly verbal dispute: that "the principle of indifference" names at least three different things, a normative claim about equal evidence and equal credence, a modelling heuristic for constructing priors, and a claim about what makes a physical randomisation device fair, and that arguments aimed at one are routinely deflected by defences of another. I suspect the residue that survives the disambiguation is a real disagreement about whether rationality permits a range of priors or demands a unique one, and I want to know whether that residue is the whole story.

I would like you to work with two things of my own. First, a case: a point is chosen on a disc by a procedure that privileges no direction; that is all I am told. What is the probability that the point falls within distance r of the centre? Rotational symmetry fixes the angular distribution and says nothing about the radial one, so any radial density is compatible with what I know, and the answer becomes determinate the moment someone adds that the point is uniform over the disc — an assumption that indifference has not supplied and, it seems to me, cannot. Is the residual freedom a defect of the principle, or merely a feature of under-described problems? Second, a distinction I am unsure how to apply: whether this freedom is better read as gauge freedom, where different parameterisations describe one situation and there is no further fact to be had, or as genuine indeterminacy, where there is a fact but the principle cannot reach it. I would also like to know whether the move to sets of credences resolves the dispute or merely relabels it.

Deliver the diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, a verbal one, two camps answering different questions under one heading, or evidence that the question is ill-posed? Support it with what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why.

Please describe positions rather than pointing me at a literature; I will add references myself, and citations would only be guesses. Finally, be explicit about the standing of your diagnosis: say which argument or consideration would lead you to a different one, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
