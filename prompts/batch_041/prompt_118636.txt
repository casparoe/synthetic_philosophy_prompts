I'm putting together a colloquium Q&A — roughly forty-five minutes, mixed graduate and faculty audience — with a philosopher who has spent several years defending accuracy-first epistemology: the view that the fundamental epistemic good is the accuracy of a credence function, that inaccuracy is measured by a scoring rule, and that the norms of credence (probabilism, conditionalization, deference to chance) are to be derived from dominance or expected-accuracy considerations rather than from pragmatic or Dutch-book reasoning. She is fluent in the machinery and will happily restate the framework, so I need questions that press it at its joints. Please draft eight to ten of them, each with a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

I need at least one question on each of the following, and I'd like them interleaved rather than run through in a block:

- The accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism and what it actually assumes: the additivity of the inaccuracy measure, the fixing of a finite partition of mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive possibilities, and what becomes of the argument when the algebra is infinite or when the agent's credences are not defined over the atoms of the space.
- The expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization, and the suspicion that it evaluates future credences by an expectation computed from the agent's present credences — so that the argument either presupposes a decision-theoretic principle she owes us an argument for, or simply assumes the coherence it means to justify.
- Deference to chance: whether an accuracy-based argument can establish a genuine chance–credence norm, and what happens to that argument if the chance function at the world is itself something that could be more or less accurate.
- The objection that accuracy is not the only epistemic good — that understanding, explanation, or knowledge do work the framework cannot capture — together with the instrumentalist worry that if an agent simply does not care about accuracy, nothing normative follows at all.
- The problem of the priors: whether accuracy-first is confined to coherence constraints and therefore silent on what to believe in the first place, and how that bears on permissivism versus uniqueness.
- Whether the apparatus extends to imprecise credences, and whether anything can play the role that strict propriety plays for precise ones.
- The fact that strict propriety makes a credence function uniquely expect itself to be most accurate, and whether that rules out the kind of rational self-doubt we seem to have in the presence of acknowledged experts.

For each follow-up, anticipate the standard escape — a retreat to a weaker decision rule, a distinction between the accuracy of a single credence and of the whole function, an appeal to a diachronic rather than synchronic standpoint, a shift from dominance to expected accuracy — and make the follow-up the question that makes that escape costly.

Keep the questions about the position rather than the personnel: they should stand on their own as philosophical challenges, not as invitations to adjudicate who said what where, and they should not require either of us to produce references.

Finally, as you draft, mark clearly where my framing presupposes something that is genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than letting a disputed premise stand as the settled background; where her likely answer turns on which of two live positions she holds, give me both branches of the follow-up instead of assuming one; and close the set with a question about what could count as evidence against accuracy-first epistemology.
