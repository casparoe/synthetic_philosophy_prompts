I chair the standing advisory panel at a mid-sized environmental regulator — nine members, mostly academics, we meet monthly and we have to give a straight yes/no on things like whether a particular level of pesticide run-off is acceptable, whether a landfill extension can go ahead, that sort of thing. It's a real vote, it goes in the record, and last year two of our decisions got judicially reviewed, so I've started to worry less about the science and more about the arithmetic of how we decide. My vice-chair keeps citing the Condorcet jury theorem at me: he says the more of us there are, and the more often we vote, the more likely we are to be right, so we should expand the panel to fifteen, and whenever a vote is close we should just argue for another hour and vote again. I'm not conviced, but I can't articulate why. What I keep noticing is that we all read the same dossier prepared by the same secretariat, that two members used to work for the same consultancy that advises the applicants, that I open every meeting by summarising what I think the answer is before anyone else speaks, and that on one recurring class of questions — the groundwater ones — the panel has turned out, on the regulator's own later assessment, to have been wrong more often than not. I've got maybe twenty minutes before each meeting to run whatever you give me, and the minutes have to be defensible in writing to the board.

Please write me a procedure I can actually run as chair: the questions to ask in order before I let a vote happen, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no — we don't vote on this at all — and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about what we're willing to risk rather than about the facts. Then run it on three live things: the proposal to expand the panel to fifteen for the next contested case; a proposal to re-vote on something that came out 5–4 against after another hour of discussion; and the groundwater cases, where I suspect the honest answer is that majority voting is the wrong instrument altogether. I'd rather you told me that if it's true than fudged it — if the procedure says something I won't like about my own panel, say so and tell me what to do instead. Be explicit about which steps are genuine inferences from the theorem and which are just my judgment dressed up as method, and please don't oversell what the theorem establishes; I keep seeing it waved around as if it proved that groups are wiser than individuals, and I don't think that's what it says. Under 900 words, in British English, and lead with the procedure itself before the worked cases.
