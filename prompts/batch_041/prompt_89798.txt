I sit on the governance committee of a mid-sized mutual — a member-owned savings and lending institution — and I have no academic training in philosophy or political theory. Over the past year, though, I have been reading on my own about the idea that votes ought to carry different weights depending on the voter's competence, which I gather is sometimes called epistocracy, and I have reached the point where I have to take a position in writing.

Our committee must decide whether to recommend a change to our rules of governance, and I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting. Please act as an expert in democratic theory and institutional design, and think carefully before you write anything. Begin by working out for yourself, in your own words, the strongest version of the proposal and the strongest version of the case against it; then produce the memo itself. I would rather have a well-reasoned recommendation I can defend in the room than a survey of the literature, and I do not need a reading list or citations — a model without access to a library should be able to answer this from reasoning alone.

The proposal before us, in brief. Every member would keep one vote. Any member who passes a short written examination on the institution's finances would receive two additional votes, but only on resolutions that our rules designate as 'actuarial' — questions of reserve ratios, capital adequacy, lending limits and the like — and not on resolutions the rules call 'constitutive', such as changing the membership rules or electing the board. The total weight created by these additional votes is capped, so that the examined bloc can never outvote the unexamined membership on any resolution. The examination would be written and marked by the board itself, which also sets the reserve policy the examination is about; it can be retaken without limit, and a short study guide would be published. There is also a sunset clause: the scheme lapses after six years unless the membership votes to renew it.

The immediate case that has forced the issue is a resolution to merge with a much larger bank. The board has classified the merger as actuarial, on the grounds that the decisive question is whether our capital position can survive alone. Several members who intend to vote against the merger argue that it is really constitutive, because a merger would change what kind of institution we are and would dissolve the membership as we know it, and that the classification has been arranged so that the examined bloc decides it. I am genuinely unsure whether they are right, and I do not want the memo to assume an answer.

Your memo should: recommend a concrete course of action for the committee — adopt the scheme, reject it, or adopt it with specific amendments — and say what we should do at this meeting and by when; explain the reasoning in a way that a member with no background in this area could follow; give an honest and serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you come out against, not a straw man; and say explicitly what the proposal gets right about the worry that motivates it. Address directly the two features I find most troubling — that the examiners set the exam on the policy they also decide, and that competence at questions about our own accounts may be little more than familiarity with the institution, which selects for long-standing and better-off members — and say whether either is fatal, and why.

Format it as a formal committee memo, roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words, with a clear recommendation stated at the top as well as in the body. Write in British English throughout, in spelling and idiom. At the end, state how confident you are in the recommendation and what new information would change it.
