For the last six months I have been enrolled in a wellness programme at work that requires a daily gratitude journal and, on especially bad days, a thank-you letter to someone who has treated me badly. I can perform both, and I can even work myself into something like feeling while I do it, but the feeling is never the gratitude I have when someone has genuinely done me a kindness, and I have come to think the reason is structural rather than psychological: the programme supplies a reason to produce the gratitude, not a reason that makes the gratitude fitting. A colleague with a philosophy degree tells me I have stumbled onto what the literature calls the wrong kind of reasons problem. I now want to teach that problem to the discussion group I run — eight or nine adults, curious, with no philosophical training — in a single ninety-minute session, with a whiteboard and nothing else. Please do not explain the problem to me; plan how I should explain it.

Begin with the order of ideas. My own hunch is that nothing can land until the group first accepts that an attitude such as gratitude or admiration can be correct or incorrect in its own right, independently of whether it is useful or obligatory, and that the wrong-kind-of-reasons question only becomes visible once that notion of fit is in place. Tell me whether the hunch is right, and then give me the full sequence, with the dependencies marked, running through to the point at which the problem itself can be stated.

Next, the carrying example. I want the session built on the gratitude-journal scenario above rather than on the demon, the mafia, or the threatening billionaire that keep appearing in the summaries I have read, and I need to know what that example would have to possess and what it would have to lack in order for the contrast between a reason for an attitude and a reason merely to bring one about to do its work — in particular, whether it can carry the whole session alone or needs a companion case, and if so, what the companion must supply that it cannot.

Then the misconceptions. I expect the group to conclude that the issue is about insincerity or hypocrisy; that a reason of the wrong kind is not really a reason at all; that "wrong kind" is a term of moral reproach; that the problem is peculiar to belief and does not touch the emotions; and that once the distinction has been drawn, the criterion for drawing it is obvious. For each of these, tell me where in the sequence it will surface and precisely how to head it off at that point, and add any further misconception you would expect from this particular audience.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes — I take the fitting-attitude analysis of value and the debate over the ethics of belief to be beyond this audience — and give me one question to put to the group at the close that would reveal whether they have grasped the problem or merely acquired its vocabulary. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations.

End your answer with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence the one idea the whole session has to land.
