So what I want from you: take the main positions in that debate and restate each one as faithfully as you can in the language I actually think in. That means the ex post facto objection, the victors'-justice charge, the tu quoque reply, the standard defenses (that aggression was already unlawful under customary law or under the interwar pact renouncing war as an instrument of policy, that the alternative was summary execution, that somebody had to make a record), and the extra twist at Tokyo, where at least one judge went further than anyone at Nuremberg and denied the tribunal had jurisdiction at all. Put all of that in terms of commitment, time consistency, ex ante versus ex post optimality, incentive compatibility, renegotiation-proofness, who holds the pen when the rules get written, and who has standing to enforce. Then the part I care about most: the residue report. Which of his claims come across cleanly, which only come across if I fudge something, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? For each piece of residue, give me a verdict: is my framework missing something real, or was the original claim doing less work than it sounded like? One thing I'd like you to keep separate while you're at it — whether the defendants got a fair trial by the standards of their time, versus whether the law they were tried under existed before they acted.
