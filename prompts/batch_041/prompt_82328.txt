For most of the past year a friend and I have been re-litigating the anthropics debates over email, and I've come to suspect that the way both sides frame the disagreement is doing more of the work than either side admits. As I understand the two principles: the self-sampling assumption says that, all else equal, you should reason as though you had been drawn at random from among the observers who actually exist in your reference class; the self-indication assumption says that, all else equal, you should reason as though you had been drawn at random from all the observers who could possibly exist, weighted by how likely each is to exist, which is why on that assumption the bare fact that you exist is itself evidence that there are many observers rather than few. The standard illustrations are by now almost liturgical. The presumptuous philosopher is told that two rival theories remain, one on which the universe holds a modest number of observers and one on which it holds an astronomical number, and the self-indication assumption tells him he can settle the question from the armchair, near-certain of the astronomical version, which everyone agrees is absurd. The doomsday argument runs the other way: the self-sampling assumption, applied to the fact that your birth rank among all humans ever born is comparatively low, makes an early end to the species likely. If I have mangled either principle, correct my formulation before you use it.

What I want from you is an example of a case that runs the presumptuous-philosopher complaint in reverse, one in which it is the self-sampling assumption, not the self-indication assumption, that manufactures the absurd degree of confidence. Two constraints. First, it has to be a case that a single person could actually be in and could have to act on, with the two assumptions recommending different actions and not merely different credences; I am not interested in the fate of the species or the size of the universe. I am not under the illusion that the disagreement can show up anywhere other than in a difference in how many observers the rival hypotheses posit, so if that is wrong, say so, but I do want the case packaged as something a person could face rather than as a question about cosmology or extinction. Second, I want to see the arithmetic, since the whole point is to watch a sampling rule do the damage, and I want the conclusion the self-sampling assumption reaches to be one its own defenders would be uncomfortable defending out loud.

My own attempt, which I do not trust: a fair coin is tossed; on heads, one copy of me is created, in a single room with a red light; on tails, a thousand copies of me are created, one per room, and the experimenter has arranged that exactly one of the thousand rooms is lit red while the other nine hundred and ninety-nine are lit blue. I wake up in my room, see that the light is red, and am offered a bet: a dollar to me if I am the lone copy, a dollar ten from me if I am not. Work out what each assumption says about my credence and about whether I should take the bet; tell me honestly whether the case does the work I want or collapses into something I have misdescribed; and then give me the example you would actually put in print, the cleanest case you know of in which self-sampling rather than self-indication is the presumptuous one. Finally, if the two objections really are mirror images, say what that does to the force of the presumptuous-philosopher argument. As far as I can tell, each principle multiplies in the same observer-count factor and merely places it differently, one in the prior and one in the likelihood; I want to know whether that makes the argument a wash, or whether something breaks the symmetry.
