For an essay on the theory of self-determination, I want you to treat the Quebec referendum of 1995 and its constitutional aftermath as a case study, and I want the mapping to run in both directions: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory.

Set up two rival accounts. The remedial view holds that a group acquires a right to secede only by suffering a serious injustice — annexation, systematic oppression, the breach of a prior agreement about its autonomy — and that wanting a state of one's own is not enough. The plebiscitary view holds that a people's decision to govern itself is itself the ground of the right, and that no showing of grievance is required. Then tell me what each account must say about Quebec: what the 1995 campaign and its near-miss look like through each lens; whether the complaints that carried the movement — exclusion from the 1982 constitutional settlement, the collapse of the Meech Lake accord — count as the kind of wrong the remedial view recognizes or as political defeats rather than injustices; and what it costs the remedial view that a durable secessionist movement in a stable liberal democracy argued for a distinct people's right to decide rather than for a remedy. Bring in the Supreme Court's secession reference and the Clarity Act that followed, and say whether the negotiated-secession position they stake out is a third theory or an evasion of the choice.

Two things I especially want you to press. First, the boundary problem: any democratic decision presupposes a demos, and the demos cannot determine its own composition without circularity. Who had standing to fix the franchise for a vote on Quebec's future, and does the answer change if the electorate is Quebec residents, all Canadians, or the Quebecois nation defined by language or descent? Second, assess a device I keep coming back to: a standing secession clause, negotiated and entrenched in advance — a supermajority threshold, a waiting period, a defined duty to negotiate — so that the question of whether a people may leave is answered before anyone wants to leave. Would such a clause have resolved Quebec, or does it merely relocate the boundary problem into its own terms?
