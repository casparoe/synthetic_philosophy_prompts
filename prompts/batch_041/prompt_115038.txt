I write software, I make decisions by expected value all day, and I've been reading about risk-weighted expected utility, which claims the expected-value rule smuggles in an assumption I'd never noticed: that my attitude to risk is already fixed by my probabilities and my utilities. On this view it isn't. The weight an outcome's value carries depends on where the outcome sits in the ranking of the lottery, not on its probability alone, so two agents with the same probabilities and the same utilities can rationally choose differently, and a risk-averse agent can rationally turn down a gamble with positive expected value.

I want that framework run over the Challenger launch decision in January 1986: the night-before teleconference where the engineers argued against launching in the cold and the management chain reversed them. Hold the probabilities fixed and identical for both sides, tell me what plain expected utility says each side should have done, what risk-weighted expected utility says, and exactly where the two accounts come apart. Then the half I actually care about: where does the framework fail on this case? My suspicion is that it needs a single agent with a stable risk attitude when the decision was really made by a chain of people with different ones, and that it needs well-defined probabilities where the engineers' own objection was that there were no data at the relevant temperature. And if risk attitude is a free parameter, can the theory still call the launch a mistake rather than merely an unusual taste? Tell me which of those it survives, which it doesn't, and what the episode shows about the theory either way.
