I teach a decision theory seminar and I keep having this same fight in office hours, so lemme just write it down. Daniel Bernoulli — or one of the Bernoullis, I'm not policing which — supposedly fixed the St. Petersburg paradox by saying the fair price of a gamble isn't expected money, it's expected utility, log of your wealth, so that doubling your money is worth the same increment whether you're rich or broke. Now put him in front of this game. A fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads; if that happens on flip n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n dollars when n is even. Expected value is the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which "equals" ln 2 only in the wishy-washy sense: the series is conditionally convergent, so you can rearrange the terms and get any total you like, negative ones and infinities included. Nobody has ever actualy played it; two philosophers cooked it up in 2004.

Predict Bernoulli's views on this. What price would he put on it, and what's the argument? Does the losing half of the payoff schedule cause him any trouble, given what log utility does with losses? What would he say the question itself is getting wrong? And where does his own machinery — moral expectation and all that — just break? Don't bother with citations, I want the reasoning.
