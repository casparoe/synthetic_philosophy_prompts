Here's the thing that's been bugging me. I cry at films. Properly cry, not just a bit misty. The film I'd like you to read through this is Stranger Than Fiction (the 2006 one with Will Ferrell). An IRS man starts hearing a woman narrating his life, works out he's a character in a novel that isn't finished yet, and tracks the author down. The literature professor he goes to for advice reads her outline and says it's her masterpiece and that Harold's death is the whole point of it, that it's a beautiful death. Harold reads it, agrees, and accepts it. But then the author can't bring herself to type the sentence that kills him off, partly because she's realised she's been narrating real people to their deaths, and she rewrites it so he lives. The professor thinks the new ending is weaker. She says the book was about a man who didn't know he was going to die, and a man who knows and accepts it deserves to live.

What I want is a reading of the film through the puzzle. Where does it dramatise the problem, where does it complicate it, where does it just quietly dodge it? And which side does the film end up on, is the professor right that the death was worth more, or is Karen's ending the film quietly agreeing with itself that a made-up person's life is worth sparing? I can't tell, and I think it matters, because its the film's own verdict on whether the suffering of people who don't exist counts for anything.

I've also got a half-baked theory of my own that I'd like you to test rather than just nod at. I've started calling it the middle-man theory. When I cry at a film I don't think the feeling is about Harold at all, I think it's relayed from the person who made him. There's a real woman at the other end who cared enough about this invented man that she couldn't finish the sentence, and what I'm feeling is her feeling, arriving late and second-hand. That way the emotion is real and has a real object and Harold is only the middle-man. Does the film bear that out, or does it undercut it? The professor doesn't care about Harold, after all, he cares about the book, and he'd rather Harold died.
