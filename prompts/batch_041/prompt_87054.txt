I am a staff software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been asked to contribute one written exam question to an internal course on causal reasoning for senior engineers. The students have had an introductory module on causal inference but are not philosophy majors. The course is not a statistics course; it is meant to make people think carefully about what claims such as the algorithm caused wait times to drop actually mean. I want a question on Simpson's paradox that cannot be answered by reciting the standard admissions example or by saying that one should always stratify. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The question should be self-contained and answerable without internet access, and it should not ask students to cite sources.

Here is the scenario I want the question to use. A ride-hailing company tests a new dispatch algorithm. The rollout was not randomized at the level of individual trips. It was introduced gradually, first in denser cities, and within each city it was assigned to a subset of drivers. Because the dispatch system is shared, a treated driver's behavior can affect the wait times of riders assigned to control drivers. The company compares average rider wait times. The results are as follows.

Overall:
- New algorithm: 1,000 trips, average wait 4.04 minutes.
- Old algorithm: 1,000 trips, average wait 4.50 minutes.

Dense cities:
- New algorithm: 800 trips, average wait 3.80 minutes.
- Old algorithm: 200 trips, average wait 3.50 minutes.

Sparse cities:
- New algorithm: 200 trips, average wait 5.00 minutes.
- Old algorithm: 800 trips, average wait 4.75 minutes.

The company also has data on driver acceptance rate. The new algorithm increases driver acceptance rate, and higher acceptance rates are associated with shorter waits.

Please build the exam question around this scenario. I want it to have five subparts, four marks each, totaling 20 marks. The subparts should ask students to do the following:
1. Explain how the overall result and the stratified results can both be mathematically correct, and identify the variable responsible for the reversal.
2. Identify the causal question that the overall comparison answers and the causal question that the dense-city comparison answers, and say which one is more relevant to a decision to launch the algorithm in a new dense city. State the assumptions that decision would require.
3. Classify driver acceptance rate as a confounder, a mediator, or a collider. A collider is a variable caused by both the treatment and the outcome. Explain what happens to the estimated effect if you adjust for acceptance rate, and when adjusting would be appropriate.
4. Explain why the shared dispatch system can violate the stable unit treatment value assumption, which is the assumption that one unit's treatment does not affect another unit's outcome, and why that matters for interpreting the estimated effect.
5. Discuss what this case shows about the idea that there is a single true effect of the algorithm, in terms of the causal question, the target population, and the role of assumptions.

For the model answer, give about 700 words and address each subpart directly, showing the reasoning rather than only stating conclusions. For the marking scheme, give five must-hit points, four marks each, and a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. Also include a short note on how an A answer differs from a B answer. Finally, in your response, state how confident you are in the model answer and marking scheme, and single out the point you are least sure about.
