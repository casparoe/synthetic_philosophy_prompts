I've been reading Berkeley on and off (mostly the Three Dialogues, on the train) and I keep hitting his claim that you can't even conceive of something existing unperceived, because the moment you conceive it you've perceived it. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I have pretty firm gut reactions to a pile of cases and I want to know what rule, if any, they add up to.

What I can and can't do:

- Picture a tree falling in a forest with nobody for a hundred miles: easy.
- Picture that same tree standing there right now, unperceived by anyone at all: also easy, I'm doing it as I type.
- Picture a shade of red nobody has ever seen: sort of, but I think I'm cheating, I'm really just picturing a reddish smear.
- Picture a sound nobody hears: fine.
- Picture a universe just like ours with every mind removed: fine, it's a movie with the audience deleted.
- Picture a prime number nobody has ever written down: obviously fine.
- Picture a pain nobody feels: I can't. Not hard — I don't know what I'd be picturing.
- Same with a doubt nobody doubts, or a thought nobody is thinking: can't do those either.

So tree-with-nobody-perceiving-it is fine, pain-with-nobody-feeling-it is impossible, and to me those are the same shape — a thing with its observer deleted. And pain-nobody-feels vs prime-nobody-wrote-down also look the same ("X with nobody") but land completely differently. What's the rule seperating them? My guess is it's about whether the experiencing is part of what the thing is — a pain is a feeling, so an unfelt pain is like a square with no sides, whereas a tree is just a tree and deleting the observer doesn't touch it. Does that hold up, or does it just move the bump around? And does whatever rule comes out actually rescue Berkeley's argument, or does the argument secretly need the extra premise that conceiving a thing is the same as perceiving it — which I don't think I buy, and which seems to be the whole thing under dispute anyway?

Also tell me where I'm being inconsistent. I have a feeling the no-minds universe is the case that should be bothering me and isn't.

Write it up in these sections:

My cases, lined up — ~150 words, just my verdicts restated, flagging the pairs that don't sit together.
Candidate principles — ~300 words, two or three rules stated precisely, each with the cases it saves and the ones it strains on.
Which one wins — ~250 words, your pick, stated plainly enough that I could apply it to a new case myself.
Fresh cases — ~200 words, three or four cases I didn't list, with the verdict your principle gives and why.
What I have to give up — ~150 words, which of my verdicts the winning rule can't keep, and how bad that is.
