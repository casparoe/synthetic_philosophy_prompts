A colleague of mine, Nadia, is an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness — she reckons the 'what it's like' is a trick the brain plays on itself rather than a further fact. She'll still call a state 'conscious' when it plays the right functional role; she just denies there's any inner glow on top. Over the years I've collected her verdicts on a pile of cases, each time asking whether there's something its like for the subject:

- Blindsight patient guessing a bar's orientation well above chance: no, nothing it's like.
- You or me looking at a ripe tomato: yes, conscious — though the redness is the illusion, and what's real is the state being available to steer talk and action.
- Subliminal priming: no.
- Dreaming: yes — same machinery, same trick.
- Anton's syndrome (cortically blind patient who sincerely reports seeing): yes, same status as ordinary seeing; the confabulation is no worse than the real thing.
- Visual form agnosia patient who can post a letter through a slot but can't say which way it's turned: no experience of the orientation.
- Deep anaesthesia: no.

Two new ones to call. First, a blindsight patient who's been trained up so he can now reliably report the shape of a stimulus when asked — but he insists he's only guessing, sees nothing, and his confidence is flat whether he's right or wrong. Second, a masked stimulus the subject can't report at all, but which he can bet on above chance, his bets tracking his accuracy — so something like a metacognitive state about it, just no report.

Predict her verdicts on both, and tell me which rules fit her earlier answers. Two jump out at me: conscious = globally available for report, and conscious = there's a metacognitive state tracking it, regardless of report. Are both live? Give me the rules that fit, what each predicts for the two new cases, which one you think she's actually running and why, and whether her illusionism even lets her give determinate answers here or just turns her yes/no into bookkeeping. Then name the further case that would seperate the surviving rules cleanly.

Give it to me in four sections, in this order: Her verdicts (~150 words), The rules that fit (~200 words), Which one she's running (~150 words), The case that would settle it (~100 words).
