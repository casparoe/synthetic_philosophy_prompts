I sit in a small Thursday reading group on formal epistemology, and one of us — Nadia, otherwise the least combative person in the room — has become our live test case for the reflection principle, the constraint that your present credence must already agree with the credence you expect to have later. Her verdicts on our hypotheticals have been rock steady, and I have been keeping them in the back of my notebook. The morning-after case: she knows she will drink tonight and that tomorrow, hungover and reckless, she will be eighty percent confident she can drive home; she is now five percent confident. Nadia: no requirement — she need not move an inch, and moving toward her future self would be the irrational thing to do. The X-ray case: she knows that tomorrow a doctor will show her a film whose contents she can already describe exactly, and that she will then be ninety-five percent confident the bone is broken. Nadia: requirement — she should already be at ninety-five percent. The slide-rule case: she knows that after a pill tomorrow she will be able to carry out a calculation correctly, and will believe its result. Nadia: requirement. The induction case: she knows a weekend with a group will leave her convinced its leader has special insight. Nadia: no requirement. The lunch case: she knows that after an hour with a colleague who has exactly her evidence and is no better at the question, she will be talked round to the colleague's view. Nadia: no requirement — the anticipated pressure is no reason to move now. The concussion case: she knows a fall tomorrow will cost her a stretch of memory and leave her believing something she now thinks false. Nadia: no requirement. The slip case: she knows that tomorrow she will notice an error in her present reasoning, an error she cannot yet see, and will then hold a different view. Nadia: requirement — she should already hold the corrected view.

Next Thursday brings the two cases I actually care about. The first, which Nadia calls the long soak, runs like this: she knows she is about to spend a month inside a philosopher's system while writing a review, that she will come out believing the system, and that she will by then know the territory far better than she does now — she will have read everything and be much harder to fool about it; but she also knows that the mechanism of her conversion will not be argument but immersion, that a month inside a system always makes her a believer, and she regards immersion as a distorting influence, roughly the way some people regard hypnosis. The second, which she calls the bare case, is this: nothing about her will change — no new evidence, no error found, no shift in alertness, mood, or standards — except that she will have spent another week thinking about the same material, and she knows she will end that week at a different credence.

What would she say about each, and, more to the point, what rule is she actually running? I am less interested in her two answers than in the other rules that fit every verdict above just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the soak and the bare case, your best guess at the rule with your reasons, and which single case — one of mine or one you devise — would best separate the surviving readings.
