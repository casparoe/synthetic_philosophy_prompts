I'm a third-year PhD student in history, and a methods chapter I've been writing has turned into something considerably more argumentative than I intended. For years I've held, loosely, that silences in an archive are not mere gaps to be lamented or filled but are themselves evidence: a source's failure to record something is a datum about the relations of power that produced it, and deserves the same interpretive care as what it does record. When my advisor pushed back in a seminar last term, I found myself adding a constraint that now does most of the work, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. The constraint is this: we may treat a silence as evidence of suppression or erasure only where the source's genre and institutional function would, in the ordinary course, have generated a record. A shipping firm's ledger that logs every cargo but never the names of the dockworkers who loaded it; a colonial census that enumerates landholdings in meticulous detail yet leaves the labor category blank for an entire district; a parish register that records every baptism for a century and then, for one community, records none — these are the cases I have in mind. Call it the expected-record condition. Without it, I worry, the whole approach collapses into reading our own political intuitions back into the past.

What I want from you is an honest map of this position's exposure. What would tell against it — and I don't mean only the obvious objection that silences have mundane causes, that records burn, that people were illiterate or indifferent or simply had nothing to say. I mean the deeper defeaters: which findings or cases should make me abandon the core claim, which should force only a qualification, and which I could concede while still holding it. In particular, I want to know whether the expected-record condition genuinely rescues the view or merely relocates the interpretive leap, since I suspect it smuggles in a norm of bureaucratic completeness that is itself modern and perhaps specifically state-centered. I would also like the view's strongest defender represented fairly — not what her critics say she must concede, but what she herself would treat as a real cost.

So: order these defeaters by how much they should move me, from the ones that ought to end the argument to the ones I can live with; tell me which are live rather than merely possible; and say plainly whether the position, once qualified this way, still risks anything at all. If I have insulated it until nothing could dislodge it, I would rather hear that, along with your judgment on whether that is a defect in a methodological commitment of this kind or simply the price of doing interpretive history.
