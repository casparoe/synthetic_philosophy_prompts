I work on inverse problems — geophysical and imaging inversion mostly — and on the identifiability questions that come with them. My working vocabulary is: a forward operator from a model space to a data space; the null space of that operator, or of its linearisation, where the non-uniqueness lives; regularisation, which buys uniqueness by importing something that was never in the data; and the difference between estimating parameters inside a fixed parametrisation and choosing the parametrisation at all. I am fluent in that material. My philosophy is second-hand: enough to recognise the shape of a debate, not enough to be confident I am not reinventing it badly.

Here is the debate I want translated. It concerns what, if anything, licenses a claim about the ontology of a system when part of the relevant information is inaccessible in principle rather than merely unmeasured. Five positions, as I have them:

1. Optimism. Accessible information determines the ontology at least in the limit; inaccessibility is a contingent limitation of instruments and patience.
2. Structuralism. Accessible information determines at most structure; any ontology is one member of a family of realisations that no accessible information could distinguish, so the further question of which realisation is the real one is not a factual question.
3. Quietism. Claims about ontology beyond what accessible information constrains are idle rather than false, and the demand for identification is a category mistake.
4. Pessimism. Even the space of candidate ontologies is not surveyable from within, so identification up to isomorphism is a fantasy: the ontology one lands on may be one of indefinitely many that one cannot presently conceive.
5. Relativity. Identification is always relative to a chosen vocabulary of kinds; the only substantive question is which members of antecedently given kinds are present, and the choice of vocabulary is not itself an empirical matter.

I want these five translated into my vocabulary, not likened to it. Read ontology as model space, accessible information as data space together with a likelihood, identification as the existence of a unique model — or a unique equivalence class of models — consistent with the data, and inaccessible in principle as whatever the honest counterpart turns out to be, if there is one. For each position give the image, and mark every point at which the translation forces a choice I would have made differently.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Three suspicions to test.

(a) I suspect the debate, stripped of vocabulary, is the ordinary non-uniqueness of inverse problems, and that inaccessibility in principle does no work the null space is not already doing. Is that right? If the honest counterpart is instead that the model space may simply fail to contain the truth, say so plainly, and say whether that is a limitation of my framework or a sign that the philosophical claim was never about inference in the first place.

(b) My framework can say whether a parameter is identified once a parametrisation is fixed; it cannot, without changing the subject, say whether the parametrisation is the right one. In practice the model space is a vector of parameters on a grid chosen before any inference begins, so the ontology is settled by a discretisation that nobody counts as part of the inference. Is position 2 anything more than that observation, or is it that observation plus a metaphysical claim my framework cannot express and the structuralist has not earned?

(c) My field's habit is to call a problem well posed once a regulariser is fixed, which looks to me like position 5 with the philosophy left out. Tell me whether that is unfair to position 5, and whether there is any sense in which the choice of vocabulary is constrained by the accessible information rather than by convenience.

End with the verdict: which of the five has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and whether the residue is a defect of the framework or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked. I would rather be told that my vocabulary cannot state something than be handed a formal shadow of it. No citations — I will not check them and I do not want them invented.
