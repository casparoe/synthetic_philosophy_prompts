I'm teaching a grad seminar on supererogation this term and I want to spend a session on an analogy a collegue of mine leans on hard. His line is that morality is a pass/fail course with a low pass mark, and supererogatory acts are extra credit: you've satisfied the requirements the moment you clear the bar, and everything above the bar is bonus points you're free to take or leave. He uses it to argue that nobody is ever required to be a saint or a hero, that the demandingness objection to consequentialism is confused (you can't fail a pass/fail course by skipping extra credit), and that we should all stop feeling guilty about not doing more.

So give me the audit. Start with the mapping -- what plays the role of the pass mark, the assignments, the grader, the curve, the transcript -- then the places the analogy breaks, seperating the breaks that matter for those three conclusions from the ones that don't. I want to know whether a bonus-point picture can even represent what supererogation is supposed to be, and whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If there's a better analogy for the floor-and-above picture, tell me what it is.
