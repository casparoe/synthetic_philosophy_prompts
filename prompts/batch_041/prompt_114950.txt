I am a clinical ethics fellow who also writes comedy on the side, and I have been asked to contribute to a satirical supplement to a hospital-governance bulletin. Please write me a mock accreditation standard: a numbered document of roughly 800–1,000 words, titled something along the lines of "Standard 7.3 — Classification, Recording, and Reporting of Omissions," issued by a fictional accrediting body of your own invention, written in the flat, cross-referenced register of a genuine quality-assurance standard (scope and applicability, definitions, classification tiers, certification requirements, grandfathering of legacy practice, audit, appeals). The target is not any individual or institution but the practice of codifying the distinction between doing and allowing — and between killing and letting die — in institutional policy, where it functions as a machine for keeping the institution's hands clean while the same patients die. Please include a short appendix of three worked cases with their official classifications, at least one of which involves an automated system, for instance a triage algorithm that "declines to escalate," since that is where the distinction is being quietly rebuilt at the moment.

The humour has to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from whimsy. Build the clauses on the actual load-bearing features of the debate: the idea that letting die is picked out by counterfactual dependence rather than by any positive act; the awkwardness of treating omissions as causes at all; the accepted symmetry between withholding and withdrawing a treatment; the handling of foreseen but unintended side-effects; the role of a duty to rescue and of having assumed responsibility for someone; and the way decisions about scarce resources hide inside omissions. I want the standard internally coherent enough to embarrass itself: at least one clause should reclassify an unmistakable killing as a recordable omission, and at least one should reclassify an unmistakable omission as a killing, while no clause ever admits that the distinction is doing no moral work — the document must never wink, and the reader should feel the pressure without being told where to look. Invented officials and invented bodies may be quoted freely, but do not cite real documents, real authors, or real cases, and do not let the piece turn into a tour of famous thought experiments; it should read as a document with an actual job to do. It should be comprehensible to someone who has never opened a book on the subject and still land, clause by clause, for someone who has.
