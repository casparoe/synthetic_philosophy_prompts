The question: "In Taurek's 1977 paper 'Should the Numbers Count?', he considers a case in which you can save either one stranger or five strangers, all of whom will otherwise die, and you can only reach one group. Taurek argues that the numbers don't count here: you are not required to save the five, and saving the one instead would be permissible. (a) Reconstruct his argument as strongly as you can. (b) Give the strongest objection to it you can think of, and say how Taurek might reply."

The student's answer: "Taurek's target is the reasoning that says five lives saved is better than one, so you must save the five. He thinks that reasoning treats the five as one thing, as if their interests get added up into one big interest five times the size of the one person's. But there is no such thing. There are six separate people, each of whom dies if you don't get to them, and the five don't merge into a single person with five times as much to lose. Since each of the six has exactly the same thing at stake — his life — the reason I have to save any one of them is the same reason I have to save any other. So the five do not, between them, have a stronger claim on me than the one does. That means saving the five isn't required, and saving the one isn't wrong. Taurek says he would flip a coin, which is a fair way of deciding when the reasons really are equal.

The deeper point, I think, is that lives are incommensurable. There's no common scale on which one person's life can be weighed against another's, so we can't say that five deaths are a greater loss than one death — that's not a claim we're entitled to make. This is basically the same thing Rawls says when he objects that utilitarianism applies to society the principle of choice for one person, treating society as one big person.

The strongest objection I can think of is that this view licenses monstrous results. If the numbers really don't count, then letting a thousand people die in order to save one is no worse than saving the thousand, and any view with that implication has to be wrong. Taurek can reply that the objection just assumes the principle he's denying, namely that more lives saved is better, and that it's no argument against a view that it has surprising consequences. He can also say the coin flip isn't a way of sneaking the numbers back in, because it doesn't aim at a better outcome — it's just a fair way of settling a case where the reasons have run out. Honestly though I think Taurek's view is closest to Parfit's priority view: both of them say that what matters is the individual's claim rather than the total amount of good, and on both views the numbers don't count."
