I'm prepping for next weeks episode of a small history-of-economics podcast, and my guest is a scholar who thinks everyone reads Adam Smith's colonies chapters wrong -- on her line they're not a cost-benefit audit showing empire didn't pay, they're a morale indictment of a state captured by merchants, with the accounting thrown in to rub it in. I want to make her earn it. Draft eight or nine interview questions, at least one each on: whether the sheer mass of cost-counting in those chapters doesn't pull against her reading (and what Smith would have said about an empire that turned a profit); on the plan he floats for the American colonies to send members to Parliament, and what that does to the thesis; and on his four-stages story of history -- hunting, pasturage, farming, commerce -- and whether planting non-agricultural peoples at the bottom of it poisons the principled reading. A few more on the East India Company, on the man of system passage in the Theory of Moral Sentiments (does the charge land on the empire-builders or on the reformer who wants to give up the colonies?), and on his case against slavery, which looks mostly economic. Open one question by reading him back: 'To found a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers may at first sight appear a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers' -- then ask what the sentence after it is doing, the one about which statesmen are capable of fancying an advantage in spending the blood and treasure of their fellow citizens. For the two or three questions where she has an obvious escape hatch, add the follow-up that closes it. No 'what drew you to Smith' filler.
