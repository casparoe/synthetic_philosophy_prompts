I teach an upper-level bioethics seminar on end-of-life decision-making, and this term the material has become unexpectedly personal: my grandmother died last spring in hospice after a long decline, and the family meetings about palliative sedation and artificial nutrition made the theoretical distinctions feel very concrete. I am now grading the midterm, and because I want to be consistent and fair, I would like a second opinion on one student's answer. The question was worth twenty points and asked students to analyze a case in which a competent patient with advanced ALS, who is not imminently dying but suffers from intractable breathlessness and panic, requests continuous deep sedation until death. The attending physician believes the sedation will relieve the suffering while foreseeably shortening life by a few days. Students were asked to (a) distinguish active euthanasia, physician-assisted dying, and palliative sedation; (b) explain how the doctrine of double effect might apply; and (c) assess whether the distinction between killing and letting die does any moral work in this case. In my instructions I said a complete answer would define the three practices accurately, state the standard conditions of double effect, apply them to the case, and discuss the killing/letting die distinction without assuming that it is settled.

The student wrote: Active euthanasia is when a doctor intentionally kills a patient, while physician-assisted dying is when a doctor provides the means for the patient to kill herself. Palliative sedation is different because the doctor gives drugs to relieve pain, not to kill, even if death comes sooner. The doctrine of double effect, which was first formulated by Kant, says that an action with a good effect and a bad effect is permissible if the act itself is good or neutral, the bad effect is not intended, the bad effect is not the means to the good effect, and there is proportionate reason. In this case, the act is giving sedation, which is not inherently wrong; the bad effect is the patient's death, which is foreseen but not intended; the bad effect is not the means to the good effect, because the sedation, not the death, is what relieves the suffering; and the suffering is severe, so proportionality is met. Therefore the sedation is permissible. The distinction between killing and letting die is not relevant here, because the doctor is not killing; she is letting the disease take its course. Some people say that if death is foreseen with certainty, then it is intended, but that would make all pain relief at the end of life impossible, so the law should reject that view. In conclusion, palliative sedation is morally permissible and should be legally protected, while active euthanasia should remain illegal because it involves intentionally killing.

Please assign a point total out of twenty and a letter grade, and then give me the specific comments I should write on the paper. I need a point-by-point assessment: what the student got right, what is wrong or questionable, and what a stronger answer would have said. Please write your comments in American English.
