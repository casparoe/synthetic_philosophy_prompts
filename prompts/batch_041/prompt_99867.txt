I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, writing a chapter on perceptual concepts, and for the past few weeks a colleague in the design school and I have been chewing over an argument that we built together and that I now cannot shake. The setting is the Letter Spirit microcosm: treat the design of a complete alphabet in a single style as a tractable stand-in for concept formation and creative design, where the task for each letter is to produce a form that (i) a competent reader will unhesitatingly identify as that letter, and (ii) plainly belongs to the same family as the forms produced for the other letters. The first constraint is the one that worries me, and it has pushed me towards a view I hold with increasing conviction and decreasing comfort: that an 'a' is not a shape but a role that a shape plays inside a system of letters — something has to do the work of a bowl, something the work of a stem, and the whole has to be legible against the background of the remaining letters in that style.

Here is the argument. (1) Take a form that is unmistakably an 'a' and a form that is unmistakably an 'e'. (2) There is a continuous sequence of forms running from the one to the other in which each form differs from its neighbour by so slight a deformation that a competent reader, shown the two side by side in isolation, would judge them to be the same letter. (3) If a competent reader would judge two forms to be the same letter when they are presented side by side in isolation, then they are the same letter. (4) Being an instance of the same letter is transitive: if B is the same letter as A, and C is the same letter as B, then C is the same letter as A — a letter-category just is the class of all instances of a letter, and identity of class is transitive. (5) So the form at the far end of the sequence is the same letter as the form at the near end, i.e. the 'e' is an 'a'. (6) But it is an 'e', and the whole point of the exercise is that 'a' and 'e' are two letters. So (2), (3) or (4) has to go.

I would rather keep (4) than anything else on the list — if sameness of letter is not transitive then a letter-category stops behaving like a category, and I am not sure what my chapter is then about — and (2) looks to me like an unremarkable fact about continuous deformation, which I do not see how to deny; though a colleague insists the fault lies there rather than anywhere else, and I cannot see how. That seems to leave (3), and yet (3) is the step that makes the whole question worth asking: if a small enough perceptual difference can change which letter a form is, then 'a'-ness has a boundary that no reader can locate, and by parity of reasoning there are such boundaries for words, phonemes, faces and species, which strikes me as a much larger bill than the problem deserves. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would like to know why the step I abandon looked so plausible in the first place, and to have the alternatives priced honestly rather than merely listed. I would be grateful if you would write in British English.
