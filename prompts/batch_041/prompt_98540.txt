So I'm doing a piece on the sortition bandwagon — citizens' assemblies, citizens' juries, all the versions where you pull a few hundred people at random off the electoral roll, sit them in a hotel conference room for a few weekends, hand them briefing packs and expert witnesses, and ask them what to do about abortion or climate or the city budget. Ireland's done it, France has done it, my own city council is floating one. And I keep noticing these things get sold with two completely different pitches at once. Pitch one: it's fair — everyone's got an equal chance, so the outcome is legitimate in a way a parliament full of lawyers and party hacks isn't. Pitch two: it's actually smarter — a room of random people with good briefing beats a room of experts, because of cognitive diversity or whatever the line is.

Here's what I want. Take the practice itself, not the theory — the actual machinery: the random draw, the quota-matching by demographics, the paid stipends, expert testimony in, recommendation out, usually non-binding. Tell me what it presupposes philosophically. What does it have to believe about political competence? About the difference between representing people and sampling them? About why randomness launders a decision into a legitimate one at all? Do the fairness pitch and the smarts pitch actually rest on the same committments, or do they pull apart — can you run a real assembly that's fair but dumb, or smart but unrepresentative, and does the machinery quietly pick a side? And the bit I really care about: it looks to me like these assemblies presuppose that ordinary people can be trusted to decide, but then someone else sets the agenda, experts write the briefing materials, and the recommendation goes to a parliament that's free to ignore it. So which commitment is actually doing the work, and which is just decoration? Same question for jury duty — we've been doing random selection there for centuries and we still let the lawyers strike people off. Is that a contradiction of the whole idea or is it consistent with some narrower version of it?

One more thing: seperate out for me what's genuinely settled among people who study this from what's contested. I don't want a confident answer that's really just one camp talking. If people who know the field disagree about whether random selection has any epistemic value at all, say so, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.

Keep it readable — I write for a general audience, I'm not a philosopher.
