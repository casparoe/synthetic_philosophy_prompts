I defended my dissertation eighteen months ago and I am now in my second year on the academic job market — a hundred and thirty-seven applications, four first-round interviews, no offer — and somewhere in the middle of that I began reading Peter Turchin on secular cycles, which I have not been able to put down and have not been able to stop applying to myself. His structural-demographic model runs societies through a recurring sequence: a long expansion, in which population, state revenues, and the number of people trained for elite positions all grow together; a stagflation phase, in which would-be elites are produced faster than the positions that could absorb them while ordinary living standards stagnate or fall; a crisis, in which elite factions, popular immiseration, and the fiscal strain of the state peak at once and the political order breaks; and a depression, or intercycle, out of which a new integrative order consolidates and the whole thing begins again. He reads Ibn Khaldun's asabiyya as the collective solidarity that accumulates in the hard phases and is consumed in the easy ones, and he treats the whole business as a dynamical system whose variables oscillate with lags, so that a correlation measured at a single moment can look like nothing while the underlying motion is regular. I hold the objections, too — that history is not a phase space, that a model fitted to the past has never been tested on the future — and I also know that a hundred and thirty-seven applications is not a data point. Both of those are true at once, and that is the thing I want the poem to be about.

What I need is a pantoum to read aloud at our department's end-of-year gathering, where a good number of us will be in the same position. The form is doing the theoretical work here, so please keep to it strictly: four quatrains, one for each phase in order, then a closing quatrain arranged so that the poem's final line is word-for-word its first, so that the poem ends where it began and the ear hears the cycle close. Lines two and four of each quatrain must return as lines one and three of the next, and the lines that travel in this way should be the ones carrying the theory — the promise that there will be room, the credential, the counting of applications — so that the same words do different work in each phase: a promise in the expansion, an inheritance in the stagflation, a threat in the crisis. The repetition should be the argument, and it should be unmistakably the same words each time rather than variations on them.

Two further constraints. First, keep the whole poem inside one family across two generations, so that the short cycle and the long one are visibly the same shape; I want a reader to feel that a grandmother's expectations and a two-century oscillation have been drawn with one line. Second, do not let the poem settle whether the cycle is real. It should be entirely readable as a prediction and entirely readable as a pattern imposed after the fact on a run of accidents, and its confidence should be exactly the confidence of a model that fits the past well and has never been tested on the future.

Around twenty lines, in a plain spoken register that will survive a bad microphone. No dedication, no epigraph, no mention of Turchin and no technical vocabulary — if the poem needs the jargon to work, it is not working. Someone who has read the theory should recognize the model inside it; someone who has not should still find it a good poem.
