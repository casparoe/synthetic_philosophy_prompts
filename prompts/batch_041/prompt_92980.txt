ok so I'm in this STS (science, technology and society) course and we just wrapped a unit on the precautionary principle — the idea that when an activity or product might cause serious harm and the science isn't settled yet, we shouldn't just sit around waiting for proof, we should act to head off the risk (ban it, restrict it, label it, delay it) even under uncertainty. For my creative piece I want to write a parody: a wellness / self-help advice column, like an "Ask Dr. So-and-so" page in a glossy lifestyle mag, where the columnist has decided to run her entire life strictly and consistently on the precautionary principle. The humour has to come from how uncomfortably consistent she is, not from her being a strawman — I want someone who actually knows risk ethics to read it and laugh because she gets the machinery right rather than wincing. So, for instance, she should clock that the burden of proof she's using (show me it's safe before we do it) can basically never be discharged, because you can't prove a universal negative; she should run what people call the reversal test — if we can't prove X is safe, we equally can't prove that banning X is safe, since forbidding things has its own body count, so the principle ends up demanding precautions against itself; and she should keep tripping over the fact that not deciding is itself a decision, so "when in doubt, don't" never tells her when to stop being in doubt. Nice if it escalates from something trivial (a new food additive, a phone, a non-stick pan) up to bigger life stuff like whether to have a kid or move cities.

Couple of constraints: keep it deadpan and fully in character the whole way through, no winking at the reader, and don't have her cite real studies, agencies, or court cases — I don't want the piece resting on facts that might be off, so made-up examples are completely fine. Around 600–750 words, give it a title, and give it the actual shape of an advice column: a reader writes in with a genuine dilemma, and the columnist's eventual advice is that she can't advise them at all — let that be the punchline instead of a tidy moral at the end. Oh and please don't make her dumb, make her too smart, the kind of voice that'd genuinely be fun to quote out of context.
