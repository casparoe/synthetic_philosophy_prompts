I am writing a novel whose central character is a young naturalist, and I have been trying to get clear on a stretch of Aristotle's Physics that I possess only in paraphrase. As I understand the argument in the second book, he distinguishes the necessity that runs forward from the material to the product — stones and timber cannot but behave as they do — from a necessity that runs backward from the end: granted that a house is to be, there must be stones, but the stones are not there because of the stones; they are there because of the house, and the matter is for the sake of the end rather than the reverse. He then seems to claim that this backward-running, conditional necessity is what natural things exhibit, so that the material prerequisites of an organism are necessitated only on the supposition that the end is what it is.

What I cannot get straight is what the for-the-sake-of relation adds to plain efficient causation. If a mason moves the stones into place, why is the mason's pushing not the whole explanation of the wall? And if something more is required, is it a fact about the future — the wall's being a wall — somehow acting on the present, or is it instead a claim about how we must describe the process? A friend who studied philosophy tells me the passage is now obsolete, because the modern dispute over biological function has settled into two camps: one holding that a trait's function is whatever it was selected to do, the other that it is whatever it currently contributes to the workings of a living system, with Aristotle belonging to the first camp. I do not trust the summary, and I would rather understand the text before deciding.

Please help me understand the passage on its own terms: what hypothetical necessity is, why Aristotle takes it to differ from the necessity of the material, and what the for-the-sake-of relation contributes that efficient causation does not. Then tell me whether the passage really does commit him to one side of the modern dispute, and if so which; if the modern dispute is asking a different question, say what question he is asking instead.

Finally, test the reading against a case from my novel. One of my characters is studying a beetle whose shell carries a groove that, by her account, arose as an incidental byproduct of the way the shell grows and only much later began channeling fog droplets toward its mouth. I want to know what each account of function would say about the groove's function now, whether a case like this forces a choice between the accounts or merely exposes what each was always talking about, and where a defender of each would dig in. I am not asking for citations or further reading; I want the argument itself, in enough detail that I can put the disagreement into a scene.
