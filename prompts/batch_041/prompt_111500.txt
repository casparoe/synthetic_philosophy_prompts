I've been following the discussion of safe Pareto improvements in delegated game playing for a while, and I want to check my understanding of the motivation, because one part of it has never sat right with me. The setup, as I reconstruct it: I hand a decision to an agent that will play some game on my behalf against another player, and I do not know what that other player's preferences are. A safe Pareto improvement is then a modification of the game, or a commitment to play it differently, that leaves the other player better off than the default under every preference profile they might have, while also leaving me better off. The point, I take it, is that this gives the other player a reason to go along with the change without either side having to know or trust the other's values.

My confusion is this. If the other player is rational, then they will accept the modification whenever it is better for them given their actual preferences, so the requirement that it be better for them under all possible preferences looks like a sufficient condition for agreement rather than a necessary one, and a wildly overstrong sufficient condition at that. Why not simply look for modifications that are Pareto improvements relative to the default, full stop? Safety then seems like a concession to paranoia about not knowing the other's utility function rather than a substantive part of the theory. Worse, if safety really is required, the set of safe Pareto improvements looks like it should almost always be empty: for any proposed change to the default outcome, there is presumably some preference ordering on the other side that ranks the default higher, unless the change simply hands the other player more of everything they could want. So either the safety condition is doing no work, or it makes the whole program trivial.

I suspect I am conflating two different things, perhaps robustness to the other agent's preferences with robustness to the other agent's strategic behavior, or perhaps the ex ante problem of choosing a policy with the ex post problem of accepting an outcome, but I cannot see exactly where the conflation lies. Can you explain what the safety condition is actually for, and why the people working on this treat preference-uncertainty as the central obstacle rather than an incidental one?
