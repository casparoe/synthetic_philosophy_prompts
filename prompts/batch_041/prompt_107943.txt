I cover science and ideas for a general audience — the beat where lab results, tech claims and philosophy all bleed together — and I keep running into a shape of argument that I can smell but can't name, and I want to write about it instead of just waving at it. So what I need isnt a tour of the debate, it's the spec. What would a theory of this actually have to get right?

Here's the shape. A company tells me its new model is trustworthy because it scores 94% on their own benchmark, and when I ask how they know the benchmark is any good, the answer is that the model does well on it. A hospital says a new rapid test is accurate because it matched the clinical diagnosis in their study — except in that study the clinical diagnosis was made by doctors who already had the rapid result in front of them. Someone argues that induction is trustworthy because it's worked so far. No single step is dishonest, each is the kind of thing you'd do anyway, and yet the whole loop seems to do no work: the thing is being used to vouch for the very thing that's supposed to vouch for it.

I've heard the words bootstrapping and easy knowledge, and I've heard clever people say that every argument is circular somewhere, and every time I read up on it I drown. What I want is the requirements list. Concretely: which cases a theory has to get right, with the case pinned to each requirement — including the boundary ones, like one instrument checking itself, versus two instruments checking each other, versus a room full of instruments that only ever agree with each other. Which distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse — having a warrant versus being able to defend it to someone; one good use of a source versus a track record built entirely out of that same source; this reasoning is fine versus this reasoning is fine only if you already trust the thing it's meant to support. Which facts about how people and machines actually form beliefs it can't contradict. And which of those requirements are load-bearing and which are just the house style of one camp — plus, if it's clean, which single requirement does the most to rule candidates out.

Then the part I actually care about. My hunch — tell me if it's naive — is that you can't have all of it: don't let the loop count, and we obviously know tons of ordinary things without first proving our senses are reliable, look like they can't both be had cheaply, and somebody has to give. If that's the real shape, say which requirement you'd sacrifice and why, and — more important — what would change your mind. Which argument or case would make you drop the requirement you're defending? Where is your own reasoning most likely to fail? I don't want the camps' positions read back to me, I want to know where yours breaks.

No citations, please, I'm not chasing papers, and I definately don't need the history of the terminology. The theory only has to be good enough to let me tell a reader when to smell a rat and when to relax, so calibrate to that.
