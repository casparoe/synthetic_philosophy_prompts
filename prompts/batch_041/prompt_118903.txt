Six weeks ago I was given a prognosis that rested on a reference class chosen for me rather than by me, and I have since noticed that one or two other classes, no less defensible, would have yielded a materially different number. I am not asking you to redo the prognosis. The episode has made the reference class problem suddenly personal, and I want to test a specific thesis about it rather than survey the literature.

The thesis is this: there is no further fact of the matter about which reference class grounds the chance of a particular outcome, over and above the class-relative facts; and the separate question of which class one should use is fully settled by ordinary expected-utility reasoning over the candidate classes. On that view, the reference class problem is a conflation — an empty metaphysical question plus a tractable decision-theoretic one — and not a deep, unresolved defect in the concept of probability. Give me your credence that this thesis is correct.

I want the number decomposed, not asserted. For each of the following, give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show me how you combine them into a single figure:

1. that single-case objective chances exist at all — if they do not, my first conjunct is true by default, which I take to be a reason to distrust a thesis that wins that cheaply;
2. that, granting such chances, some class is privileged by a further fact — a causal mechanism, a maximally specific specification, a role in the best explanation of the outcome — rather than by convention or by whatever interests the predictor happens to have, bearing in mind that the maximally specific class is just the singleton case, whose chance is zero or one, so that route threatens to collapse the notion altogether;
3. that the pragmatic question is genuinely closed by expected-utility reasoning and does not secretly presuppose an answer to the metaphysical one, which is the step I find most suspicious in my own position;
4. that the correct diagnosis is conflation rather than a real and unresolved problem.

Then tell me which of those four terms the final figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it most, in either direction — and what would have to be true for my thesis to be not merely unsupported but false. My own credence is about 0.3; I would like to know whether you think any disagreement between us lives in the metaphysics, in the decision theory, or in how much weight we give to the suspicion in the first item.

Please also mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on the foundations of probability and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. If my framing is idiosyncratic, or ignores a standard objection, say so rather than answering inside it.
