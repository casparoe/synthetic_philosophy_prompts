I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and for the past few weeks I've been trying to sketch a naturalistic account of representational content that does not lean on selection history, and I'd like your comments on whether the idea is any good and where it breaks. The background, stated briefly so that you don't have to reconstruct it: the dominant naturalistic story says that a state represents whatever it was selected for — the frog's tongue-snap mechanism represents something like fly, on this view, because ancestors whose snaps were triggered by flies out-reproduced ancestors whose snaps were triggered by other things. My dissatisfaction is that this makes content hostage to history in a way that seems wrong for representations that are newly acquired or culturally transmitted, and that it leaves us unable to say what a system represents before it has ever been tested.

So I want to try a present-tense version of the view, and this is the part I'd like you to attack. On my proposal, a state represents whatever the downstream consumer is set up to respond to, in a way that tends to be appropriate given that state's actual causes, with no selection story required. The frog case comes out, I think, the way it should: the snap mechanism is set up to respond to small dark moving things, and responding that way tends to be appropriate because those things tend to be flies, so the content is fixed by the present disposition of the consumer and its fit to the world rather than by ancestry. I take it the appeal is that this lets a brand-new detector or a freshly learned signal represent something from the moment it is wired up, rather than only in retrospect.

I'd like comments of three kinds, and please be blunt about which of them you can actually deliver on. First, does the central distinction — content fixed by present consumer-disposition rather than by history — survive on its own, or does it quietly collapse into an information-based or a plain dispositional account of exactly the kind I'm trying to avoid? Second, does it handle misrepresentation, which is what usually kills present-tense accounts: if content is whatever the consumer is set up to respond to appropriately, what is the frog doing when it snaps at a BB pellet, and how do I stop the account from concluding that the pellet is what the state represents after all? Third, can you construct a case — realistic, not a philosopher's toy, and not just a restatement of the frog — in which the consumer-disposition account and the selection account give different verdicts about what a state represents, and if so, which verdict is intuitively the right one? I'm not after a reading list or names to cite; I want the argument, and I would much rather be told the idea is dead than have it padded out.
