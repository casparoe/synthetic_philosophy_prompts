I am preparing a ninety-minute session for a mixed group of ML engineers and policy analysts at an internal safety workshop on the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax. I am not asking you to explain these ideas to me; I am asking you to plan how I should teach them. By the alignment tax I mean the capability or efficiency cost a developer pays for a given safety property. By the competitiveness constraint I mean the requirement that this cost not be so large, relative to what non-adopting competitors pay, that the adopter is selected against in the market or in the race for capability. I want to distinguish the private tax, borne by the adopting developer, from the public tax, borne by the field if adoption is penalized; the static tax, a one-time capability cost, from the dynamic tax, where safety work may also improve capability; and a threshold reading of the constraint, on which a single line matters, from a distributional reading, on which what matters is the distribution of competitor costs. Plan the explanation. In what order must the components be introduced so that the constraint emerges as a structural consequence rather than a moral exhortation? What single carrying example should anchor the session, and what must that example have and lack? Why not the standard two-labs-racing-to-deploy story, if not that? Which misconceptions will this audience predictably bring—for instance, that the alignment tax is a fixed number, that competitiveness is just benchmark performance, that the race is a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, or that regulation removes the constraint—and exactly where in the sequence should each be headed off? What question should I ask at the end to see whether they have understood the constraint rather than merely learned to repeat it? And what should I explicitly leave out at this level, and say that I am leaving out?
