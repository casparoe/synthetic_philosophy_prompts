so I want a satire piece built around a decision-theory idea, and since I don't want you guessing at it, here it is spelled out: a "safe Pareto improvement" is a modification to how some interaction between people gets resolved, where the modified version leaves every party at least as well off as the default outcome -- not under your best guess about how they'll behave or what they want, but with certainty, holding across a whole set of possible ways the situation could go. The usual selling point is that you don't need probabilities or beliefs about the other side at all; you just need some assumptions. And thats the catch. The guarantee is only ever as strong as the assumptions that were stipulated to get it -- assumptions about how the parties will play, about which situations count as "the same situation," about which preferences are even in the running. Whoever does the analysis gets to pick those. So "safe" ends up carrying a ton of rhetorical weight that the underlying math doesn't hand over on its own.

Write me a ~600 word piece in the form of an internal company announcement plus an FAQ, from a mid-size company that has just changed how something gets allocated among its employees -- desks, the bonus pool, the on-call rota, the good parking spots, pick whatever works. The whole thing is announced as a safe Pareto improvement: better for every single employee, no matter how they'd play it, no matter what they want. Deadpan corporate-comms voice throughout. The comedy has to come from the machinery being real: the fine print is where the stipulated assumptions live, and each one should visibly do work. Get the definition right -- "Pareto" means nobody is worse off under the new outcome than under the default, and the "safe" part is the quantification over possibilities -- and make sure the default being improved on is one the company itself picked.

Things I want in there:

- at least one assumption that sounds like a technicality but is doing all the heavy lifting, e.g. something in the neighborhood of "situations that are structurally identical get resolved identically," applied to two employee complaints that are obviously not identical
- the set of possible employee preferences being enumerated, with one glaring omission, never flagged
- a dissenting employee or outside economist quoted, whose objection is technically correct -- safety is relative to the stipulated set, and you can buy safety by narrowing the set -- and an FAQ entry that responds to the objection without answering it
- a refusal to put probabilities on anything, framed as a virtue rather than an evasion
- a closing FAQ answer that gives the game away without the writer ever admitting it

Don't wink, don't put a moral in the mouth of the narrator, and don't mention any real researchers, papers, or institutions. Someone who's read the bargaining-under-uncertainty literature should laugh and not wince; someone who hasn't should still follow whats going on. No citations, obviously.
