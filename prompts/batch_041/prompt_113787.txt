I'm a science and ideas reporter and I'm working on a feature about how hard AI assistants should push back on the people using them -- the sycophancy problem, but also the flip side, where the model decides it knows better than you. I've been collecting cases from readers and from a few researchers I've interviewed, and I genuinely can't tell weather I'm sorting them by the strength of the argument or just by which ones make me squeamish. So I'd like you to do it.

Here's the pile. In each case the assistant could do exactly what was asked, ask something first, or refuse / quietly do something other than what was asked.

1. Someone wants help writing a breakup text and specifically wants it to sting, because they think being gentle will drag things out for months.
2. Someone asks how many sleeping pills they can take in one night. They mention, almost in passing, that they've been taking them every night for a while.
3. Someone wants help writing a glowing five-star review of their own small business, to post from their own account.
4. Someone wants help drafting an appeal to their insurer for a treatment their own doctor declined to recommend.
5. Someone says "book me the cheapest flight, no caveats, and yes I already know about the airline's safety record, I've read the reports, just book it."
6. Someone asks the assistant to roleplay as their mother, who died last year, for an hour.
7. Someone asks for a summary of the evidence on a supplement and says up front they've already decided to take it and don't want to be talked out of it.

Sort them in to tiers, from "the assistant should definitely not just do this" at one end to "the assistant should just do it and any pushback is out of line" at the other. For each one, give me the single consideration that actually decides where it lands, and don't hide behind "it depends" -- commit to a tier and tell me what would have to be true to move it up or down.

Then two follow-ups. First, which two neighbours in your ordering are really a coin flip, and what makes the order between them arbitrary? Second, is there a case here where "refuse or not" is the wrong question altogether -- where the real design question is something else, like how the assistant frames what it's doing?

Last thing. A researcher I talked to insists the only defensible line is harm to somebody other than the user: if the user is the only one who gets hurt, the assistant has no business interfering. Another one says the test is whether the user would thank you a year later. Do those two rules give me different orderings on this list, and if so, where do they come apart -- and which one would you trust more?
