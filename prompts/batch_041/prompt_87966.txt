I'm putting together a seminar on philosphy of history and I want to do a Max Weber thought experiment. Weber died in 1920, so he never saw Hitler's rise, never saw the Nazi regime, and obviously never saw the whole postwar arguement between Great Man history and structural explanation. That's the point. I want you to reconstruct what he would plausibly have said about the counterfactual: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?

Here's the framing I've been using with students, some of which I made up. I give them a three-way taxonomy of positions on the counterfactual. First, necessary-individual: Hitler is a necessary condition; without him there's no regime, or nothing we'd identify as the same regime. Second, structural-slot: some authoritarian nationalist regime was coming regardless, and Hitler filled a slot another figure would have filled, with a different style but roughly the same trajectory. Third, contingent-intensifier: the structure made some authoritarian outcome probable, but Hitler changed the magnitude, timing, and above all the specific ideological core, the racial-exterminatory program, so removing him leaves dictatorship but not this dictatorship. Some of the students also point out that without Hitler is ambiguous between deleting him from the world and swapping in a different leader, and that those two readings give different answers.

I also hand them a distinction I invented, between load-bearing and signature individual contributions. A leader's contribution is load-bearing if removing them changes whether the structural outcome happens at all; it's signature if the outcome still happens but with a different form, intensity, or ideological content.

What I want from you: reconstruct Weber's likely response to all this using his actual toolkit, not a vibe. Specifically his methodological writing on objective possibility and adequate causation, which came out of his fight with Eduard Meyer over how historians justify causal claims; his ideal-type method; his insistence that collective entities like the state or capitalism are, for sociological purposes, to be treated as products of the actions of individuals; and his sociology of domination, especially charismatic authority and what happens to it when it gets routinized into bureaucracy. Don't just tell me he's on the Great Man side or the structural side, that's the boring answer. I want the specific moves. Where would he say the question is ill-formed? Where would he say it's answerable but only relative to the historian's value-laden selection of what counts as the relevant historical individual, i.e. which features of Nazi Germany are we even asking about? And where do his own commitments pull in opposite directions, charisma looking like it gives the individual leader real causal weight, routinization and bureaucracy looking like they make the individual replaceable?

Then land it. Which of the three positions does he end up nearest to, and does my load-bearing/signature distinction survive his critique, or does he say it smuggles in a binary that his account of objective possibility as a matter of degree won't allow? Also, he died before Hitler was anyone. Would he say that makes the whole counterfactual unanswerable, or would he say it's irrelevant because counterfactual history for him is about what was objectively possible given the rules we know, not about what anyone actually predicted?

No reading list, no citations, I don't need titles. Just the argument, and be honest about where you're extrapolating rather than reporting.
