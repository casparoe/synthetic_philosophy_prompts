A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection, and it gradually became clear that we disagreed less about the positions than about what a theory in that area is even supposed to deliver. So before I read anything else on it, I want the specification. Assume the view is meant to be usable in public reasoning about policy — something that has to tell a government what to do, not merely what to admire. Set out the desiderata that any adequate account of the badness of inequality, and of the extra weight given to benefiting the worse off, has to meet.

Give me the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, rather than a survey of who holds what. I want your judgment about which of the following are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one camp:

- the levelling-down cases: bringing the better off down when no one gains; a natural event that does the same; the better off choosing to come down; and the variant in which the advantage removed is partly positional, so that the better off really are made worse off by the levelling. Does the objection still run, and in what form?
- cases in which every person's level rises while the gap widens, against cases in which the gap narrows because the better off lose. Can the account treat these as evaluatively different without relying on the comparisons it says it does not need? And how does it stand on the Pareto and anonymity conditions?
- the trade-off case: a fixed benefit that would do more for someone better off than for someone worse off. The account owes me a rate of exchange and an explanation of where the rate comes from.
- scale: whether the extra weight given to the worse off survives a change in the unit of the well-being measure, and if it does not, what fixes the unit.
- continuity: whether the priority is lexically absolute, and if so, how it avoids letting a hair's-breadth gain to the worst off outweigh catastrophic losses to many others.
- ex ante versus ex post: whether priority attaches to a person's prospects before the lottery is run or to their level in the outcome, and whether one account can hold both.
- the metric: what is being compared, and how the account avoids the happy-slave and adaptive-preference embarrassments.
- the status of the claim: is the badness of inequality a reason for action, a reason for regret, or only a respect in which one outcome is better than another — and can the account concede intrinsic badness without conceding that anyone ever has reason to level down? Relatedly, say whether the deliverable is an axiology or a decision procedure, and what follows if it is only the former.
- population: what the account must say, if anything, about non-overlapping lives and about how many people there are.

Then tell me which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are optional or purpose-dependent, and — the part I care most about — which pairs cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for the conflict rather than the assertion. Finish by saying which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard views, and where exactly the gap lies in my friend's claim that anyone who thinks inequality is bad in itself must want the better off knocked down.
