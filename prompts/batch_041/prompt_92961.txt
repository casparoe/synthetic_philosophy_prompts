So I quit vaping last spring by making a bet with my brother: one hit before July and I owed him 800 bucks, and he kept the money in his own account the whole time so I couldn't weasel out of it. It worked, which annoyed me more than it should have, because I'd always assumed "deciding to do something" was the basic unit of self-control and this felt more like a con I'd run on myself. Thats the mood I was in when Marta and Dev — two people from the ethics reading group I go to on Tuesdays — got into it over the toxin puzzle at the pub afterwards, and I've been chewing on it since.

Here's the case as I understand it: a billionaire puts a vial in front of you. Drink it and you're painfully ill for a day, no lasting harm. He'll wire you a million dollars tomorrow morning if at midnight tonight you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon. You don't have to actually drink it — you're free to change your mind once the money lands — and he can somehow tell whether the intention was sincere. Marta's line was that there's no puzzle here, just a fact about intentions: you can't intend to do something you already know you won't do, same as you can't decide to believe the moon is made of cheese for cash, and wishing you could intend it is like wishing for a time machine. Dev said that's too quick, because unlike belief you can engineer the intention. Take a pill today that rewires you so that tomorrow you genuinely want to drink the stuff, and at midnight you can sincerely intend, since you know what tomorrow-you will want. Or skip the pill and sign an irrevocable side-bet with a third party, escrowed before midnight, that costs you more than a day of nausea if you don't drink — then drinking is rational tomorrow, so you can intend tonight. Marta said fine, but then you've paid for the million out of the million, and anyway the pill case is a prediction that you'll drink, not an intention to drink, and a bet you arranged so that you'd have to follow through is just the original problem shoved into the future. Dev said the machine reads the state you're in at midnight, not the story of how you got there.

Then it went somewhere I couldn't follow. Dev said the embarrassing version is the repeated one: same billionaire, same midnight check, every night for a month, and each morning after you collect you can drink or not. Night one you take the pill, intend, collect, drink, feel awful. By night five he claimed you don't need the pill anymore — you've got a track record with yourself, you know you're the kind of person who follows through, so you can intend straight, and it's free money from then on. Marta said that's exactly backwards: all you know is that a drug made you drink once, and a past compulsion isn't a present reason, so without the pill tomorrow-you still won't drink, and tonight-you still can't intend. Dev said that amounts to saying you can never bind yourself by adopting a policy, which is all a promise is; Marta said no, a promise works because another person is counting on you, and you can't be your own hostage. Dev also threw in that if the intention-reader is only 90% accurate you should obviously gamble on it, which I couldn't tell was a real point or a change of subject. Could you referee this for me, move by move — Marta's claims and Dev's claims seperately — rating each from clearly wrong to clearly right, including the two variants they invented (the pill, the month of repeats), which I don't think either of them handled fairly? I don't need a history of who said what about the puzzle; I want to know which of their moves actually work, whether the repetition dissolves the puzzle or just relocates it, and what both of them are missing about why the original case is supposed to be troubling in the first place — plus who's more right overall.
