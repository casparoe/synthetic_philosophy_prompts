I am writing up the report on a hypothetical excavation, and I need to decide what I may legitimately claim about the beliefs of the people who made the deposit. Write out the dispute for me as a dialogue between two archaeologists, each holding one of the two positions I keep being pulled between.

The deposit: a sealed, plastered pit at a site with no habitation remains within a day's walk. Inside, forty small fired-clay figurines, every one of them decapitated, all at the same point of the neck; the forty heads are laid out in a rough ring around the heap of bodies, and a single intact figurine stands at the centre. A thin ash layer lies beneath the figurines, and the pit was closed over in one action. There is nothing else in it.

The first archaeologist works in the processualist tradition. She holds that we are entitled to infer past behaviour when we can anchor it to regularities or to middle-range correlations — links between a kind of behaviour and a kind of material signature — but that beliefs are not like that: they leave no signature of their own, so wherever a behavioural or functional account of the deposit will do (deliberate decommissioning, controlled disposal, keeping the objects out of circulation), it should be preferred, and any talk of what the makers believed should be left out of the report as untestable.

The second works in the interpretive tradition. She holds that the deposit is not a heap of behaviour but a meaningful arrangement; that the contrast between the ring of severed heads and the one figure left whole is exactly the sort of structured meaning that demands interpretation rather than functional explanation; and that the processualist's demand for an independent correlate smuggles in an assumption of its own — that belief is a private inner state, rather than something enacted and public, and therefore recoverable from what was enacted.

Let them argue it out properly, answering each other's actual points. I want them to press at least these two things: whether "deliberate decommissioning" is really a belief-free hypothesis or a belief-claim in disguise; and whether the ring-and-centre arrangement can carry any interpretive weight without an independent account — ethnographic, experimental, or otherwise — of what such arrangements mean, and if it cannot, whether the appeal to ethnographic analogy rescues it or merely begs the question.

Neither should be a strawman; both should be competent, and the weaker point on either side should be conceded rather than ignored. But do not let the exchange end in "each has a point." Bring it to a definite verdict on the central question — is an inference to the makers' beliefs licensed by this deposit, yes or no — and have the side that has the better of it say why, granting what is genuinely strong in the other's case. I want reasons I can act on in the report, not a survey of the options.
