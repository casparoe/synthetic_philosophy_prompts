Two friends of mine, Ines and Karl, fell into an argument last week after Karl's account in a persistent online world was robbed: another player used an exploit to strip him of a rare item he had spent months assembling. Ines thought he should shrug it off, on the ground that nothing real had been taken; Karl thought something real had been taken. I was present for most of it and have tried to set their exchange down faithfully. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made, not a general verdict on the topic.

(1) Ines: What was taken is a pattern of bits on a company's server. You cannot hold it or cut anything with it, and if the servers were switched off tomorrow it would simply cease to exist. Real things do not wink out because a company goes bankrupt.

(2) Karl: Money is likewise nothing but entries in bank computers, and it too can vanish when a bank fails, yet we do not conclude that money is unreal. The item is real in the same way money is: its reality consists in a shared practice of treating it as valuable, and in rules that enforce that treatment.

(3) Ines: That tells against you. Money is real because it is a social institution underwritten by law and by the willingness of strangers to accept it. No one outside the game is obliged to care about your sword. What you lost is a toy, and your grievance is of the same order as losing at Monopoly.

(4) Karl: The harm does not need outsiders to ratify it. I lost months of effort and something I cared about, and the thief knew precisely that. If I strike you, the wrong lies in what it does to you, not in whether bystanders approve. Why should the distress of losing the sword be different?

(5) Ines: Because you consented to a world in which exactly this can happen. The terms of service state that items are not property, and being killed by strangers is a designed feature of the place. You cannot consent to being struck. What is really rankling you is a disappointed expectation, not a theft.

(6) Karl: On your reasoning a broken promise would be no wrong either, since no physical thing changes hands and you entered the arrangement freely — which cannot be right. And notice that the same people who insist virtual theft is nothing grow uneasy at the thought of virtual cruelty to a child; almost no one says that is permissible because it is only pixels. If pixels cannot be wronged, that unease is simply a mistake.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify the rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular, whether their disagreement rests on an ambiguity they are talking past.
