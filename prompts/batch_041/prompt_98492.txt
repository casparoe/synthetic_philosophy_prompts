I recently came across a piece in a general-audience technology magazine that summarizes Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", and I would like a careful audit of how faithful it is. The article's account, as I remember it, runs roughly as follows. Winner's answer to his own question is yes, and his central illustration is the low bridges Robert Moses had built over the Long Island parkways, deliberately designed to be too low for buses to pass beneath, so that poor and nonwhite New Yorkers would be kept away from Jones Beach. The general lesson the article draws is that a technology is political whenever its designer embeds a political agenda in its design — the bridges are politics frozen into concrete. Winner's larger claim, the article says, amounts to a version of technological determinism: once a technology such as the parkway or the automobile is in place, it dictates the social arrangements that follow, so the only real remedy is to design more democratic technologies in the first place. The article closes by observing that because the political effect of the bridges depended entirely on Moses's intentions, cases of this kind show that artifacts are political only when someone deliberately makes them so; disputes about whether a given technology is political therefore reduce to disputes about the intentions of its designers, which historians can settle by consulting the archives.

I find this persuasive, but I have a nagging sense that it flattens something, and I don't know enough about the essay to say where. Please go through the summary clause by clause and tell me which parts a careful reader of Winner would accept, which are standard popularizations that survive with qualification, and which Winner explicitly denied. In particular, I want to know whether his thesis really reduces to a claim about designers' intentions; whether "political," as he uses it, is meant to cover artifacts generally or only certain ones; whether it is fair to file him under technological determinism; and whether the article's closing inference about intentions and the archives still stands once the rest of the summary is corrected. Where the summary conflates two things the essay keeps apart, or converts a modest claim into a sweeping one, say so plainly, and indicate what a defender of Winner would put in its place. I am asking for an assessment of the summary, not a bibliography or a reading list.
