Ok, so I'm 68, retired four years now. I spent my whole career in precision measurement — I ran a metrology lab for the last decade of it — and since I stopped working I've been reading philosphy, mostly epistemology, and I've hit the regress problem and I can't shake the feeling that I've already had this arguement, just in a different room.

Here's what I want from you. Take the three standard answers to the regress — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — and restate each one in the vocabulary I actually used for thirty-odd years. That means calibration hierarchies and traceability, primary standards and how they get to be primary, working standards, the fact that my instrument was never better than the chain above it, uncertainty budgets and error propagation, and the global least-squares adjustments we'd run when we had a redundant network of measurements that all had to sit consistently with each other. Say what each position looks like when you put it that way, and be honest — don't just dress up the philosophy in lab words and call it a translation.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue. What doesn't survive the trip?

First, when I trace a measurement up the chain it always bottoms out in something that isn't justified at all, it's just defined — a committee says the second is however many oscillations of a caesium atom, and from then on that's the answer. Is that foundationalism, or is it a dodge a foundationalist wouldn't accept? I never felt I was standing on a foundation. I felt I was standing on an agreement.

Second, the coherentist story is basically a redundant measurement network, right? Everything checked against everything else, nothing privileged, residuals telling you whether the whole thing hangs together. But we always had to fix at least one thing — you can't solve the adjustment without anchoring the scale somewhere, or you get a whole family of solutions that fit equally well. If that's right, is coherentism in metrology impossible without smuggling in a foundation, and is that a fact about knowledge or just a fact about my linear algebra?

Third, infinitism. I can always ask who calibrated the calibrator, and in practice the honest answer is that it stops because at some point we stop asking. That's not the same as the chain being infinite. Does the infinitist have anything to say to me, or is infinitism just an accurate description of a chain before somebody declares a top?

Then the big one. My gut says the epistemologists are arguing about reasons and beliefs — normative stuff, what you're entitled to think — while I was dealing with procedures and instruments, which is a causal story, not a normative one. If that's right, is the residue a defect in my vocabulary, i.e. I simply can't say the normative thing, or a sign that the regress problem is really two problems wearing one coat? I'd rather you tell me it's the first, if it is.

Last thing, and this is the bit I'd really like you to engage with. In the lab the clean chains were never the interesting ones. The interesting ones were where two measurements disagreed and we had to decide which one to distrust. The friction was the only place anything pushed back. Do any of the three positions account for that — that the warrant comes from the friction rather than from the bottom or from the whole? If none of them do, say so, and tell me whether that's a real gap in the debate or just something I've reinvented badly on my own.
