I retired about two years ago after 34 years in geochemistry, and I've been spending my new free time on philosophy, mostly 18th century moral stuff. Right now I'm stuck on Adam Smith and empire, and I'd like help figuring out what's actually worth thinking about rather than what's merely fun to read.

Here's what got me going. Everyone knows the Smith who says the colonies were a bad deal for Britain: the monopoly of colonial trade enriches a handful of merchants and planters at the expense of nearly everyone else, and the empire costs more to defend than it returns. That's an accounting argument. But there's also a Smith who treats the colonial monopoly as an injustice, as an infringement of rights that people hold as such, including the colonists' right to trade with whoever they please and to govern and tax themselves, and he contrasts the ancient Greek colony, which he pictures as a child that has grown up and moved out, owing gratitude but owing no obedience, with the Roman colony, which he pictures as something like a garrison planted to keep a newly conquered province in line. So there seem to be two ledgers running at once, a prudence/accounting ledger and a rights/justice ledger. What I can't decide is whether these are two seperate arguments that could come apart (empire profitable but unjust, or just but unprofitable) or whether in Smith the rights talk is probly just decoration on the accounting, or the other way round.

I'm also bothered about who counts as injured. In Smith's telling the losers seem to be home consumers and the colonists denied free trade and self-taxation. But the people who lose most are the ones already living where the colonists landed, and I don't know whether Smith's theory of justice, which looks to me mostly about property and contract and injury to persons, can even register their claims, or whether his story about cultivating and improving land quietly undercuts them.

So here's my ask. Say a small group of us, five or six people, mostly philosophers, a couple of economists, no archival historians, nobody who reads manuscript sources, one semester of part time attention. Which open questions about Smith on empire and colonies are most worth working on, and in what order? I want the reasoning more than the list. For each: is it upstream of other questions, i.e. would settling it change how we read the rest of his moral philosophy or only a footnote; is it tractable for people like us without archives; is it neglected; and would the answer change anything downstream, either for reading Smith as a systematic thinker or for live arguments about free trade, decolonization, or reparations for dispossession. Where those criteria pull against each other, say so and tell me which one you'd sacrifice.

Please don't hand me a reading list, I can't check it and it isn't what I need. And tell me where you're least sure: what argument or piece of evidence would flip your ranking, and which step in your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
