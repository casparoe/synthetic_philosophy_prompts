I teach 10th grade biology and I just had one of those classes where the whole lesson comes apart. We were doing the characteristics of life -- organization, metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, homeostasis -- and a kid asks whether a virus is alive, and I say no, and then another kid points out that mules cant reproduce and nobody says a mule isnt alive, and I hear myself saying "well, its a list, not a checklist," which is not a thing. So I've spent two weeks reading about this and I've landed somewhere, and now I want you to try to knock it down.

Where I've landed: reproduction is a red herring. The real criterion is self-maintenance. A thing is alive if it's a chemical system that keeps itself going -- takes in energy and matter and uses them to repair and rebuild itself, holds itself away from equilibrium, and does it with its own machinery. On that view viruses are out, prions are out, and a seed or a spore or a frozen embryo is in, because the machinery is all there and would run given the right conditions. What I like is that it makes "alive" about what a thing does or would do on its own, rather than about whether it happens to sit in a lineage that continues.

So what would count against it? I want the cases where my definition forces an answer I'd have to swallow, the rival definitions that handle those cases better, and which of these I'd have to actually concede vs. which I could wave off -- sorted by how much they should move me. Things people have thrown at me that I dont know what to do with:

- a flame is self-maintaining too, and a corpse is still chemically busy and still nowhere near equilibrium, so how does my criterion separate maintaining from just happening?
- there are bacteria that can't survive outside a host cell at all, and everyone calls them alive, so how much work is the "self" in self-maintenance actually doing?
- a friend says I'm asking the wrong question, because life is a property of lineages rather than of individual things -- does that solve anything or just relocate the problem?
- another friend says the word is a tool, and that astrobiologists, doctors and lawyers need different things from it, so there's no fact of the matter -- real position or a dodge? (if that one's right I think I'm sunk)
- and the one that actually keeps me up: a dormant seed is "not doing anything but has the machinery," a virus particle is "not doing anything and doesn't have the machinery." Is that asymmetry as clean as I think, or am I just drawing the line where I want it?

Also tell me if I've built something that nothing could count against, and whether that's a problem for a definition of this kind. And say how confident you are in what you claim, and flag the one point you're least sure about.
