Ok so I write a column on science and ideas, and I've been trying to get my head around the Bernard Williams objection to utilitarianism — the drowning wife case, Jim and the Indians, the general gripe that impartial morality makes you have one thought too many at exactly the moment you shouldn't be thinking at all. I want to actually write an essay on this, with a verdict at the end, not a both-sides-is-fine thing. No reading list needed, I know the usual suspects.

Here's the thing that's bugging me and that I want the essay to be about. A philosopher friend claims the whole debate conflates two seperate complaints, and that you have to pick which one you're running:

(a) impartial morality demands an *extra* thought — a counting/justifying step — and that could in principle be removed, in which case it's a complaint about bad deliberation habits, not about the morality itself; versus

(b) it gives the *wrong ground* for the action, so no amount of "just don't deliberate that way" fixes it, because whatever you tell yourself in the moment, the justifying reason still has to be the same one underneath.

I think these really do come apart and I want you to say which one Williams is actually running, and then whether utilitarianism (or any impartialist view) can be repaired by the route in (a). Two further cases I keep chewing on and want folded in: does the objection have any bite against someone whose deepest project *is* being impartial — the hardcore effective altruist who sincerely wants to be the kind of person who saves the stranger — or does the objection presuppose that your ground projects are partial, which would make it question-begging? And what about when the loved one themself would want you to save the stranger — does that dissolve the complaint, or just relocate it?

So: an essay that stakes out a position and defends it. Don't hedge into "both readings capture something true." And at the end, be honest about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would actually change your mind — which argument or example, going the other way, would flip the verdict.
