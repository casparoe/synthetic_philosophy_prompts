Alright, I want you to actually argue for a position here, not hand me a survey of views. Here's the setup and the thing I want you to defend.

Everyone in animal and AI welfare talk does the same move: "sentience comes in degrees, so moral status comes in degrees" — insect low, fish higher, octopus higher, thermostat at zero, done. I think that move quietly fuses two quantities that need to be kept apart, and I want you to argue that they have to be pulled apart. The two quantities are (a) how sentient a thing actually is, i.e. the richness and valence of its inner life if it has one at all, and (b) how confident we are that it's sentient in the first place. Argue that what should drive moral status is expected moral value over our credences — the probability side — and that most people who announce "sentience is a continuum" are really running the richness side while pretending it's the probability side. Then show me what the corrected picture does to verdicts, and defend it.

Specifically:
- Take an insect that you're, say, 85% sure has some faint but real experience, and a system you're only 5% sure is richly sentient. Are these morally on par? Argue yes or no and say what that does to the "ladder from insects to thermostats" picture.
- Does your view make a thermostat's moral status nonzero, since presumably you can't rule it out with absolute certainty? Is that a reductio of the view or a bullet you should bite? Argue it.
- Somebody will say credences about sentience are hopelessly underdetermined, so the whole apparatus is rigor theater on top of guessing. Give that objection its strongest form and say honestly whether it sinks the position.
- Also say what happens to your own position if sentience turns out to be an all-or-nothing property that we merely can't detect in degrees — does the argument survive?

Write it as an argument with a clear verdict, not a both-sides review, and don't bother with citations — I know you can't look things up, I want the reasoning.
