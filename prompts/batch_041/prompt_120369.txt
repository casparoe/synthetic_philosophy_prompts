my old dissertation advisor died in january and i'm giving the eulogy at her memorial in three weeks. i've been trying to write it for a month and it isn't working. i need an actual speech, not a essay about her.

heres what you need to know. she spent forty years arguing that moral talk isn't description — that when you say something is wrong you aren't reporting a fact about the world, you're doing something more like booing. she taught me that in my second year and i still think she was right.

and here's my problem. her brother is speaking right before me and he's going to say, in front of everyone, that she was wrong to spend the last of the family money on the tuesday night thing she ran at the county jail, knowing she was sick and knowing her nieces were counting on that money. i want to say he's wrong about that. not that's one view, actually wrong. but every sentence i write turns into a sentence with the moral words buried in the middle of it. if it was wrong for her to do that then the rest of us were wrong to let her do it by herself, and i'm not going to say that from a pulpit. or: it isn't true that she was wrong — and then i hear myself and i don't know what i've just done, because i don't believe there was a fact she could have gotten right. and if i just say, well, some of us see it one way and some see it another, i've thrown the whole eulogy away, and i've said something she'd have spent her life arguing against.

(this is the frege-geach problem, i know. knowing the name has never once helped me write a sentence.)

the room is going to be about sixty people. her brothers and sisters and their kids, who have never read a page of philosophy and mostly want to cry and go home. and maybe fifteen of her former students, who will hear it instantly if i cheat.

so: write the eulogy. four minutes, out loud, warm. it has to be kind to the people in the room who aren't philosophers and it can't turn into a lecture, but the disagreement has to be in there, and it has to be in there honestly given what she believed. if the only way to make it work is to quietly upgrade her view into something more realist, then do it, but do it out loud and own it.

don't hand me options or two drafts. pick the reading of her position you think can actually carry a eulogy, commit to it, and write the whole thing that way. then, in a short note at the end, tell me what you decided about the buried sentences — the if/then one and the not one — and where the speech leans on a reading of expressivism that her colleagues would fight me about. i'd rather have a speech with a spine that takes a contested line than a safe one that works no matter what.
