I serve as a policy analyst to a cross-party commission reviewing our country's system of welfare conditionality — benefit sanctions, work-search requirements, and time limits on unemployment support — and one phrase has been doing an extraordinary amount of argumentative work in our hearings: a hand up, not a handout. The chair invokes it to justify tightening sanctions on claimants who decline job offers; one member cites it to argue for a state job guarantee; a charity witness used it to defend unconditional cash transfers, on the grounds that what makes assistance a hand up is the security and dignity it preserves rather than any condition attached to it. The slogan is being asked to settle questions it cannot settle by itself, and I need to know exactly what it commits its users to before we draft our recommendations.

Please give me a careful disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making as a menu I can circulate to the commission. For each reading, state the thesis exactly, and say whether it is trivial (something virtually no one would deny) or substantive, whether it is a conceptual claim, an empirical or behavioural one, or a normative one, and whether it is defensible or false. Then, for each of the three uses above, identify the reading the speaker must be relying on if the slogan is to support their conclusion, and say whether it actually does so — in particular, whether any defensible reading licenses the inference from the slogan to tighter sanctions. Finish by telling me which reading you take most people to mean when they repeat the phrase, which readings quietly presuppose the older distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor, and whether any reading survives once that distinction is rejected. A numbered list with short verdicts, please, rather than an essay.
