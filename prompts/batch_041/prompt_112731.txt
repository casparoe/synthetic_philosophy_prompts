I have spent most of the past year reporting on the naturalistic study of religion — the cognitive psychologists, evolutionary theorists and behavioural economists who try to account for religious belief and practice without invoking anything supernatural. The series is nearly finished, and I have run into a problem that is mine rather than theirs. Granting one of their explanations for the sake of argument, when should it cost a religious belief anything? I have firm verdicts on individual cases and no principle that survives contact with more than two of them.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. Agency over-detection. Our minds are quick to posit agents, and the faculty we use to read other minds runs even where there is no mind to read, so a world populated by disembodied agents is one we generate cheaply. My verdict: this costs nothing. It describes the kind of animal we are, not whether any of those agents exists.

2. Transmission. The religious ideas that survive are the ones that break our expectations once or twice — a person without a body, a statue that hears — because those are the ones people remember and pass along, while ideas that break expectations wholesale are dropped. My verdict: nothing. A fact about memory is not a fact about the world.

3. Operative concepts. Asked about God under time pressure, or while distracted, believers answer as though he were a person with ordinary perceptual limits; the unrestricted theology surfaces only in slow, deliberate answers. My verdict: nothing against the theology, though it tells me that the theology is doing less work in ordinary religious life than its defenders suppose.

4. Death. Belief in an afterlife survives because it defuses the terror of death; where that terror is defused by other means, the belief thins out. My verdict: nothing. I want it to be true, and that is a fact about me rather than about what happens when I die.

5. Social payoff. Belief in a god who watches what you do and punishes it spread because communities that held it cooperated more effectively and outlasted their neighbours; the content of the belief is what it is because that content, and not some other, produced the cooperation. My verdict: this one does cost something. It makes the doctrine look like a piece of social technology that a group stumbled into rather than something anyone found out.

6. An experience. A woman reports that after a night of drumming and fasting she was in the presence of something. The procedure reliably produces that sense of presence in people who expected nothing. My verdict: it costs her report something, since a feeling that can be produced on demand is not a detector, though I would not extend that to every claim of contact.

7. The case that undoes me. Take case 5 and suppose that the doctrine is also true, and that I have independent grounds for it that I accept. My verdict flips: the very same explanation now costs nothing. If the belief is true and I have other reasons for it, the fact that it also did social work for the groups that held it seems to me no more troubling than the fact that the germ theory of disease spread because doctors who believed it cured more patients.

The principle I would like to hold is roughly this: an explanation counts against a belief when it shows the belief's content to have been fixed by something other than the truth, and counts for nothing when it shows only why the belief is easy for us to form, hold or pass on. That principle gets 1, 2, 3 and 5 right. It makes 4 corrosive, which I resist, and it makes 6 inert, which I also resist — and it has nothing to say about why 7 flips.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be checked against these cases; the case that forces each clause; your verdicts on at least one further case of your own choosing; and an honest account of which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them you would overrule.
