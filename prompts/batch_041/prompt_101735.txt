I moved to a new city about three months ago, and last week I ended up listening to two friends, Rana and Devi, spend most of a dinner going round and round about how much of the local etiquette I am actually bound by. The question is not academic for me, but I kept out of it — they were arguing with each other, not with me — and I would now like it settled properly.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. The three things in dispute are the ones I keep getting wrong: leaving a tip at the café (where a service charge is added automatically but a cash tip is still expected), queueing for the tram (a loose line, with the local custom that you may leave your place to sit down or buy a ticket and reclaim it by asking who is last), and crossing the road on a red light, which is against the law here but which everybody, locals included, does the moment the road looks clear.

Rana opened by saying that I am obliged to leave the tip, to hold my place in the queue properly, and to wait for the light rather than crossing on red with everyone else. Her reason was the familiar one: when you are a guest somewhere, you follow the house rules.

Devi said the three cases are not alike and cannot be covered by one slogan. The tip moves money to a worker who is paid on the assumption that she will receive it, so that is a question of what she is owed. Queueing and waiting for the light are, in her words, pure coordination — any workable arrangement would do, and the local one is simply what everyone happens to have settled into. She said Rana was treating a coordination equilibrium as though it were a moral requirement.

Rana replied that the difference Devi was drawing matters less than she thinks. Each of the three conventions, whatever its origin, is something other people rely on: the café worker counts on the tip as part of her wage; the queue works because each person keeps their place; the drivers at the crossing rely on pedestrians not to step out. On Rana's account the obligation is not owed to the convention as such but to the people whose expectations it has stabilized — you wrong them by defeating a reliance you knew they had. She added that there is also a force to the done thing itself: once a practice is firmly entrenched, the mere fact that it is what is done here becomes a reason for a newcomer, quite apart from whether the practice is a good one.

Devi said that if reliance is the test it proves too much and too little at once. Look at the crossing: nobody relies on pedestrians waiting, because the standing local practice is that everyone crosses when the road is clear and the drivers know it perfectly well. So by Rana's own standard I wrong no one by crossing on red, since the convention that actually operates here is the practice, not the statute. And that undercuts the slogan she began with: if what binds the guest is the local practice rather than the official rule, then the rule about following local rules tells me to cross on red along with everybody else. As for the "force of the done thing," Devi said that this is just the force of the majority's preferences, and a preference is not an obligation.

Rana answered that this misses the asymmetry between hosts and guests. Locals get to live by the practice; the visitor is held to the rule. Nobody minds when a local crosses on red, but people notice and resent it when a foreigner does. So the norm that applies to me is not the locals' actual behaviour but the standard the locals apply to outsiders — and those are two different things. There is, she said, a convention about how strictly visitors are held to conventions, and that second-order convention is what binds me.

Devi said that if the binding norm is simply whatever the locals will resent me for ignoring, then we have stopped talking about morality and started talking about prudence. Avoiding resentment is a reason, but it is not the kind of reason that makes an action obligatory; it makes it advisable. Rana said the two cannot be pulled apart that cleanly, because part of what it is to wrong someone is to disregard a legitimate expectation they hold of you, and the locals' expectation that a guest will be scrupulous is not obviously illegitimate.

They left it there, and neither convinced the other. I would like a real adjudication rather than a verdict on who "won." Please go through the exchange move by move, saying where each of them is right and where each overreaches, and then tell me what both of them are missing. I am especially interested in three things: (a) whether the three conventions really do fall into different categories, or whether Rana is right that a single reliance-based account covers all of them; (b) whether the host/guest asymmetry she raises is a genuine feature of obligation or a piece of special pleading on the locals' behalf; and (c) whether Devi's prudence objection is fatal to Rana's position or merely a boundary that any account of convention-based obligation has to draw. If it helps you keep the thread, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one-line justification for each rating.
