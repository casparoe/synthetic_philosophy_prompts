I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am putting together a feature on the afterlife of one very old anecdote: Zhuangzi, having lost his wife, is found by a friend singing and drumming on a tub, and explains that he wept at first but then considered how she had come out of and returned to a larger transformation, of which the turning of the seasons is the image, at which point he stopped. My sources — grief writers, celebrants, a couple of palliative-care physicians — use the story to argue that mourning is a failure of understanding and that death is not an evil; I have seen it pressed into service for the claims that funerals should be cheerful, that prolonged grief is a category error, and that fear of death dissolves once you accept that you are part of nature.

What I want from you is not an essay on Zhuangzi but an audit of the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first: what stands in for the seasons, for the transformation, for the chamber where the body lies, for the survivor, and for the standpoint from which the whole process is judged. Then show me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in three places: whether the seasonal image quietly imports a cyclical return that a secular reader has not paid for, whether it supplies an impersonal evaluator whose verdicts need not match the mourner's, and whether the weeping at the beginning of the story is a stage within the view or an embarrassment to it. Separate the disanalogies that bear on the practical conclusion people actually draw — that grief is irrational — from the ones that are merely atmospheric, and tell me whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table. I would also like to know how much of the argument depends on the cosmology the popular retelling has dropped, and what remains of it once the cosmology is gone.

Finally, bring in the awkward neighbours. The same book contains the skull episode, in which the dead describe their condition as the better one and decline the offer of a return to life, and the account of Zhuangzi's own instructions about his burial, where he declines to have one set of scavengers robbed to feed another. Say what those do to the reading my sources are running. And if the seasons picture cannot carry the weight, propose a better analogy for what the story is actually up to — one that preserves the unknowability of the far side of death instead of pretending to view it from outside.
