I've been defending something I call the dimmer-switch view of moral status and I'd like you to try to break it. Here's the view. Sentience isn't a yes/no property; it comes in degrees. Moral status is a monotone function of that degree and nothing else — more sentience, more status, no other inputs — and the scale is smooth: for any two systems that differ in how sentient they are, there's a possible system in between, so there's no sharp line anywhere, whether between a bee and a human or between a thermostat and a bee. I want to fix the degree itself by something like the number of independent valenced channels a system can integrate at once and how finely it can discriminate within them — roughly, how many distinguishable ways it can be doing well or badly, and how sharply it can tell them apart. Thermostats come out just above zero. Epsilon status, not none. My main reason for the smoothness part is that every cutoff I try to draw looks arbitrary, and I can always imagine a small tweak that nudges a system just barely across it.

So: what would count against this? Not just the obvious "thermostats don't matter, therefore you're wrong" move — I want the real defeater map. A few things I already suspect are in there and want you to deal with rather than skip: the chain argument that walks from a bee down to a thermostat in small steps, where either each step keeps some status and I end up a panpsychist, or some step hits zero and I've got a threshold after all; the possibility that having experience at all is binary even if the *content* of experience is graded, so that I've conflated two different scales; cases where richness and valence come apart, like a system with enormously rich experience but nothing it can do well or badly, versus one stuck in a single steady dull ache forever; whether the trade-offs the view seems to license need cardinal comparability rather than just a ranking, and what's left of the view if only the ranking survives; whether moral status might have inputs other than sentience — relationships, agency, membership in a community; and the aggregation mess, where billions of epsilon-status things might outweigh one high-status thing and I have to either accept that or sneak a threshold back in.

For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where the view forces me to say something about a creature that actually exists and I can't stomach it — or a demonstration that I can't ever be forced to say anything in particular, which would be its own kind of bad news.
