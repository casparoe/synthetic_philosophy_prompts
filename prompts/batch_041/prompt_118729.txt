I am an illustrator designing a fictional alphabet for a long graphic novel, and I have run into a problem I did not anticipate. The conceit is that the book's world writes in a script the reader has never seen, but the reader must be able to follow it well enough to read short passages — signs, names, a few lines of a letter — without a key. Every glyph therefore has to read as a letter of our own alphabet: an 'a' must be an 'a' to someone who has never seen this script, while looking unlike any 'a' they have seen. I assumed this would be a pleasant puzzle. It has become a year of arguing with myself and with my editor.

My working method, adopted for the sake of consistency: all twenty-six glyphs are assembled from the same small inventory of strokes placed on the same fixed grid, and any transformation I apply to one glyph I must apply to its relatives — whatever I do to turn 'b' into 'd' I must also do to turn 'p' into 'q'; whatever distinguishes 'a' from 'o' at one weight must distinguish them at the next. This keeps the script coherent and makes new glyphs almost write themselves. The difficulty is 'a'. To get an 'a' that satisfies the system I must either smuggle in a stroke no other glyph uses or break one of the transformation rules, and the result is a shape that is perfectly consistent and no longer looks like an 'a'.

My editor's position is that if a reader needs the surrounding context, or a key, or a moment's hesitation, then it is not an 'a': the glyph has failed, and the system, not the reader, should give way. My own view, when I am being honest, is that this cannot be the whole story either — every 'a' a reader has ever met is already a different shape (roman, italic, blackletter, a child's scrawl, a digital face), and they read them all. Whatever makes them all 'a' is not any particular arrangement of strokes. But then I cannot say what it is, which leaves me unable to tell when my system should bend and when my glyph is simply wrong.

I would like help with the practical question and with the one underneath it. Practically: when a glyph fights the system that generated it, which should give way — the glyph, the system, or my rules for deriving one glyph from another? And conceptually: what is doing the work when a reader recognizes a shape as an 'a'? Is there an invariant form I should be preserving, or is an 'a' constituted by its relations to the other letters, by what it is not, or by the reader's expectations and the conventions of the script as a whole? If the answer is the last, I would like to know how much of the alphabet can be redesigned before the reader's expectations break — how much of an 'a', in other words, is the letter itself and how much is the company it keeps.
