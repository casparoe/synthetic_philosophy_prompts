My friend Rosa has a problem that I have somehow been drafted into, and I keep giving her bad advice, so I'm outsourcing it.

Here's the situation. She's got a free summer for the first time in years — no teaching, a bit of savings — and she's trying to decide between two things to do with it. Option one: train for a hundred-mile race in October. Option two: stay home, learn to bake bread, read novels on the porch, see her friends. She has done the race thing before. She describes the training as nine months of misery — shin splints, 5am alarms, hating everyone — and also says it was the best thing she's ever done and she'd sign up again tomorrow. The porch option she describes as "fine, genuinely nice, no notes."

She keeps trying to settle it by asking which one would give her more pleasure, and that's where she gets stuck, because she says she can't tell whether the two things are even the same kind of thing. Then she started listing, and it got worse. She loves the smell of gasoline. She loves the burn of hot sauce and the ache after a long hike. She loves being scared on rollercoasters. She loves — and is a little ashamed of loving — the jolt she gets when a colleague she dislikes embarrasses himself in a meeting. She loves the moment a proof finally works, which she says feels like nothing else in her life. Put all of those side by side and there's no common ingredient; they don't feel alike at all. So "do whatever gives you more pleasure" either tells her nothing, or it just means "do what you like," which is the same sentence again.

She also read somewhere that the brain system that makes you chase a thing is seperate from the one that makes you like it, and now she's worried that she's been chasing the race because of the chase, not because of anything she'll actually enjoy when she gets there. And there's one more wrinkle. When she asks herself whether she enjoyed last year's race, the honest answer is that she was miserable for most of it and only started calling it wonderful about a month afterward. She doesn't know which report to trust — the one from the person who was there, or the one from the person remembering it.

I've read enough to know there's a real problem buried in all this, and I keep half-answering it and then trailing off. What I want from you: (1) tell me what the actual problem is, in its general shape — is this a problem with Rosa or with the idea that pleasure is one thing at all? (2) walk me through the honest ways of rescuing the idea that pleasure is one thing, what each one costs, and which you'd bet on; and (3) then actually help me advise her, because she has to decide by Friday and I don't want to hand her a slogan. Don't give me a reading list, I've got the books — I want the shape of the thing and something I can say to her.
