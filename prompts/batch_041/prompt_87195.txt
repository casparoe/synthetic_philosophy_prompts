I am a retired geologist — thirty years in sedimentary stratigraphy and basin analysis — and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of science on my own, chiefly Popper and the demarcation literature. I have the chance to spend six months as a visiting affiliate in a philosophy department, with roughly twelve to fifteen hours a week to give to one project, and I need a research proposal of about two thousand words to submit with the affiliation request. Please write that proposal.

The subject is Popper's treatment of Darwinian natural selection. I know the received story and do not want it retold: he called Darwinism a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory, on the ground that the principle of the survival of the fittest, with fitness cashed out as actual survival, admits of no possible falsifier; biologists and philosophers of biology objected; and in the later 1970s he partly withdrew, conceding that natural selection can be given formulations that are testable. What interests me is that this story leaves two things unresolved, and I suspect they are really one thing.

First, at least two quite different complaints can travel under the heading of unfalsifiability, and Popper's discussion slides between them. One is that the principle has no empirical content: no conceivable observation counts against it. The other is that the evidence base on which the principle would be tested is itself a product of the process being tested — the lineages that failed are, by that very fact, largely absent from the record, so that what survives to be observed is a biased sample of what occurred. The first complaint, if sound, is repaired by reformulating the theory, which is roughly what Popper eventually said had been done. The second is not obviously repaired by any reformulation, because it is a point about the structure of the evidence rather than the content of the hypothesis. Having spent a career on a record that is fragmentary and non-randomly preserved, I find the second complaint more interesting than the first and do not think it has been given its due.

Second, Popper himself treated the resemblance between Darwinian explanation and what he called situational logic as a point in Darwinism's favour, even while calling it metaphysical. A situational explanation accounts for an outcome by reconstructing the agents' situation and aims and showing that the outcome was what one should have expected, without deriving it from a law plus initial conditions. In the social sciences he took such explanations to be genuinely explanatory and genuinely criticisable — criticisable by asking whether the situation was described fully enough to exclude the alternatives that were live at the time. I want to know whether that same standard can be turned on selection explanations, and whether doing so dissolves the unfalsifiability charge or sharpens it.

So the question I would like the proposal to state precisely, and to argue is both open and answerable in six months, is roughly this: once the tautology charge is set aside, does anything of Popper's verdict on Darwinism survive — and is whatever survives better characterised as a defect in the theory's content or as a defect in the evidential situation in which the theory is assessed?

The proposal should do the following. State the question so that it has a determinate answer. Explain why the standard positions — Popper's original verdict, his critics', and his own later concession — do not settle it, in terms of arguments rather than citations. Set out what I would actually do, month by month, and why this approach rather than the obvious one of simply adjudicating whether Popper got the science right; I want to avoid that, both because it has been done and because I am not competent to do it at the level the professionals do. Specify a deliverable I can genuinely produce with my background: a table of four to six test cases, real or constructed, which the two readings of the unfalsifiability charge would classify differently, with an argument for each classification. Say what would count as the project succeeding, what would count as failing, and which outcome is most likely. And give an honest assessment of scope — whether this is a six-month project or a three-year one in disguise.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list; I will supply those myself. Assume the reader is a philosopher of science who will want to know why this is not a hobbyist's rehash.
