I am a high school student, and I am preparing a piece for my school's philosophy club in which I stage a dialogue between two people who disagree about Simpson's paradox. I would like you to write that dialogue. Neither speaker should be a strawman; each should be as sharp as the other, and each should answer the other's actual points rather than restate their own position.

To keep the exchange concrete, I have invented the following data, which I would like the speakers to argue over. A school is deciding between two methods of tutoring, A and B, for an end-of-year exam, and its students are divided by their prior grades into a strong group and a weak group. Of the students taught by Method A, 18 of 20 strong students passed and 16 of 80 weak students passed. Of the students taught by Method B, 68 of 80 strong students passed and 3 of 20 weak students passed.

The two speakers hold opposing views about what these numbers show. One holds that Method A is simply the better method: it has the higher pass rate within each group of students, and the pooled figure that favors B is nothing but an artifact of the two methods having been given to different mixes of students. The other holds that this confidence is misplaced: which comparison deserves to be called the effect of the method depends on assumptions the numbers themselves cannot supply, and a comparison that looks like the sober, disaggregated one can turn out to be the misleading one when the variable you split on is itself downstream of the thing you are studying.

I would like the dialogue to work through the following. First, what each speaker thinks the pooled comparison and the subgroup comparisons are actually measuring, and whether the two of them are even answering the same question. Second, whether there is a fact of the matter about which method works that is independent of any causal model the reasoner brings to the table. Third, at least one further case, invented by the speakers themselves, in which a reversal of this kind occurs and it is the pooled comparison, not the subgroup one, that a careful reasoner should trust. Finally, I would like each of them to say plainly, before the dialogue ends, whether they think they have been disagreeing about the data, about the meaning of the word effect, or about something else, and whether anything the other said should change their mind.

Please do not include citations, sources, or a reading list; I want the two of them to reason it out from the case in front of them.
