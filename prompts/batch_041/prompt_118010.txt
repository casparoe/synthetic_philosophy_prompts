For twenty years the hollow at the bottom of my land has been fenced off and left alone. My mother farmed the rest of the eleven acres badly and then gave up on it, and the hollow — a spring-fed dip that has silted into a small marsh — became the one thing about the property I was proud of. In June it is loud with frogs and dragonflies, and a heron my mother used to watch from the kitchen window still comes at dawn. I had assumed that leaving it wild was the one unambiguous good I had done with the place.

A colleague who studies freshwater insects walked it with me last month and took the pride out of it. Her argument was this. The marsh is not a refuge but an engine: every spring it manufactures hundreds of thousands of eggs, tadpoles, and nestlings, and nearly all of them die within weeks, by starvation, exposure, or by being eaten while still alive. The heron eats well precisely because of the glut of doomed young. On her account the arithmetic only gets worse the more fertile the ground is, since fertility shows up as lives begun rather than lives completed. If I cared about the creatures rather than about the number of creatures, she said, I would drain the hollow, put all eleven acres into rotation, and run a dozen well-fed cattle on it: far fewer lives, and most of them good ones. When I objected that the cattle would end at the abattoir, she said that a beef animal that lives well for two years and dies in a slaughterhouse is still, by the arithmetic, better off than ten thousand mayflies that drown in a puddle.

I do not know how to answer her, and I do not trust my own reaction. Part of me thinks she has mistaken a wetland for a ledger. Part of me thinks she has merely followed my own convictions further than I was willing to follow them. And part of me suspects that we are both guessing about the inner lives of animals that live for a day — and about the heron, which is the real reason I want to keep the water.

I have to decide what to do with the hollow before the autumn, and I am not asking whether the question is hard; I know it is. I am asking what to do. Please advise me in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing paragraph.
