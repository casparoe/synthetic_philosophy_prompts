One 90-minute session, about 20 students, upper-level seminar on constitutional theory. I want a runnable simulation that makes them feel the countermajoritarian objection in their gut, then makes them feel the strongest reply to it -- that a legislature can be trusted to police itself -- and then shows them why that reply is shakier than it looks. Setup I've half-built, improve it as you like: a fictional country, a legislature made of most of the group, a court of five, and a short fictional constitution that you draft (I need one clause abstract enough to be genuinely arguable -- something about security, something about liberty). Four rounds, each with an escalating crisis you supply as event cards; the legislature passes an emergency law, the court reviews it. Scoring, no real money: legislators live on approval points from a public-opinion track I move on the board and lose points when their district's interest is hurt; justices score on consistency with their own earlier reasoning and on not being overruled. Rounds 1-2 have no override available, and I want the mechanics tuned so the court strikes down something roughly 70% of the room wants, so they feel the "five unelected people just decided for all of us and there's no recourse" thing. In round 3 I hand the legislature a notwithstanding-style override (the kind Canada has) -- they can re-enact the struck law over the court, at a cost. I want the rules arranged so it's genuinely unclear whether they should, and so the result is a real coin-flip between "they never dare use it, so weak-form review quietly becomes strong review" and "they use it and immediately hate themselves." Give me roles, materials, the opinion track and payoff sheet, the round-by-round timeline with event cards, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the difference between strong-form and weak-form review, onto the point that courts have neither purse nor sword (so a defiant legislature can simply not implement), and onto judicial minimalism -- the thought that a court can decide less than its asked to. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (my bet: the justices just vote their politics and the legislature never feels constrained), how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- I assume it can't reproduce the professional norm of fidelity to law that does most of the work in real judging, but say if I'm wrong.
