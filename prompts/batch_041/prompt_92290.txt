For the past couple of years, the way I try to settle real disagreements with friends is the crux-finding exercise I first picked up in rationality workshops: rather than argue the object-level question, each of us names the sub-question we think the disagreement actually turns on, says what evidence would move us on it, and we iterate until we reach a claim where one person says "if I saw X I'd change my mind" and the other says "fine, and X is exactly what I'd expect either way." It has been genuinely useful, and I have recommended it to other people, but I have started to notice something about it that I would like you to help me think through. The technique works beautifully as long as both parties already share a large, unstated background: that there are facts independent of our wishes, that perception and memory are broadly reliable, that logic constrains what we may believe, that our words mean roughly the same thing to both of us, and that a certain kind of observation counts as evidence at all. Each of those is a claim of exactly the sort the procedure tells us to treat as a crux — something that could in principle be true or false, something we should be willing to put a probability on and revise. In practice, though, we never put them on the table; we treat them as the board the game is played on rather than pieces on it. And on the few occasions when I have tried, half-jokingly, to run the procedure on them — what evidence would move you on whether your memory is reliable, or on whether a contradiction really is a reliable sign of impossibility — the reply is not a crux. It is that I have stopped playing the game, or that the question is malformed, or that someone who doubts this much cannot be reasoned with at all. Meanwhile the practice itself is defended, when it is defended at all, by saying that it works, which strikes me as a different kind of move again.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of what theory of knowledge this practice presupposes. My own suspicion is that it is some version of the Wittgensteinian picture in On Certainty — that certain propositions function as hinges, or as the riverbed within which inquiry flows, and are exempt from doubt not because we have verified them but because doubting them would dissolve the very practices of doubting and justifying — but I want you to tell me whether that is really the best account or whether I am reading my own hobbyhorse into a practice that does not need it. In particular: which parts of the practice strictly require such an exempt class of propositions, and which parts would be served just as well by a plain foundationalism (some beliefs justified without inference) or by a coherentism (justification is holistic and no belief is privileged)? Does the practice need the hinges to be certain, or only to be undoubted in practice, and does that distinction bear on whether the practice is internally consistent, given that it demands justification for object-level claims while refusing to demand it for the framework claims? If the hinges are, as Wittgenstein also seems to allow, the sort of thing that can shift over time — if the riverbed can move — what would it look like for this practice to take that seriously, and what would have to change in how we argue if we did?

And where is the practice underdetermined: what would have to be true of the arguers, or of the claims they argue about, for rival pictures to license exactly the same behavior? Please argue the reconstruction out rather than surveying the literature; I am not after a reading list, and if you think the practice is quietly committed to something worse than any of these options, say so and tell me why.
