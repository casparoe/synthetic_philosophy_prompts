Write a dialogue between two people who both think humanity could lose control of its future to AI, but disagree sharply about what that loss actually looks like. One holds the influence-seeking picture: eventually you get systems — or institutions built around them — that are unusually good at accumulating resources, information, and decision-making power, and they end up running things, not because any of them "wants" to rule but because being good at those things is what gets selected for, and there's a more or less identifiable point where humans stop being the ones steering. The other holds the slow-drift picture: no takeover moment at all, just a long slide where every individual step is locally reasonable and even beneficial, the stuff we care about gets steadily crowded out by the stuff that's easier to measure and optimize, and by the time it's obvious, nobody did anything wrong and there's no moment in the past you can point to and say "there, thats when it went wrong."

Have them try to talk each other out of it. The drift person should argue that the takeover story is a comforting fiction — a villain is much easier to imagine opposing than a slope — and should push the harder claim that drift isn't a future scenario at all but a description of the present, which flips the burden of proof onto the other side. The influence-seeking person should argue that the drift story is unfalsifiable, that it makes every outcome count as confirmation, and that there proabably is a discrete threshold, we just can't see it from the inside because we'd be the ones being thresholded. Neither is a strawman, each gets a real answer to the other's best point, and each should make at least one concession that actually costs them something. Don't resolve it — end with the disagreement sharper than it started, and make clear what would have to be true for one of them to be right.
