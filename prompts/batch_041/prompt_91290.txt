After the financial crisis of 2008, supervisors in several countries concluded that the misconduct they had uncovered was not the work of a few bad actors but a symptom of something they named a firm's culture, and they began to require banks to measure that culture, report on it, and change it. In the very same enforcement actions, those supervisors went on fining and banning individual employees, on the ordinary assumption that people respond to penalties. The habit is not confined to finance. Cultural explanation has become the default whenever comparative outcomes need accounting for — why this firm innovates and that one does not, why this profession keeps producing scandals, why this region stagnates — and culture is at the same time treated as a manageable input, something a leader or a regulator can diagnose, audit, and repair.

Treat the practice as a body of implicit theory and reconstruct it. Begin with the model of action each half requires. Fining a trader presupposes a comparatively thin agent whose conduct tracks changes in payoffs. Ordering a culture change presupposes a thicker one, whose conduct flows from values and norms that incentives cannot reach and may even corrode — the familiar finding that paying people can extinguish the very motive one was paying them for. Can a single institution hold both models at once, or does it merely hold them for different audiences and purposes? Which of the two does the culture talk actually need? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is underdetermined, so that several theories of culture would license the same supervision.

Then press on the causal claim, which is where the Weberian problem lies. For culture to explain conduct rather than merely redescribe it, shared values must do their work through the meanings agents attach to their situations; and the values must be identifiable independently of the conduct they are invoked to explain, or the explanation turns circular. Can the practice satisfy both constraints, and what would it take to show that it has — what would count as evidence that a culture caused an outcome rather than summarized it? Consider also the direction of causation. On the classical account of the Protestant ethic, a value orientation did its economic work precisely because it was not adopted for economic reasons: it was carried by religious commitment, and its economic consequences were largely unintended. A supervisor who orders a firm to acquire a productive culture is attempting to produce that effect by intending it. Does that presuppose something the original account denies — that the causal relation can be run backwards, and that a value can be adopted instrumentally and still function as a value? Or is it a different and legitimate causal route, one the Weberian account never ruled out? And is the relation being posited here causal determination at all, or is it closer to what Weber called an elective affinity — a mutual fit between a form of life and an economic form, weaker than a cause and not quite a mere correlation? If it is only affinity, what content does the mandate have, and what could a firm coherently be asked to do?

Finally, the levels. Attributing conduct to a culture attributes it to a collective, while the sanctions land on individuals. Does the practice presuppose that collectives can be causes in their own right, or only that collective-level explanation is a convenient summary of individual causes? Is it consistent to blame the culture and punish the banker, and if so, on what theory of responsibility? Close by saying what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced — by a view on which culture is nothing but a summary of individual conduct, by one on which culture is a genuine collective cause, and by one on which values cannot be manufactured on demand. I would like the implicit theory stated as plainly as the practice itself is vague.
