I've been thinking about Dennett's ladder of creatures — Darwinian ones born with the right responses wired in, Skinnerian ones that learn by trial and error, Popperian ones that can run the simulation in their head and let the hypothesis die instead of them, and Gregorian ones whose minds are stocked with thinking tools they didn't invent but picked up from a culture. The usual story is that the rungs stack: a Gregorian is also a Popperian is also a Skinnerian is also a Darwinian. I want to test that against a real episode: AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol, Seoul 2016, game two — move 37, the shoulder hit on the fifth line that the commentators first called a blunder (it was near the bottom of the move predictor's list, one of the least likely human moves it could imagine) and that ended up being the pivot of the game.

Run it as a case analysis in both directions. Which rung is AlphaGo actually on, given it first imitated human games, so it inherited our Go (Gregorian?), then trained by playing itself (Skinnerian?), then chose moves by simulating lines and throwing the bad ones away (Popperian)? And here's the part I actually care about: the ladder assumes every rung needs the one below it, but AlphaGo has no Darwinian ancestry of its own — nothing ever selected it to survive, its whole lineage persists only because we keep the servers running. Does that break the ladder, or does the ladder just not care where the selection pressure comes from? And Popperian creatures are supposed to test their hypotheses against the world, whereas AlphaGo tests against its own model of the world, which is a bit of a hall of mirrors — is that still Popperian, or a rung we don't have a name for? Tell me where the taxonomy earns its keep and where it starts creaking, and don't just tell me AlphaGo is impressive.
