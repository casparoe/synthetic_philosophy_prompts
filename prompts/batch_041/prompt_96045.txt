For the past year I have been sitting in on a reading group on democratic backsliding — Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and more recently the United States — and one comparison keeps doing the heavy lifting. A backsliding country, the group says, is a frog in a pot of water brought slowly to a boil: no single step is dramatic enough to trigger the jump reflex, so the electorate sits placid until the thing is done. The analogy is made to license two conclusions: that the danger lies in imperceptibility rather than in any decisive break, and that the remedy is early warning — finer-grained indices, lower thresholds, alarm raised before the coup — while the voters themselves are to be pitied for not noticing rather than blamed.

I am a retired physiologist and the biology bothers me. The experiment usually cited, Friedrich Goltz's of 1869, was performed on frogs whose brains had been removed; the intact animals tried to escape as the water warmed, and the later claims that normal frogs stay put depend on heating rates of a fraction of a degree per minute. The other half of the parable fares no better: a frog dropped into already boiling water dies rather than leaping out.

What I want is an audit. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the frog, the water, the heat source, the jump reflex, the experimenter standing outside the pot — then tell me where the mapping fails, which failures bear on the two conclusions above and which are idle, and whether either conclusion survives once they are on the table. I am especially interested in whether the metaphor has already settled the question of responsibility: a frog that fails to jump is the victim of its own reflexes, whereas electorates that vote for the heat are agents. If the metaphor is doing partisan work, say so, and if a better analogy is available for what actually happens in these cases, offer it. I am after the audit, not a reading list.
