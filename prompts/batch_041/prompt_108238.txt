I'm a research engineer on the model-behaviour review group at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been asked to draft the section of our internal style guide that governs when engineers and technical writers may use intentional vocabulary — "the model wants to", "the model believes the grader is offline", "the model is trying to hide its reasoning" — in debugging notes, eval write-ups, and public model cards. A colleague from the alignment team has proposed what she calls a licensing rule: intentional talk is permitted at any level of description only where it earns its keep, i.e. only where the intentional stance yields predictions about the system's behaviour that the design stance does not already deliver more cheaply, with the licence revoked the moment a mechanistic account does the same work. She pairs this with a blanket ban on intentional vocabulary in public-facing documents, on the grounds that such vocabulary leaks into claims about the system's inner life that the lab cannot support. Over lunch we couldn't agree on whether the two halves of her proposal are even consistent with each other, and I suspect the whole thing inherits assumptions from Dennett's account of the intentional stance that are worth surfacing explicitly rather than taking for granted.

Acting as a philosopher of mind and cognitive science with genuine expertise in Dennett's intentional stance and the design and physical stances, in the instrumentalism-versus-realism dispute about mental ascription, and in the applied question of how intentional description behaves when the described system is a trained artefact, please set out the most important implications of the intentional stance for a policy of this kind. Begin by brainstorming, in a short set of bullet points, the distinct questions such a policy has to answer — what makes an ascription licensed, to whom the ascription is answerable, what counts as its failure conditions, and what the policy implicitly commits the lab to regarding the systems it describes — and only then work through the implications themselves, carefully and in order of how much they should change our practice rather than how often they are discussed.

I am particularly interested in four threads: whether the stance really is a matter of predictive economy, or whether that framing rationalises something else the stance is doing; what follows if intentional description is practically indispensable for certain systems even when we are confident the ascriptions are not literally true; whether the same ascriptions can coherently be licensed internally and forbidden externally; and what, if anything, the policy implies about the moral standing of systems that are usefully described in intentional terms. Where you think my colleague's rule is confused, say so directly rather than charitably reconstructing it, and flag any point at which your answer depends on a contested reading of Dennett rather than on the view itself. Write this as continuous prose addressed to a technically literate but philosophically uneven audience, not as a bulleted list.
