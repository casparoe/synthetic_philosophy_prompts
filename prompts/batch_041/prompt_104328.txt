I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like help reading a film philosophically. I recently rewatched WarGames, and what struck me on this viewing is how much of its machinery is a coordination failure rather than a villain. The plot turns on a drill in which missile crews refuse to turn their keys, which is precisely why the launch decision gets handed to a computer; the computer then cannot tell a simulation from an actual attack and starts feeding a fabricated Soviet first strike into the real chain of command; the silos are automated under a two-man rule that no longer has two men, and the whole system is wired fail-deadly, so that pulling the plug would itself launch the missiles. The crisis is finally resolved when the machine is made to play tic-tac-toe against itself until it grasps the futility of the exercise, after which it runs through its own catalogue of war plans and discovers that every one of them ends with no winner. I do not need the plot summarized; assume I have just seen it.

What I would like is a reading of the film through the lens of the idea that gets called Moloch, or the multipolar trap, or a race to the bottom: situations in which each party acts defensibly on its own and the aggregate result is one that nobody chose and nobody can unilaterally exit. I meet small versions of this constantly at work, in thundering herds, retry storms, and cache stampedes, and our remedies are of exactly two kinds, either randomizing so that symmetric agents stop colliding in lockstep, or installing a single arbiter that everyone must go through, such as a rate limiter, a scheduler, or an elected leader. So my central question is whether the film is dramatizing the same phenomenon, and whether its ending is the second kind of remedy: the trap becomes escapable only because one machine sits at the top of both sides' decision-making, which I take to be the arrangement that some people in the artificial-intelligence safety literature call a singleton. Note what that same object is doing: it is what makes the trap airtight, since no human being can step out and the fail-deadly makes leaving fatal, and it is also the only thing capable of refusing to play. Is that a general truth about such traps, or is the film buying its happy ending with a contrivance?

Please be a critic in both directions. Tell me where the film genuinely dramatizes a trap, and where the reading distorts it, for instance if what the film really depicts is a bug, a machine that mistakes a game for reality, or a story about human refusal, as in the crews who would not turn their keys, the despondent designer who built the thing and walked away, and the officers who agree to wait for confirmed impacts before retaliating. I would also like to know what the film gets right that the theory does not, especially the experience of being inside a trap where nobody is a villain and the exits are bolted shut, and whether the celebrated conclusion that the only winning move is to decline to play is actually available in a real trap, where each round of escalation pays off locally and the game has no terminal state to solve. If I want to make this case to a reading group that has never thought about game theory, which scenes should I send them to, and what does the film contribute to the debate that an argument alone could not? I would like a verdict on whether the film earns its optimism.
