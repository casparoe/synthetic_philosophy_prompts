I teach political philosophy and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about conscription by going with my gut on cases, and I can't find the principle. Maybe you can.

The Swiss militia: you do your basic training, take the rifle home, show up every year for a refresher course, and you're on the list until you're middle aged. I think skipping the refresher is wrong — not just illegal, wrong, roughly the way bailing on a shift your coworkers are covering for you is wrong. If you're declared unfit or you refuse outright, you pay the exemption tax instead, a percentage of your income. Fine, that seems like the right price, and dodging the tax is not fine.

Finland: you can do civilian service instead, but the civilian track is a good deal longer than the standard military one, and I think that surcharge is a straight penalty on conscience. An unjust one.

A colleague of mine left his country rather than serve in what he took to be an unjust war. I think he was entirely within his rights and his country has no claim on him. Meanwhile I think the Swiss refresher call-up is binding on Swiss men. Those two verdicts have never sat comfortably together for me.

The all-volunteer force: nobody's coerced, so I think it's fine, and a kid who signs up for the tuition money isn't doing anything wrong. But when I look at who actually signs up — the poor, the rural, the ones with no other options — something feels off in a way I can't turn into an argument. And a contractor doing the exact same job for a paycheck strikes me as not fine at all, which I suspect is sentiment rather than principle.

Sweden scrapped the draft for a few years and then brought it back. I think both moves were defensible, which probably means my intuitions aren't tracking conscription itself but something behind it.

Same story with a small state that just abolishes its army and leans on guarantees from bigger neighbors: I think that's respectable, not cowardly. But I also think Switzerland keeping the militia is respectable. So apparently everything is respectable.

The neutrality part bothers me most. I think the Swiss guy who wants to go fight in somebody else's war should be allowed to, even though I think the Swiss guy who skips his refresher should be fined. The state can compel me to defend it and forbid me from defending anyone else, and I only think one of those two is legitimate.

My tentative principle is a reciprocity one: if the good is shared and non-excludable and the burden is shared too, compelling contributions is fair and free-riding is wrong. That gets the Swiss refresher, maybe gets the exemption tax, and it makes the all-volunteer force fine. But it doesn't get my unease about the poor kids, it doesn't get the wrongness of the contractor, and it doesn't get my colleague who left.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all of these? State it precisely, tell me which clause is doing the work for which verdict, and tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed — a small state that replaces conscription with a lottery, where every year 5 percent of nineteen-year-olds are drafted by lottery into a professional army, paid market wages, and exempted from income tax for life. Then tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up. I suspect it's the one about the all-volunteer force, but I'd rather you say so.

One last thing, because it's the real reason I care. In the armed-neutrality cases the army isn't supposed to win. The whole point is to make an occupation expensive enough that nobody tries it. If that's the doctrine, the people being conscripted are being asked to die in a war that's designed to be lost slowly. Does that change anything about whether the compulsion is legitimate, or does the reciprocity principle simply not care whether the war can be won?
