I run the evidence review desk at a policy institute: we commission social-science reports from economists, sociologists and epidemiologists, and before anything reaches the minister I have to sign off on whether the causal claims in the executive summary are adequately supported. The failure mode I keep meeting is not bad statistics but the wrong kind of explanation — a claim in which an aggregate or institutional fact (a labour market, a housing system, a culture of overwork, an inspection regime) is said to do the causal work with no account of how it bears on anyone's behaviour, or the reverse, a claim that is entirely about individual choices and is nonetheless offered as an explanation of a population pattern those choices cannot generate. My reviewers get two hours per report, never see the underlying data, and are not philosophers.

Write the decision procedure I can hand them, ordered so that the cheap tests come first and grounded in the debate between methodological individualism and holism rather than in our in-house habits. I do not want a summary of that debate: for each step, give one line on which part of it the step is trying to preserve and what it is giving up. The procedure has to separate three things my reviewers currently run together under the word "microfoundations" — a claim about what exists, a claim about what an explanation must cite, and a claim about how to find good evidence. It has to say what a genuinely individual-level account adds that a restatement of the macro pattern in individual terms does not. And it has to handle the cases where the aggregate appears to be doing the explaining in its own right — where a macro fact constrains or causes individual outcomes rather than summarising them, and where a macro property is partly constituted by the individual behaviour and so cannot be a rival cause.

Deliver the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a rejection, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because what is at issue is a judgement about what counts as an explanation rather than a fact. Mark the steps that are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on two reports: (a) one arguing that a rise in local housing costs explains a fall in internal migration, with the mechanism given only as "people respond to prices"; and (b) one arguing that a change in a school funding formula raised attainment, evidenced by a discontinuity across district boundaries with no individual-level data at all. At least one of the two should come out against my reviewers' instincts. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do and where its reasoning is most likely to fail — what argument or kind of case would make you give up the individual/aggregate distinction as a reviewing device altogether.
