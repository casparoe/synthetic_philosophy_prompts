I run a small reading group and next month we're doing The Remains of the Day, and I want to read it through Grice — the implicature stuff, the cooperative principle and the maxims, what is said versus what is implicated, speaker meaning. I don't need a summary of the novel, I know it well enough; I want the actual reading, and I want you to argue with me rather than just agree.

Here's my hunch. Stevens is a machine for generating implicatures. He never lies. He answers exactly as much as he's asked and not a word more, he changes the subject with perfect politeness, and by the end we know the whole shape of his life — his father, Miss Kenton, Lord Darlington — almost entirely out of what he declines to say. So the maxims look tailor-made for him: quantity, relevance, all of it flouted, and the reader does alot of the work of recovering the meaning he won't state. Fine so far.

But here's where I get stuck, and this is the part I actually want you to settle. Grice ties speaker meaning to the speaker's intention being recognized. Does Stevens intend any of it? My read is that he would sincerely deny every implicature we draw — he thinks he is just being a good butler, being discreet, not gossiping, and if you pressed him he'd have no idea what you were accusing him of. So one of two things is going on, and they pull in opposite directions. Either the novel shows implicature happening below intention, which is trouble for Grice, or it shows that Stevens does mean it and is lying to himself, which is Grice-friendly and just makes him a hypocrite. Take a side and defend it. And while you're at it, use the distinction between flouting and violating: flouting is ostentatious, you're meant to catch it; violating is quiet deception. Does Stevens flout, or does he violate? It matters, because if he only violates, then by Grice's own lights there's no implicature to recover and we're reading in meanings the theory says aren't there. Also tell me whether the implicatures we draw are generalized or particularized — I can never keep that straight in a case like this.

The second thing is harder. There are two speakers. Stevens implicates one set of things, and Ishiguro implicates another through him — we catch meanings Stevens would never avow and Ishiguro never states. So whose speaker meaning is the book's meaning? Grice is a philosopher of conversation, of two people actually talking and cooperating; a novel isn't cooperative in his sense, it's built. Does the machinery even transfer, or does fiction break it? Don't just tell me it transfers, tell me where it strains.

Then walk me through the scenes that carry this — the bantering, his father dying upstairs while he keeps serving the guests, the evenings with Miss Kenton in his pantry, the last scene on the pier — and for each one say whether Grice lights it up or whether the scene resists him. End with what the novel adds to the Grice debate that a philosopher's made-up example sentence doesn't.

If Grice turns out to be the wrong lens and someone else fits better, say so plainly instead of forcing it. I'd much rather you tell me my frame is wrong than hand me a smooth essay that pretends it isn't. And if I've got the novel wrong anywhere, or I'm misremembering Grice, correct me.
