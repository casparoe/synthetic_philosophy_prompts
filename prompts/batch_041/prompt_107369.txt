I'm a third-year undergraduate in a course on collective decision-making, and we have just finished the unit on the discursive dilemma. I've ended up holding a position I'd like you to attack rather than restate: the dilemma is not an artefact of our voting rules but a genuine structural tension in collective judgement. Any procedure that aggregates individuals' judgements issue by issue over an interconnected agenda must give up either the group's logical consistency or its responsiveness to the individuals; and the right response is premise-based aggregation, letting the group's verdicts on the premises settle the conclusion, and accepting that this costs us independence between the issues.

I want a map of what would count against that, and I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a note on which ones a defender of my position ought to concede rather than dismiss. Four things I'd particularly like addressed:

First, whether the dilemma is really about groups at all. A classmate claims (and I'm unsure I've got the statement right) that majority voting is guaranteed to yield consistent collective judgements exactly when the agenda has a tree-like shape — roughly, when the propositions can be arranged so that every triple of them has a unique median — which would make the whole dilemma a fact about the combinatorial structure of agendas rather than about aggregation. If something like that is true, how much of the dilemma's force survives?

Second, whether the escape I favour is even coherent. Premise-based aggregation seems to presuppose a principled division of the agenda into premises and conclusions. I can build agendas where the same substantive question is formalised either as a single atomic proposition, in which case nothing goes wrong, or as a composite one, in which case the paradox appears; and I suspect that adding a proposition already entailed by the others can flip the collective verdict on the originals. If so, does my response simply relocate the arbitrariness rather than remove it?

Fourth, whether my phrase "the right response" is even well defined. Premise-based aggregation buys consistency, but does it buy truth-tracking? Under what assumptions about voters' competence and the correlation of their errors would the conclusion-based procedure be the better epistemic bet, and does that make my preference a substantive claim rather than a default?

Be willing to tell me that the position as stated risks nothing and has been insulated by the way the axioms are chosen, and whether that would be a defect in it.
