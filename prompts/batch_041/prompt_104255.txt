Ok so I write about philosophy and religion for a general readership, and I'm stuck on a piece about Jain anekāntavāda — the many-sidedness thing, with syādvāda (the seven-fold predication) and nayavāda (the standpoints). Every popular treatment does the same three moves: blind men and the elephant, wave-particle duality, and then the smug conclusion that everybody is a little bit right. I want to comission one designed case that actually earns the doctrine instead of decorating it.

Build me a thought experiment to these specs. Two people, fully informed, sincere, no hidden evidence, contradict each other about one and the same ordinary thing — not two different questions, not a pun, not two senses of a word, not vagueness, not a shift in context or in standards of precision. Same question, clashing answers. Yet each answer is fully warranted from where they stand, and neither is merely half-right in the boring sense where a third answer just adds them together. Ordinary setting — a kitchen, a repair shop, an insurance desk, whatever; no exotic physics, no quantum anything, no elephants. One paragraph for the case itself.

Then do the actual work. Lay out the seven predications of syādvāda and say for this case what each one asserts and why it's warranted — and if one of the seven turns out to have nothing to do here, say so rather than padding it. Explain what nayavāda contributes that bare predication doesn't. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the load-bearing work, and what happens if I delete it — does the whole thing collapse into ordinary context-sensitivity, in which case I've wasted your time and mine? Then put on the hat of a hard-nosed bivalent logician and give me his best objection, which I'd guess is equivocation or a suppressed parameter, and then tell me honestly whether he's right. Finally, give the two parties something practical to fight over — money, custody, a permit, something an adjudicator has to rule on — and say what anekāntavāda actually licenses that adjudicator to do, since she can't just announce that both sides are correct and go home.

No citations or reading lists, obviously, and don't lean on any particular text. And please don't tell me it's all about the limits of language — I already know that line, I want the case.
