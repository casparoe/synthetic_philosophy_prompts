I sit on, and do the paperwork for, the five-member panel our order convenes to review reports of apparently miraculous healings at our two pilgrimage sites. We get somewhere between twenty and forty a year. Each one arrives as a folder — the pilgrim's own account, whatever medical records exist, statements from bystanders, sometimes a note from the parish priest, sometimes a press clipping — and we have ninety minutes per case to decide whether it goes forward to the diocese, is held open for more evidence, or is closed. The panel is a general practitioner, a neurologist who is not always able to attend, a historian of the order, a canon lawyer, and a lay member who is there to speak for the ordinary faithful. None of them will sit still for a lecture on probability theory, which is why I am asking you rather than them. I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about this with a friend who teaches philosophy of science. She opened by saying that Hume settled the whole business two and a half centuries ago and that everything we do in that room is theatre. I said that a maxim is not a procedure, and that if she were the one holding the folder she would have to offer something more usable than the claim that no testimony should convince us of a miracle unless it would be more miraculous still for the testimony to be false. I also said that this is not even one argument: the Hume who insists a proof can only be outweighed by an opposite proof and the Hume who tells us to proportion belief to evidence are doing different work, and the second, once independent testimony is allowed in, does not deliver the blanket conclusion people quote him for. I think the familiar complaint is right — that he runs together how unlikely the event is with how unlikely it is that the reports exist at all — and it matters to us, because four tourists who never met and cannot have coordinated are a different object of evidence from four members of one prayer circle who heard the story from a single person and then repeated it to us in nearly identical words. She replied, not unreasonably, that this is exactly the move a person makes when he wants to go on believing something. Then she said something that has stayed with me: that we never write down what the alleged event is supposed to have been until after we have read the statements, so of course our events always come out extraordinary. Specify the recovery narrowly enough and its base rate is essentially nothing; describe it loosely enough and it is a Tuesday. I have also never been able to settle for myself whether calling something a miracle means that it broke a law of nature, in which case the procedure is being asked to certify a contradiction, or merely that we cannot yet explain it.

What I want from you is a procedure I can actually run in that room and read aloud: a sequence of questions in a fixed order, in continuous prose. It should begin with the question we currently ask last — what precisely the alleged event is, written down before anyone looks at the testimonies, and what the relevant comparison class is for how often such things happen anyway, with a rule for what to do when our two doctors disagree about the diagnosis, since that is where most of our files quietly die. Then a step on the testimony itself, which must say how we are to count statements that are not independent, how to weigh interest and motive on both sides, and what a single first-hand witness is worth against a dozen third-hand ones. Then a step on how the alleged event stands against the rest of what we believe, which has to force us to choose explicitly between revising our view of what is medically possible and revising our view of what the witnesses did, rather than letting us have both. Then a stopping rule: when to close a file without further discussion, when to hold it open, and when to stop pretending the question is factual and hand it to the panel as a judgment about what we ought to publish, given what publication will do to the people involved. It has to be usable by the historian and the lay member without statistics, so wherever a number would be needed, say what to do in its absence. I would rather have an honest procedure with a marked line where judgment takes over than one that pretends the line is not there.

Then run it on three files. A woman with a long-standing spinal condition reports immediate and lasting relief while praying at the shrine; there are four witnesses who arrived separately, and a clean set of before-and-after scans. A boy recovered from a febrile illness; the account comes from his mother and from nine members of her prayer circle, all of whom had heard it from her before we ever spoke to them, and there is no imaging. And a man's tumour genuinely shrank in a way our own doctors cannot account for, but the shrinking happened over months at home, after he had left the shrine, so that there is no event at the shrine to report. I expect the procedure to close the second file, and I will not enjoy it, because the mother is not lying and her community will read the closure as a verdict on their faith; tell me whether the procedure has anything to say about that or whether it is simply doing what it was built to do. Say, finally, what this procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, what sort of case it will get wrong, and which of its steps are value judgments wearing the costume of method. Write it as continuous prose, the way one writes a letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables.
