You are a political philosopher working on the ethics of the welfare state, with a strong background in the philosophical literature on desert, reciprocity, distributive justice, and the moral psychology of blame. Think carefully and at length before you answer; begin by brainstorming the candidate theories that could be in play, and only then commit to a reconstruction.

I am trying to work out what my country's benefit system actually believes about people, because I keep running into asymmetries that don't seem to fit together:

- Working-age benefits come with behavioural conditions: job-search requirements, mandatory appointments, documented applications, and financial sanctions for non-compliance. The state pension and the main pensioner benefits are paid with no behavioural conditions at all.
- The system polices overpayment aggressively — investigations, civil penalties, prosecution for fraud — but does almost nothing to ensure that people claim what they are entitled to; unclaimed entitlements are not treated as a scandal. And where an overpayment was caused by the department's own administrative error, it is still, in many cases, recovered from the claimant, so the risk of bureaucratic mistake sits with the person least able to bear it.
- Benefit fraud is politically salient and criminalised, while tax avoidance by higher earners is treated as a compliance problem at worst, even where the sums are larger.
- Conditionality tracks paid employment rather than social contribution: a parent who provides substantial unpaid care is expected to take a job, but nobody is ever required to provide care.

Assume I have no philosophical training and am genuinely puzzled, not looking for a debating position. I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory these practices presuppose, as precisely as if you were writing the political philosophy that the system has never written down for itself.

Please address each of the following:

1. What conception of desert, entitlement, or reciprocity does the pattern presuppose? State the implicit theory in a form sharp enough that it could be criticised, and say what it implies about who counts as a full member of the scheme of cooperation.
2. Separate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that a luck egalitarian, a prioritarian welfarist, a civic republican, and a Kantian contractualist could each license it? Which rival view does the pattern actually rule out, and where exactly does the exclusion happen?
3. Assess internal consistency. If the underlying justification is reciprocity — support in return for contribution — what does that do to recipients who cannot contribute, to the unconditional treatment of pensioners, and to the recovery of overpayments caused by the state's own error? Is the pattern a coherent single theory, or a coalition of incompatible ones held together by administrative convenience?
4. The claimant commitment is signed as a contract. What does treating it as a contract presuppose about the voluntariness of consent given when the alternative is destitution? What would have to be true of the claimant's options for the contractual framing to be more than decorative?
5. Consider the rival explanation that no moral theory is doing the work here at all — that the pattern simply reflects which groups have political voice and which are easy to sanction. How much of the pattern does that explain that the moral reconstruction cannot, and can the moral theory be defended as anything more than a rationalisation?
6. Contrast case: suppose a jurisdiction replaced fixed-period sanctions with sanctions that taper automatically with each week of compliance, and allowed documented informal care work to discharge part of the conditionality requirement. Would that reform be licensed by the same implicit theory, or would it require a different one? Identify the smallest change to current practice that would make the whole pattern coherent.

Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I will add those myself. Argue in terms of positions and considerations. Finish by naming the single asymmetry that is hardest for your reconstructed theory to accommodate, and say whether you think that tells against the theory or against the practice.
