I'm inclined to think the Anthropocene is a genuine geological epoch and not merely a popular label, and that the stratigraphic community erred in 2024 when it declined to ratify the mid-twentieth-century boundary marked by the plutonium fallout from atmospheric bomb tests. My reasons: the postwar Great Acceleration leaves a globally synchronous, chemically distinctive, and durable signature in lake sediments and ice, and a boundary that future geologists could in principle read off the rock is exactly what an epoch boundary is supposed to be. Tell me what would count against this, sorted from fatal to merely awkward.

Three things interest me especially. First, whether the boundary is really as synchronous and preservable as I assume, and what the sedimentary record would have to look like to defeat that. Second, whether the standard objections behind the rejection — that the record is too shallow, that the proposed start is too recent, that any epoch boundary is ultimately a convention — are genuine defeaters of my view or merely reasons to expect that competent people will disagree. Third, whether my position risks unfalsifiability: if I would still call the Anthropocene real even when the bodies charged with adjudicating such matters say it is not, is there any evidence that could change my mind, and is that a defect or simply what a claim about the world being one way rather than another looks like? Note also that the question of when it started seems entangled with who gets to decide, and I'd like you to say whether that entanglement tells against the reality of the epoch or only against a naive picture of how the science works.

Give me the defeaters a defender of the rejection would concede as well as those only critics press, tell me which of my own commitments I'd have to abandon first, and be willing to conclude that my view is well insulated if that is what you find. Answer in no more than 550 words, leading with the single most damaging consideration.
