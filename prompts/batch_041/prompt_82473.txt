I'm a third-year student in a metaphysics seminar, and I'm writing a paper on whether the disputes we've been having all term — over whether a certain mental state counts as a belief, over whether a machine could be conscious, over whether composition ever occurs — are genuinely substantive or merely verbal. Before I commit to a verdict on any of them, I want to be clear about what I'm asking for, so please write me the desiderata: a specification of what any adequate account of verbal disputes would have to deliver. I want a checklist I can hold candidate accounts to, not a survey. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or really a preference of one camp. Where you think some subset of the requirements cannot be satisfied together, say so explicitly and explain what the incompatibility shows — that is the part I most want from you. And tell me which single requirement, if I insisted on it, would do the most to narrow the field. A short note on how the leading families of accounts fare against the list is welcome, but keep it brief; the list is the deliverable.

To keep this concrete, I'd like the requirements tested against a set of cases, some of which I've built myself and some of which I've adapted from seminar discussion. (i) Two researchers argue for a decade about whether a certain state is a belief; they agree on every behavioural and neural prediction and differ only in where they place a threshold on a functional-role continuum. (ii) Two parties use what sounds like the same word in the same way, and their predictions come apart — yet a third party eventually shows that each was covertly using the term in a different sense. (iii) Two disputants each slide between two senses of the term at different points in the argument, so neither has a single consistent usage. (iv) One party's usage is idiosyncratic, but it carves a distinction that turns out to track something in the world better than the ordinary usage does. (v) The disputants agree on all the first-order facts and agree that their disagreement is verbal, but disagree about whether verbal disputes are worth having; I want to know whether that second-order disagreement is itself verbal, and whether the account can stop the regress. (vi) The disputants have ten minutes and must decide whether to keep arguing or to stipulate definitions and get on with the work.

Finally, some constraints I'd like the account to respect, and for each I'd like you to say whether it is a genuine requirement or an overreach. The account should not presuppose a controversial theory of meaning, so it should be stateable without deciding between a truth-conditional and a use-based semantics. It should be usable by a third party with no access to the disputants' intentions, and by the disputants themselves mid-argument. It should not classify so many disputes as verbal that the diagnosis loses its point — an argument over a vague predicate, for instance, should not automatically come out verbal. And it should have something to say about whether verbalness is an all-or-nothing verdict and what follows from it: whether a verbal dispute can still be worth having, and whether the diagnosis licenses dismissing the dispute, resolving it by stipulation, or nothing at all.
