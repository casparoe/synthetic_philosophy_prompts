Depression screening for adolescents is now routine in many clinics and schools: the teenager fills in a symptom checklist, a score above a cut-off triggers a fuller assessment, and a diagnosis and a course of treatment often follow, on the strength of trials showing that treated adolescents do better than untreated ones. The trials measure improvement with the same checklist that does the screening, and the rise in recorded cases that follows the introduction of a screening programme is ordinarily read as cases that would otherwise have gone unnoticed. What does this arrangement presuppose about the thing the checklist measures? Does it require that the checklist reports a condition that was already there and would have run the same course had nobody asked — that the adolescents being classified are, in the relevant sense, unaffected by the classification? Or could the whole practice be justified by a rival picture on which a person's learning that she falls under the category changes what she reports, how her family, school, and doctors treat her, and hence what the category is true of, so that the checklist is part of what it measures and the improvement the trials record is partly an effect of the screening itself? Say which elements of the practice strictly require the first picture and which merely sit comfortably with it, whether the practice is genuinely underdetermined between the two, and what a service that took the second picture seriously would have to do differently.
