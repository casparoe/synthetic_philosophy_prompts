ok so in my seminar on decision theory there's this one guy who, every single time escalation comes up -- vietnam, the arms race, two streaming services bidding up the same show, a founder pouring good money into a dead product -- says the same thing: it's just the dollar auction. you know the demo. you auction off a dollar bill, bids in 5 cent increments, highest bidder pays what they bid and gets the dollar, second highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing. and the bidding goes past a dollar basically every time. he uses this to argue that escalation isnt a choice people make, its a structure they fall into: once you're in, quitting means paying for nothing, so you keep going, so the people who escalated arent really to blame, the game is, and the only sane move is to never sit down at the table in the first place. he says it with this little satisfied look and nobody in the room ever pushes back, including me, and I want to know if it actually holds up.

so audit it. map it: what plays the role of the auctioneer, the dollar, the two bidders, the both-pay rule, the increment, the moment bidding crosses a dollar. tell me where the mapping is tight, where it gets loose, and where it breaks entirely. then separate the breaks that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just trivia about the demo.

things I want in the break column, at least: real escalations have a prize whose value is uncertain and often partly *made* by the bidding itself; you can usually walk away (badly, but you can); the rules can sometimes be renegotiated or an outside party can intervene; there are usually more than two players; the auctioneer is often also a player; there may or may not be a deadline and a known increment; and sometimes the prize is worthless or negative to win (mutual destruction), which the demo never allows.

then two variants, because I think theyre where the real work is:

(a) the pot grows. every bid raises what the prize is worth to the winner -- status, prestige, "we cant be seen to lose now" politics, the dollar becomes a five becomes a twenty as the bidding climbs. does that make the trap deeper, or does it make overbidding *rational* and blow up his point?

(b) either player can, at any moment, pay a flat fee to cancel the whole auction for both of them -- some third party offering to buy out the game. does that rescue the analogy, break it, or just relocate the trap one level up (now the question is who pays the fee, and when)?

(c) if the increment is tiny and you dont know when it ends, does "never sit down" even name a choice anyone can make? you often dont find out youre in an auction until youve already bid.

last thing: is there a better analogy for the thing hes actually pointing at -- escalation as a trap rather than a series of decisions -- one that doesnt smuggle in a fixed, known, cash-valued prize? if yes say what it is and what it does better, if no say why the dollar auction is really the right one.
