I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on virtual worlds, and I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts about them add up to. Stated flatly: when a stranger's avatar gropes mine in a VR social space, I was harassed — a real violation, not a rude gesture in a cartoon. When I shoot a scripted NPC that begs for its life, I have done nothing to anyone. When another player kills my character in a PvP zone I knowingly entered, likewise no wrong; but when a player uses an exploit to follow me out of that zone and kill me over and over for an hour, I have been harmed, and it is harassment rather than bad manners. When my account is broken into and my rare items are sold, I have been robbed, though the items are rows in a database. When the company that hosts the world I spent a decade building a house in shuts it down, I have lost something real, but no real thing was destroyed. When a friend dies and their avatar still stands where they logged out, my visits to it are real mourning, but I am not visiting them; and when a chatbot trained on their messages takes the avatar over and speaks in their voice, it is not them, and being comforted as if it were is a kind of self-deception.

My tentative principle is what I have been calling the presence view: a virtual entity is real, and a proper object of concern, exactly insofar as some real person is actually present in it or actually stands behind it — my avatar is me while I am in it, the NPC is nobody, the abandoned avatar is a shell. That seems to give me the groping, both killings, the standing avatar and the griefbot; but it says nothing about why the consensual killing and the exploit killing come apart, since I am equally present in both, and it seems to make the theft unreal, since no one is present in my items, only an institution everyone is treating as real. Tell me what the principle should be, or what the smallest set of them is, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work and which of my cases forces each clause; the verdicts it forces on cases I have not listed — a company that sells a memorial copy of a dead user's avatar to strangers, and a world whose NPCs have become indistinguishable from players, so that no one in it can tell who is present; and which of my verdicts it cannot save. The one I keep going back and forth on: someone builds a replica of my late friend's avatar from their chat logs, passes it off as them, and gets me to confide what I would tell no stranger. I want to say I have been defrauded, but on my other verdicts nothing real was done to me, since the thing that fooled me was never my friend and may not have been anything at all.
