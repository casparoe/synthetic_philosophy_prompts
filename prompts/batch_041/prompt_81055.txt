I would be grateful for a satirical piece of roughly 700 words, written in the form of a complaint filed in a federal district court by a plaintiff proceeding without counsel. The plaintiff is a retired chemist who has lately taken up social ontology; he seeks declaratory and injunctive relief against his mortgage servicer on the ground that money is a social construction and that his debt is therefore void. Please keep the piece deadpan throughout — no winking, no narrator's asides, no jokes that step outside the document — and let the humour come from the argument itself, so that a philosopher of the social sciences would find it fair rather than a straw man.

Let the plaintiff make the strongest version of his case, in numbered paragraphs and the flat diction of a pleading, and let the reader feel the pull of the argument before the institution registers its cost. I care more about the philosophical content than about the plaintiff's eccentricity, and I would like that content carried by arguments rather than by names: please cite no real philosophers, books, statutes, or decided cases, and do not invent citations that look authentic. The complaint should turn on the thought that something counts as money only relative to rules that people collectively accept; that because the acceptance is collective, no single holder can withdraw from it unilaterally; that a contingent realization of an institution must be distinguished from the function it serves; and that the suit's own forum rests on the same kind of acceptance as the debt it attacks. Include one paragraph in which the plaintiff concedes what he takes to be a minor caveat but which a careful reader will see is fatal to his case, and close with the court's disposition in a sentence or two — dismissal, costs to the defendant, the foreclosure allowed to proceed — recorded without comment.
