I'm meant to be writing a seminar paper on Keynes and probability, but I've ended up keeping a notebook instead, and now I can't tell whether my gut is consistent. The game is: someone hands me two uncertain things and I have to say whether one is more likely than the other, or that I can't say. Verdicts so far. Fair coin I've flipped a thousand times: heads is 1/2, easy. Novelty coin from a joke shop, never flipped, some no-name company: heads is also 1/2 — I'll say that without blinking — but I'd pay a pound to swap it for the first one before betting on heads, and I don't think that's me being silly. A machine at the fair either makes coins that land heads 40% of the time or coins that land heads 60% of the time, but the label fell off: I won't say the probability is 1/2, and I won't say it's 40% or 60% either, which my flatmate Ravi insists is incoherent, since a probability just has to be a number. 'The next person through the door is left-handed' vs 'it'll rain here tomorrow': no answer, I refuse, there's no fact of the matter about which is more likely. But 'the next person through the door is left-handed and a redhead' vs 'the next person through the door is left-handed': the second, always, every time, no exceptions, even though I've no idea what the actual rates are. And the one that's really bothering me: my friend seals a card in an envelope, drawn from a deck that was either 70% red or 30% red, and she's forgotten which. I won't rank that against a plain draw from a known fair deck — not equal, just unrankable — and yet I'd definately rather bet on the known deck. That looks like it contradicts the coin case, or like I'm quietly tracking a second thing that isn't probability at all: how much is behind the number, how much I'd trust it to still be there tomorrow.

So what I want is a principle, or a shortlist of them, that gets as many of these as possible, stated properly, with the case that forces each bit of it, plus an honest note on which of my verdicts I'd have to give up — I'd rather give up a verdict than end up with a theory that has a special clause for every case, if that's the choice. I half-remember Keynes having something about probabilities that aren't comparable to each other at all, and that might be the shape of what I'm doing, but I'd like it argued out rather than just named. Also, my notebook has a hole in it: I can rank A above B, and B above C, but I genuinely can't rank A against C. If the relation is supposed to be transitive then the hole has to close somewhere and I want to know where — does A really beat C, does C beat A, or is there a third option. And say what the principle does with a case I haven't written down: a 1-in-3 risk I've pulled off a proper medical study, vs a 1-in-3 I made up over breakfast. Same number. Is there anything to say about those two or not?
