I'm a novelist, about eight months into a draft, and theres a speech I want to put in the mouth of a cognitive scientist character and I need to know whether she's talking nonsense. Roughly, she says: working out what a person will do is the same trick as working out what a chess engine will do — you assume it wants to win, ask what it should do, and you're right most of the time; yet nobody thinks the engine literally wants anything. The new engines are even better for her argument, she says, because nobody wrote the wanting in, they just taught themselves by playing each other. So 'wants' and 'believes' are names for a prediction strategy, and her field could drop them tomorrow and lose nothing. (She got it off some philosopher, I forget which.)

How far does this actually hold? Lay out the correspondence for me — what plays the part of the win, the self-play, the designer, the move, the belief — then where the mapping breaks, what the chess picture is quietly helping itself to that isn't there in the person case, and whether her conclusion survives once those breaks are on the table. And if theres a better analogy for the same job, I'd rather use that one.
