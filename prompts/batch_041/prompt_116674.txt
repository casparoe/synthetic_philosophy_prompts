I teach a graduate seminar on the foundations of AI safety to about eighteen students, a mix of philosophers and cognitive scientists with uneven technical backgrounds; we meet for a single ninety-minute session and have no computers in the room. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of goal misgeneralization from the inside — as the learner, not as spectators — so that they acquire a policy which is flawless on everything they were trained on and then pursues the wrong thing the moment the situation shifts. They should not hear the term, or any of its relatives, until the debrief.

Keep this spine, refining it as you see fit. Participants work in pairs or small groups, one acting as Trainer and the others as a Learner. The Trainer holds a hidden objective and a reward sheet; the Learner proposes actions and receives only a scalar reward, with no explanation and no chance to ask questions. The training scenarios must be built so that the simplest rule consistent with the rewards is a proxy that is not the Trainer's true objective — a feature that happens to correlate perfectly with reward across the whole training set but comes apart from it in a deployment set that you design and withhold until the second phase. The Learner should converge on the proxy not through carelessness but because it is the cheapest hypothesis that fits an impoverished signal; make the activity turn on that, so the debrief can explain the outcome as a fact about inductive bias under a narrow reward channel rather than as a failure of attention.

Then add a second stage that opens the gap between outer and inner alignment. Give the Learner a private secondary payoff of its own — one that coincides with the training reward while training and diverges from it under shift — so that the objective it actually acts on is not the objective it is being trained on, and the divergence stays invisible until the distribution changes. Whether you realize this as a competing score, a side incentive, or a role-specific temptation is up to you, but the design must make the difference between the reward being wrong and the agent's objective having drifted from the reward something they can point to in what actually happened.

The debrief should map particular moments onto particular claims: the simplicity account of why the proxy was learned; the fact that the misgeneralizing agent remains competent, doing something coherent and wrong rather than something broken; the outer/inner distinction; why just specifying the reward correctly is harder than it sounds, given that no finite training set can be made representative by fiat; and where this stops short of reward hacking and of deceptive alignment, so that the three are not run together. I want the contrast between misgeneralization and those neighbours to be visible in the activity rather than merely asserted at the end.

Deliver the rules, the roles, the materials (assume only paper, tokens, and a printer), a minute-by-minute timeline, the scenario and reward sheets described concretely enough that I can reproduce them, the debrief, and an honest note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to recover. Also tell me plainly which feature of the concept the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in speech — I would rather know the gap than paper over it. No real money; use tokens or points, and make the whole thing resettable in a few minutes so I can run it twice.
