I'm a novelist, about 70 pages into a book about a woman who has what the neurologists call pain asymbolia. She had a stroke that landed in the insula, and now she feels pain — or at least feels something where the injury is — but it doesn't bother her at all. She doesn't flinch, she doesn't pull her hand off a hot pan, she'll smile and laugh in the middle of a procedure and offer you the other arm. Her doctors think she's lucky. Her sister thinks she's faking it, or performing some kind of heroism. I haven't decided which of them the book believes, or whether it's in first person or told by the sister.

What's stalling me is the philosophy I've been reading. This condition is apparently the test case for whether pain is bad because of how it feels or because of what it does to you: one reading is that she's got the sensation and lost the awfulness, another is that what she's lost is the capacity to care about it — which would make her not a stoic but a person missing a part — and someone else argues she isn't really in pain at all, just behaving strangely around noxious things.

So what are the most important implications of that mess for how I write her? I'm stuck on whether she's suffering, on whether the reader can trust her report about her own body, and on whether her calm is a stance I can defend or just a lesion I'm romanticizing. Theres a version of this book where she ends up a case study with a name, and I'd like to know how to not write that one.
