I'm a journalist who writes a column on science and ideas, and I keep running into the same proverb doing heavy lifting in places it may not deserve to be. Last week, while reporting on how institutions make decisions under deep uncertainty, I interviewed a former regulator about why her agency stayed with a well-understood technology rather than adopting a newer one that its own models scored as having a higher expected payoff. The newer option, she conceded, looked better on the spreadsheet, but its failure modes were poorly characterized, and she said the agency had gone with 'better the devil you know than the devil you don't.' She wasn't being flippant; she was offering it as a reason — a consideration that she thought ought to weigh in the decision, and maybe even settle it.

I want to write a column that takes that sentence seriously as an argument rather than a mood, and for that I need to see the distinct claims it could be making. Please lay them out for me as a menu, ranging from the near-vacuous to the radical, and give me your verdict on each: which are trivial restatements of common sense, which are substantive and defensible, and which are actually false or confused. I'm especially interested in the ones that would genuinely license my source's conclusion — choosing the known option even when the models favor the unknown one — and in whether any of them can do that without begging the question or quietly collapsing into a different argument.

Some threads I'd like you to pull on, though don't let me constrain you: the difference between an option whose probabilities are known and one whose probabilities are merely unknown (the distinction that the two-urn setup people call the Ellsberg paradox is usually used to dramatize); whether preferring the known is a description of how people in fact behave or a norm about how they should; the possibility that what makes the known devil tolerable isn't its probability at all but the boundedness of its worst case; the thought that 'the devil you don't know' might be unknown precisely because someone better informed is the one offering it to you; and the possibility that the whole proverb is a fallacy — that if you can assign any probabilities whatsoever, the identity of the devil should be irrelevant, and that preferring the familiar one is just a bias with a flattering name, perhaps one that violates the axiom saying your choice between two options shouldn't turn on outcomes that come out the same either way.

Please structure the response as follows. Open with a section titled 'The Setting,' about 150 words, restating what my source's sentence was being asked to do in that decision. Then 'The Readings,' the main body — a numbered list of the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, each stated in one or two sentences and then unpacked, ordered from the trivial up to the radical; take your time here, since this is the part I'll actually be working from. Then 'Verdicts,' roughly 400 words, going back through the readings and labeling each one a platitude, a live and defensible claim, or false or confused, with a sentence or two of justification. Then 'What the Argument Needed,' about 200 words, saying which reading my source most likely meant, whether that reading actually supports the conclusion she drew, and — if it doesn't — which reading she would have needed to make the argument go through.

Write it as something I can carry into the piece: plain enough to quote from, precise enough that I won't be embarrassed by it. Don't worry about naming who first proposed any of these ideas, or where to read more; I don't need citations or a reading list, and I'd rather you reason from the content than from who said it.
