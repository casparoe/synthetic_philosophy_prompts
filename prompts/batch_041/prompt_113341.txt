For most of the past decade I've been a steady reader of the rationalist blogosphere — the Sequences, the old decision-theory flamewars, the endless threads on doing versus allowing — and the one topic I have never managed to think cleanly about is whether an omission can be a cause. So I'd like you to write me an argument, not a survey: stake out a definite position, defend it against the strongest objection you can find, and define your terms instead of leaning on labels I might not recognize. The position I want you to argue for is this. Omissions are genuine causes; there is no deep metaphysical difference between causing an outcome and allowing it to happen, only a difference in which of the many conditions that were needed for the outcome we single out and call 'the cause.' On this view the gardener's failure to water the plants really is a cause of their death and the doctor's failure to diagnose really is a cause of the patient's; and if that threatens to make nearly everything a cause of nearly everything, that is the point rather than a reductio, because the interesting question is not whether omissions are causes but why we single out some of them, and not others, for the label.

I want the argument to carry a specific load, and to stay clear of the standard trolley fare. First, explain why the counterfactual account of causation — an event is a cause of an outcome just in case, had the event not occurred, the outcome would not have occurred — appears to force the conclusion that omissions are causes, and why it simultaneously threatens to make everything a cause, since for any outcome there are indefinitely many things that did not happen and without which it would not have occurred. Second, argue that this over-generation should not be patched by narrowing the definition of causation but read as a clue: distinguish the causal structure of the world from the process of causal selection, our singling out one condition among many and crowning it the cause. Third, test that distinction on a case I invented, which I suspect is where the account either earns its keep or falls apart. An operator sits at a dead man's switch: the core stays cool only while she holds a button down, and if she releases it, the coolant stops and the core melts. One afternoon she is distracted, her hand slips, and it melts. Was the meltdown caused by her omission, by the machine's action, or by a flaw in the design? And what, if anything, changes if instead she reaches over and deliberately presses a second button marked 'coolant cutoff'? If your selection principle cannot say why we fault her in one case and the designers in the other — or cannot say why we might be wrong to — then say so plainly.

Finally, calibrate the result rather than simply delivering it. State how confident you are in the conclusion you reach, and single out the one step in the argument you are least sure of, with a sentence about what would have to be true for that step to fail.
