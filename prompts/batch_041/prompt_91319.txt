I'm building an interactive flowchart for readers of a long-form piece I'm writing on the philosophy of reasons, and I'd like you to design the instrument itself.

Set the scene inside the instrument. The anchoring case is a powerful being — call him the demon — who will reward you lavishly if and only if you admire him. Readers who grant that the promise gives them *some* reason to admire him, but not the kind of reason that makes him admirable, have just walked into the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: if to be good is just to be fitting to admire (the fitting-attitude analysis of value), and a reward can make admiration fitting, then a reward can make its target good. Assume readers arrive already granting that the promise is not the right kind of reason to make the demon good; the flowchart's job is to sort them by what they think is going on instead. Feel free to seed the tree with one further case of your own invention, in the same vein but with a twist that the standard example lacks — a case where the reason is offered on condition that it does *not* do the work it is offered to do, or where the reward attaches to your having a reason rather than to your having the attitude, or some other wrinkle you think sharpens the diagnostic. Whatever you add, state it plainly in the prompt's terms so that a reader meets it fresh rather than as a reference to something they are expected to have read.

The deliverable is a decision tree, built from the logical space first and from a list of names second. Each branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may simply ask "are you a buck-passer?" — and each leaf should carry a short descriptive label for a position (you may note whose view it is, but the label must be intelligible on its own). Work through the tree in this order: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space, then a note on which answer combinations are unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Aim for six to eight leaves, and make sure the tree can distinguish, at a minimum: the view that the wrong-kind "reason" is really a reason for a different thing (for wanting the attitude, or for bringing it about, rather than for the attitude); the view that it is a reason for the very same thing as a right-kind reason, so that the fitting-attitude analysis simply fails; the view that the difference lies in what the reason is a reason *for* — the state of having the attitude versus the object the attitude is about; the view that the difference lies in whether the reason bears on the question the attitude constitutively answers; and the view that value is primitive and was never owed an analysis in terms of fittingness in the first place. If two of your leaves come apart only verbally, flag that pair explicitly rather than pretending the tree separates them.

The whole thing should read as a working tool rather than a literature survey: no citations, no reading list, no names required at the leaves, and nothing that would send a reader looking for a source you cannot supply. Keep the prose brisk enough to sit inside a flowchart box, with at most two sentences of gloss per node where a case needs explaining.
