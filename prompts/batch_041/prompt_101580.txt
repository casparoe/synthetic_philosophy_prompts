I'm grading an upper-level undergraduate exam on consent and autonomy, and I'd like your independent assessment of one student's answer. Please assign a grade (state the scale you use) and give the specific comments you would return: what the answer gets right, where the reasoning goes wrong, and how much the errors should cost. Grade the answer as written rather than completing or correcting it yourself.

Exam question:

A graduate student, R, is recruited by Dr. M, a professor in R's department, to participate in a study. Dr. M describes the study as involving "a routine cognitive task" and asks R to sign a consent form. In fact the study also includes a mildly aversive procedure to which R has a strong aversion; Dr. M omits this from the description. R, who depends on Dr. M for a stipend and a letter of recommendation, signs and says, "fine, I'll do it," although R does not welcome the prospect and is visibly anxious. R is an adult with no diagnosed cognitive impairment and understands the information Dr. M did disclose. Was R's consent valid? In your answer, distinguish the roles that deception, coercion, and impaired competence each play in defeating consent, and defend an overall verdict.

Student's answer:

Consent is valid only when an agent authorizes an act with adequate understanding and free of controlling pressure. I take deception, coercion, and competence in turn.

Deception. Dr. M's omission is a material deception. What was concealed is a feature of the act itself — the aversive procedure — rather than a mere side effect or incidental detail, and R would not have agreed had R known. Because R's agreement rested on a false description of what was being authorized, the deception defeats consent, and it is the central defect in this case.

Coercion. Dr. M has real power over R's funding and prospects, so R faced genuine pressure. But pressure is not coercion. Coercion requires a threat that leaves the agent no acceptable alternative; an unattractive or exploitative offer does not by itself invalidate consent. R could have refused and borne the consequences, so Dr. M's leverage did not coerce R, and voluntariness is not defeated on this ground.

Competence. R is an adult with no cognitive impairment who understood everything that was disclosed. Competence is the capacity to grasp the relevant facts, and one either has that capacity or one does not; R plainly does. R's anxiety is a feeling rather than a failure of understanding, so it does not bear on competence.

There is a further reason to doubt the consent. R did not want to take part: "fine, I'll do it" was grudging and unwelcoming. An agreement given without any welcome is not a genuine authorization of the act.

Overall, R's consent was not valid, chiefly because it was procured by material deception; the absence of welcome supplies an independent ground for doubt.
