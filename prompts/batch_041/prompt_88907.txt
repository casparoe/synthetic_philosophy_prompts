I serve on the board of a small nonprofit that documents a single mass-atrocity episode — testimony, satellite imagery, forensic and archival records — and also runs a field office in the affected region with fourteen locally hired staff. I am a software engineer by profession, with no legal or philosophical training; I was asked to draft the board's decision memo because I am the member who writes carefully. I am genuinely undecided, and I have deliberately described the case rather than naming it, so that the memo turns on the record and on our criteria rather than on whatever a reader already believes about a familiar case.

What we must decide. Our annual report goes to the printer in three weeks and the board meets Thursday. The report's opening statement either will or will not use the word genocide about the episode, and the same choice governs the metadata in our public archive. The options before us are: (1) use the word without qualification; (2) do not use it, describe the acts, and let readers draw their own conclusion; (3) use it with an explicit qualification — state that the record meets our published criteria, note that no court has so found, and say plainly that we are not a court; (4) adopt the criteria document described below but defer the naming decision until a specified piece of evidence arrives. Recommend one, with a date and a named signatory.

The record, briefly. Over roughly eight months in one district, about nine thousand people were killed, almost all of them men of military age plus teachers, clergy, and local notables; some two hundred thousand were displaced; churches were demolished and a manuscript library was burned; children were placed in state boarding schools and their names were changed. No recovered document uses the word destroy or identifies the group as a group to be eliminated. Internal memos speak of restoring the demographic balance and clearing the corridor. A recorded speech by a mid-level commander to his unit contains the sentence, "these people will not be here in ten years." Against intent: a rival armed group carried out reprisal killings in the same district during the same period, the campaign was publicly framed as counterinsurgency, and in a later phase the state permitted some displaced families to return. Our research director argues that pattern evidence alone cannot establish the intent the law requires; a partner organization's legal memo argues that it can, and routinely does.

The draft criteria. Our research committee has circulated a criteria document the board has never adopted. It requires (a) a finding that the victims were targeted as a group of one of the four kinds the 1948 Convention protects, and a rule for whether that group is defined by how its members see themselves or by how the perpetrators saw them; (b) a finding of specific intent; (c) a threshold for what counts as destroying a group in part — whether it is enough that the victims were the group's leadership and its capacity to reproduce itself; and (d) a rule for handling cultural destruction, which the Convention's definition does not cover even though Raphael Lemkin's original conception of genocide included it. The document also proposes what it calls a two-key rule: the word may be used publicly only if both a legal-category finding and a separate communication judgment are satisfied, the latter being a counterfactual about whether naming improves protection or instead hardens denial and closes access. I cannot tell whether that second key belongs in a criteria document at all.

Also relevant: the host state's foreign ministry has said publicly that organizations adopting the term will lose accreditation, and two of our field staff have already been questioned. One major donor has indicated they would fund a genocide documentation initiative but not an atrocity documentation one; another has said they will withdraw if we politicize the archive.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend one course of action, with a deadline and a named signatory. It should apply the draft criteria to the record and tighten every term too vague to decide the case, written as if for two analysts who must reach the same answer on the same record — I want the criteria to behave like a specification, not a set of aspirations. It should say whether we ought to be using the word in its legal sense, its ordinary moral sense, or both, and whether one word can do both jobs without corrupting either. It should take the strongest version of the option you reject, not a straw one. It should say what the naming decision obliges us to do next for people still in the region, given that we cannot protect anyone, and separately what we owe the fourteen staff whatever we decide. Give me the criteria document you would actually adopt, in the tightest form you can defend. Do not give me citations or a reading list — I have no library and no way to check references — but name a concept plainly if it has a standard name.

Finally, include a section stating what would change your recommendation: the specific factual finding or argument that would flip it, and where in your own reasoning it is most likely to fail. I would rather circulate a memo that names its weak joint than one that projects confidence it has not earned. Three pages at most, with headings.
