For my school's philosophy club zine I'm writing a parody of an employee handbook, and I'd like you to draft it. The conceit is that a company has quietly abolished the category of the supererogatory: every good deed that used to be merely praiseworthy — staying late to cover a colleague, giving a kidney to the founder's nephew, throwing yourself on the proverbial grenade — has been reclassified as a baseline condition of employment, and the reasoning is laid out in earnest, faintly menacing human-resources prose. The humour should come from getting the philosophy right rather than from generic corporate jokes: the distinction between what duty requires and what exceeds it, why praise rather than blame is the fitting response to the latter, what happens to that distinction once the supererogatory is made obligatory, and the uncomfortable possibility that if the category collapses, morality either becomes impossibly demanding or loses something it needs. A reader who has thought about supererogation should recognise that the author knows the terrain; a reader who hasn't should still find it funny and slightly unsettling. Please invent a plausible company so the piece doesn't read as a swipe at any real employer, keep it to around 500 words, and let the ending leave the reader genuinely unsure whether the concept of the supererogatory is coherent or whether the handbook is right that there was never anything beyond duty. Don't name any actual philosophers or works — I want the ideas to carry the piece, not the citations — and write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, numbered clauses, bold text, or tables, the way a memo would read if someone simply wrote it out.
