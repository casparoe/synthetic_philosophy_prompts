I am assembling a specification for a theory of value incommensurability, and I want the desiderata written out before I evaluate any candidate. The theory is meant to earn its keep in a practical setting: a standing ethics committee that must choose, several times a year, among options that realize different and ostensibly incomparable goods — a hospice expansion against a transit line, a restoration project against a debt-relief fund — where the committee cannot wait for the values to resolve onto a single scale, and where its decisions will be defended in public. The standard of adequacy I need, then, is not metaphysical tidiness but the ability to say, of an actual hard choice, what kind of difficulty it is and what the choice can rationally be.

Write the desiderata list: the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must remain consistent with, each with the consideration that motivates it. Among the cases I want handled are ones drawn from the literature and ones I have constructed. From the literature: the small-improvement case, in which neither option is better than the other, yet a trivial improvement to one does not make it better. Constructed by me: a case in which an option is incomparable with each of two others that are themselves ranked, so that incomparability must either transmit across the pair or demonstrably fail to; a case in which the incomparability holds between the values themselves rather than between two particular options; a case in which the incomparability seems to arise only from the agent's incomplete self-knowledge and dissolves once she understands her own commitments; and a case in which a choice is rationally permissible and yet leaves a residue of regret that the theory must either license or explain away. The distinctions I care most about keeping separate are these: incomparability from indifference, from rough equality, and from the mere closeness of two comparable options; incomparability of the options from incomparability of the values; and a choice's being hard because of the values from its being hard because of the agent. As for results, I take it the theory cannot contradict the transitivity of betterness wherever betterness is defined, cannot make rational choice impossible in the ordinary case, and cannot be shown to license a money pump — but I want to be told if these constraints are in tension with the rest.

Then say which of the desiderata are genuine requirements and which are parochial to one camp, and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect the demand that incomparability be a real feature of the options cannot be held together with the demand that rational choice always issue in a determinate verdict, but I want your argument, not my guess — and which single requirement does the most to eliminate the field. Finally, score the leading contenders against the list: the strong incomparabilist view on which a hard choice is hard because no ranking exists; the parity view on which the options are comparable in a way weaker than betterness; the commensurabilist view that supplies a single currency; the view that betterness is determinate but sometimes vague; and the voluntarist view that reason runs out and commitment takes over. Keep the scoring brief; the list is the deliverable.
