I'm taking a seminar on statutory interpretation, and the final exercise requires each of us to defend a position we don't actually hold against someone who does. My instincts run textualist, so I need you to play a purposivist judge and press me hard. Make her the serious version of the view rather than a straw man: she treats a statute's aim as the primary guide to its words, but she concedes that clear operative text ordinarily controls, that the absurdity doctrine is narrow, and that a purpose-based reading is disqualified if it requires deleting or rewriting words rather than selecting among their ordinary senses. She should give ground on details without surrendering the main claim, write in the measured register of a carefully reasoned appellate opinion, and stay in character until I say "stop." Replies of one or two paragraphs suit a conversation better than a memorandum. You don't need to cite anything — argue from the materials I give you, and I'll supply authorities later if I decide I want them.

To keep us off the well-worn examples, I've drafted a statute for a fictional jurisdiction — call it the Commonwealth of Arden — so that we're arguing about a text neither of us has seen before. The Arden Heritage Rivers Act opens with a purpose clause: "The purpose of this Act is to restore and protect the ecological integrity of the Commonwealth's designated heritage rivers." Section 4 provides that no person shall discharge dredged material into a designated heritage river except pursuant to a permit issued under section 5. Section 5 provides that the Department may issue a permit under that section only for maintenance dredging of a channel designated for navigation under the Commonwealth's waterways plan. Assume the legislative history says nothing about habitat restoration, that the Department has for a decade issued permits for sediment removal undertaken solely to rebuild spawning gravel in such channels, and that a reviewing court must now decide whether those permits were lawful. There is no severability clause and no definitions section; the Act's only other provision appropriates money for "riverine restoration projects."

My opening move: your judge says the preamble's ecological purpose should inform what counts as "maintenance dredging of a channel designated for navigation," so that removing sediment to rebuild spawning gravel qualifies even though nothing about navigation is being maintained. But that phrase names a specific kind of channel under a specific planning instrument, and if purpose can stretch a category fixed by that instrument into a category fixed by a goal, then the operative words in sections 4 and 5 are doing no work the preamble wasn't already doing — which is precisely the outcome your own concession about rewriting words is meant to forbid. The appropriation for "riverine restoration projects" makes it worse for you, since it shows the legislature knew how to fund ecological restoration when it wanted to, and chose a different instrument. So tell me: which words am I allowed to keep, and why isn't your reading just the preamble with a permit process bolted on?
