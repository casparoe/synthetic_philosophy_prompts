The question was:

Question 3 (20 marks). A fictional community of craftworkers uses the term 'brindle' for a class of hand tools. Four tools are uncontroversially brindles:
A: curved blade, wooden handle, used for scraping.
B: curved blade, wooden handle, used for cutting.
C: curved blade, metal handle, used for cutting and scraping.
D: straight blade, wooden handle, used for cutting and scraping.
The community withholds the term from E (curved blade, metal handle, used only for measuring) and F (straight blade, wooden handle, used only for measuring). When teaching the term, they point to A as the clearest instance, and they extend it to new tools by judging how close a tool is to A.
(a) Explain what it would be for 'brindle' to be a family-resemblance concept, and assess whether A–D fit that account. (6 marks)
(b) Distinguish the family-resemblance account of concepts from prototype and exemplar accounts, and assess what the community's use of A as the clearest instance shows about which account is correct. (8 marks)
(c) The community later encounters G, which has a curved blade and a wooden handle but is used only for measuring. Does the family-resemblance account give a determinate answer as to whether 'brindle' applies to G? Explain. (6 marks)

The script reads:

"(a) A family-resemblance concept is one whose instances are not unified by any single feature possessed by all and only those instances. Rather, the class is held together by a network of overlapping similarities: each member resembles some others in some respects, and no one respect runs through the whole class. The tools A–D fit this description. A curved blade is shared by A, B and C but not D; a wooden handle by A, B and D but not C; use for scraping by A, C and D but not B; use for cutting by B, C and D but not A. There is therefore no necessary and sufficient condition for being a brindle. The withholding of the term from E and F is also consistent with the account, since E and F each share only one of the four features with the class and so fall outside the network of resemblances.

(b) The family-resemblance account says that membership in a category consists in resemblance to other members. The prototype account says that membership consists in resemblance to a single summary representation, the prototype, which is the most typical instance; the exemplar account says that membership consists in resemblance to stored particular instances. Wittgenstein's own version of the family-resemblance view requires a single best example to anchor the class, since without a paradigm there would be nothing for the resemblance relation to be a resemblance to. That is the point of his idea of a language game: the meaning of a term is fixed by the community's agreement on the paradigm case. The community's practice of pointing to A as the clearest instance, and extending the term by closeness to A, is thus really a prototype account rather than a family-resemblance account. The stipulated case therefore tells against Wittgenstein's view and in favour of prototype theory, because the family-resemblance view cannot allow one member to be more central than the others.

(c) For a family-resemblance concept there is no rule that settles hard cases, so the community's practice is what decides. G has a curved blade and a wooden handle but is used for measuring. On the family-resemblance account there is no fact of the matter about whether G is a brindle: since there is no definition, any answer is as good as any other, and the community could as reasonably apply the term as withhold it. The account therefore gives no determinate answer, which shows that family-resemblance concepts are ultimately subjective or arbitrary."
