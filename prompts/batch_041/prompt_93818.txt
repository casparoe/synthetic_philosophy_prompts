Our philosophy class has been working through Aristotle's treatment of exchange, and I have been trying to extend it to an arrangement that could not have existed in his world. In that treatment he separates the household art, which uses money as a tool for getting what the household needs, from acquisition pursued for its own sake; he describes currency as an institution that arises by convention rather than by nature, whose office is to serve as a measure and a medium of exchange; and he singles out lending at interest as the most objectionable form of acquisition, on the ground that money is barren by nature and ought not to breed.

Now consider two features of the present financial order. First, several central banks have set their policy rates below zero, so that banks pay a charge for holding reserves at the central bank rather than receiving a return on them. Second, and more thoroughly, economists have repeatedly proposed a money that carries a fee for being held: a currency that depreciates automatically at a fixed rate, so that hoarding is costly and idle balances are pushed out into circulation, and which a programmable digital ledger would make trivial to administer, down to the level of the individual account, with the decay rate varied by circumstance. Ancient coinage could be debased, but no authority could arrange for every coin quietly to lose a fraction of itself each month.

What would Aristotle have made of this? Does a currency deliberately engineered to lose value fulfil his account of what money is for, or subvert it? Is the charge on idle balances an instance of the unnatural breeding he condemns, or is it the reverse, a device that restores money to its proper role as medium and prevents it from behaving like a commodity that one can keep and profit from? If the measure of value is itself decaying, can exchange still perform the commensurating work he assigns to it, or does a moving measure undermine the very possibility of the just equivalence between goods? And does a programmable currency, whose quantity, lifespan, and rate of decay are set by a central authority, confirm his view that money is conventional through and through, or does it expose something he missed about the extent to which money depends on the shared practices and expectations of those who use it?

Please commit to a definite reading of Aristotle rather than listing options, and say which elements of his position would have to be given up for him to accept such a system.
