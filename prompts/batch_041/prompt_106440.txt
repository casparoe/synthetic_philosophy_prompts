I reconstructed the following argument for a seminar, from a remark a colleague made and from my own attempts to make it precise. It is meant to show that the indicative/counterfactual contrast is not a semantic contrast at all.

1. An indicative conditional is accepted, relative to a body of information, exactly when the consequent holds throughout those possibilities that the information leaves open once the antecedent is added to it.
2. A counterfactual conditional is accepted exactly when the consequent holds throughout those possible worlds in which the antecedent holds and which are otherwise as close as possible to the actual world.
3. The two procedures differ in exactly one component: which worlds the antecedent is permitted to range over — worlds compatible with the information at hand in the first case, worlds selected for closeness to the actual world in the second.
4. The counterfactual marking — the backshifted tense together with *would* — contributes no further element of content. It signals only that the antecedent is being treated as closed rather than as a live possibility (in the usual formulation, that the antecedent is taken to be false).
5. That signal is a presupposition, and presuppositions are not part of what a sentence says. They are conditions on felicitous use, not on the world the sentence describes.
6. Therefore an indicative and a counterfactual that share an antecedent and a consequent have the same truth conditions, and so the same truth value whenever both can be used felicitously.
7. The counterfactual's presupposition is satisfied whenever the antecedent is false, and when the antecedent is false there is nothing to prevent the indicative from being used too.
8. The indicative *If Oswald did not shoot Kennedy, someone else did* is true.
9. So the counterfactual *If Oswald had not shot Kennedy, someone else would have* is true.

Here is my difficulty. I am happy to grant (1), (2) and (8), and I cannot accept (9): (9) is a substantive claim about a second gunman, and it ought not to be settled by the bare fact that Kennedy was shot by someone. Worse, the argument seems to generalize. Wherever the consequent is actually true, the corresponding counterfactual comes out true, so counterfactual reasoning collapses into noting how things in fact went. Two complications I have noticed and cannot place. First, some people will want to say that (8) is only vacuously true or merely assertible rather than true; I want you to tell me whether that escape is available and what it costs, not to assume it. Second, counterfactuals whose antecedents are in fact true can still be used and can still be informative — a diagnostician may reason that if the patient had taken arsenic, he would have displayed precisely the symptoms he in fact displayed — which makes me wonder whether (4) is even the right account of the marking.
