I'm a philosophy PhD student who's been doing a part-time embedded thing with a lab's safety team for the last year — mostly sitting in on their model review meetings — and there's something about the shape of the practice that I can't stop chewing on. Every time a new model finishes training, before it goes anywhere near users, they run what they call dangerous capability evals: a set of standardized tasks probing whether the model can actually help someone do specific bad things (design a nasty pathogen, find and exploit a vulnerability in real software, run a persuasion campaign at scale), they score it, and if the score crosses a threshold the model gets held back regardless of anything else about it. Whats striking is how deliberately those evals are kept away from the alignment side of the house. The capability scores get run on base models or lightly tuned ones, the evaluators are told explicitly not to worry about what the model is trying to do, and the score is treated as a property of the model — one number that's supposed to mean the same thing no matter what the model's dispositions turn out to be. Then in the same meeting, whenever someone has to justify why the threshold exists at all, the argument is always the power-seeking story: a system that good at bio design will, whatever its goals happen to be, end up in a position to grab more resources, dodge being shut down, resist correction, and so on, so capability is the thing to measure and capability is the thing to worry about. A postdoc told me the first half of what I'm describing is just the orthogonality thesis and the second half is instrumental convergence and that I was confusing them, which may be true, but I want to know which one the actual practice is leaning on.

So: what is this practice presupposing, exactly? That capability and motivation come apart, so you can hold the first fixed and vary the second, and the eval measures something real independently of what the model wants? That there's a goal-independent notion of how capable a system is, or at least a goal-independent notion of how dangerous its capabilities are? That instrumental drives converge across goals, which is what turns a capability score into a threat score no matter what the goal is? Are those three the same commitment or three different ones, and which does the practice genuinely need versus which is just the story people tell when a funder asks? The part I really want is the counterfactual: if we stopped believing capabilities and values are seperable — if we thought training bakes them into one package, so that being able to do X isn't well-defined apart from being disposed to do X — what breaks first? Can the practice survive on per-goal threat assessment instead of a single capability number, or does it need the convergence claim in order to say anything at all? And is there a hidden fourth commitment, that more capable always means more dangerous, which I suspect is stronger than either of the two theses and not implied by them?
