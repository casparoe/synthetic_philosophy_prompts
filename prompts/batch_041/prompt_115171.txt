Here's a position I've talked myself into. Kant's Perpetual Peace isn't just a nice thought experiment — I think the substantive thesis is basically right: perpetual peace isn't a utopain hope but a natural, and therefore achievable, outcome, and you get there with three ingedients. First, states with republican constitutions. Second, a voluntary league of those states rather than one world republic. Third, the pressure of commerce, which makes war too expensive to be worth it. And the elegant part is that you don't need anyone to become an angel: it's a mechanism, not a moral conversion. A world of trading republics drifts toward peace on its own.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which are empirical and which are conceptual. A case of two republics going to war is the obvious empirical kind — but what exactly does such a case have to look like to be a real counterexample rather than one we can wave away?

On the conceptual side, here's the worry I keep circling. A league that can't coerce its members is just a treaty, and treaties get broken. A league that can coerce them is a world state, which is more or less what the second article is written to avoid. So either the peace is toothless or it violates its own conditions. Is that a real dilemma, or am I missing a third option?

A few other things I'd like you to chew on:

- The thesis seems to come in two flavours: a hard empirical one (republics in fact almost never fight each other, and it's because they're republics) and a soft regulative one (reason tells us to act as if peace were reachable, so no pile of history could ever refute it).
