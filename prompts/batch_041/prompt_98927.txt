A friend of mine, Maya, is trying to recruit four of us into a business she wants to start, and she has proposed a condition for joining. Before we begin, each of us is to place a substantial sum — for me, roughly a year's savings — into an escrow account that we forfeit if we leave the venture within its first two years. Her argument is that this is the only honest way to learn who is serious: anyone can say they are committed, but only someone who genuinely is will risk real money, and it is the risk itself that makes the promise believable. She has added that she would think less of anyone who refused.

In the abstract I find this persuasive, but something about our particular group bothers me. The person most enthusiastic about the escrow is Maya's cousin, who inherited enough that losing the entire sum would not change how he lives; the person most reluctant is a single mother who has been the steadiest and most generous of the four of us, and for whom a forfeited year of savings would be ruinous. So an arrangement meant to expose who is committed seems, in this group, to be sorting us in something closer to the opposite direction.

I want to understand what is actually happening here, and I do not want to be told merely that incentives do their work. Under what conditions does requiring a costly sacrifice genuinely reveal commitment, and under what conditions does it instead reveal something else — the ability to absorb the loss, or a willingness to gamble with money that is not really at stake? Does it matter, for what the sacrifice tells us, whether the cost falls more heavily on the people who would keep their word or on the people who would not? And setting that aside: even if the escrow would function as a filter, does its functioning justify it, given that it deliberately destroys value for everyone who honors it? Please treat these as separate questions, and tell me how they connect.
