I spent thirty years doing experimental work in a physical chemistry laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than competence. The habit I cannot shake from the first career is asking of any number: what is this a measurement of, over what range, with what error, and what would count as evidence that it has failed? I keep finding that the numbers I encounter in my new reading are asked none of these questions. I would like you to write me a satirical op-ed that turns on exactly that gap.

The subject is the annual international happiness rankings — the league table whose headline placement of each country rests on a single survey item in which respondents rate their own current life on a ladder running from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. The same publication reports a decomposition of national scores into a few national-level factors (income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption), which is offered as explanation rather than as part of the score.

Write the piece in the voice of the director of a national well-being office in a fictional country of your own invention — nothing that could be mistaken for a real place — which has just risen to first place in those rankings. It runs as an op-ed in that country's leading newspaper, under a headline and the director's invented byline. He is delighted, fluent, and entirely sincere. He never winks at the reader. The comedy should arise from the reader's slow recognition that every triumph he lists moved the number without making anyone's life better, and that in boasting he has explained, in perfect good faith, how the number is manufactured.

Let him describe at least four such triumphs. You may use or replace these: a national campaign instructing citizens that the ladder question asks about life as a whole rather than the past week; a decision to field the survey immediately after the harvest festival; a change in the order in which the ladder question is put relative to the income questions; a reclassification of nonresponses; a gratitude initiative; and, as his crowning achievement, a rise of a few hundredths of a point. If it suits him, he may also treat the unexplained residual in his country's score as evidence of national character.

He should also make one argument that is genuinely good — that a government which pays no attention to how its citizens feel is failing them, and that a crude index is better than no attention at all — and then betray it by treating the index itself as the thing to be maximized.

Three thoughts should be left with the reader without ever being stated: that a figure can be stable, replicable, and comparable across countries while still not measuring the thing it is named after; that a country can raise its score without making anyone happier, and make people happier without raising its score; and that the whole exercise presupposes that one person's seven can be weighed against a stranger's seven. Do not have the director voice or rebut any of these. Have him walk past them.

About 700 words of continuous first-person prose, deadpan throughout, in American English. Do not name real people, real countries, or actual published scores; the country, the office, and the figures are yours to invent. Someone who has handled survey data should laugh; someone who has not should still understand the joke.
