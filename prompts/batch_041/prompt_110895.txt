I retired three years ago after a long career in experimental physics, and since then I have been teaching myself philosophy, chiefly epistemology, in a small reading group that meets every other week. At our most recent meeting two of the other members, whom I will call Marta and Daniel, fell into an argument that I found myself unable to referee, and I would rather have your adjudication of the argument as it was actually made than a summary of what the literature says about the subject.

Daniel began from what he took to be an unremarkable fact: two people, both long dead, once had a private conversation that was never recorded and left no trace, so that what they said to one another is a truth that no one will ever know. He concluded that not every truth is knowable, and that any view which holds that every truth is knowable is simply false. Marta answered that he had shown only that we do not know what was said, not that it could not in principle be found out, and she added that if truth were allowed to float free of what could in principle be discovered, the notion would lose its connection with inquiry altogether. Daniel then produced what he took to be a decisive case. Take that conversation, whatever it was, and consider the compound claim that it concerned a particular subject and that no one knows that it did. That claim, if true, is a truth, so by Marta's principle it must be knowable; but whoever came to know it would know both that the conversation concerned that subject and that no one knows that it did, and the first of those makes the second false. Daniel concluded that her principle therefore fails on a truth of an entirely ordinary kind. Marta replied that the case shows at most that her principle needs more careful statement — that it should be confined to truths which do not themselves mention knowledge — or else that the fault lies in Daniel's assumption that knowing a conjunction amounts to knowing each of its parts. Daniel's final word was that a restriction of that kind is ad hoc, and that once she has restricted the principle she owes an account of why the restricted version is the interesting claim, given that the point of her position was that truth cannot outrun what could be discovered.

Please adjudicate the five moves in order. Give me exactly five numbered items, one for each move, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion; in each item, say who has the better of that move, what the other party has and has not established, and what the move overlooks.
