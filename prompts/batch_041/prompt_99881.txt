Here's the thing that keeps me up at night: if experience really does go all the way down, then presumably suffering, or at least something that can be bad, goes a long way down too, and nobody seems to have a clean story about what that means for how we should act. I want to use this for a session with my little reading group (a mix of ex-scientists and one actual philosopher), so I need a decision tree that sorts out the positions on whether fundamental physical entities can be harmed — whether there can be suffering at the level of basic physics — and what follows from that ethically.

Please build it as a series of branch questions, each phrased so my group can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, starting from the broadest split and narrowing. Work from the logical space (which combinations of answers are even available) first, and only then put names on the positions; I don't need citations or a reading list, obviously. It has to include at least: constitutive panpsychism, cosmopsychism, the Whitehead-ish process/panexperientialist option, a Russellian-monism-with-neutral-micro-experience option, plain emergentism, and the deflationary line that the whole question is a non-question (the combination problem belongs somewhere in there too, and you can decide where). Pin each branch down with concrete cases, and please use these: two electrons sitting there; an entangled pair or a superposition; a photon getting absorbed; a lump of granite; a particle collision in an accelerator; and my personal favorite, the vacuum state. Two things about the answer itself. Second, give it to me as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no intro, no wrap-up, a couple of sentences each — with each item being one branch question plus a note on which positions fall on either side of it.
