I'm a fourth-year PhD student in constitutional theory, and since last spring I've been on placement with the research office of a state legislature — I write the constitutional-risk notes that go to committee chairs before a bill gets scheduled. Four or five a week, an hour or two each, and I'm the only person in the building with any con law training. The chair reads my note and decides; but if I flag a bill and it turns out to be nothing, I've cost the sponsor a session, and if I clear something that later draws a declaration, the office looks like it was asleep at the switch. My superviser keeps telling me I write law-review paragraphs when what she needs is a procedure — an ordered set of questions, with places where it stops and hands the decision back to a human. Take the role of someone who has actually done both constitutional litigation and legislative drafting, and start with a few lines on where you think the real difficulty in this translation is before you write the procedure itself.

Two things about our setting you should take as given rather than as loose analogies to federal doctrine. First, our bill of rights is enforceable but our review clause is weak-form: if a court finds a statute 'manifestly incompatible' with a listed right, it issues a declaration that doesn't void the statute, and the legislature then has to respond within one session. So the live question for me is never just would this be struck down — it's would this draw a declaration, and how much does that cost the chamber. Second, the rights list is mixed: maybe half are specific (search, jury, counsel), and half are open-textured — dignity, cruel or degrading treatment — plus one clause the courts have read as implying unenumerated rights. Most of my hard cases live in the second half.

So: write me the procedure. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps are identical whether a court is applying strict scrutiny or the deferential 'manifest' standard and which steps aren't, where the procedure terminates in a clear no, and where it has to stop and say this is a value call for the chair and not for me. Include a step that runs the same analysis on the status quo the bill would replace, because nobody ever declares a baseline incompatible and I keep forgetting that. It has to be runnable in ninety minutes on a bill I didn't draft. Then apply it to two things I'm actually sitting on: a bill tightening voter ID and cutting early voting from three weeks to one, and a bill letting a patient's family keep a comatose relative on life support for sixty days over the treating physician's objection, even where there's an advance directive saying otherwise. I like the first and find the second genuinely troubling, so if the procedure says the opposite about them, say so plainly and don't soften it. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do — which of my cases it will just get wrong.
