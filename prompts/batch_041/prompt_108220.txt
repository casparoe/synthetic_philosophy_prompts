I'm putting together a session for a little reading group on acausal / evidential cooperation — the idea that you might have reason to cooperate with agents you can't causally affect, because your choice is somehow evidence about theirs, or shares a cause with theirs. We keep getting bogged down because everyone labels themselves "evidential" or "causal" and then it turns out they mean totally different things, so I want to build a decision tree that sorts the actual positions rather than the labels. Don't worry about citing anything, I'll handle references myself.

Here's the angle I care about: don't start from "which decision theory do you endorse." Start from the structure of the *reason* someone gives for cooperating. I want the first branch to be whether the reason runs through any causal influence on the other agent at all; if it doesn't, the next branch should be what the non-causal link is — (a) your act is evidence about what a similar agent will do, (b) you and they implement the same decision function, or (c) you and they occupy the same situation index. Then I want a branch on whether the argument is offered as a claim about individual rationality or about impartial morality, and a branch on whether the "large world" — lots of potential partners — is doing any real work or whether the argument would go through with exactly one partner. Please seed it with these cases: two identical reasoners in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma who each know the other is identical; you and a distant civilization that has read the same evidence and reasons the same way but can never interact; and a case where you're unsure whether the other party is your exact algorithm or merely a close cousin. Leaves should include causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, functional/timeless-style views, superrationality, a Kantian universalizability view, rule-consequentialism, and whatever else the space makes room for — plus at least one leaf for "there's no reason to cooperate at all." Make each node a question someone could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to.

Give me: "The branching questions" — every node as a plain question, with the branches labeled, about 400 words; "The leaves" — for each, the position name, one line on what it says about the three seed cases, and its most awkward commitment, about 400 words; "Empty branches" — the combinations of answers that no position seperate, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that's incoherence, unattractiveness, or just unexplored, about 150 words; and "Pairs I can't separate" — any leaves my tree fails to distinguish, flagged honestly, about 100 words.
