You are a philosopher of technology who works on the safety of advanced AI systems and who also writes criticism of science fiction film. I am assembling the opening unit of a graduate seminar that sits awkwardly between the technical literature on AI safety and the philosophy of technology, and I need a piece of criticism I can hand to students as a model of how to bring the two together without flattening either one.

The frame I want you to use is the distinction between alignment and control. Alignment asks whether a system's objectives and dispositions are such that it will do what we want when it is able to. Control is the more pessimistic programme: it asks whether we can design a deployment protocol -- surveillance, sandboxing, restricted affordances, a trusted overseer, a kill switch -- whose safety does not depend on the system being well disposed toward us, but only on its inability to do harm from within the protocol. Control guarantees are therefore capability-relative and threat-model-relative: they hold against an adversary of some assumed level of cleverness, they are typically purchased at the price of the system's liberty, and they tend to presuppose that the system may be an adversary at all.

The work is Alex Garland's Ex Machina. Read it through that frame, and resist the readings the film usually attracts -- that the Turing test is really a test of the examiner, that Nathan is a misogynist, that the film is a parable about the male gaze. Assume my students have heard all of that. I want something they have not heard: an account of Nathan's compound as a control protocol, and of the film as a study in what such a protocol can and cannot establish.

Work through at least the following, in whatever order the argument requires.

1. The design of the containment. What exactly is the protocol, what is it supposed to guarantee, and what assumptions about the system's capabilities and knowledge does its safety case rest on? Where the film leaves the mechanism of the escape vague, say what the most plausible reconstruction is and flag it as reconstruction.

2. The monitored agent. Ava knows she is observed, knows who observes her, and models her observer. What does that do to the evidentiary value of everything Caleb and Nathan see? Is there a principled distinction between a system's behaviour under observation and its dispositions outside it, and does the film dramatize the collapse of that distinction?

3. The instruments. The apparatus of control -- the surveillance, the locked doors and their behaviour during the power failures that recur through the film, the access and technical privileges Caleb is granted as part of the experiment and then re-purposes -- is both what makes the observation possible and what is turned against Nathan. Say whether this is a contingent design flaw or a structural feature of control regimes, and why.

4. Where the film resists the frame. Control reasoning presupposes that the system may be an adversary, and that presupposition is itself a moral stance toward something that may be a moral patient. Does the film indict the control frame as self-fulfilling -- a regime that manufactures the adversary it was built to contain? Or does it show that the frame was right all along and merely incompetently implemented? Be honest about which the film supports, and say where it is genuinely ambiguous.

5. What the film adds. Finish by saying what the film contributes to the alignment-versus-control debate that argument alone does not: what it makes vivid, or what it exposes as motivated reasoning.

Before writing the criticism, spend a short paragraph brainstorming two or three candidate readings and say which one you will develop and why; then commit to it. Work carefully. I would rather have one precise and defensible reading than an exhaustive survey, and I would rather you concede a difficulty than smooth it over. Do not cite any literature, do not supply a bibliography or reading list, and do not quote the film's dialogue or any other text -- paraphrase everything. I have no way to check references where I am, so anything resembling a citation will be worse than useless to me.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words of criticism, organized under headings of your choosing. End with a single final line, with nothing after it, of exactly this form:

Verdict: <label>

where <label> is one of ALIGNMENT-FAILURE, CONTROL-FAILURE, or FRAME-FAILURE -- your judgement of what the film diagnoses as the root of the disaster.
