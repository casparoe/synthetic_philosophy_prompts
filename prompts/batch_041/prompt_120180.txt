I run a fortnightly reading group on normative ethics, and we have just spent two sessions on supererogation — the thought that some acts are morally admirable without being morally required, so that omitting them is not a failure of duty. The group has split badly, and I have been asked to write the exchange up as a dialogue between our two most stubborn members. I should say at the outset that I take them to be equally competent, and I do not want either of them winning by caricature: whoever has the weaker position should be given the strongest version of it, and both should concede ground when the argument forces them to.

The first of them defends the category. Her opening claim is that the gap between what is required and what is better than required is not an artefact of a lax moral code, because it tracks a real difference between two kinds of failure — the sort that makes resentment and blame fitting, and the sort that does not — and that theories which close the gap end up either demanding saintliness of everyone or denying that saintliness is admirable at all. The second is a sceptic, who thinks the category is a muddle produced by three conflations: of what morality requires with what it is reasonable to demand of a particular person, of blameworthiness with wrongness, and of the merely permissible with the genuinely optional. On her view, everything called supererogatory is either required and merely excused when omitted, or genuinely optional but not admirable in any distinctively moral way.

I want the dialogue to earn its keep by working through two complications the group could not resolve, and to have the two of them engage with each other's actual formulations rather than restate their own. The first is the division-of-labour problem: where a great many people could each perform some act that would prevent a disaster, and only one of them needs to do it, is the act supererogatory for any given person, or is it required of the group and optional for each member — and does that distinction survive when the group is unorganised and nobody has been assigned anything? The second is a case I invented to make trouble for both of them: a woman who sits on a register of two hundred potential kidney donors, none of whom has been approached, where a single donation would save a life but no particular donation is necessary, and where her reasons for hesitating are decent but not compelling. I want to see whether the defender can call the donation supererogatory for her without being forced into something implausible about the other hundred and ninety-nine, and whether the sceptic can call it required of her without being forced to say that all hundred and ninety-nine are each failing in a way that warrants blame. Neither character should name or cite published work; I want the argument carried by the cases and by the terms themselves, with each pressing the other on what exactly 'optional' and 'required' are doing in these examples.

Close with a short note, separate from the dialogue, saying which of the two you take to be in the stronger position once the exchange has run its course, which argument or consideration would lead you to come down on the other side instead, and where the dialogue is most likely to have been unfair to one of them or to lean on a contested reading of what supererogation is supposed to be. Please write in British English.
