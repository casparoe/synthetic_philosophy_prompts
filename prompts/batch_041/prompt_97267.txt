I have just come out of a long and fairly bruising argument with an old friend — she works on nineteenth-century narrative history, I have drifted towards the quantitative, structural-demographic end of the discipline — and it has left me considerably less confident than I was that my position actually hangs together. I should like you to audit it before I commit it to paper.

Here, roughly, is where I have landed.

1. Social formations are governed by robust and recurrent structural dynamics — the interaction of population pressure, elite overproduction, and fiscal strain of the sort modelled as secular cycles — and these are genuine causal processes, not patterns we merely project onto the past after the fact.

2. Because those dynamics are real, history is in principle predictable at the coarse grain: given a sufficiently rich quantitative description of a society's structural state, one can forecast its broad trajectory — say, an elevated probability of a particular kind of instability within a generation — with better-than-chance accuracy.

3. Explanation in history is nevertheless irreducibly narrative in form: to explain why some particular event occurred one must trace the contingent sequence of choices, accidents, and local conditions that produced it, and no subsumption of the event under a general regularity can take the place of that narrative.

4. Path dependence is real and pervasive: small early differences get amplified, so the same structural macro-state can issue in quite different outcomes, and the eventual result is not fixed by the macro-state alone.

5. Historical agents can come to know the very regularities that govern their societies, and when they do, their conduct alters accordingly — so a published forecast may be self-undermining or self-fulfilling.

My friend's sharpest objection was that I was quietly trading on retrodiction and calling it prediction. I am not sure whether that charge lands, or where.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and — for the genuine ones — rank them by severity. For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost. I would much rather you discriminate than hedge: where two of my commitments turn out to be compatible once a distinction is drawn, say so and say which distinction does the work; where one of them is doing two jobs at once, name it. Answer in British English, please.
