I am a retired limnologist — thirty-odd years spent on inland fisheries and lake nutrient budgets — and since retiring I have been trying to teach myself philosophy, chiefly by arguing with former colleagues and, now, with you. I should like to rehearse a position that I have never found entirely convincing but cannot shake, and I want you to defend it against me rather than explain it to me.

Please play a committed defender of what I shall call the structural-pessimist line about the commons: that a rivalrous, non-excludable resource tends to degradation by default, that the much-celebrated cases of successful community self-governance are not counterexamples to this but special cases which quietly presuppose favourable conditions — a small and stable group, cheap monitoring, low exit, and a resource that responds visibly and promptly to restraint — and that where those conditions are absent, neither privatisation nor state regulation nor communal norms is likely to save the resource. Give the position its strongest form: concede particular points where honesty requires it, but do not concede the main one, and do not retreat into saying that it depends — I want the default stated and defended. Stay in character until I say stop, break character only to ask a clarifying question if a term of mine is ambiguous and then return to it, and keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, in British English. If I press you with an empirical case, engage with the case rather than asking me for sources.

My opening move concerns a bay I shall call Kestrel Sound, which I studied on and off for a decade. The inshore fishery there was governed for about forty years by nothing more than an informal association of some sixty skippers: no quotas in law, no privatisation, no state enforcement, just an agreed rotation of grounds and a norm against fishing the spawning shoals. It worked, and the stock was stable. In the late 1980s the regional authority, worried about the tragedy of the commons, replaced the arrangement with individual transferable quotas — and within a decade the stock was in worse shape than before, because the quota-holders, now legally secure, raced one another to the shoals. You will say: special case, all my conditions were met, the community was small and could watch itself. But here is what makes me doubt that the commons is the real subject. Kestrel Sound's stock has a threshold: below a certain spawning biomass the recruitment curve collapses and does not recover on any timescale that matters to a skipper. The old rule was never aimed at limiting the total catch; it was aimed at keeping the stock above that threshold, and I think it was the threshold, not the excludability, that disciplined them. Suppose the same species had had a smooth, linear recruitment curve: I suspect the same association would have drifted into overfishing. Suppose it had had an even sharper cliff: I suspect they would have been stricter still. If the shape of the regeneration curve, rather than who may exclude whom, is what decides whether a commons survives, then what is left of your structural story? Is the tragedy of the commons really a tragedy of nonlinear dynamics, with property rights and norms merely riding on top — and if so, what work is the word commons doing at all?
