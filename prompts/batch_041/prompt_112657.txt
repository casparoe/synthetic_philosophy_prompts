I sit on the clinical-ethics review panel of a specialist neurology service. Our remit is to approve, refuse, or attach conditions to protocols for gradual neural replacement — treatments in which a patient's biological neurons are replaced, over weeks or months, by implanted silicon units that reproduce the input–output behaviour of the cells they displace. Until now we have reviewed these one at a time and argued our way to a decision; the service is expanding and we are now seeing a steady stream of protocols, several a month, and our disagreements have become unstructured. Some members treat the patient before us as one and the same person throughout; others ask at what percentage of replaced tissue the original 'ends'; others refuse to engage and vote on instinct. We have no shared method for sorting which of our disagreements are empirical and which are about values, and the decisions are grave: we are deciding whether to permit, in a conscious adult, an intervention that may or may not be an intervention on the same person throughout. I need a decision procedure the panel can actually run, in the same order every time, within a ninety-minute meeting, that does not presuppose that any of us has read the philosophy of personal identity.

Please write that procedure. Set out the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, given the material a protocol will actually supply; the point at which the procedure returns a refusal; and the point at which it must hand the decision to the panel as a matter of values rather than fact, marked as such. It has to remain usable by the lay members of the panel and by the chair, who signs the outcome. Keep clearly separate (i) the questions the procedure can settle on evidence from the protocol — what is actually replaced, at what rate, whether the patient's psychology, memory, and attachments persist, whether an earlier version continues to run or is stored elsewhere, whether consent is refreshed and by whom — and (ii) the questions it cannot settle without adopting a contested view of what a person is. Where a question in the second class has to be asked, say so plainly and say who should answer it.

Then run the procedure on three protocols:

(a) Replacement of each neuron by one functionally equivalent unit, one for one, over several months, with the patient awake, consenting, and psychologically continuous throughout.

(b) A protocol in which a continuously updated cold standby of the patient's neural state is maintained on the same device and activated only if the biological tissue fails, so that at some moments two systems both embody the patient's psychology and either could be the one that continues.

(c) A faster protocol in which the patient's interests, attachments, and avowed projects change substantially during replacement, and the person who emerges asks us to stop while the person who entered asked us to continue.

For each, give the verdict the procedure yields, the step at which it is reached, and what would have to be different for the verdict to change. At least one verdict should be one the panel will find unwelcome; do not soften it.

Finally, present the outcomes of the three applications as a table with exactly these columns, and say what goes in each:

- Protocol — a short label and one-clause description.
- Step at which the procedure concludes — the numbered question that decides the case.
- Governing question — the question, in the words the panel would use.
- Verdict — permit, refuse, or refer to a named person, with any conditions attached.
- What would change the verdict — the single alteration to the protocol that would move it to a different verdict.
- Residual values question — the contested question the procedure cannot answer, or 'none'.
- Who must sign off — the role, not the individual.

Below the table, add a short JSON object with the fields procedure_version, unresolved_issue, and limits_of_procedure, the last summarising in plain terms what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.

Write in British English.
