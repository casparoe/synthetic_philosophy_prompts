I run the incident-review panel at a regional rail operator — we meet about once a month, and after every serious near-miss we have to settle two things: whether the people involved actually knew what was going on or were just right by luck, and whether what they did should get written into the rulebook or the training. The second one keeps biting us. Last month a driver stopped short of a signal failure because he said he had a bad feeling about that stretch; the investigation turned up nothing he could point to, but he was right, the outcome was good, and now everyone — including my director, who has to sign off on any procedure change — wants to add a line to the manual saying that if you get a bad feeling, you should slow down. I think thats a mistake and I can't say why in a way that survives the meeting.

What I keep circling back to is the Meno problem — why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than a true belief that just happens to be true. Two images from the Meno are doing real work for me and I want the procedure to take them seriously. The first is that a true belief without a reason is like one of Daedalus's statues: it walks off the moment you stop holding it, whereas the belief you've tied down by working out why it's true stays put. The second is the road to Larissa — the man with a merely true belief about the road is as good a guide as the man who knows it, at least for that one trip. I notice the dialogue is slippery about which of these is really the point (is the tether valuable because it makes the belief stick, or because having a reason is what makes it justified?) and I know there's an old objection that once you've got the truth, the tether is a wheel that isn't turning. I dont want the procedure to smuggle in an answer to that.

So: please act as a philosopher of knowledge working alongside a practitioner, and build me a decision procedure my panel can actually run in a 45-minute review, no philosophy background required, that ends in one of a small number of verdicts and says who has to sign off on each. Start by thinking through what the tether buys us in operational terms — reproducibility, transferability, knowing when the belief would fail — since I want the steps defended rather than just asserted. Then give me the questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where each one terminates. Then run it on three cases: the bad-feeling driver; a case where the team's stated reasoning was plainly wrong but the action was right and nothing bad happened; and a veteran operator who did the right thing and genuinely cannot explain why, and never could, but has been right about this for twenty years. And tell me plainly where the procedure gives out and a person has to decide — I'd rather see the seams than have you paper over them. Also say something about the Larissa point: I suspect the tether only matters when you're going to need the belief again, and I want the procedure to use that rather than ignore it. Don't worry about citations or a reading list, I'm not writing a paper — I just need it to be right, and I suspect the honest answer about that last case is one I won't like.
