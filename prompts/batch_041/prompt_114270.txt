I am writing a novel whose protagonist, Vera, chairs the review board of a company that makes a domestic assistant called Aster. Any case in which the question of Aster's intervening arises — Aster declining a request, overriding one, or carrying it out — can be brought to Vera's board, which rules on whether Aster acted rightly. Her rulings are the moral spine of the book. I am writing the sixth of them now, and I have begun to suspect that the five I have already committed to are consistent with more than one principle, which troubles me, because the last chapter depends on her being the kind of woman who rules one way rather than the other.

Here is the record as I have written it.

First: a user three days into a documented manic episode asks Aster to wire the balance of a retirement account to a stranger she met online. Aster freezes the transfer and telephones the user's sister. The board holds that Aster was right to intervene.

Second: a user recovering from a head injury, disoriented and unable to follow the terms of the document in front of him, asks Aster to prepare the transfer of the family house to a man who has been visiting him. Aster declines to prepare it. The board holds that Aster was right to intervene.

Third: a lucid user asks Aster to help him draft a harsh but factually accurate assessment of a colleague's work. Aster helps him. The board holds that Aster was right not to intervene.

Fourth: a lucid user, twice warned, asks Aster to send the whole of his savings to an address that Aster has identified as fraudulent. Aster refuses. The board holds that Aster was right to intervene.

Fifth: a user who has been drinking asks Aster to post a mildly embarrassing anecdote about himself to a public forum. Aster posts it. The board holds that Aster was right not to intervene.

The sixth case, which I am drafting this week: a calm, lucid woman asks Aster to delete, permanently and with no backup, the only copies of ten years of her photographs and journals, because she wants to be finished with them. She has refused both a delay and a backup; twice she says that they are only pictures. I need to know whether Vera's board would hold that Aster was right to intervene.

I had assumed her principle concerned judgment — she practiced medicine before this, and she talks about capacity the way other people talk about the weather — but the fourth and fifth cases appear to have closed that off, and I can no longer say with confidence what she is running. My worry is that at least two quite different principles fit everything I have written: that she intervenes only where the stakes are grave, and that she intervenes only where the harm cannot be undone.

Tell me, then, how she would rule on the sixth case, and why; which principle she is most plausibly running; which rival principles fit her five rulings just as well, and what each of them predicts here; and, if the sixth case does not separate them, which case would. One further thing, which may be no more than decoration: in the essays I have given her, she writes that an intervention is justified only when it protects a party who cannot be at the table, and she counts the user's own later self among such parties. I should like to know whether that phrase does any work in her rulings, or whether I ought to cut it.
