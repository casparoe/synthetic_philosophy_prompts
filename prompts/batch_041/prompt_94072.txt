I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been trying to think through a distinction on my own and I would rather test it against a serious opponent than have it explained to me. Please play a committed defender of what I will call the co-authorship view: the view that an influence counts as legitimate persuasion only when it engages the other person as a co-author of the reasons she acts on, and that manipulation is whatever bypasses or pre-empts that co-authorship, even when it produces true beliefs and even when it is done for the other person's good. Make the character sharp rather than a strawman: he should concede that the line is fuzzy in practice, that motives are usually mixed, and that some manipulation may be morally permissible, but he should never abandon the core claim that co-authorship is what separates persuasion from manipulation. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, stay in character until I say stop, and do not drift into a general essay about what other writers have said. Here is my opening: I have been turning over a case I call the borrowed reason. A friend of mine quit smoking, not because anyone gave her an argument she could assess for herself, but because she wanted to be the sort of person I would admire. I never told her to quit; I only lived in a way she found admirable. She now gives reasons for quitting that are really my reasons in her voice. The co-authorship view seems to say that this is manipulation, because the reason was not hers to begin with. But it also seems like the best thing that ever happened to her, and I did not intend it. Which is it, and does your view have the resources to say?
