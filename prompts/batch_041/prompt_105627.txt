Consider a decision procedure that defends itself against acausal threats by adopting a surrogate goal. An acausal threat is a threat whose credibility does not rest on the threatener's ability to causally identify or reach the threatened agent, but on a correlation between the threatened agent's decision procedure and the threatener's policy: the threatener commits to penalizing agents whose procedures would not comply, and the threatened agent's procedure is itself one of the things that determines whether the penalty is triggered. A surrogate goal, in this context, is a commitment to inflict a serious penalty on some target if one concludes that one is the object of such a threat—a target with no intrinsic connection to the threat, whose suffering is not among one's final ends. The point of the surrogate is not the target's fate but the fact that the commitment makes one the kind of agent that acausal threateners will leave alone. Please describe the most important implications of this strategy for the design of a rational agent's decision procedure, for the ethics of precommitment when the penalty would fall on an innocent third party, and for the distinction between acausal deterrence and ordinary causal deterrence. I am especially interested in implications that are easy to miss: what happens when many agents adopt surrogate goals at once, whether the strategy is stable under reflection and under the knowledge that the target may itself adopt a counter-surrogate, and whether it can be distinguished from simply having a taste for retaliation. Please answer in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just paragraphs, as one would write in a letter.
