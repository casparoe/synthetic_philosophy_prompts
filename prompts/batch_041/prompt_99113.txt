Last night I had a long and increasingly heated argument with a close friend about P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes in "Freedom and Resentment," and we ended somewhere I cannot get out of my head. My friend insists that the participant attitude and the objective attitude are mutually exclusive: to the extent that you genuinely adopt the objective attitude toward a person — treating him as something to be managed, studied, predicted, or treated rather than as a party to whom you address demands — you have thereby stopped holding him responsible, and whatever resentment or indignation you continue to feel is merely a failure to carry the objective attitude through. I took the opposite view, and I want to see whether it can be made precise: that there is a class of cases in which the objective attitude is not a suspension of the moral demand at all but one of its most forceful expressions, so that the two attitudes can be fused in a single stance rather than merely alternating.

Act as a philosopher who works carefully on moral responsibility and on the interpersonal character of blame, and help me work this out. Begin by laying out, in your own words, the pressure points in Strawson's taxonomy — in particular the difference between excusing a particular act and exempting a person from the moral community altogether, and the fact that the objective attitude can take both benign and punitive forms. Then give me one example of a conceptual issue in this territory, worked out in concrete detail: I want a single carefully constructed case — a scenario, not a survey — in which adopting the objective attitude toward someone appears to be precisely how we hold them responsible, or in which the participant and objective attitudes turn out to be inseparable. I am especially drawn to cases where a person's incapacity to answer to reasons is itself something he brought about, since there the move that writes him off as beyond the moral community and the move that holds him answerable for what he has made of himself seem to collide; but construct whichever case you think best makes the difficulty vivid, and give it enough texture that I can see how a real person would be caught in it.

Do not survey the literature or give me reading suggestions — I want your own reasoning. Take a definite position on how the taxonomy should be repaired: say plainly whether Strawson's distinction can accommodate the fused case I am describing or whether it breaks under it, and defend that verdict rather than laying out the options for me to choose between. Think carefully and commit.
