Two philosophers have argued for years about vagueness without converging. One is a committed epistemicist: every vague predicate has a unique, fully sharp extension, fixed by facts about our language that we cannot in principle discover; borderline cases are cases of irremediable ignorance; the sorites premise is simply false, if unknowably so. The other is a committed supervaluationist: a vague predicate admits many equally admissible precisifications and no privileged one; a sentence is true when true on all of them, false when false on all of them, and otherwise neither; the sorites premise is therefore not true, though not simply false; borderline cases are genuine truth-value gaps rather than objects of ignorance. They have agreed to a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they can both sign.

It should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both accept: about the data to be explained (the sorites, borderline cases, higher-order vagueness, penumbral connections, the apparent tolerance of vague predicates), about the formal machinery each side is entitled to invoke, and about what would count as evidence for or against either view. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, with each side's position stated in wording its own proponent would accept. Third, the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side -- and, for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

To keep this from recycling the standard examples, test both views against the following constructed cases and record what each side predicts. (a) A safety committee, needing a minimum height for a ride, stipulates 122.0 cm, choosing that number because it is round: before the stipulation, was there a fact of the matter about who was tall enough, and does the stipulation create a sharp predicate or merely select among pre-existing precisifications? (b) The same committee makes the stipulation, but the record is destroyed and the decision forgotten, so that no one will ever know the cutoff -- does anything change for either view? (c) The committee also stipulates that children within one centimetre of the cutoff are to be treated as undetermined -- does this manufacture genuine higher-order vagueness, or only a first-order vague predicate with a perfectly sharp meta-boundary, and can either side live with the result? Close by saying which parts of the dispute, if any, are merely verbal, and whether any argument exists that both sides agree would actually settle it.
