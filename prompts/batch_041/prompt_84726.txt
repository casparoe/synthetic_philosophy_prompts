I'm in a little reading group that meets above a laundromat, and we've now spent three weeks chewing on Death in Damascus, the one where your in Damascus, Death is on its way to get you, and Death has already worked out where you'll be — so if you stay put, it's waiting for you in Damascus, and if you run to Aleppo, it's waiting for you there instead. The whole point of the example is supposedly that your running doesn't *cause* Death to be anywhere, it's just evidence about where Death already is, so the causal-minded theorist tells you to run anyway while the evidence-minded theorist shrugs and says you're dead whichever way you go. Everyone in the group has a take and their all boring, and my friend Priya keeps saying the example is just a trick and we should drop it, which I don't buy. So here's the take I want you to build and defend for me, as hard as you can.

My claim is that the whole fight is downstream of a mistake about what Death is predicting. Stop picturing the agent as choosing between two acts, stay or flee. Picture it choosing a *procedure* — a rule for how it'll act — and picture Death locking in after it sees the procedure but before it sees, say, the coin the procedure tells you to flip. Now run the numbers: always-stay dies, always-flee dies, but the coin-flip procedure survives about half the time, because Death can only camp out in one city and the coin sends you to the other. So at the level where the choice is really being made, the random procedure strictly beats every pure one, and Death's perfect prediction gets beaten by construction instead of by out-thinking it.

So: make that argument, tight, and don't let me off with hand-waving. Take the obvious objections seriously. First — doesn't Death just predict the coin too, in which case flipping buys me nothing and I'm back to being dead either way? If your answer is that a predictor who can see an indeterministic coin isn't the kind of predictor the example is even about, say exactly why, and be honest about what breaks if we let that predictor in. Second — if my argument goes through, does it actually vindicate the causal theory, or does it show that a decision theory has to be in the business of ranking procedures rather than acts? Those are different claims and I only want to win one of them, so pick and defend. Plain language please, and skip the history of the problem — I don't care who said what, I care whether the move works.
