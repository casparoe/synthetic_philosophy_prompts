I'm putting together the handout for the grad seminar's week on verisimilitude — Popper's problem of truthlikeness — and I want it to open with the spec, not the contenders. So write me that spec.

Here's what I mean. Before we start grading Popper's old sixties definition, or the Tichý–Miller style repairs, or the similarity-based approaches, I want the criteria list up front. What does any account of "this false theory is closer to the truth than that one" actually have to deliver? Which intuitive judgements have to come out right — one falsehood closer than another falsehood, one truth closer than another truth, and the nasty one, a falsehood that maybe beats a truth — which distinctions it can't collapse (accuracy vs. content vs. informativeness; a qualitative ordering vs. a numerical degree of closeness; the truthlikeness of a whole theory vs. of its individual predictions), which formal results it has to survive (the triviality arguments that killed Popper's original definition, and whatever else boxes a proposal in), and which of the usual demands are actually contested rather than non-negotiable — e.g. the demand that the ranking not depend on the language or the partition we happen to formalize the theory in.

For each desideratum give the case or the result that motivates it, and say how much weight you'd put on it: non-negotiable, or just a prejudice of one camp. Then flag any pairs you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — if some cluster is jointly unsatisfiable, that's the most interesting thing on the page, thats basically a result and I'd rather see it than another scorecard.

And keep your epistemic house in order: separate what's genuinely settled in the literature from what's still contested among people who know this stuff. Don't hand me one school's wish-list dressed up as the consensus. I dont need a bibliography or citations, I know the players — I want the criteria, argued.
