I chair the AI review committee at my university — we're the people who have to sign off before anything automated that makes decisions about students or staff gets deployed, admissions screening, plagiarism flags, that kind of thing — and we meet Thursday. The item I'm stuck on is from the registrar. They want to roll out one system that does two jobs at once. The first module assigns students to seminar sections. The second assigns faculty to committees, and yes, one of the committees it assigns people to is ours, and the whole pipeline is supposed to "audit itself" — it flags its own decisions for human review and then feeds the results back in as training signal. The registrar's framing is that this makes it more accountable than a human process, since every decision gets a second look.

Two of my members want to reject it on the grounds that we can't be the body that signs off on a thing that decides who's on us. We'd be marking our own homework, and the "self-audit" is a loop with nothing outside it. One of them keeps bringing up Escher's Drawing Hands — the lithograph where each hand draws the other — and her point is that for a picture like that to even make sense there has to be a level that isn't itself drawn, the paper or the artist, a set of rules the loop runs on without being part of it. (The phrase she uses for it is the inviolate level.) Her worry is that we don't have one here: every rule in this setup, including our own mandate, is itself revisable and was made by somebody downstream of the same process.

I need to write the committees decision memo for Thursday: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a recommendation we can actually act on and reasons the registrar can respond to. Take the "we're inside the loop" objection seriously rather than waving it off as an abstraction. If the right answer is that we can manufacture an inviolate level — a charter, a sunset clause, an external auditor, something — say what it would have to be made of to actually work, and what it would and wouldn't cover. And if the honest answer is that there's no such thing, say that too, and tell me what we should do instead.
