I finally got around to reading Kafka's The Metamorphosis last month — I know, everyone reads it in school, but I somehow got through my whole education without it — and I've been cornering my friends about it ever since. What set me off is that around the same time I'd been reading about the debate over whether insects can feel pain, and this argument people make about scale: there are so unbelievably many insects on earth that even if the odds any single one of them is actually sentient are tiny, the sheer number of them means the amount of suffering potentially in play is astronomical, way more than all the mammals put together. And then I pick up this little story about one bug, and it struck me that Kafka and the insect people are circling the same question from opposite ends — one gives you a single insect with a fully human inner life, the other gives you a number so big it stops meaning anything. As far as I can tell Kafka never even tells us what kind of creature Gregor turns into (translators seem to disagree about how to render the word he uses), and I keep thinking that has to matter somehow.

So what I'd like is a real reading of the story through that lens — insect sentience and the scale argument. Could you take the role of someone who actually works on animal minds and philosophy of mind but also genuinely cares about literature, think it through carefully, and maybe start by jotting down the scenes you'd build the reading around before you commit? The stuff I keep chewing on: the fact that Gregor keeps his human memory and his taste in music and his guilt about the family, but also starts wanting the rotten food and climbing the walls; the way the family's treatment of him slides from care to disgust to just wanting him gone, with the sister at the front of that; the father's apple; and that last bit where they're on the tram and everyone seems lighter, and the parents notice the sister has turned into a young woman. Is the story making a claim about whether a creature like that matters, or is it just about the family and shame? And here's the thing I can't get past — the scale argument lives or dies on a probability, but Kafka hands us the inner life outright, so does the story secretly assume the very thing the real debate can't settle, or does it show why we can't do the ethics without imagining one of them up close? I want it faithful both ways: don't just press the story into service as an illustration of a position, and don't bend the philosophy to fit the story — if the reading doesn't hold somewhere, say so, and tell me what the story adds to the argument that you can't get from the argument alone. Give it back to me as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
