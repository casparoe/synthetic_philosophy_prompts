After thirty-five years at the bench I retired, and I have spent the last two years reading philosophy with more appetite than method. The problem that got me reading was not one from the textbooks but one I lived inside for decades without noticing. The scientific society to which I have belonged since graduate school holds its members to a strict code: we may not accept gifts from companies whose products we evaluate or whose fortunes our judgments touch, and we must disclose every consulting fee, honorarium, and equity stake. The same society keeps a lobbying office in Washington, trains its fellows to make specific requests of their representatives, and spends substantial sums trying to shape appropriations and regulations — and the tax law under which it operates permits exactly that, allowing an organization of its kind to spend real money attempting to influence legislation while forbidding it to spend a single dollar for or against any candidate for office. Both the ethics code and that line in the tax law seem to me to encode a view of what is actually wrong with money in politics: a view that separates a private gift from an institutional campaign, and separates shaping a law from shaping who makes the law. I want that view reconstructed and judged, not catalogued. Take a definite position rather than surveying the field. Say what theory of improper influence the practice commits us to; what it assumes about representation and about what a legislator owes to the people who elected her; what it assumes about the difference between my pen and the society's lobbyist; which rival accounts of the wrong — a structural one that locates it in unequal access, or one that locates it in domination rather than in any bargain — it rules out, and which it merely happens to fit; and what would have to change in the practice itself if a rival account were right. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
