I am a novelist by trade, and next month I am giving the keynote at a small interdisciplinary conference on narrative and cognition. I am not a professional philosopher, but I have been reading Dennett on real patterns and the three stances, and I want to defend a view I call narrative pattern realism. The thesis is this: fictional characters can be real patterns in Dennett's sense, and the intentional stance toward them is legitimate, but this does not make them persons, concrete agents, or minds. A character is real as a compressible pattern that supports counterfactual predictions under the intentional stance, re-identifiable across independent readings, and grounded in the design stance we take toward the author's construction. The physical stance sees only marks; the design stance sees a designed artifact; the intentional stance sees a character with beliefs and desires. My new move is what I call the lending-library constraint: a character earns realist status only if it can be re-identified across readers and readings and supports counterfactual predictions about what it would do in situations the text does not describe. I want to avoid both the view that characters are mere textual constructs and the view that they are abstract persons.

The audience will be mostly analytic philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who work on Dennett, plus a few narratologists. They know the real patterns literature and the three stances. They are naturalists but suspicious of extending mentalistic vocabulary to fictional entities. Some are fictionalists about characters; some are realists about social artifacts; none will be moved by appeals to the magic of storytelling. They will press on whether my pattern realism is just fictionalism with extra steps, whether the design stance already does all the work, whether compression is too interest-relative to ground realism, whether fictional characters have causal powers, and whether I am confusing the intentional stance toward the author with the intentional stance toward the character. They will likely also raise the disquotational move from true-in-the-fiction to true simpliciter, the problem of inconsistent characters, the causal exclusion problem for abstracta, the interest-relativity of compression, the stance-stacking problem, and whether the counterfactual test is determinate when the text is silent.

Act as an expert in philosophy of mind, Dennett's real patterns and three stances, philosophy of fiction, and narrative theory. Think carefully. If you think my thesis is indefensible at a point, say so and say what I should concede instead. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I need arguments and phrasings, not references. Do not pretend to quote anyone.
