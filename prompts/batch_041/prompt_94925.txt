I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on Warring States Chinese thought, and we have been working through the disputers associated with the School of Names. I want you to compare two readings of the white horse argument attributed to Gongsun Long, and then compare the more defensible of those readings with the hard-and-white puzzle from the same milieu. The first reading takes the claim that a white horse is not a horse as a claim about non-identity: the compound expression white horse is not the same term as horse, since the former carries a color determination that the latter does not. The second reading takes it as a claim about kind-membership: the kind white horse is not included in the kind horse, because what satisfies the one does not satisfy the other. The word fei in the original can be read either as is not the same as or as is not a member of, and I want you to use that ambiguity explicitly. For each reading, explain how it would answer the objector who says that if you have a white horse you thereby have a horse, and say what notion of a name or kind the reading requires. Then bring in the hard-and-white puzzle, which asks whether hardness and whiteness can both belong to the same thing when they are known through different senses, and say whether the same semantic apparatus—treating names as designating determinate aspects or pointings rather than as labels for classes of objects—can handle both cases. If it can, show how; if it cannot, identify the additional distinction the hard-and-white case demands. Finally, tell me which of the two readings of the white horse argument makes the School of Names look like it is engaged in serious semantics rather than mere sophistry, and what the other reading would have to concede.
