Ok so background: I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political economy and I somehow ended up as the grad student rep on the advisory board of a small foundation (the Halden Trust) that funds governance and livelihoods work in Marenda, a landlocked country with one river port and a coastline nobody uses. We meet next Tuesday and I have to circulate the decision memo because our chair is traveling. Here's the mess I need help with.

The Trust has been funding a six-year "good governance" program — civil service reform, audit capacity, local council elections — in four districts, and it's up for renewal at about 40% of our annual budget. Meanwhile our program officer has been pushing a completely different theory of the problem. She says Marenda's rich/poor split lines up almost perfectly with soil quality and distance to the port, that the districts that stayed poor are exactly the ones the colonial railway bypassed in 1911, and that the governance program's apparent successes are just the richer districts being richer. She's got what she calls a quasi-experiment: a district boundary that follows a river and splits what everyone agrees was one precolonial polity into two districts with the same language, same chiefly lineage and same rainfall, but one side got a railway station and the other didn't. The station side is now roughly three times richer and scores much higher on our governance index.

The governance people say that proves nothing. The railway came because the colonial administration picked the side with the better anchorage and the existing trade, so the "treatment" was chosen on things that also predict income today; and anyway the index is measured by surveyors we trained, in districts we chose, so of course the districts we invested in score well. One trustee (an old economist who's been quiet for a year) has now sent round a one-paragraph rule he wants us to adopt: a factor counts as a cause only if, holding everything else fixed, removing it would remove the outcome in most of the nearby ways history could have gone. The program officer says fine, apply the rule and the railway passes and the governance program fails. A second trustee says both sides are rehashing a fight that's unfalsifiable and we should fund on our values and stop pretending it's an empirical question at all. And then there's a fourth position, from a visiting historian, who says the whole framing is wrong: Marenda's districts were drawn in 1962 by a minister who gerrymandered them to weaken one region, so the units everyone is running regressions on are themselves the residue of one guy's choices, and if you redraw the units the correlation flips.

I need a memo I can actually circulate Tuesday. Give me a concrete recommendation — renew as is, renew at half and shift the rest to a port-corridor and soil program, kill the governance grant and go all in on the corridor, or something else — with a time frame and the reasoning. Please take the causal-inference objections seriously rather than just intoning "correlation isn't causation," and say plainly what we can and can't know here and what follows for what we should do given that we have to decide now. Don't just tell me to "do more research," we have one more budget cycle and the districts need an answer this year. Keep it to a page and a half, and be honest about the strongest point on the side you come out against — I have to sit in a room with these people afterward and I dont want to be the one who strawmanned the trustee who pays for the coffee.
