I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Hacking's looping effects that a student can't get through by reciting the "interactive kinds loop, indifferent kinds don't" line — I want them to actually have to think. Here's the situation I want them to work with.

In their response papers, half the class keeps saying some version of: if calling someone depressed, or autistic, or whatever, changes how they act and feel, then the diagnosis is just a self-fulfilling prophecy, which shows psychiatric categories are labels we impose rather than real kinds. I want the exam question to force them to do real work with that instead of nodding along.

So the question should ask them to do all of the following:

(a) Pull apart two things the student could mean — that looping makes the extension of the category unstable across time and place, versus that looping makes the category's validity unassessable — and explain why those come apart. A category can have a drifting extension and still be perfectly assessable, or a rock-steady extension and be a mess to validate, and I want them to see that.

(b) Take the usual external-validator checklist (you know the idea — a decent category should predict course, run in families, respond to something specific, separate cleanly from its neighbours) and say which of those validators looping actually sabotages and which it leaves alone, and why. The move I want them to hit is that a validator is only threatened when the evidence it collects has to pass through the patient's own self-understanding or the clinician's expectation. So a validator that routes through the person's self-report is in trouble; one that doesn't, isn't.

(c) Deal with a hypothetical I'll hand them: someone proposes a new diagnosis whose criteria include, as a constitutive part, that the person understands themselves as having it — being someone who has X is partly what makes it true that they have X. Ask whether that's a fatal circularity or just looping of a special kind, and make them say what the difference is between a category that loops causally (the label changes you) and one that loops constitutively (the label is partly what the condition is).

(d) Say where Hacking himself lands — the dynamic nominalism bit, the mutual shaping of classifications and the people classified, and the fact that he later pulled back from the idea that looping was doing all the heavy lifting people wanted it to, and that making people up isn't just top-down imposition from the experts.

Also make sure they can't just write "looping undermines realism about psychiatric kinds" and stop. I want them to keep the metaphysical question (does the category latch onto a real kind?) apart from the epistemic one (could we find out whether it does?), because collapsing those two is the lazy move and it's what I'm trying to catch.

Then give me a model answer, say 700 to 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should knock it down to a B — things like confusing looping with plain iatrogenesis or nocebo, or treating looping as a blanket thesis about all psychiatric categories when it's contingent and has to be argued case by case.

Oh, and don't make it a bibliography exercise. None of them have read Hacking closely anyway, so keep it to stuff they can reason about from what's in the prompt.
