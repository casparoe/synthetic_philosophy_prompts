I teach a graduate translation workshop — twelve students, most of them fluent in two or three languages, one ninety-minute session — and the unit I'm preparing is built on the problem that Douglas Hofstadter made the center of Le Ton beau de Marot: that rendering a poem is not the transfer of a meaning but the simultaneous satisfaction of many constraints at once (meter, rhyme, sound texture, line shape, register, connotation, and whatever else the source happens to be doing), almost none of which survive a literal rendering, so that the translator is always forced to decide which constraint to sacrifice and how to compensate for the loss somewhere else. I want an activity that makes the students feel this pressure in their own hands rather than hear me describe it, and I want the concept to arrive only after they have already run into it. I'll hand each of them the four-line poem below, which I wrote for the occasion; it carries three constraints simultaneously — it rhymes ABAB, every line is exactly seven syllables, and the first letters of the lines spell L-O-S-S, a word that also names its theme:

Lonely winds begin to blow,
October burns the leaves red,
Summer's warmth has fled, and so
Silence settles in the bed.

Please design the full ninety-minute session around this text. I'd like it to be genuinely runnable: materials, roles or pairings if you want them, a clear sequence with timings, and a rule for what counts as success in each stage. I want the group translating into languages they actually command (their own strongest non-English language, or a language they share with a partner), and I want the rules arranged so that they cannot keep everything — that they discover by trying that the acrostic, the meter, the rhyme, and the literal sense pull against one another, and then have to argue about which losses are acceptable. Build in at least one stage in which the acrostic is declared untouchable and the sense may be freely altered, and one in which the sense is untouchable and the acrostic may go, so that the two horns of the choice cannot be blurred; I also want a closing moment where the room compares the surviving translations and sees that there is no single correct answer but a space of defensible ones, each trading differently.

Finish with a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the constraint-satisfaction picture of translation — where the group felt the generative side of constraint (solutions no one would have reached without it), where it felt the cost, and where the very word "form" turned out to be ambiguous, since sound, meter, visual acrostic, and semantic echo are not the same thing and cannot all be honored at once. Tell me candidly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — I'm thinking in particular of the slow, months-long craft of a real literary translation and the way an accomplished translator will sometimes rewrite the source's content wholesale to save its music. No lecture up front, and please don't let the vocabulary of the theory appear until the debrief.
