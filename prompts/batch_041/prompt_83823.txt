Ok so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm about forty pages from the end of a book and I need a poem to sit near the last page -- something my narrator could have copied into a notebook years earlier and still not be done with. Please take this on as a poet who has also read enough moral philosophy not to fudge it: act like someone who knows the literature on the non-identity problem and the person-affecting restriction, and who cares more about getting the ethics right than about sounding profound. Take your time with it, this one has to land.

The setup in my novel is basically the classic one. My narrator's mother, while pregnant, kept taking a drug, or skipped the test, or stayed in the flooded town, pick whatever is cleanest -- the point being that if she'd done otherwise, the timing of conception would have been different, and the person telling the story would not exist. Someone else would have, or no one would have. So my narrator can't say she was harmed, and can't point at anybody who was, and yet the whole book is soaked in the sense that something was done to her, or done to someone. Thats the thing I need the poem to hold.

Form: a pantoum. Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the closing quatrain brings back the opening lines so the poem ends where it started -- the circle is the point, nothing gets discharged. Here's the hard part, my real ask: I want the you the poem is speaking to to change, quietly, as it goes. The repeated lines have to stay word for word identical, so the reader can only work out from the surrounding lines whether the you is the mother, the narrator, or the sibling who was never conceived. By the end, someone who knows this problem should see that the you in the first stanza and the you in the fourth can't be the same person, and feel the ground shift under that, without you ever saying so out loud. And it has to work as a poem for a reader who's never heard of any of this.

Things to avoid: don't hand me a victim -- the cheap version of this poem smuggles in a harmed child and the problem evaporates. Don't drift into the repugnant-conclusion stuff about total welfare or future generations in general; stay with the same-number case, one child either way, and with whether you can wrong someone without making them worse off. I'd rather the poem end owing something to no one in particular than resolve into a tidy moral.

Before you write, brainstorm a little: list six or eight candidate lines that could carry the repetitions, say which ones could plausibly be addressed to more than one person, then pick. Then write it -- four or five stanzas plus the closing quatrain -- and after the poem give me three or four sentences on where you think the form is doing the philosophical work and where it can't reach. I'd rather see the seams than have them papered over.
