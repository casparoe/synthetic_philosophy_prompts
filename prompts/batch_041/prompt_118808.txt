After thirty-five years in experimental geophysics, I retired and have spent the last several years reading philosophy of mind with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have arrived at a package of views about consciousness that I would like audited for consistency before I inflict it on a seminar. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want to know which commitments pull against one another, which only appear to, and what the cheapest repair would cost.

My commitments are these. First, I accept the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness: there is something it is like for a subject, and separately there is information globally available for report, reasoning, and control. Second, I think global workspace theory is the best-supported empirical account of access consciousness, perhaps the only scientifically tractable part of the problem. Third, I am a realist about phenomenal consciousness: it is not merely a verbal or introspective illusion, and it can in principle occur without being globally broadcast. Fourth, I think the only evidence we can have for phenomenal consciousness in others is access-based, through reports, discrimination, and flexible control, so any ascription of phenomenal consciousness to another system must be grounded in access capacities. Fifth, I have recently come to think that access is not one thing: there is upstream access, a content's availability to bias downstream processing before it enters the global workspace, and downstream access, its availability for report and deliberate control after broadcast. I call this the two-door picture, and I hold that phenomenal consciousness attaches specifically to upstream access, while global workspace theory as usually stated is a theory of downstream access.

Please audit this package. I would like the tensions ordered by severity, with an honest discrimination between real tensions and apparent ones, and for each real tension a statement of what would have to give and what that would cost. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first two items should be the genuine tensions from most to least severe, the third and fourth should be apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, and the fifth should be the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most, together with its cost.
