I'm working through Thomas Paine's Agrarian Justice (1797) for something I'm writing about inheritance and inherited advantage, and there's a stretch of the argument I can't get straight. Here's the bit, from the middle of the pamphlet:

"It is a position not to be controverted, that the earth, in its natural uncultivated state, was, and ever would have continued to be, the common property of the human race. In that state every man would have been born to property. He would have been a joint life-proprietor with the rest in the property of the soil, and in all its natural productions, vegetable and animal.

But the earth, in its natural state, as before said, is capable of supporting but a small number of inhabitants compared with what it is capable of doing in a cultivated state. And as it is impossible to separate the improvement made by cultivation, from the earth itself, upon which that improvement is made, the idea of landed property arose from that inseparable connection; but it is nevertheless true, that it is the value of the improvement only, and not the earth itself, that is individual property. Every proprietor therefore of cultivated land, owes to the community a ground-rent; for I know no better term to express the idea by, for the land which he holds"

Two things I can't get my head around.

First, that inference in the second paragraph. He says you can't separate the improvement from the land it sits on, and then says landed property "arose from that inseparable connection." That looks backwards to me. If the improvement can't be detached from the soil, why does that give anyone a claim to the soil, rather than making the soil itself part of the common estate and the improvement the only thing anyone could own? What's the actual step he's taking, and is there a charitable reading where it isn't just a non sequitur? Or is he quietly assuming something he hasn't said — like that whoever improves a patch gets to hold the patch, because there's no other way to secure the improvement?

Second, if the improvement really is the cultivator's own — he says it's "the value of the improvement only, and not the earth itself, that is individual property" — then why does that same cultivator owe a ground-rent to the community for the land he holds? Isn't that rent a charge on the very thing he's just been told he owns? And what is the rent a rent *for*, given that the community never did anything to the land, and given his own admission that there's no way to separate the improvement from the bare earth? How would anyone even set the figure? I keep feeling the two halves of the paragraph pull against each other and I can't tell if that's Paine being sloppy or me missing the point.

I also want to know how much work an earlier premise is doing here. He'd said, a bit before this: "the first principle of civilization ought to have been, and ought still to be, that the condition of every person born into the world, after a state of civilization commences, ought not to be worse than if he had been born before that period." Is that the thing that generates the entitlement — i.e. the ground-rent is owed because the landless would otherwise be worse off than they'd have been pre-civilization — or is it a seperate argument that just happens to sit next to this one? And does the argument still work if you drop that premise and keep only the stuff about common ownership and improvement?

What I need isn't a summary of the whole pamphlet. I need the joints of this one arguement: what each sentence is doing, which steps are load-bearing, which are just asserted, and where a sympathetic reader has to supply something to keep it from falling apart. Plain prose, no reading list.
