ok so for my school paper i have to write an op-ed and i want to do a satire piece. here's the situation. our student council is trying to kill like four rules that nobody can explain -- no hats in the hallway, the thing where you can't sit on the steps by the gym, seniors getting the good parking spots even though there's no actual seniority rule written anywhere -- and every single time we bring one up, some teacher or the vice principal says some version of "well, you can't take a fence down until you know why it was put there." which is Chesterton, i looked it up, it's from a 1929 book. and honestly? the argument is kind of good?? which is the whole problem.

so what i want: write the op-ed as if the assistant principal wrote it. defending every one of those rules with that one principle, completely straight, dead serious, no winking, no jokes in the text itself. the funny has to come from how well it actually works, and then from how it starts eating itself. like, if the answer to every proposal is "go find out why the fence is there," what happens when we go find out and the reason turns out to be "the 1998 vice principal didn't like hats." is that a reason? does the argument need the reason to still be a good reason, or does any reason at all do the job? and if a rule surviving counts as evidence it's serving some purpose we just can't see, then the argument proves way too much -- it would have blocked basically every change ever made, including changes this school already made and is proud of. i want all of that in there without anyone in the piece ever saying it out loud.

one more thing and i'm not sure it's even right so don't build the whole thing on it: my debate partner says Chesterton was some kind of socialist-ish guy who wanted to tear down capitalism, so it's wierd that this is the argument people use to defend stuff, and that the actual conservative version is more Burke, where you don't need to recover a reason at all, the fact that it survived is itself the reason. if you use that, flag it as contested, don't let the piece depend on it.

about 650 words, formatted like an op-ed with a headline, voice of an administrator who is genuinely not trying to be funny and is maybe a little hurt that students don't appreciate the building. someone who's read the Chesterton passage should laugh instead of wince, and a kid who's read nothing should still get why it's funny. please don't let it turn into just "school rules are dumb" -- that's a different, worse piece.

at the end, after the op-ed, add a short note to me that is not part of the piece, saying which version of the fence argument you had the administrator lean on, where the satire cheats or leans on a reading of the argument that people actually fight about, and what would change your mind -- like what would have to be true about these specific rules for "we don't know why it's there yet" to be the correct answer and not just a polite way to end the conversation.
