A friend who is teaching himself Gödel, Escher, Bach explained Hofstadter's MU-puzzle to me, and I would like to know how much of his account to trust. He told me the following. You begin with the string MI and four rules for rewriting strings of the letters M, I, and U, and the puzzle is to reach MU. You cannot, and the reason is a clever trick: count the I's and notice that they never occur in a number divisible by three, while MU contains no I's at all, so it can never be reached. The moral, he says, is that some questions cannot be settled from inside a system, and that this counting trick is an example of stepping outside the system. He adds that this is Hofstadter's miniature version of Gödel's incompleteness theorem, showing that formal systems always contain questions they cannot answer; that the only way to see the truth here is to step outside and use a kind of human insight no machine could ever have; that the argument is therefore not itself checkable mechanically, since it is no longer a piece of formal reasoning; and that the invariant in fact proves MU is false rather than merely underivable. Which parts of this would someone who knows the material accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and which are simply wrong? I am inclined to believe the part about stepping outside the system being the real lesson, so tell me if that is where I should be most careful. Please lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then work through his account point by point.
