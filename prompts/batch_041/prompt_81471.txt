I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, most of it on long-term monitoring programmes, where the hardest part was never the measurement but the aggregation — deciding what a scattered set of noisy, unequal observations collectively supported. Since retiring I have been reading in social epistemology, and I now chair a nine-member science advisory committee for a regional environmental agency. We meet monthly and rate roughly forty proposals and draft assessments a year; our ratings steer about two million pounds of programme money, and our written advice is accepted or rejected by a board that meets quarterly. I can change our process — the order of business, who speaks when, what gets written down and when, what we publish — but not the membership, the timetable, or the fact that each item must end in a single recommendation. I have come to think the committee routinely mistakes "we agree" for "the evidence supports it," and that the two come apart for structural rather than psychological reasons.

Here is the machinery as I understand it. I would rather you correct this than build on it uncritically; if I have it wrong, say so at the top.

1. In an information cascade, when people decide in sequence and can see one another's choices but not the reasons behind them, each person's optimal move — once the public record is strong enough — is to disregard their own private information and follow the record. The group's answer is then fixed by the first few signals rather than by all nine; it is path-dependent, in that the same nine people holding the same total information reach different conclusions under a different order of decision; and it is fragile, in that a small addition to the public record, including the public disclosure of one person's private signal, can overturn a conclusion that had looked settled. Because the mechanism is rational rather than a failure of nerve, exhortation is useless, and the member who breaks with the record is not necessarily being heroic — it may only mean he has not taken in what the others saw.

2. A second mechanism produces the same surface pattern for different reasons: imitating the group in order to protect one's standing as a competent judge, rather than because one has actually updated. From outside the two are indistinguishable, and the remedies are not the same.

3. Both worry me less than the independence assumption. Condorcet-style aggregation and the wisdom-of-crowds argument both require the individual signals to be independent. If my nine members have all read the same three papers, or all run models that share one structural assumption, their agreement is not corroboration, and no ballot design repairs it. A related failure: when a group holds pieces of a puzzle rather than opinions about a question, it spends its time on the pieces everybody already has and never surfaces the one fact that only one member knows.

I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the machinery made usable by someone who has never heard any of it. Please give me a protocol I can hand to a new member and actually run, in the following sections.

1. Summary (about 150 words): the single most important thing I should change, stated as an instruction.
2. Why the obvious remedies fail (about 250 words): the fixes I would reach for first, and what is wrong with each.
3. The protocol (about 800 words): numbered steps in the order they are performed, divided into what happens before a meeting, during it, and after it. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what I am to do when I cannot get one. Include a short table mapping each warning sign to its remedy, marking the warning signs that look identical from the outside but call for different remedies. It must be runnable inside a ninety-minute meeting by people with no statistical training, and it must say which steps I can impose on my own authority and which need the board's agreement.
4. Three worked cases (about 650 words in total): (a) our ordinary monthly rating round, in which I currently open the discussion and the two most senior members speak next; (b) an item on which we are asked to endorse a position that is already the consensus in the field, so that our endorsement would be presented to the board as independent corroboration; (c) an item on which a junior member holds the one unshared piece of information, and the protocol requires me to do something I will find personally costly. Where the protocol returns an answer I will not like, say so plainly rather than softening it.
5. What the protocol cannot do, and the choices left to me (about 300 words): the failure modes it cannot touch, the points where it produces a clean-looking output but the decision is a value judgment I must make and defend, and how I should describe the committee's confidence when the independence assumption is broken.
