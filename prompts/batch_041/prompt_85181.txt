Me and my friend Dan argued for like three hours last night about whether history is locked in, and I came out of it less sure of my own view than when I went in. Here's roughly where I stand — I want to know if it actually hangs together:

1. Path dependence is real. Increasing returns plus a couple of small early accidents can lock in a standard, a technology or an institution that nobody would pick from scratch, and it can stay locked in for centuries. So the outcome was contingent.
2. But for the big stuff — which societies industrialize and when, the broad shape of inequality — deep structure does nearly all the work. Rerun the tape and you get roughly the same world with different flags on it.
3. Historical counterfactuals are legitimate, and often the only way we've got to test a mechanism claim about a particular case.
4. Comparative history with a handful of cases can't establish causation — too many variables, too few cases. You need large-N comparisons or natural experiments.

Dan's move, which is what got me going, is that 1 and 2 are perfectly compatible so long as you're careful, because lock-in only decides which country gets there first, not whether anyone gets there at all — so the contingency is about who, not what. I definately can't tell if that's a real resolution or just relabeling.

So: which of these pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension, and for each real tension, what's the cheapest thing to give up and what does giving it up cost me? Order them by how bad they are, don't just list them. And if 1 and 2 really do conflict, tell me whether Dan's who/what line actually fixes it.
