I retired from a career in marine biology a few years ago and have spent the time since reading philosophy, which I'm enjoying more than I expected but which keeps tripping me up. At the moment I'm stuck on karma — not the pop-culture version, but the serious claim that what you get is what you deserve, that the universe keeps some kind of moral ledger. Being a biologist I keep wanting a mechanism, and I can't tell whether the doctrine is meant as a causal claim at all or just as bookkeeping. Either way I can't work out what principle my own gut is running on, so here are my reactions to some cases, and I'd like you to tell me what, if anything, they add up to.

A man embezzles from a children's charity for thirty years, never gets caught, never feels a thing, dies old and admired. My gut says he got away with it — which, if karma is a theory of desert, looks like a straight counterexample, unless something catches up with him later. A woman spends twenty years nursing a violent, thankless parent, ruins her health, and dies at fifty; that strikes me as undeserved in the other direction. Then a torturer who was raised from birth to believe he was doing holy work, and who is serene and unrepentant — I want to say he still deserves something bad, but I can't say why, since by his own lights he never chose evil. And a burglar who breaks in meaning to rob the place, happens to rouse the family just as a fire starts, and saves four lives — I give him no moral credit at all; it was pure accident. A small child who pulls the wings off insects feels to me barely blameworthy. And a man who watches a drowning child from the bank when he could easily swim in feels to me worse than the serene torturer, even though he didn't lift a finger — which seems to cut against any principle that tracks what you actually did.

The tentative principle I've been fiddleing with is this: what you deserve tracks the gap between what you did and what you could have done — so desert is about unrealised power, not outcomes. That gets the burglar right (he did nothing he could have helped) and the man on the bank right (he could have swum and didn't), but it makes a mess of the torturer (he could have done otherwise, but couldn't have known to) and it says nothing at all about the embezzler, who could have stopped any day.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts would it force me to give up, and why that one rather than another? And please tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say a woman who spends her life campaigning against a disease and, through no fault of her own, ends up making it worse.
