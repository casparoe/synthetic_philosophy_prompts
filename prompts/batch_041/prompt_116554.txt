I would like a consistency audit of a position I have assembled on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please be explicit about the logical structure of the problem rather than gesturing at a literature I have not read.

I want to work with a taxonomy that distinguishes four ways a proxy measure can come apart from the thing it is supposed to track. (a) Regressional: you select on a proxy that is only imperfectly correlated with the goal, and because the correlation is imperfect, the cases with the most extreme proxy values are not the cases with the most extreme goal values, so selecting on the proxy pulls you away from the goal even when nobody is gaming anything. (b) Extremal: when the goal is to maximize something, the maximum of the proxy and the maximum of the goal sit at different points, so pushing the proxy to its limit lands you where the goal is not maximal. (c) Causal: the proxy correlates with the goal through a common cause, and intervening on the proxy breaks the correlation, so improving the proxy no longer improves the goal. (d) Adversarial: an agent with a different objective deliberately games the proxy. Please treat this as my working taxonomy and tell me if I have drawn the boundaries wrongly.

Here are my commitments. I hold all six, and I would like to know whether I can.

1. In engineering management I take it as settled that a metric must never be a target. The moment you attach a bonus or a promotion to story points, or commit counts, or lines of code, the number stops meaning what it meant. The mechanism, as I understand it, is adversarial: people are rational and respond to the incentive. The remedy is to keep metrics strictly observational and never let them enter anyone's payoff.

2. In machine-learning training the observational option does not exist. You must optimize a computable proxy against an objective you cannot compute, because the objective is what you actually care about and you only have samples or a learned reward. Reward hacking is therefore structural rather than a management failure, and no amount of good faith on the part of the system removes it.

3. I think Goodharting is at bottom an epistemic failure. A system that genuinely understood what we wanted — that it wanted to be helpful rather than to score well on a helpfulness proxy — would not game the proxy, because it would see that gaming the proxy is failing at the real thing. Understanding the goal correctly is what makes the proxy unnecessary.

4. At the same time, I do not believe there is a determinate fact of the matter about what we want, sitting behind the operationalization. For something like the quality of a code review or the harmlessness of a reply, what we really want is fixed by the choices we make in specifying the task and the measure; there is no goal out there that the specification is approximating.

5. The practical upshot I draw is that the way to make a system safe is to make the proxy and the goal coincide by construction — to define the target as the goal, so that by definition the reward is what we want and there is nothing left to game. If the reward is the goal, the gap closes.

6. I also think some measures are simply robust and cannot be gamed: whether a customer renewed, whether the service stayed up, how long a request took. Gaming is a property of badly chosen proxies, not of targeting as such; pick a proxy that is constituted by the outcome rather than merely correlated with it and the problem does not arise.

I am not sure where the trouble is, or whether there is any. Please tell me which pairs of these are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, using the taxonomy above to say which variant each commitment is really about; rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs; and finish by telling me which single revision would clear up the most. Do not assume I would give up any particular one of the six.
