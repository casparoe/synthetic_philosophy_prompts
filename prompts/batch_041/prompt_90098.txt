I chair the collections committee of a small university museum, and an accident in the courtyard has landed us in a dispute about identity that I would rather not resolve by fiat. The museum's founder commissioned a bronze equestrian figure in 1908; the sculptor's original plaster maquette is still in our stores, and the bronze was cast from it in 1911 and stood on the same plinth until a storm brought down an old elm last autumn and shattered it beyond repair. Wishing the courtyard restored before convocation, our director had the foundry cast a replacement from the maquette — same alloy, same scale, using the same mold taken from the maquette that was used in 1911 — and it now stands on the original plinth. One further detail has made the argument worse rather than better: rather than sell the shattered bronze for scrap, we had the fragments melted down and blended into the new pour, so that roughly a tenth of the metal in the figure now standing outside is metal that was part of the figure that fell. My committee meets Thursday and must settle four things: whether the registrar retires the 1911 accession number or opens a new one; what the rededication plaque may say (the alumni association wants it to read that this is the same statue, restored); whether the 1911 bequest, which obliges us to keep the statue in the courtyard in perpetuity and funds its upkeep, still binds the endowment or has lapsed — the donor's granddaughter sits on the board of trustees and the development office is watching this closely; and whether we can accept a lender's request to borrow the 1911 bronze for an exhibition in the spring.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. I want a concrete recommendation on each of the four points, with a time frame for acting on it, and a principled test I can state in a sentence and defend, rather than a pragmatic "let us call it the same for the alumni." The committee is split. Our curator says it is the same statue because it came from the same mold and looks the same from the courtyard path; our conservator says it is the same statue only if it retains the original material, and points to the salvaged metal as evidence that it does; a colleague from the philosophy department says the bronze never was the statue but merely constituted it, that the statue is the shape, and that the shape was re-created rather than continued, so the 1911 statue is simply gone and what stands outside is a new work of the same design; the director says that whatever we call it, the courtyard is restored and the paperwork should reflect the institution's interests. Engage the strongest of these positions on its own terms before recommending against it — in particular, if you conclude that the 1911 statue no longer exists, say plainly what follows for the bequest and the loan, and if you conclude that it does exist, say exactly what work the salvaged metal is doing in your reasoning, since I suspect that is where the real argument lies. No need to cite any literature; argue from the case.
