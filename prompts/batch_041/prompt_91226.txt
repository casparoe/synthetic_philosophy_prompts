A seminar discussion of Putnam's later writing on religion has left me with an argument I cannot shake and cannot accept, and I should like to know which step to abandon. As I read him, Putnam holds two things at once. First, the dichotomy between fact and value collapses: no description is wholly value-free, and evaluative judgments are answerable to reasons, capable of being warranted, criticised and revised. Second, the religious attitude is neither a body of empirical hypotheses — nothing that science could confirm or refute — nor a mere expression of feeling. The argument below seems to follow from those two commitments, and it arrives somewhere Putnam would surely refuse to go.

(1) If the fact/value dichotomy collapses, then no description of the world is free of evaluation, and so there is no standpoint from which a practice's evaluations can be assessed that is not itself internal to some practice.
(2) If the warrant for a claim is internal to the practice that sustains it, then a claim is warranted just insofar as it is licensed within its form of life.
(3) Religious claims are licensed within the religious form of life; claims about the reading of entrails are licensed within the diviner's.
(4) Hence religious claims are no better warranted than divinatory ones, and there is no rational difference between them.
(5) Therefore either religious claims are empirical hypotheses after all — badly supported ones — or there is no rational difference between a faith and a superstition.

I should like to keep (1) and the second of Putnam's commitments; it is (4) and (5) that I cannot accept. But I cannot see where the chain breaks, or what it would cost to break it.
