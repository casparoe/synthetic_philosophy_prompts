I'm an undergraduate taking a course on twentieth-century moral philosophy, and the final third of the term is devoted to intention, action-description, and the ethics of killing. I've been asked to help prepare interview questions for a visiting philosopher who defends a broadly Anscombean line on the atomic bombings: she holds that Oxford's decision to honor Truman was indefensible, that Truman's responsibility is not dissolved by the fact that he authorized the attacks rather than flying the plane, and that the civilians of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were for the most part not engaged in the fighting. She has lately been extending the position to remote and autonomous weapons, which is the part I find most interesting and least worked out. My worry is that the obvious questions — wasn't it necessary to end the war, wasn't it a lesser evil, doesn't the number of projected Allied deaths settle it — will let her simply restate the position in a slightly different register, and I want questions that force her to do actual work.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give for each, organized so that the pressure accumulates rather than resets. I'd like at least one question on the sleeping-soldiers objection she considers in the pamphlet against the honorary degree — the thought that if killing the innocent is murder, then killing enemy soldiers while they sleep must be murder too — and whether the combatant/noncombatant line can be made principled given how many combatants are conscripts with no real share in the decision to fight; at least one on whether the distinction between intending deaths as a means and merely foreseeing them can bear the weight when the deaths of children are certain, immediate, and known to the bombardier, including a follow-up that refuses the reply that this is just a redescription of the same act; at least one on whether her necessity claim is really empirical, since she has argued that the bombings seemed necessary only because the Allies insisted on unconditional surrender, and on what happens to the moral verdict if the historical counterfactual goes the other way; and at least one that carries the paper-signing defense into the present, asking whether a drone operator or the person who writes targeting software is differently situated from Truman and why. Two further pressures I'd especially like included: first, a question that turns her own account of acting under a description against the absolutism, asking whether the moral verdict is being smuggled in by the choice of description rather than derived from it, with a follow-up that anticipates her saying the description is fixed by the agent's intention; and second, a question about whether the intention/foresight line survives contact with sanctions, blockades, or infrastructure attacks that foreseeably kill large numbers of civilians without anyone aiming at them — if it survives, what does the distinction actually track, and if it collapses, what is left of the absolutism. Please close with a question about what evidence or argument would move her, and note for each question where the literature actually presses rather than where a casual interlocutor would.

Since I'll be reading these aloud and may have to defend them if the guest pushes back, I'd like the response to state how confident it is in its characterizations of what the position is committed to, and to single out the one point it is least sure of — I would much rather have a candid note that a given question rests on a reconstruction of her view than a smoothly confident summary that turns out to attribute something she never held.
