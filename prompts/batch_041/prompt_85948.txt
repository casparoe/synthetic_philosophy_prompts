I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (with one foot in the algorithmic game theory reading group), and I need a proposal for a small summer research grant. The deliverable at the end of it is a ~5000-word paper, and I've got about ten weeks of real working time.

Here's the thing I want to write about. Safe Pareto improvements (term comes from Oesterheld and Conitzer and coauthors, algorithmic game theory / cooperative AI side of things — I'll fix the refs myself, don't worry about them). The idea, as I understand it: rather than trying to predict how a game will actually get played, you look for a way to *change* the game — bolt on a commitment device, delete some options, add a cheap-talk stage, whatever — such that every player ends up at least as well off as they would have in the original game, no matter how the original game would have been played. The "safe" bit is that you need no beliefs or probabilities about the other side's play at all; instead you lean on structural assumptions about how the two games correspond to each other (something like: games that are identical up to relabeling get played in corresponding ways), and if the improvement holds under every way of playing that's consistent with those assumptions, it's safe.

My half-formed worry is that those correspondence assumptions are doing all the work, and they're the kind of assumption that's plausible about agents who *aren't* thinking about the relationship between the two games. But an SPI gets proposed. The agents know it's been proposed. And the moment they know, they become exactly the kind of reflective agents the assumption is least plausible for — they're now explicitly reasoning about G vs. G′. So I want to ask whether there's a fixed-point version of the notion: a transformation that's a safe improvement on the game *as it actually gets played, given that everyone knows the transformation is on the table* and may play the original game differently because of it. Let's call it stable safety, provisionally. Concretely I want to know: (a) do the naive and stable notions come apart, and can I build a toy example — Chicken or Battle of the Sexes or something with a cheap-talk stage — where the naive safety claim goes through and the stable one fails? (b) Is the stable notion ever non-empty outside degenerate cases, or does common knowledge of the proposal kill the whole framework? (c) If it's non-empty, what characterizes which transformations are stable, or which correspondence assumptions survive being common knowledge?

The funders want the usual: the question stated so it has a determinate answer, why the existing positions leave it open, what I'd actually do with the ten weeks, what would count as a result versus a failure, and a realistic scope.
