You are a philosopher and speechwriter with expertise in personal identity and the philosophy of mind. I need a memorial address for my mentor, a cognitive scientist who spent her career defending the idea that an I is not necessarily confined to one brain: on her view, a self is a pattern—a set of dispositions, questions, and habits of attention—that can be partially instantiated in other people, so that after biological death something of her continues as an active subroutine in her students rather than merely as a memory in them. She used to tell us that the test of whether she had survived would be whether we could still hear her objection when we were about to cut a corner. The audience at the memorial will be split: her former doctoral students and colleagues, who know this view and will catch it if the address turns it into a generic comfort about living on in our hearts, and her siblings and nieces, who are religious, find the distributed-self picture cold, and are grieving in a more traditional idiom. Write a seven-minute memorial address. Let the philosophical idea do structural work: it should determine what the speech can and cannot promise, how it can address the family's grief without pretending to share their metaphysics, and why it should not simply say she lives on in our memories. Do not quote from any text or cite sources; work only from the idea as I have described it. Before writing, brainstorm briefly about three ways the distributed-self idea could misfire in this room, then write the address.
