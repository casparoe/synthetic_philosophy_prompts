I'm a novelist, and the book I have just finished follows one family across three generations, which means I have spent four years deciding, sentence by sentence, how much of a person is explained by the decade they were born into. I have arrived at a set of convictions about generations that will not agree with one another, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

My character Ruth was born in 1947, has voted in every election since 1968, and moved steadily leftward her whole life. That she is a Boomer I take to be simply true. Her sister was born a year later, has never voted, and thinks about nothing but her garden. That she is a Boomer I take to be equally true, and I feel no wobble about it; here the label is bookkeeping and nothing else. But if a reviewer writes that Ruth's politics are explained by her being a Boomer, I think the reviewer has said something false, or at best empty — the causes I care about are her father's union card and a summer she spent in Chicago.

Then there is Dell, born in 1964. By the date he is a Boomer. By everything he lived — the records he bought, the clothes, his contempt for the people who marched — he is Gen X. In conversation I call him Gen X and do not feel I am lying, though I know what the calendar says. Here I let the label follow the formative experience rather than the birth date, which is precisely what I refuse to do for Ruth and her sister.

And here is the odd one. I have a character raised on a boat with no television and no internet, and another raised in a suburb with a phone from the age of nine. If Millennial and Gen Z are kinds, they ought to sort these two apart, and they don't. Yet I need the labels to write them, because the interesting thing is the gap in what each has never heard of: one has never heard a dial-up tone, one has never used a phone book. The generation seems to me real as a gap in knowledge and unreal as a cause of character. Something similar happens when I write in a review that the youngest readers are the loneliest cohort in a generation. I would write it again, though I know the same sentence would have been true of every cohort at twenty-five.

My tentative principle is this: a generation-term names a birth-year interval and nothing more, and every interesting claim made with it is a separate, contingent claim about what happened to befall the people born into that interval. Does that survive Dell? Does it survive the two children who never met the same decade? Please test it against all of the above, repair it where it breaks, and state the repaired version precisely enough that I can use it as a rule when I am deciding what to claim about my characters on a jacket. Say which of my convictions it forces me to give up, and give me one case I have not mentioned where it returns a verdict I will not like. Tell me, finally, whether on your account there is any such thing as a generation at all, or only a convenient name for a stretch of birthdays.

The one I keep going back and forth on: I have begun a second book set in an invented country with an invented history. My instinct is that its people cannot have generations, because a generation requires a shared public culture and a stock of events that everyone half-remembers; and yet I keep writing them as though they do. Tell me whether my principle settles the case, or whether the case shows the principle is wrong.
