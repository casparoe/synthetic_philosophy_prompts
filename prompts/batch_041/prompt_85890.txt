I work on retrieval and ranking systems and have no formal training in philosophy or cognitive science — everything I know about this I picked up on my own, mostly from Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, which I read because a colleague kept insisting it was the most important book ever written about what our team actually does all day. Next month our internal cognition reading group finishes its stretch on that book, and I volunteered to give the wrap-up talk, where I plan to defend three claims together: first, that analogy is not one faculty among many but the whole of cognition, and that a concept just is a bundle of analogies to particular cases rather than a definition or a stored prototype; second, that because of this, the standard objection to large language models — that they manage only surface similarity and never the deep relational analogy that real understanding requires — is not merely overstated but unprincipled, since nobody has ever produced a non-question-begging way to draw the surface/deep line, and the loop we already build (retrieve a similar case, project its structure onto the new one, notice when it misfires, repair) is exactly the loop the book says thinking is; and third, that this yields a testable prediction, namely that the highest-leverage way to improve a system's apparent reasoning is to improve its ability to notice and repair a bad analogy rather than to retrieve a better one. I want to be honest that the third claim is the one I am least sure of and the one I most want stress-tested.

The audience is small and I need to be ready for them in particular. Three engineers from my own team work on embedding-based retrieval; they are, I suspect, quietly hostile to the whole framing, because for them analogy just means a high cosine score, and they will want a definition they can compute, a baseline they can beat, and a prediction that could come out false. Two design researchers with psychology backgrounds put the book on the list in the first place and are protective of it; they will insist that its entire point is that concepts are fluid and that the analogy is made under pressure rather than looked up, and they will hear my talk as a cheapening of the book into a claim about nearest-neighbour search. One visitor — a psycholinguist a friend of mine is bringing along — comes out of the structure-mapping tradition and thinks the book is undisciplined; she will argue that analogy is defined by the systematic mapping of higher-order relations, that the experiments show people retrieve candidate analogues largely on surface similarity but only map them structurally, and that my collapse of retrieval into mapping is precisely the error she has spent a career documenting. My manager will attend, is a pragmatist, and will ask what any of this changes about the roadmap. The book's defenders will also bring up Hofstadter's old letter-string analogy programs and ask why those are supposed to be the gold standard when a language model does the same thing without being handed the alphabet; I do not have a good answer to that yet.

What I want from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a defence of my position: roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they do to the thesis rather than by how likely they are to come up, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would actually phrase it, each followed by a reply I can deliver out loud in about two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is soft, and a line telling me what to concede up front versus what to hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly what it is about my framing that makes it dangerous. Take a definite position throughout: I would much rather you tell me which of my three claims is false and why, and what the strongest version I can still defend looks like, than hand me a balanced menu of considerations and leave me to choose — if the structure-mapping objection sinks the second claim, say so and tell me what to cut from the talk and what to replace it with. No reading lists, no citations, no names of papers or authors, since I cannot look anything up while I am standing there and I want arguments rather than references; if a point genuinely depends on a specific finding or a specific title, either make the argument without leaning on it or flag it as something I should verify before I present.
