Bit of an odd request, this one. I cover science and ideas for a living — long features, some radio — and for my sins I've spent two years as the lay member on the editorial standards panel of our national public broadcaster. We meet monthly, we sign off on the tricky stuff, nobody reads the minutes. That was the deal.

Something's landed on us and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday, and I'm out of my depth in a way that's slightly embarrassing for a man with a philosophy degree.

Here's the case. A production company has delivered the first cut of a two-part documentary about Anatoly Fomenko's so-called New Chronology — the claim, from a Russian mathematician, that the standard chronology of antiquity is a fabrication, that events we date to Rome and Greece and Egypt actually happened in the Middle Ages, and that the timeline we all use (the one that goes back to Scaliger in the sixteenth century) was assembled out of duplicated records and then policed by institutions with an interest in it. Fomenko says he got there by applying statistical methods to texts, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes our factual programming people nervous in a good way.

The catch is that the film doesn't assert any of it. That's the pitch: "we're not saying he's right, we're telling the story of how a mathematical outsider was frozen out by the historical establishment." Which is, on its face, a perfectly legitimate subject — there are real cases of the establishment being wrong about big things, and of outsiders being treated badly, and everyone involved has read their Kuhn. Newton himself rewrote ancient chronology and got it wrong; that's in the film, and it's true.

But the framing does a lot of work. The structure is: outsider, method, rejection, insinuation. The critics they've interviewed are cut so they come across as annoyed rather than informative, and there's a stretch where a historian says something about the sources and the edit stops before the actual reason. There's a line in the commentary about "questions the experts would rather you didn't ask", which I think is the bit that decides it for me, but I don't want to decide it on vibes.

What I need from you is the written opinion I take to Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course — run it as is, don't run it, or run it with specified conditions (and if conditions, say exactly what: a caption at the top, a rebuttal segment, a historian on the panel afterwards, an on-screen label that follows the claims, whatever actually works and isn't just theatre) — with a time frame, because they want to schedule it.

Two things I specifically want you to take on, because I can't:

1. What is the actual difference, in a form a lay panel can apply, between pseudohistory and a genuine revisionist challenge? Everyone in the room keeps saying "falsifiability" and I don't think that does the work, since Fomenko's claims are in principle checkable — they're just wrong, and checkably wrong, which is a different complaint. And I want criteria that will still be usable on the next three pitches, which are Atlantis, ancient astronauts, and something about a lost global civilisation that got wiped from the record. Those three are easy to say no to. The Fomenko one isn't, and I want to understand why, precisely, so that our policy isn't just "we can tell".

2. Our impartiality obligations were written for political controversy. Does treating a claim about what happened in the eleventh century as a contested matter to be balanced actually misapply the rule? If it does, say so, and say what the panel should do with that — including whether refusing to broadcast anything that upsets a professional body is itself a failure, given that we do broadcast things historians hate.

Don't hedge. If the framing of my request is confused — if "where's the demarcation line" is the wrong question and the real one is about the production's editing choices, or what a broadcaster owes an audience, or who gets to sit on these panels as the lay member — then say that plainly instead of giving me a tidy answer I'll have to defend to a room that includes two lawyers. I'd rather take an argument I disagree with into the meeting than a summary of the obvious. And if you think I should recuse myself, because I'd quite like to write about this myself afterwards, tell me that too. Definately don't assume I've got the premise right.

Roughly 800 words, please, laid out as a memo with the recommendation in the first paragraph.
