(b) Boltzmann's H-theorem was supposed to derive the second law from the dynamics. Say what actually went wrong with that derivation. (c) "In the end there's only one arrow: the thermodynamic arrow. And here's the student's answer:

(a) If the dynamics are time-reversal invariant, then for any microscopic process the reversed process is equally allowed. So the asymmetry we see in macroscopic processes can't come from the laws; it has to come from the boundary conditions. The universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate, and since the vast majority of microstates compatible with any given macrostate are higher-entropy ones, an evolution away from that state almost always moves toward higher entropy. That's why the second law is statistical rather than strict: it says entropy increase is overwhelmingly probable, not necessary. The past hypothesis is doing the real explanatory work here — the dynamics on their own give you nothing directional.

(b) The H-theorem derived entropy increase from the dynamics plus an assumption of molecular chaos. Loschmidt's recurrence objection showed the theorem can't be right in general: because the dynamics are reversible, any entropy-increasing trajectory has a reversed counterpart that is equally allowed and decreases entropy, and by Poincaré's theorem the system will eventually return arbitrarily close to its starting state. So the derivation smuggles time-asymmetry in through the molecular-chaos assumption rather than getting the arrow for free.

(c) Basically right. If the entropy gradient is the only source of asymmetry in the world, then the other asymmetries have to be downstream of it. Memory is a physical record, and making a record is an entropy-increasing process: you can't store a reliable trace of an event unless the recording process is thermodynamically irreversible. Causation is harder, but the story is the same shape — an effect is a state reached along an entropy-increasing path. I'd only add that this makes the arrow depend on coarse-graining, since entropy is defined relative to a partition of phase space that we choose.

(d) In the second half, entropy would have to decrease as time moves forward, which contradicts the second law. Since the second law is one of the best-confirmed generalizations in physics, a universe like that is impossible — the two-sided constraint is a mathematical artefact.
