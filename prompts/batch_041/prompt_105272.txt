I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I keep circling back to a puzzle about knowledge that I would like you to dramatize. The puzzle is this: if knowing something means that what one knows is closed under logical consequence, then anyone who knows a few axioms knows every theorem that follows from them, however long the proof — an ideal knower would know all logical truths and could never be surprised by a valid inference. I would like a dialogue between two philosophers who both understand the issue deeply but disagree about whether this logical omniscience is a tolerable idealization or a fatal defect in the standard treatment of knowledge as truth across a range of possible worlds. Let the first defend the idealization, insisting that knowledge is a normative notion and that a theory of knowledge need not double as a psychology of proof; let the second press the objection that a theory which cannot represent an agent who fails to see an obvious consequence cannot represent learning, surprise, or the very point of proving anything. I want them to grapple with a concrete case: two colleagues who share every axiom and inference rule of a mathematical theory, one of whom immediately sees a theorem and the other of whom, though equally competent, has simply not yet noticed it — does the second know the theorem, or merely stand in a position to know it? Have each speaker answer the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them, and let neither be a strawman. Close with a brief note on which position you find more defensible and what consideration would move you toward the other.
