I'm a philosopher working on distributive justice, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on aggregation and the moral significance of boundaries between lives. The thesis I'll be defending is this: the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, as it is usually deployed, runs together three claims that need to be pulled apart — first, that persons are not to be treated as mere containers or receptacles of impersonal value, so that gains and losses can be summed across lives without remainder; second, that there is no moral relation across the boundary between one person's life and another's, so that a benefit to one person can never in itself count as a reason to impose a cost on someone else; and third, that the value of a life is not simply the sum of the values of its moments, so that how a life is arranged in time matters independently of how much good it contains. My argument is that only the first of these has real force against any version of consequentialism worth defending, that the second is either a restatement of the first or else a claim so strong it would make ordinary beneficence unintelligible, and that the third — which is where the deepest work actually gets done — cuts against the objector, because the very considerations that make the shape of a life matter to its value also make the boundaries between lives matter in a way that a sufficiently sophisticated consequentialism can accommodate rather than deny. I've been kicking this around with a colleague for months and I keep discovering that the version in my head is more fragile than the version I say out loud, which is why I want the objection sheet rather than a defense.

The audience is what worries me. The workshop is mostly Rawlsians and Scanlonians, people who take the separateness of persons to be the decisive objection to utilitarianism and who will hear my paper as an attempt to dissolve a constraint rather than to understand it; there are also two people who work on population ethics and will press me from the Parfitian direction, asking what my view does with the repugnant conclusion and whether the shape-of-a-life point can do any work once lives are being added and subtracted rather than rearranged; and there is one legal scholar who works on regulatory cost-benefit analysis and the valuation of statistical lives, who will want to know whether my argument licenses the kind of aggregation she sees every day in agency rulemaking, or whether it quietly condemns it. I'd like you to act as a sharp philosophical interlocutor and prepare my question-and-answer sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this particular room, in roughly the order they will come and phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how much damage it does to my position rather than by how interesting it is. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, or where I am conceding more than I can afford to concede. End with the one objection I am hoping nobody in the room thinks to raise, and say what I should do if someone does raise it.

Before you write anything, think carefully and at length about the structure of my position and about where it is genuinely vulnerable, and sketch to yourself the strongest version of the objector's case before you decide what this room will actually say — I don't want the standard talking points assembled in order, I want you to work out what this specific combination of commitments will generate. Then write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. Write it the way you would write a long and candid email to a colleague who is about to walk into a room that is not on his side.
