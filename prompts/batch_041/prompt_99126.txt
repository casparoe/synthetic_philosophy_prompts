I'm giving a talk at a reading group next week and I need my Q&A prep. Heres the thesis, in the version I'll actually be defending: an ant colony can have a mind in a real but deflated sense — it can have colony-level beliefs and wants, genuinely attributable to the colony and not to any individual ant — but it has no inner feel, nothing it's like to be the colony. So: a cognitive system without a conscious one. My empirical hook is the standard ant house-hunting story, where a colony seems to size up candidate nest sites and commit once enough of the ants have seen the good one; I read that as the colony forming something belief-like about the world. My argumentative hook is a deflationary line about belief (beliefs are just the states that play the right functional role in steering behavior) plus a claim that representation can be distributed — no single ant carries it, the representation lives in the interactions.

The room is mixed and I know exactly who's in it. Two people are Searle-flavored biological naturalists: for them only the right kind of biological machinery can realize a mind, so "colony beliefs" is a category error and I'm just dressing up a metaphor. Two are card-carrying functionalists who will happily give the colony a mind but will immediately ask why I stopped at belief and denied it experience — they'll say I got squeamish about multiple realizability halfway through. One person works on collective intentionality and thinks the whole point of shared intention is that it needs individual commitment and mutual recognition, so a colony can't have it no matter how clever the signaling is. One eliminativist thinks my belief/desire vocabulary is the actual problem and the colony is a red herring. And one person who thinks the entire exercise is a metaphor and wants to know what work "the colony believes p" does that "the colony behaves as if p" doesn't.

Draft me the prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular person would put it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede up front. I want the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. And please dont give me the generic Dennett-versus-Searle back and forth I already know — I want what this specific room will throw at me, in their vocab, not the textbook version.
