I live in a farming county, and I have been trying to work out what the pest control around me actually assumes, morally speaking. The pattern is not what I expected. Nobody here treats insects as things to be destroyed wherever they are found; they are treated as a cost to be managed. The ordinary rule is to spray only after the pest population crosses the point at which the crop value that will be lost exceeds what the spray costs, and below that line the insects are left alone — not out of mercy, but because killing them would not pay. Meanwhile the same creature can be a pest in one field and an asset in the next: a bee or a ladybird is worth something because it pollinates or eats the pest, and regulators will demand that a chemical be tested for what it does to honeybees while saying nothing about whether the insects that die suffered. There is a further wrinkle. Organic certification forbids certain synthetic sprays but allows naturally occurring ones, some of which are harder on the things living in the field than what they replace. And the money that does get spent on insects goes to kinds rather than creatures — habitat for a declining butterfly, protection for a bumblebee — never to the fate of any individual insect.

What theory of value does this pattern presuppose? Does it assume that insect experience does not count at all, or only that insect death matters insofar as it registers in human costs — yield, price, pollination, the cost of a spray? Is "pest" a fact about the insect, or a relation between the insect and whatever we happen to be growing that season? And does the habit of protecting species while ignoring individuals presuppose that the value lies in the kind and not in the living thing? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Tell me which parts of the practice strictly require it and which merely happen to fit it, since I suspect a great deal of it would survive under quite different views. Say where the practice is underdetermined — whether someone who took insect suffering seriously could keep the same thresholds and simply change the numbers, or whether the whole apparatus would have to go — and tell me what would actually have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which each insect's death is a cost in its own right.
