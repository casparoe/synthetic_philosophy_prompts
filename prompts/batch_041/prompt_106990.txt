I'm a third-year philosophy student, and I'm currently taking a seminar on consequentialism and its critics. There's a pattern that keeps surfacing in our readings and discussions that I can't find a clean name for, and I'd like help carving it up rather than just labeling it.

The pattern is this. Take a harm so small that a single instance would be beneath notice — a momentary itch, a barely perceptible flicker of annoyance, a fraction of a second of mild boredom. Now suppose it is inflicted on an enormous number of people at once, or on one person across an enormous stretch of time. Two intuitions collide. If you simply add the small harms together, the total grows vast, and it seems hard to call a vast total trivial. Yet it also seems absurd to say that any number of such trivialities could outweigh one person enduring something genuinely terrible for an extended period; the sense that no quantity of pinpricks can match a single atrocity is very strong.

What interests me is not only the collision but the fact that people resist the aggregative conclusion in several distinct ways, and I want to know whether each has a name. Some insist that small harms are not the sort of thing that can be summed across persons at all, because persons are separate and their experiences do not form one pool. Some hold that harms below a certain threshold have zero weight, so any number of them still sums to zero. Some treat the aggregative pull as a cognitive illusion, a failure of imagination once numbers get large. And some deny that the two cases lie on a common scale, so the comparison is meaningless.

There is a second version that puzzles me even more. Instead of each of many people suffering a small harm, each of many people faces a tiny probability of a serious harm — a risk so small that no individual's expected loss is noticeable, yet spread over a huge population the expected number of victims is large. Here the tiny quantities are probabilities rather than experiences, and I can't tell whether the same resistance, or a different one, is in play.

Is there an established name for this configuration — the clash between many tiny harms and one great harm — and for the distinct strategies of resisting it? I suspect it sits near things I've heard named (the repugnant conclusion, the separateness of persons, thresholds and lexical priority), but I don't think any of those is quite it, and I'd like to know precisely how what I'm describing differs from each and which fits best. If nothing established fits, I'd rather have a careful definition plus boundary cases than a bare label: what makes a harm tiny for this purpose, whether the parallel puzzle arises for tiny benefits, whether the number of people must merely be large or actually infinite, and whether the probabilistic version is the same phenomenon or a cousin.
