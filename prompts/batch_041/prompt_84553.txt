I'm a novelist and I'm deep in a draft where my main character, a lapsed philosophy of religion grad student, gets asked to close out a seminar with a poem — and I want the actual poem on the page, not a description of one. So write it for me. Think of yourself as a philosopher who also writes formal verse, the kind of person who can scan a line, and before you write anything spend a few sentences thinking out loud: what the contingency argument actually claims, how it differs from the kalam version, and what a sharp objector would say back. Don't skip that part — I've found that when I ask for verse cold I get greeting-card stuff, and I need this one to be load-bearing.

The poem should be a terza rima in English — aba bcb cdc and so on, five or six tercets and then that single closing line the form always ends on. Here's what actually matters to me: the interlocking rhyme IS the point. It should do the work of the regress of explanations, each stanza's middle rhyme coming back as the next stanza's outer rhyme, an answer that immediately generates the next question. And the last line, standing alone, is where the asking stops. I want that closing line to be genuinely double — a reader who thinks the argument lands should hear a necessary being in it, and a reader who thinks the regress just runs on forever should hear the poet running out of breath or nerve, and neither should be able to prove they're right. A specialist should recognize the argument underneath it; nobody should catch you cheating.

Accuracy stuff, since this is the whole reason I'm asking a machine instead of doing it myself at 2 a.m.: the kalam version needs the universe to have a beginning, so don't let the poem rest on whatever-begins-to-exist-has-a-cause as if that were the contingency premise — the contingency version is supposed to work even if the universe is eternal, because the series it cares about is the kind where everything is being held up right now, all at once, not a line of dominoes falling forward in time. Chain hanging from a hook, not dominoes. The poem should feel like simultaneous dependence. And it has to let the objection breathe — the suspicion that the chain only closes because the form demands closure, that a rhyme scheme needs a last line the way an argument needs a terminus, and maybe thats a fact about us and not about the world. Let the poem notice that without settling it. No philosophers named in the verse, no epigraph, no footnotes, and it cant read like a lecture in rhyme — iambic pentameter or close, slant rhymes totally fine, American English please. After the poem, give me a short paragraph on the closing line and what you were doing with the double reading, and tell me which rhyme-words you leaned on hardest and why. Thanks.
