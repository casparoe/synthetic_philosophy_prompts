I'm a PhD student in philosophy, midway through a dissertation on population ethics and the aggregation of welfare, and I want an audit of my current position before I commit it to writing. Here is what I hold.

(1) Only welfare is intrinsically good, and welfare is a single cardinal quantity comparable across beings, so that gains and losses in different lives can be summed into an overall verdict about what to do.

(2) The utility monster is a curiosity, not an objection. Stated at its most extreme, the case posits a being whose hedonic machinery has no ceiling, so that each additional unit of resource it consumes yields at least as much additional welfare as the last. I think such a being is conceptually coherent but biologically impossible, and that for anything that could actually exist, the diminishing marginal utility of consumption guarantees that no single creature's welfare can outrun the aggregate welfare of everyone else.

(3) The repugnant conclusion is a genuine reductio of totalism. I therefore accept a non-totalist aggregative principle on which the value contributed by an additional life diminishes as the population grows — a critical level or variable value view, roughly.

(4) Severe suffering has lexical priority. No amount of aggregate benefit to others can justify imposing severe suffering on someone; extreme suffering is not tradeable against mild goods.

(5) Moral status is equal. No one's interests count for more than anyone else's; there are no persons who matter more.

(6) In practice, scarce health resources should go where they buy the most health, measured in something like QALYs per dollar.

I suspect these cannot all be right together, but I cannot see where the seams are. Please audit rather than summarize: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and rank the tensions by severity, say for each what specifically has to give, and say which apparent conflicts dissolve once you look closely. Finish by naming the single revision that would repair the most while costing the least. Be explicit about how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point you are least sure of.
