I am assembling the take-home final for a graduate seminar that sits between formal epistemology and information economics, and I need help drafting one of its essay questions. The topic is no-trade results and their bearing on the claim that market prices aggregate dispersed information — the Milgrom–Stokey line of results on one side, the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox on the other. What I do not want is a question that a student can pass by reciting either result, or by reciting Aumann's agreement theorem, or by gesturing at "adverse selection." It has to be answerable only by working through an argument, and the student should have to construct that argument rather than remember its name.

To that end, please build the question around a concrete two-trader market specified inside the question itself, with numbers the students must actually use. The scenario should run roughly as follows, though I would like you to tighten the exposition: a binary state of the world; an asset paying 1 in one state and 0 in the other; two risk-neutral traders who share a prior of one half on each state; each trader receives a private binary signal that is correct with probability two thirds, with errors conditionally independent across traders given the state; and the two may exchange the asset for cash at a posted price before the state is revealed. Part of the question should make the students compute the trade that first suggests itself — the one in which one trader's signal is high and the other's is low — and then discover what happens to each trader's posterior once that trader conditions not only on her own signal but on the fact that the counterparty is willing to trade at that price. I want them to see that willingness to trade is itself evidence, and that this is precisely what destroys the trade. Please make sure the arithmetic in the scenario makes that collapse visible and checkable rather than merely asserted, so that a student who only paraphrases the theorem will not be able to fake the computation.

The question should then sharpen the point in a second and a third part.

In the second part, replace the bilateral haggle with a market maker who posts a price that both traders observe and update on, and ask what happens to their disagreement as the posted price becomes more informative. I want the students to see the self-undermining structure the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox turns on: the more the price reveals, the less anyone stands to gain from the private information the price is supposed to be aggregating, and a fully revealing price is one that no one had any incentive to produce. The intended payoff is that the two results are not in tension by accident — the same common-knowledge assumptions that force no trade are what would make the price informative, so each result is the mirror image of the other, and the student should be able to say why.

In the third part, ask the students to identify the minimal departure from the idealized setting that would restore trade, and to say what that departure costs the epistemic claim that prices aggregate information. The familiar answers — heterogeneous priors, noise or liquidity traders, uncertainty about the information structure itself, hedging motives — should be compared rather than merely listed: some of them leave the price reflecting beliefs rather than evidence, others leave it a noisy signal, and at least one of them quietly surrenders the common prior on which the original argument depended. A strong answer will rank or weigh these against one another.

Requirements for what you return to me:

First, the student-facing question itself, self-contained, with the scenario given in full and with no expectation that students cite literature or recall names or dates. The sub-parts should be weighted so that a weaker student can earn partial credit on the computation, while the discrimination between a strong and a middling answer comes from the last part. The question should fit roughly seventy-five minutes of work and be worth twenty-five marks.

Second, a model answer of about seven hundred to nine hundred words that actually performs the computation in the scenario, gets the collapse of the two posteriors right, and states clearly which assumption each step of the argument uses — separating in particular the role of the common prior from the role of common knowledge of the traders' willingness to trade. The gap between "willing to trade" and "commonly known posterior" is exactly where I expect the best students to distinguish themselves, so the model answer should not paper over it.

Third, a marking scheme built from five or six must-hit points whose marks sum to twenty-five, in which each point names the specific move required rather than the topic it is about, and in which at least two of the points are ones only a genuinely strong answer will earn. For each must-hit point, add a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it — for instance by running the "their signals differ, so they trade" intuition and forgetting to condition on the counterparty's willingness to trade, or by treating the common prior as a dispensable technicality rather than the load-bearing assumption.

Please put no verbatim quotations from the literature into the question or the model answer, and do not let the question depend on students knowing the exact statement of any named theorem.
