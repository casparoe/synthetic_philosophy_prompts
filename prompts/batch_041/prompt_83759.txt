For the monthly reading group I host — a dozen retired scientists, two philosophers, all of us at ease with equations and uneasy with the idiom of the field — I would like a decision tree on the causal exclusion argument. Please do not explain the argument to me; I have read enough of it to lose sleep over it. What I need is an instrument. Start the participant from the two commitments most of the group would sign without hesitation — that mental properties are not identical with physical properties, and that every physical event that has a sufficient physical cause has one — and let the tree show, question by question, what it costs to keep both. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: settle first which questions genuinely divide the positions, then locate the positions among the answer combinations those questions generate. Every branch node must be a single question a working scientist could answer without knowing which label it leads to, so nothing of the form "do you believe the mental is efficacious?"; every leaf must carry the name of the position and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. The tree must include as leaves, at minimum: reductive physicalism; the several non-reductive physicalisms, distinguished from one another by the manoeuvre each uses to keep mental causation; emergentism; epiphenomenalism; interactionist dualism; the option on which physics characterizes matter only by its relational or dispositional structure, so that experience occupies the intrinsic role (name it if the literature has a settled name for it); the option that rejects the exclusion principle outright by treating causation as a difference-making or proportionality relation rather than a relation among sufficient conditions (again, name it if there is a name); and a quietist leaf holding the dispute to be verbal, which should hang from the node at which I would have to concede that point. No more than twelve branching questions, no fewer than eight leaves.

Two things I want the instrument to handle that the textbook treatments I have found do not. First, I have been worrying about a case I will call recurrent realization: a system in which the physical realizer of a mental state at one time has, as part of its causal profile, the fact that it was itself produced by an earlier token of the same mental property, so that no inventory of the present physical state alone tells you what the system will do next. I want the tree to be able to place someone who takes that case seriously, and I want you to say whether it forces a new branch or merely re-describes one you already have. Second, state explicitly which combinations of answers no one occupies, and for each say whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot in fact separate, rather than papering over the gap.

Give it to me in a form I can photocopy: nodes numbered, each leaf named with its awkward commitment, and a closing section for the unoccupied combinations and the indistinguishable pairs. Finally, tell me where the instrument is most likely to fail — which of its branch questions is doing contested work under neutral-sounding phrasing, which leaf attribution you are least confident in, and what answer to which question would force me to rebuild the tree from the top. If you think a tree is the wrong instrument for this dispute — that the positions do not sit in a common space of commitments at all — say so and give the argument.
