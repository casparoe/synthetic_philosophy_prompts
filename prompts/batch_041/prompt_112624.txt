I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course in moral psychology, and I should like some advice about a situation in my own family that has troubled me for several months. My mother has a degenerative neurological condition which produces episodes — sometimes lasting a day or two — in which she becomes suspicious, cruel and verbally vicious towards me in ways she is never otherwise. Between episodes she is entirely herself: warm, funny, and painfully aware of what has happened. My father's position is that I must not hold any of it against her, because 'it isn't her, it's the illness'; my therapist says something similar in the language of not taking it personally; my brother says I am allowed to be angry at the disease but not at her. I have tried to follow this advice, and I find that I can only manage it by adopting a kind of clinical detachment — I anticipate her, I manage her, I speak to her as one speaks to a patient — and I have come to dislike myself in that mode more than I dislike myself when I am angry with her. When I am angry, at least I am relating to her as the person she is; when I am not, I feel as though I have quietly demoted her. The complication is that she has asked me, explicitly and more than once, to go on blaming her for what she does during the episodes. She says that if I forgive her in advance, or explain it all away as the illness, she stops feeling like a person and starts feeling like a case; she would rather be resented than managed, and she has asked me to promise that I will not let the condition become a blanket excuse for everything she does. In a seminar we touched on the difference between setting an act aside because of the circumstances in which it was done and setting a person aside altogether, but I could not see how the distinction was supposed to help me here.

I do not know what to do with this, and I do not trust my own instincts. Is her request coherent — can one meaningfully go on blaming someone for acts performed when they were, in some sense, not themselves — or am I being asked to indulge a fiction that will ultimately make things worse for both of us? And is my anger genuinely a form of respect, as it feels, or is that merely a flattering story I tell myself so as not to have to do the harder work of letting go? I should be grateful for an answer that takes the particulars seriously rather than telling me simply to be kind, or simply to look after myself.
