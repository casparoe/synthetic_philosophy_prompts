In the Letter Spirit project, a group of cognitive scientists set out to build a program that could be shown a handful of letters drawn in a novel style and then design the rest of the alphabet so that every new letter appeared to come from the same hand. Time and again the work circled back to a deceptively simple question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The class of a's is wildly heterogeneous. The single-storey 'a' of a geometric sans-serif and the double-storey 'a' of a traditional serif share no stroke, bowl, or contour in common; a child's first scrawl and a calligrapher's italic are both unmistakably a's; and yet a shape differing from an 'a' by a hair's breadth may be an 'o', while a shape differing from an 'e' by far more may still be an 'a'.

Write an essay on the controversy that this raises about the identity of a letterform, and reach a verdict. Your essay should weigh at least three broad positions: (i) that there is an intrinsic, style-independent essence — a determinate shape or shape-schema — possessed by all and only the a's, with style as mere surface modulation; (ii) that a letter has no intrinsic essence, its identity being conferred relationally, by its contrasts with sibling letters and by the conventions and perceptual habits of a community of readers; and (iii) that a-ness is graded, a matter of similarity to a central prototype, with fuzzy boundaries and no sharp fact of the matter at the margins.

Test whichever positions you adopt against the following cases, and say what each case shows:

1. A society that writes with exactly one symbol, a mark shaped like our 'a'. Is it a letter at all, and if so is it the letter 'a'?
2. A typeface whose 'a' is drawn in the shape we would call 'e' and whose 'e' is drawn in the shape we would call 'a'; within a week its readers read it fluently. Is the e-shaped glyph the letter 'a'?
3. A shape that is a Latin 'P' and, in the Cyrillic alphabet, the letter standing for the r-sound. Is this one letterform with two identities, or two letterforms that happen to coincide?
4. A shape halfway between a canonical 'a' and a canonical 'e', presented in isolation. Does it have a determinate letter-identity, or does a-ness come in degrees?

Finally, draw out what your verdict implies for style: is a typeface's style a set of free variations played around a fixed essence, or does the essence of a letter itself depend on the style, so that a blackletter 'a' and a geometric 'a' are a's for different reasons?

Begin the essay with its verdict in the first sentence, then justify it. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
