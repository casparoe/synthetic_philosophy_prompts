I am a staff software engineer at a company of about 250 engineers, and I chair our architecture review board, which meets weekly. A recurring move in those meetings is the claim that we are locked in. Someone proposes reopening an old decision — our in-house build and configuration system, a data model that predates our second product line, a proprietary messaging primitive that has leaked into every service — and the answer comes back that the matter is settled, that we cannot go back, and that reopening it would waste the room's time. I have no formal background in philosophy, but I understand there is real work on path dependence, on whether historical outcomes are contingent or inevitable, and on what it even means to say things could have gone otherwise. I would like that work turned into a procedure my board can actually run, rather than summarized.

What makes me want it is that "we are locked in" seems to bundle at least four different claims: (1) we literally cannot change this; (2) we could change it, but the cost exceeds the benefit; (3) the original decision was reasonable given what was knowable at the time, so there is nothing to relitigate; and (4) even had we decided otherwise, we would have arrived here anyway, because some other force would have pushed us. These seem to me to have very different consequences, and I watch them get used interchangeably. There are three further questions I cannot answer on my own. A state that merely persists is not the same as a state we cannot leave. Showing that a decision was contingent is not the same as showing it was a mistake. And if the state we are locked into is no worse than any feasible alternative, I am not sure the lock-in claim matters at all. The procedure should force all of these apart, and it should say what evidence would distinguish "we would have arrived here anyway" from "we would not have," given that we cannot rerun the decision.

Please write it as a decision procedure I can hand to the board. It has to fit a forty-five minute review slot; the presenting engineer should be able to fill in a one-page template beforehand; the board renders a verdict; anything costing more than an engineer-quarter goes to the CTO for funding. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and a small named set of verdicts with the action each one triggers. Where a number is needed and unavailable — which will be most of the time — the procedure should say so rather than manufacture a cost estimate. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say how a presenter could game each step. Write it for someone who has never heard the term path dependence, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I cannot check them, and I would rather have the ideas in plain terms.

Then run it on three cases: (a) migrating off the in-house build system to a commercial alternative, with two hundred engineers trained on the current one and a plugin ecosystem built around it; (b) the claim that we would have ended up on our cloud provider's proprietary queue no matter what we chose four years ago; and (c) a proposal to split a shared data model that has been blamed for slowing down two product lines. At least one of the three should come out in a way that I, as the person running the procedure, will not like, and you should say plainly why.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will return a confident-looking verdict on something that is not really decidable, and where the remaining question is about what we value — how much we are willing to pay for optionality, how much churn we will impose on people — rather than about the facts of how we got here. Please write the answer in American English.
