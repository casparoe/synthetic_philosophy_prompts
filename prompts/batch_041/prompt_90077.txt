Ok, novelist here, not a historian and not a philosopher, so bear with me. I've been at a big novel for ages now — an invented empire that rises, gets rich, rots from inside, falls — and I keep hitting teh same wall: I honestly can't tell if I'm writing history or astrology. Someone told me to look into cliodynamics, this Turchin thing about secular cycles and elite overproduction, supposedly putting real numbers on the rise and fall of states and even forecasting the next breakdown. Half of me wants to use it as scaffolding for the book, half of me wants to have a character tear it apart in a monologue.

So before I do either, I want the spec. If a theory claims it can quantify history and predict it, what does it actually have to deliver to be worth taking seriously? Give me the requirements: the phenomena it has to explain; the things it can't just wave away — one person who changes everything, a plague or a volcano out of nowhere, the awkward fact that 'state' and 'elite' and 'cycle' are all fuzzy, contested units; the distinctions it can't collapse, like a real cycle versus a trend versus plain noise, or a genuine forecast versus a retrodiction dressed up as one; and what would even count as the theory being wrong. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are just physics-envy, and which single one definately rules out the most would-be theories of history.

And the part I care about most: is there a pair on that list that can't be satisfied at once? Can you really have predictive power and still leave room for the individual to matter? If some of the requirements are jointly impossible, that's not my question failing, that's the answer I'm after.

Last thing, be straight with me. If 'desiderata for cliodynamics' is a confused way to ask, because cliodynamics isn't one theory so much as a loose research program, say so and reframe it — don't just humor me.
