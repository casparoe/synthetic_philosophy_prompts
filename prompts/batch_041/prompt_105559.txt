I'm teaching Antigone to a undergrad seminar next week and I want to come at it through the ethics of compromise instead of the usual Hegel bit about a clash of two rights, because my students just parrot that back at me without thinking. The frame I want you to use is a little homemade, so bear with me. Think of a moral compromise between two people with different value systems as a kind of trade: each gives up something that ranks low in their own ordering to get something that ranks high, and if their orderings differ enough there's a deal that leaves both better off by their own lights, a moral version of gains from trade. But the trade model seems to break when one side holds a value as non-negotiable, something that can't be put on a scale or exchanged at any price, where even entertaining the trade is itself a betrayal. So read the play as a test case for that model. Is there a deal Creon and Antigone could have struck, some arrangement where he keeps the public order he wants and she gets the burial rites she wants without either surrendering the thing they care most about, or does the play dramatize that their values are exactly the non-tradeable kind, so the gains-from-trade picture is a fantasy when it comes to the things people actually fight over? Which reading is more faithful to Sophocles and not just more convenient for my lecture? Point me to where the trade frame illuminates a scene, the first confrontation, the Haemon scene, Creon's late reversal, and where the play resists it and I should just admit it does. And tell me whether the tragedy is that compromise was possible and missed, or that it genuinely wasn't possible, because those are very different lessons and I keep flip-flopping between them.

Also, push back if I've set this up badly. If the trade metaphor is just wrong for tragedy, or if dragging sacred values and taboo tradeoffs into a Greek play is anachronistic in a way that wrecks the reading, say so plainly instead of humoring the frame. I'd rather be told the premise is confused than get a tidy essay that pretends it isn't.
