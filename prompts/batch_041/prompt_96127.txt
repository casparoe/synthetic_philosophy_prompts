I am teaching a seventy-five-minute session in an intermediate game-theory course. My students can already spot a dominated pure strategy and find Nash equilibria in small games; the new material is iterated elimination of dominated strategies once mixed strategies are permitted. I do not need the theory explained to me — I need a plan for how to explain it. The session should be built on the following two-player game, where the row player picks a row, the column player picks a column, and each cell lists the row player's payoff first and the column player's payoff second (L, C, R are the column player's three strategies):

                L        C        R
    T        5, 5     1, 1     7, 4
    M        2, 2     2, 2     2, 2
    B        0, 0     4, 1     6, 6

Work out what iterated elimination actually does here before you design around it; the instructive structure is not on the surface. Then give me the teaching plan: the order in which the ideas must be introduced, and the dependency that forces each idea to wait on the one before it; which single feature of this example should carry the whole session, and what it must contain and conspicuously lack; the misconceptions the students will arrive with or develop — I expect at least "a strategy is dominated only if some single pure strategy beats it" and "the order of elimination cannot change the outcome" — and exactly where in the sequence I should defuse each; one closing question that would reveal whether they understand the role of mixtures rather than having merely memorised a procedure; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so.

By the end they should be able to state, without proof, three things: how a pure strategy that no pure strategy dominates can still be eliminated; why iterated elimination behaves differently under strict and under weak dominance; and how "strictly dominated" and "never a best response" relate once mixtures are allowed. Do not send me to any readings. Open with a single sentence giving the crux of the ordering, then the plan, in no more than 650 words.
