I have been reading a popular essay on the history of artificial intelligence, and I would like a careful audit of its central characterisation of the frame problem. The passage runs roughly as follows (this is my reconstruction from memory, but I believe it is faithful to the content):

"Artificial intelligence has been struggling with the frame problem since the 1960s. The difficulty is that a reasoning system must register not only what its actions change but everything those actions leave alone. Move a robot from one room to another and an enormous number of facts survive untouched: the walls are still standing, the table has not moved, the door is still shut, the battery is no more charged than it was. A system that had to re-establish all of this after every action would be paralysed, so the early researchers wrote frame axioms — one axiom for every fact that every action leaves unchanged. The number of axioms explodes, and because the world contains an unbounded number of facts, no finite list of them can ever be complete. The frame problem is therefore not a bug that somebody will eventually patch but a proof that symbol manipulation cannot scale: a rule-based system will always be missing something, which is why the field abandoned logic in favour of learning from data. Later systems tried to sidestep the difficulty by assuming that whatever an action does not explicitly change stays as it was, but that only moves the problem elsewhere, since the system still has to determine which of the countless things it has not been told about are the ones that matter."

I do not know enough to judge this, and I would rather have an audit I can actually use than a general introduction to the frame problem. Specifically:

1. Go through the passage clause by clause and tell me, for each, whether someone working on knowledge representation or a philosopher of AI would accept it, treat it as a harmless simplification, or reject it — and where it should be rejected, what is wrong and what the accurate statement would be.

2. The concluding claim is far stronger than anything in the passage's own description of the difficulty. Does the impossibility claim follow from the observation that there are unboundedly many facts? If not, what would actually be required to establish it? And what is the strongest defensible version of the essay's thesis — does it need the frame problem at all?

3. I suspect that more than one distinct difficulty is travelling under the single name "frame problem" here. If so, separate them, say which one the passage is genuinely describing, and identify any others it has quietly substituted in its place.

4. The sentence about the "assume nothing changes unless told otherwise" strategy strikes me as describing the failure in the wrong terms. What is the actual failure mode of that strategy, and is it a version of the frame problem or a further problem?

5. I have also come across the phrase used in a much broader setting, in discussions of how a mind — human or artificial — determines what is relevant to a situation in the first place. Is that the same difficulty as the one the essay describes, a different problem that has borrowed the name, or the essay's own topic under a description it never gives? I ask because the essay's rhetoric depends on the problem being both very old and unsolvable.
