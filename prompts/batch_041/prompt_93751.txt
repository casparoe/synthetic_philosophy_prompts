Two friends and I spent most of a long dinner on the old illustration of the donkey that starves between two identical bales of hay, and we ended up somewhere I didn't expect: we agreed that the story is obviously false as psychology — no real animal or person starves in that situation — and then couldn't agree on whether it is false as philosophy. One friend says the donkey is irrational because it fails to act, full stop. The other says the donkey has no reason to move, that this is simply true, and that the story is a trick: it invites us to think a choice must be licensed by a preference and then punishes the donkey for accepting the invitation. I think the second friend is closer to right, but the more cases I write down, the less my verdicts look like they come from a single view. I'd like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually fits them, and to be straight with me about which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are my verdicts, as concretely as I can put them. (1) Two apples in front of me, identical in every respect I can perceive, and I like them equally: taking either is fine, and if you ask why I took that one, the honest answer is that I have no reason and don't need one. The only irrational outcome is standing there until I starve. If there were a thousand identical apples I would take the nearest, and I notice I feel no difficulty at all, which suggests the trouble in these cases is never the number of options. (2) Vanilla or chocolate, liked equally: same verdict, each time. But if a friend observes that I have chosen vanilla every single time for a year, I feel I owe an explanation, even though I have just said that no individual choice needed one. (3) Two sealed envelopes, one containing a hundred pounds and one empty, and I know this but cannot tell which is which: I have no preference between the envelopes and a very strong preference between the outcomes, and a coin is a perfectly good way to pick — but I don't think this is the same phenomenon as the apples, since my indifference here is about my information rather than about the options. (4) Two job offers I have checked as thoroughly as I know how, with no difference that matters to me: choosing either is fine, and I would rather be the person who looked hard and then flipped a coin than the person who agonised for a year; but someone who flips on the first day, before looking, seems to me to have done something wrong, and I cannot say whether the fault is irrationality or something else, because by hypothesis the later flip was no better informed by reasons than the early one — it merely came after a search that found nothing. Relatedly, I don't think a status quo has any tie-breaking weight of its own: if the flat across the hall were identical to mine and moving were free, I would say the two were perfectly symmetric, and my reluctance to move would be nothing but the cost of moving. (5) A choice between two lives that are not equal in value but not comparable either — a life as a musician against a life as a doctor, where I don't think either is better and don't think they sit on a scale: I have to choose, the choice isn't irrational, and I think the choosing makes one of them better for me rather than merely recording which was better. A coin flip here seems worse than in the apple case, and not because the stakes are higher — I don't think stakes are what is doing the work. (6) One of my two children can be saved, both equally placed, and I love them equally: I grab one, and if I flip a coin first it feels like a betrayal, though I suspect that feeling is a superstition about agency rather than a moral fact. (7) I am allocating one kidney between two equally matched patients I don't know: here I think a lottery is required, and that choosing on a whim — I like his name better — would be a wrong. I cannot see what separates (7) from (6), and that gap is one of the things I most want you either to close or to tell me is unclosable. (8) Two friends both want to sit next to me at dinner and I am genuinely indifferent: my indifference does not license a coin flip; their preferences, which are equal, are what make the matter arbitrary, and if one of them cared more, that would decide it. So my own indifference is neither necessary nor sufficient for a choice to be arbitrary. (9) I say I am indifferent between two candidates and then discover, when the second is appointed, that I am disappointed: I was not indifferent, and indifference turns out to be a counterfactual matter rather than a report on what I can introspect.

My working hypothesis is crude: that a choice made without a preference is never irrational, because rationality governs the move from reasons to action and here there are no reasons to move from; that the only failure is failing to act; and that the felt need for a reason is a psychological quirk. I already know this does not cover (4), (5), (6), (7) or (8), and I suspect it is the wrong shape rather than merely incomplete. So: give me a principle, or a small set of principles with the division of labour between them spelled out, precise enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it. Then run it on cases I haven't listed — choose the ones that will stress it hardest, including at least one where the agent is deciding on someone else's behalf and one where the choice is irreversible and will make the agent into a different person — and tell me where your verdicts diverge from mine. Finally, say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would rather give up the principle or the verdict. I am not asking for a tour of what others have written about this; I want the principle itself, argued from the cases.
