I am drafting a position paper for a graduate seminar on idealisation and normative theory, and before I commit to its central package I should like an audit of whether the commitments hang together, rather than a defence of any one of them. My guiding thought is that facts about what an agent ought to do are facts about what that agent would have reason to do if they were fully informed, instrumentally coherent, and free of distorting influences; I treat the verdicts of this ideal counterpart as truth-makers for ought-claims, not as a useful heuristic. I also hold that the idealisation must be procedural: the ideal counterpart is specified only by constraints on information, coherence, and freedom from distortion, and not by building substantive moral verdicts into the description. I call the next constraint the laundering constraint: the ideal procedure must begin from the reasons the actual agent already has and filter them for coherence and information, rather than generating a wholly independent set of reasons; this, I think, keeps the account from alienating agents from their own normative standpoint. The relevant would is not a causal prediction about what some possible agent would do; the ideal is a standard, and it can hold even if no actual or possible agent could satisfy the description. I further hold that an actual agent's reasons are the reasons their ideal counterpart would recognise, so a consideration the ideal counterpart would ignore is not a reason for the actual agent, however important it may seem by ordinary lights. Even where idealisation is impossible—say, in choices under severe cognitive impairment or in conflicts between values that cannot be ranked—I want to say that there is a fact of the matter about what ought to be done, namely whatever the ideal procedure would output if it could be run. At the same time, I want the account to be action-guiding for non-ideal agents: it should tell us at least sometimes what to do now, not only what our ideal counterparts would do. I am content to say that ideal deliberation is not a decision procedure, but I do think its verdicts are the normative ones, with non-ideal verdicts serving as heuristics for approximating them. Finally, here is a case verdict that matters to me: in a medical decision involving a patient with moderate dementia, what the patient ought to do is what their ideal counterpart would advise, yet the patient's actual expressed preference should carry independent weight even when it diverges from that advice.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which are jointly tenable. Order the genuine tensions by severity, explain for each what would have to give and what it would cost, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Write the audit in American English.
