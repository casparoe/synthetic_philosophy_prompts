I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on the economics-and-epistemology literature about whether markets aggregate information, and one of the members — a friend who is far more confident about economics than I am — has been pushing an argument he says he worked out himself from first principles and has nicknamed the "Groucho principle." He presents it as a proof that the epistemic case for markets is incoherent, and he lays it out so smoothly that every step sounds like common sense. It runs roughly like this. (1) A trade happens only if both parties expect to gain from it. (2) A buyer expects to gain only if he believes the asset is worth more than the price, and a seller expects to gain only if she believes it is worth less; so at any agreed price the two parties disagree about the asset's value. (3) Two agents who are rational, share the same evidence, and share the same prior cannot disagree about that value — Bayesian agents with a common prior and common knowledge of one another's opinions are forced to agree — so any real disagreement must trace to a difference in evidence or in prior. (4) If it traces to evidence, one party is better informed, and the worse-informed party should read the other's willingness to trade as a warning and walk away; the trade unravels. (5) If it traces to a difference in prior, the trade is a bare bet on that difference, and neither side has any reason to expect an edge — they are taking opposite sides of a coin each believes to be biased in his own favor. (6) So every trade in an asset whose value turns on information is either an exploitation of the less-informed party or a wager with no expected edge. (7) Markets consist almost entirely of such trades, and yet we are told that prices aggregate dispersed knowledge and that participating in markets is a way of learning what others know. If (6) holds, that story is either a mirage or a con, and the person who thinks he is learning from prices is the mark.

I can't tell whether the argument is airtight and something simply has to give, or whether one step is quietly doing illegitimate work, but I do know the conclusion is too strong for me: it would seem to indict not just speculation but ordinary liquidity provision, insurance, and lending, none of which I am prepared to call irrational. I would like to keep (1) and (2), which read to me like nothing more than what it means for a trade to be voluntary, and I certainly want to reject (7). Tell me which step or steps to abandon and what abandoning them costs. If the trouble is an equivocation, name the two readings being run together and show where the slide occurs; if the argument is valid as far as it goes, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what a defensible account of markets has to look like once I do. Lead with your bottom line — the step you would reject — in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
