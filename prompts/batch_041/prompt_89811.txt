A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about consciousness, and the disagreement keeps circling back to a picture she leans on and I can't quite accept. Her picture is the one that turns up constantly in popular writing on the topic: consciousness is like the desktop interface of a computer. The icons, folders, and windows are what you see and manipulate; underneath them is a vast machinery of disk sectors and voltages that the interface conceals. On her telling, consciousness is exactly that kind of surface, a simplified and usable display the brain generates for itself, and the payoff she draws is that consciousness is therefore not a window onto anything real but a convenience, that introspection is systematically misleading about what is actually going on, and that the self sitting at the center of the screen, the thing that seems to be looking at the icons, is itself part of the display rather than a user behind it. I think the picture is doing more work than it can bear, but I could not say precisely where it fails, and I want a careful audit rather than a verdict.

Give me the correspondence table first. In the computer picture, what plays the role of the hardware, the operating system, the application, the icons and windows, the file that seems to be inside the folder but is not really there in that form, the mouse and pointer, the designer who built the interface, and the user who reads it? Then tell me which of these have any plausible counterpart in the consciousness case and which are being smuggled in.

Then the disanalogies, ordered by how much they matter for the conclusion she wants. I am especially interested in four. First, an interface is built by a designer for a user, and its whole point is to be legible to that user; if the brain's display evolved with no designer and no external user, does the analogy still run, or does it quietly require the very audience it was supposed to eliminate? Second, an interface can be more or less faithful: a well-designed one lets you act correctly on the underlying states even though it does not show them, and it can also be actively deceptive rather than merely coarse-grained. Is the conscious display accurate in the action-guiding sense, and if so, is the point that it is coarse, or that it is deceptive, or neither? Third, if the self at the center of the screen is part of the display, who or what is having the illusion, and if the answer is that there is no one, say plainly whether the analogy survives that answer or collapses into it. Fourth, an interface is a representation of something underneath it; is the conscious display meant to be a representation of neural activity at all, or is it just the thing itself under another description?

I would like you to separate what is actually settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and not to present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion, that consciousness is a usable fiction rather than a report on anything, is actually carried by the interface picture or whether it was going to be asserted with or without it, and if there is a better analogy for what she is after, what it would be.
