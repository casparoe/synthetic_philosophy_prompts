"Bostrom introduced the notion of a 'singleton' to talk about possible futures for humanity. (a) Define a singleton and explain how it differs from a world government. (b) Explain why the concept is supposed to be a useful tool for thinking about the long-term future. And here's what the student handed in:

"A singleton is a world order in which there is, at the highest level, a single decision-making agency. It isn't the same thing as a world government. A world government would be one way of producing a singleton, but a singleton could also take the form of a single superintelligent AI, a global empire, or a set of rules and institutions that nobody is really in charge of. What makes something a singleton isn't who holds power in the ordinary sense, but whether, at the top, there's one locus of decision-making rather than several rival ones.

The concept is useful because it lets us pull apart two questions that usually get tangled together: how concentrated power is, and how it's structured. For the long-term future, the interesting point is that a singleton would dissolve the coordination problem that makes competition between great powers so dangerous in the first place. If there's only one decision-maker at the top, then there's no war between rival top-level powers and no race to build some dangerous technology first, because there's no 'first' to beat. Bostrom therefore argues that a singleton is not just likely but necessary if humanity is going to survive the next few centuries.

Whether a singleton is good or bad depends entirely on what the singleton is and what values it has. A singleton run by a benevolent and well-informed agency could be a paradise; a singleton run by a misanthropic or merely indifferent one could be the worst outcome imaginable. The standard objection is value lock-in — whatever values the singleton has when it forms would be frozen in place forever, because there'd be no competing power left to correct it. That's why even people who think a singleton might be needed tend to want it constrained.

And yes, the scenario in (d) would count as a singleton. Nobody planned it and it isn't a government, but that doesn't matter, because the definition is about structure, not origin or intention. If a handful of interlocking institutions effectively set the terms for almost every important decision on Earth, then at the highest level there's one decision-making system, not several genuinely independent rivals, which is exactly what the definition asks for. It's a bit like an invisible hand producing a single decision-maker, which is a nice illustration of why the concept is useful: a singleton doesn't have to look like a dictatorship."
