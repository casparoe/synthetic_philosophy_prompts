Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether consequentialism can survive the fact that we have no idea what we are doing. Her case ran like this. Take almost any act, and its consequences ramify through a causally tangled world in ways no one could have anticipated: whether a donation funds a research programme or a grudge, whether a chance encounter sets a stranger on a path, whether a technology outlives and outgrows its inventors. The effects that dominate the ledger are the distant and the diffuse ones, and our evidence about them is nearly silent. So the expected value of an act is not merely hard to estimate but arguably undefined — the sum diverges, or depends on assumptions we cannot defend — and a doctrine that tells us to maximise it is therefore telling us nothing. I gather philosophers have a name for this, cluelessness, though I had never heard it before last night. My reply was that this is simply what uncertainty looks like from the inside, and that we act anyway.

I am a catastrophe-risk actuary at a reinsurer, so my working vocabulary is not the philosophers'. I price books of business by building a scenario catalogue — a few hundred or few thousand simulated futures, weighted by something we are careful to call a frequency rather than a probability — and then loading the result with a margin for the fact that the catalogue is itself a guess. I am fluent in the difference between parameter uncertainty (we know the shape and not the numbers) and structural uncertainty (we may have the wrong shape entirely); in dependence modelling, and in the difference between correlation and genuine tail dependence; in the fact that our estimates are driven by the tail, which is precisely where we have almost no data; and in the habit of pricing to survive ruin rather than to hit an expectation. I have read essentially no moral philosophy.

Put her argument into my terms, as carefully as you can: model specification, scenario generation, parameter and structural uncertainty, dependence, tail behaviour, ruin versus expectation, margins, model risk. Then tell me what the translation loses. Which parts of her case carry over cleanly — I suspect the butterfly-effect point is nothing but structural uncertainty, and I want to know whether that is right or a fudge. Which parts carry over only with distortion, and where the distortion comes from. And which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am suspicious of three items on my own side of the ledger, and I want to know whether they are genuine asymmetries between her position and mine or merely artefacts of how I earn a living: that I am permitted to discount the far future while she is not; that I can diversify across a portfolio of risks while she is staking a single world; and that I am allowed to answer to a ruin constraint rather than to an expectation, which is a different decision rule from the one consequentialism names.

Finally, tell me what the residue implies for the argument itself. If the parts that fail to survive the move into my vocabulary are the parts doing the real work in her case, then my reply was a non-answer and she wins. If they are the parts that were never doing any work, then the dispute is less substantive than three hours suggests, and I would like to know which it is — and whether anything in my vocabulary, such as treating model risk as a first-class quantity, reasoning about ambiguity rather than probability, or optimising against ruin instead of expectation, is a live option that the philosophical discussion has left out.
