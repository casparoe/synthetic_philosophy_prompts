I chair the Supplementary Materials Review Committee for the secondary social studies program in a mid-sized public school district, and I need to circulate our written decision memo before next week's meeting. A local parent group has donated forty copies of a self-published booklet and asked us to approve it as an optional enrichment unit for the junior-year world history course; the booklet, by a retired civil engineer in our town, is titled *When Was the World? A Skeptic's Guide to Ancient Dates*, and its argument is that conventional ancient chronology is a house of cards. Its first half is, as far as I can tell, mostly respectable: it walks through the chain of reasoning behind the standard Egyptian timeline — the Sothic cycle, Manetho's king lists, the Assyrian synchronisms — and observes, correctly enough, that credentialed Egyptologists disagree among themselves about the dates by decades, that the dating of the Thera eruption is genuinely contested, and that the conventional date of the Trojan War rests on a mixture of legend and inference. So far, so ordinary; this is the kind of uncertainty our own textbook acknowledges in a footnote. But the second half takes the same evidence and runs with it: if the dates can move by decades, they can move by centuries or a millennium, so the Exodus and the Trojan War are unhistorical; Atlantis was a real Bronze Age kingdom destroyed by the Thera eruption; and the pyramids' true builders left records that mainstream archaeology suppresses.

Our district policy requires that supplementary materials be "accurate, balanced, and free of claims that are widely rejected by the relevant scholarly community," but in fifteen years no one has ever defined what "widely rejected" means, and the committee is split. Two members want to reject the whole booklet because it mixes live scholarly debate with fringe claims in a way that will teach students that the two carry equal authority; two others want to approve it precisely because it would teach students to tell the difference, and they argue that refusing a locally donated resource on viewpoint grounds looks like censorship; the parent group has already warned the school board that a "no" will trigger a formal hearing. I have also been handed a memo from our curriculum director arguing that the booklet is harmless because its claims "can't be falsified, so they aren't even scientific claims" — which strikes me as both philosophically shaky and institutionally useless, since our policy says nothing about falsifiability and our teachers are historians, not physicists.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. I need a concrete recommendation — adopt as is, reject, or adopt with specified conditions — that the committee, and if necessary the board, can actually act on, with the reasoning laid out so that the losing side's strongest point is treated honestly rather than waved away. The memo should also give reviewers workable criteria for the next such donation, since I expect more of them, and it should say plainly what we are and are not claiming when we certify a resource as "accurate."
