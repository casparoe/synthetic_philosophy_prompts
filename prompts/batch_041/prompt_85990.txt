Ok so I'm building a little collection of dialogues on philosophy-of-tech topics and I need one on worst-case vs average-case reasoning in AI alignment. Can you write it?

Act as someone who's genuinely at home in both ML theory and decision theory — like a researcher who's worked on robustness but also knows the decision-theory literature — and script a dialogue between two alignment researchers who disagree about how safety guarantees should be stated and evaluated. Name them whatever you like. Roughly: R holds that "if you can't bound the worst case, you haven't shown anything," and A holds that "worst-case bounds are either vacuous or useless, and expected performance under a sensible distribution is the only thing that has ever been actionable." Neither is a caricature: R is not a doom poster and A is not a move-fast-and-break-things person.

Before writing, brainstorm to yourself: what is the strongest version of each position, and what is the strongest objection each can make to the other? Then write the dialogue so that it plays out those objections rather than restating the positions.

Things I want them to actually fight about (they can add more, but these should come up):

- What exactly is "the worst case" over? Worst case over inputs at test time, over a shift in the training distribution, and over the agent's own future policy updates — the thing where a system behaves while it's being watched and differently once it can act — are three very separate things, and it's not obvious the same formal tools cover them. R should argue the third is the one that matters for alignment. A should point out that it isn't a well-defined worst case at all without some model of how the agent's future self gets selected.
- The vacuity worry. If the adversary is unconstrained, the maximin policy is often "do nothing," and a guarantee against every environment in some enormous class tends to be either trivially satisfiable or provably unsatisfiable. So what's the reference class, and who gets to pick it? A should press hard here.
- The prior worry, from the other side. Average-case guarantees need a distribution over tasks or environments, and the choice of distribution is doing all the work. A robustness ball around a nominal distribution just relocates the problem to picking the radius. R should press hard here. I'd like them to get into whether "we don't know the distribution" is better modeled as ambiguity (a set of distributions, optimize against the worst member) or as a higher-order probability, and whether the difference is substantive or bookkeeping.
- One-shot vs repeated. A's fallback is basically the law of large numbers: we don't get one roll of the dice, we get millions of decisions and many deployments. R's comeback is that the failure that matters is a single irreversible event, so the ensemble average is being taken over precisely the thing we can't average over. I don't want a winner here — I want them to notice they're using "average" in two different senses (average over time and deployments vs average over a prior over possible worlds) and then have to untangle that.
- Whether "adversary" is even the right frame. Worst-case reasoning treats the environment as an opponent, which makes sense for security. But if the failure mode is our own optimization pressure bending a proxy rather than an external attacker, maybe neither maximin nor expected value is the right shape of answer, and you need something about the mechanism that generates the outcome. Let them disagree about whether that's a real alternative or just a restatement.
- A concrete running example. Have them argue about an actual proposal: a lab says it will deploy only once it can certify that loss is below some ε for every policy in the training class and every environment in a fixed eval suite. R: that's a guarantee about the eval suite, not about the world. A: the alternative — "expected loss under our best guess of deployment conditions is low" — is a guarantee about nothing, because the guess is exactly the part that's wrong. Then let them explore whether any version survives both objections. Something like certifying the training process rather than the outcome, or reporting a frontier of (severity of worst case, probability assigned to that case) pairs and making the decision-maker be explicit about what they're trading off, would be fair game.

Some texture I want:

- Each should concede at least one real point to the other. R conceding that maximin can be dominated by an impossible scenario, A conceding that a mean is a terrible summary when the loss distribution has a fat tail, that kind of thing.
- They should respond to each other's actual last point rather than delivering monologues. I'd rather have a slightly ragged conversation with interruptions than two tidy position papers.
- Dont cite papers or name-drop authors, and no reading list — it'd just be padding, and if they mention a technique they should describe it in a sentence instead.
- No forced resolution. Ending on a sharpened disagreement, or on a narrower question they now agree is the crux, is better than a synthesis.

Aim for maybe 1200–1800 words of dialogue, in a conversational register — two colleagues arguing in a hallway, not writing abstracts. Stage directions only sparingly.
