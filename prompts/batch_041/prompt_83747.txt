My nephew is four and was born with cataracts in both eyes, so he's basically never seen anything, and there's a surgery coming up this spring that has a decent chance of giving him some vision for the first time. His eye doctor was careful not to promise much, and when my sister asked whether he'd be able to tell a ball from a block once he can see, the doctor said something like he'll have to learn what things look like, and no one can really say how much of that comes for free. Which sent me down a rabbit hole, because it turns out philosophers have been going back and forth about almost exactly this for centuries — the old question about the person blind from birth who learns to tell a cube from a sphere by touch and then gets his sight, and whether he recognizes them right away. I'm taking a seminar in perception this term and I have to turn in a proposal for my term paper (one semester, so like 6000 words max, and please don't give me a reading list or citations — I'll add those myself).

Here's my half-formed idea, and I'd like you to turn it into an actual proposal. I think the old question is three questions wearing one coat, and that alot of the stalemate comes from different people answering different ones. (a) The psychological one: would the newly sighted person in fact recognize the shape, which seems empirical and might come out yes for simple shapes and no for messy ones — I have no idea and I'm not betting either way. (b) The conceptual one: is there any *reason* available to him, any inference or a priori route, from his tactile knowledge of the shape to a visual judgment about it, or is the link between the shape that feels like this and the shape that looks like that something only experience can forge? (c) The one I actually care about: what does the same shape even mean across the two senses, and does the answer to (b) depend entirely on how you answer (c)? Because if a shape concept is a geometric description (six equal faces, eight corners) maybe there's a route, and if it's a recognitional capacity or a bundle of sensorimotor contingencies (the way it feels when you run your hands over it, the way it looks when you walk around it) then there's no route at all and the question seems to answer itself — which makes me suspicious that the whole puzzle is really about how you individuate shape concepts rather than about perception.

So what I want is a proposal where the question is stated so that it has a determinate answer, and where the payoff is deciding whether this is a substantive question about cross-modal perception or a question about concept individuation in disguise — and if it's the latter, whether that makes it a merely verbal dispute or still a real one. Say why the usual positions (the ones that say obviously not, those are two different ideas, and the ones that say the shape is just the same shape) don't settle it; what method I'd actually use, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives like doing a history of the debate or a rundown of the sight-restoration cases; what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; and what would count as the project failing. Also be honest with me about whether this is too big for one semester, and if it is, what to cut — I'd rather write a tight paper on (c) than a survey that touches everything and lands nowhere.
