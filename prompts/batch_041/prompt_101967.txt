I chair the standing advisory committee of a state environmental-health agency. Our charge is to recommend a single numeric limit for a regulated airborne contaminant: the concentration at or below which the agency declares exposures acceptable, and above which it requires abatement. We have been deadlocked for two cycles, and I need to circulate a written opinion before the next vote.

Two positions have hardened. One holds that because every reduction in exposure lowers the expected number of illnesses, each successively tighter limit is simply better than the looser one, so any limit we name is indefensible and we should adopt the tightest limit industry can technically meet. The other holds that shaving the limit, say, from 40 to 35 parts per billion buys so little health and costs so much abatement that it is not an improvement at all, and that below some point tightening makes things worse.

Three features of our situation make this harder than an ordinary cost-benefit dispute. First, our bylaws instruct us to adopt the option that is better than every other option considered, and we have always read that as licensing a sequence of pairwise comparisons. Second, two years ago we adopted a ratchet clause: once a limit is set it may be tightened but never loosened, so every concession is permanent and the sequence of small steps runs in one direction only. Third, a member has proposed that we set the limit at the point of diminishing returns, but no one has been able to say what that is without circularity.

Here is the pattern I cannot shake. Consider a sequence of candidate limits, each one step tighter than the last, with each step chosen small enough that no member of the committee is willing to say the tighter limit is worse. Nearly everyone grants that each step, taken alone, is at least an improvement or at worst a wash. But almost no one is prepared to say that the tightest limit in the sequence is better than the loosest; the cost would be enormous and the health gain negligible. The first camp says this shows we must go all the way. The second says it shows the small steps were never improvements to begin with.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should reach an actionable bottom line — a specific limit, or, if the honest conclusion is that the number cannot be read off the philosophy, a clear statement of what the number should be fixed by and what the philosophy does constrain — and it should give reasons both camps can engage with rather than dismissing either. It must address, in terms we can actually vote on: whether better than is transitive, that is, whether a chain of pairwise improvements must add up to an overall improvement; whether our instruction to adopt the best option is satisfiable at all if it is not; what precisely goes wrong in the sequence above — a false premise at some step, a failure of transitivity, or a mistake in how we are comparing options; and whether the right response is to accept the uncomfortable conclusion, to deny transitivity, or to decline to compare options that differ by imperceptible degrees. Please also say how the ratchet clause changes the answer, if it does, and give the strongest version of whichever position you end up rejecting. I would rather have a memo that marks plainly which steps are logic and which are value judgment than one that pretends the number follows from the premises.
