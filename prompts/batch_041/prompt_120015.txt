I've spent the term in a seminar on comparative epistemology, and I've talked myself into a position I did not expect to hold. I now think the Nyāya enumeration of the pramāṇas is a substantive thesis about the sources of knowledge rather than a piece of scholastic bookkeeping, and that it can be defended on the merits. As I would state it, the thesis has four parts: there are exactly four means of valid cognition — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogy (upamāna, the cognition that arises when someone who has been told that a gavaya is like a cow comes upon one and recognises what it is), and verbal testimony (śabda); the list is complete and principled rather than gerrymandered; testimony is a genuinely independent source, not a disguised inference from the reliability of the speaker; and the validity of a cognition is established by subsequent verification rather than being delivered along with the cognition itself, which is where the Nyāya account parts company with the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of intrinsic validity. What I want from you is a map of what this position risks — not a survey of who held what, but the arguments.

Take the rival enumerations seriously as potential defeaters rather than as curiosities: the Buddhist reduction of the pramāṇas to two, with testimony folded into inference; the Mīmāṃsā list of six, which adds postulation (arthāpatti — as when, seeing that a man who eats nothing by day is nonetheless growing fat, one concludes that he eats at night) and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi — knowing that a thing is absent simply from the conditions of its not being perceived); and the Cārvāka insistence on perception alone. Which of these reductions, if any, actually succeeds, and if one does, would that show the fourth pramāṇa to be redundant, or only that the Naiyāyika's way of drawing the boundary is stipulative? I'd like the modern analogue pressed too, not waved at: the dispute between reductionists and non-reductionists about testimony, the reliabilist's willingness to count any reliably truth-conducive process as knowledge-conferring, the internalist's demand that the subject have some appreciation of the warrant.

And I'd like the hard cases pushed hard: practical know-how, memory, the counterfactual reasoning (tarka) that Nyāya itself leans on everywhere while refusing to call it a source of knowledge, and whatever it is we get from a map, a diagram, or a well-built model. If you can describe a case in which we plainly come to know something and the scheme must either shoehorn it into one of the four or deny that it is knowledge, that is the sort of thing that would move me. So: sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, distinguish the ones a committed Naiyāyika would themselves concede from the ones they would simply refuse to count as evidence, and tell me honestly whether the enumeration risks being true by definition — and if it does, whether that is a defect in the position or a sign that the question was never empirical in the first place.
