I work on higher-order evidence, and I have been trying to determine what my own verdicts about it come to. I would like you to extract the principle — or the best small set of competing principles — that fits the pattern below. State each candidate precisely, attach each clause to the case that motivates it, say what it predicts for cases I have not described, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

In each case I give the situation and then my verdict.

1. Long sum, general news. I am a reliably accurate mental calculator. Working through a long sum, I reach 4,872 and feel confident. I then learn of a well-conducted study (stipulated, for our purposes) finding that people of my general profile, on sums of this length, make undetectable errors about one time in five. My verdict: I should markedly lower my confidence and should not assert 4,872 to others, but I am still rationally permitted to believe the answer is 4,872 rather than suspend judgment.

2. Long sum, present-state news. The same sum, the same felt confidence — but I have had three drinks and know that people in my state make frequent undetectable errors at exactly this kind of computation. My verdict: I must suspend judgment; believing 4,872 is not rationally permitted, even though I feel just as sure.

3. Peer disagreement. Two equally trained and equally careful paleontologists examine the same fossil and reach opposite conclusions about whether it belongs to a new species; each knows the other's credentials and that the other is about as likely to be right. My verdict: each should move substantially toward the other's view, and neither may retain full confidence.

4. An undetectable condition. A mathematician completes a long proof and is convinced it is valid. She is then reliably informed that she has a stipulated condition — call it Lund's drift — that leaves her reasoning unimpaired as she experiences it but raises the rate of undetectable slips in multi-step derivations to about one in three. My verdict: she must suspend judgment about the proof's validity; continued confidence is not rationally permitted.

5. Screened-off higher-order evidence. A detective reasons to a conclusion using a method that is, in general, unreliable. She then learns that the method fails only because most users omit a particular cross-check, and that she in fact performed that cross-check. My verdict: she may retain full confidence in her conclusion, with no discount whatsoever.

6. Akrasia. A philosopher is persuaded by her first-order evidence that a thesis T is true. She is also persuaded, on higher-order grounds — she knows that people who reason about T the way she did are usually mistaken — that her believing T is irrational. My verdict: she cannot rationally occupy both states at once; the combination is impermissible, even though there is something to be said for each state on its own.

Two features of this list are what make it hard. Cases 1 and 2 both give me news that I am unreliable at the very task in front of me, yet I treat them differently. And case 5 appears to give higher-order evidence no weight at all, which sits uneasily with cases 2 and 4.

What I want from you:

(a) The principle, or the small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without further interpretation. If more than one candidate is needed, say what each gets that the others do not.
(b) For each clause, the case that forces it.
(c) The verdicts of your preferred principle on at least two further cases that you devise: one in which the higher-order evidence is itself supported only by testimony I have reason to distrust, and one in which the first-order and higher-order evidence bear on the same proposition rather than on the same faculty.
(d) An honest statement of which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and, if you think a verdict should be revised rather than saved by added complexity, say which one and what the revised set of verdicts looks like.

Please write in American English.
