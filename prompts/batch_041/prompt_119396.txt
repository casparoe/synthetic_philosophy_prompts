I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-four years in a materials laboratory, mostly on the thermodynamics of thin films — and for the past four years I have been reading moral and political philosophy with more enthusiasm than system. I have written a short essay, which I set out below, about an idea I suspect is too popular for its own good: that a compromise between two value systems is a case of gains from trade. I should be grateful for your comments.

Gains from Trade in the Moral Case

§1. The analogy. When two parties exchange, each surrenders something it values less for something it values more. The agreements that neither party would reject define a frontier; every point on that frontier is an improvement on the disagreement point; and where on the frontier the parties actually settle is settled by bargaining power rather than by welfare. Applied to moral disagreement, the analogy promises a great deal. It explains why compromise is normally thought better than deadlock. It makes the refusal to compromise look like a failure of rationality rather than a failure of virtue. And it supplies a criterion — the compromise should lie on the frontier — that appears to be independent of anyone's substantive moral view.

§2. First presupposition: a transferable currency. In my own field, an exchange is intelligible only because something conserved is measured in common units: one can trade work for heat because both are counted in joules. Money plays that role in markets. Because a common medium exists, every efficient arrangement can be reached, and the parties need not agree about the relative worth of the goods they trade — the price does the work for them. Moral compromises ordinarily forbid side payments. One cannot compensate a pacifist for a war by remitting his taxes, nor buy a vegetarian's acquiescence in an abattoir with a subsidy for vegetables; the rate of exchange between two permissions is precisely the thing in dispute. So the bargain is barter, under bilateral monopoly, without a price. I anticipate the reply that axiomatic bargaining theory supplies price-free solutions: the one that maximises the product of the parties' gains over the disagreement point, and the one that equalises their proportional gains towards the jointly ideal point. I know these exist. My objection is not that they are unavailable but that their inputs are not given. Each requires a feasible set, a disagreement point, and a cardinal representation of each party's preferences, and in the moral case all three are contested. The feasible set is contested because what counts as an available compromise is itself a moral question. The disagreement point is contested because there is no neutral description of what happens if we fail to agree. And the utility representation is contested because the parties dispute not merely the weights of the goods at stake but which goods are at stake at all.

§3. Second presupposition: a fixed reference point. The analogy treats "no agreement" as a datum. In moral cases it is a characterisation, and the parties characterise it differently — present law for one, the just baseline for the other. Worse, the compromise becomes the new status quo, so every settlement alters the reference point for the next round. Each bargain therefore imposes a precedent externality on parties who were not present. A trade in two goods between two neighbours has no such downstream effect. Moral settlements are ratchets.

§4. Third presupposition: closure of the bargain. A trade between two parties improves the position of both and leaves everyone else alone, the familiar externalities aside. Moral compromises bind non-parties: dissenters within each camp, the unconceived, the inarticulate. A bilateral agreement that imposes costs on those who were not at the table is not a Pareto improvement, and none of them has consented. The trade framing thus converts a political question — who is entitled to be at the table — into a bilateral one, and the conversion is invisible because the table is the picture's frame.

§5. What I want to put in its place. If the analogy fails at these three points, compromise may be better understood as the joint authorship of a new norm than as the exchange of existing entitlements. Two authors of a paper do not exchange paragraphs; neither could have written it alone; and each is answerable for the whole, including the parts written by the other. On this account the currency objection dissolves, since nothing is priced because nothing is transferred. The closure objection softens, since a co-authored norm is publicly owned and its terms can be contested by those it binds. And the reference-point objection becomes an insight rather than a complaint: what a compromise does is to manufacture a new object for future disagreement to be about. It also preserves a distinction the trade model flattens — the difference between a compromise we admire and one we call a sell-out. A trade that leaves both parties better off cannot be a betrayal; an authorship can, because the author is answerable for a text that is partly not hers.

§6. What I should like from you.

(a) Is §5 a genuine alternative to the trade model, or a redescription of it in more flattering terms? If the latter, say so plainly, and tell me what the redescription achieves that the exchange model cannot.

(b) Where have I mis-stated the economics? I am confident about the physics and unreliable about the rest, and I would rather be corrected here than anywhere else.

(c) Of my three objections, which is the weakest and ought to be dropped? I suspect it is §3, on the grounds that the ratchet is a general feature of institutions rather than a defect of the analogy — but I cannot tell whether that is a defence of the analogy or an admission that it has no distinctive content.

(d) Give me the strongest counterexample you can: a moral compromise that is plainly an exchange of entitlements, and in which the authorship picture is a sentimental overlay. One devastating example would serve me better than five plausible ones.

(e) If the authorship model makes compromise too easy — if any party may say, "I have not abandoned my values, I have co-authored a new set" — what constraint would you add to keep the model honest?

Please answer in British English, and please do not send me a reading list. I have neither the library nor the patience, and I am more interested in whether the argument works than in who has made it before.
