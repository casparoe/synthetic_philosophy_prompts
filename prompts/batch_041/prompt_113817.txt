For the final exam of an upper-level epistemology seminar, I need a single essay question on the regress of justification, and I want it to be one that cannot be answered by reciting the trilemma. Every year the same script comes back: foundationalism stops the regress in basic beliefs, coherentism makes it a circle, infinitism lets it run and calls that acceptable, and the candidate declares one horn intuitively best. I want a question that forces the machinery of the argument into view rather than its conclusion, and in particular forces the candidate to say which of its assumptions is doing the work of making the three options look exhaustive in the first place.

Build the question around the following angle. The regress is usually run on beliefs and on a relation of full, transitive support: A is justified by B, B by C, and each link must be intact before the next can do its work. The version I actually worry about runs on credences rather than beliefs, in the style of a Bayesian agent, where a credence is licensed by prior credences together with conditionalization, and one then asks what licenses the priors. Two features of that setting look as though they might matter. First, probabilistic support is partial and in general non-transitive: a high probability of B given A, and of C given B, does not by itself yield a high probability of C given A. Second, the chain of licensing never has to be completed inside the agent's head for the agent to be rational, since the question is whether the reasons are available rather than whether they have all been traversed. I want to know whether those two features defuse the regress, relocate it, or leave it exactly where it was.

Please write the essay question, then a model answer of roughly 600 words, then a marking scheme, with these constraints.

The question should make the candidate do at least three things. Reconstruct the regress argument in a form whose premises are explicit enough to be attacked one at a time. Say precisely which premise the partial-and-non-transitive-support observation would have to dislodge, and whether dislodging it is a genuine answer to the argument or a change of subject. Then run the regress a second time at the level of the standard or criterion rather than at the level of the item being justified — what justifies the claim that justification is a matter of coherence, or of basic beliefs, or of available reasons — and say whether that second regress is the first problem in new clothes or a distinct one.

Aim the question at candidates who have done the standard reading; the difficulty should come from the machinery, not from unfamiliar terminology. Do not build it around any named author or text, since I do not want an answer that can be graded by name-dropping. It sits in a fifty-minute slot and is worth twenty marks. Give the must-hit moves as separate items with the marks each is worth, and for each one a single line on how candidates typically lose it. Make the A/B line visible: an A answer gets to the criterion-level regress and takes a definite stand on whether the non-transitivity point helps, while a B answer reconstructs the trilemma cleanly and evaluates one horn well but never arrives at the standard-level question. Write the model answer the way a strong candidate would write it under time pressure — compressed, argumentative, no throat-clearing — and have it land on a verdict rather than survey the options.

One more thing, since I have spent too long in the rationalist blogs and forums and it has warped me: I do not want the model answer treating the availability of reasons as obviously sufficient. Availability is doing real work there, and I want the answer to say what it would take to earn that notion rather than assume it. An A answer, in my book, is one that would survive a hostile comment thread.
