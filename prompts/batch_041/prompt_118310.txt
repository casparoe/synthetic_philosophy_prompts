For several months I have been reporting a profile of a philosopher who works on convention and on how norms evolve, and one of the things I have been collecting is her rulings on everyday cases — the verdicts she delivers at the end of a seminar when someone puts a practice to her. Her record so far: driving on a particular side of the road is a convention; standing to the right on an escalator is a convention as well, and she reads the irritation aimed at people who stand on the left as impatience rather than moral reproach; saying “bless you” after a sneeze is not a convention, since there is no coordination problem to solve and nothing about whether I do it depends on whether you do; a rule that people observe only because breaking it draws a fine is not a convention either, because conformity has to be something people prefer on the condition that others conform, rather than something they are merely compelled to do; and a practice does not stop being a convention merely because it began as someone’s arbitrary decree a century ago, so long as the mutual expectations are still in place.

The case I need to sort out before I file is this. At a mid-sized firm I have been interviewing, most of the staff are bilingual in Spanish and English and the meetings move between the two languages. Over the years a pattern has settled: whoever called the meeting opens in whichever language they used in the calendar invitation, and everyone else answers in that language for the first few minutes, after which the meeting drifts into English. Nobody decided this, nobody has ever discussed it, and when I asked around, no one could state the rule — two people who insisted there was no rule then followed it in the meeting I sat in on. New hires pick it up within a week or so, mostly by copying. Last month a new engineer sent a Spanish invitation and then opened the meeting in English, and the room stalled for a beat; two people looked irritated, though nobody said anything and nobody thought she had done anything wrong. Opening in the invitation’s language and opening in English would work equally well; what keeps the first few minutes from turning into a scramble is simply that everyone does the same thing, whichever thing it is.

I do not need a survey of the literature. I want a prediction of her verdict on the firm’s practice. Set out the rules her record is compatible with — I take it these include the common-knowledge account of the kind David Lewis gave, the evolutionary account on which a convention is just the stable outcome of imitation and adaptive learning and requires no common knowledge at all, and the view that the real dividing line is whether deviation draws normative expectations of the sort that license resentment — say what each rule predicts here, tell me which one I should bet she is running and why, and, if the earlier verdicts genuinely leave the case open, describe the further case that would tell the rules apart. Please write your answer in American English.
