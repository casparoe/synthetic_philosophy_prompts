I am trying to decide how much of the foundation of normativity to rest on indirect normativity — the strategy of specifying what is good or right not by stating a first-order criterion but by specifying a procedure, an idealized deliberation, whose output is then stipulated to be authoritative. I want your credence in one specific, load-bearing claim, with the reasoning that produces it laid out so I can see where it is weakest.

The claim: some specification of the idealized-deliberation procedure escapes both of the following horns at once.

The vacuity horn: a procedure that holds the agent's own ends, commitments, and reflective self-understanding fixed, and idealizes only her information and her reasoning, cannot generate reasons that bind an agent who rejects its verdict — its output is at best a description of what she already cares about, so the view stops being a foundation and becomes a redescription of the agent.

The estrangement horn: a procedure that idealizes the ends too — subjecting them to whatever scrutiny the ideal deliberator can bring to bear — may produce a verdict the actual agent has no reason to accept, since the deliberator is no longer her in any sense that matters; she is being told to obey a stranger who happens to share her name.

Two clarifications, so we are talking about the same thing. By "does not presuppose substantive first-order normative content" I mean that the specification of the procedure — who the deliberator is, what she knows, how she reasons, what she cares about — can be given without assuming any particular answer to questions such as whether well-being is aggregable, whether equality is intrinsically valuable, or whether the claims of a distant stranger have the same force as those of one's own child. By "genuine normative authority" I mean the verdict supplies the agent a reason she has even when her ends, her commitments, and her reflective endorsement all point the other way — not merely a prediction about what she would want were she different.

Decompose your credence into at least the following terms, and add any you think belong.

1. That ideal reasoning can be specified without normative content — that "reasoning flawlessly" is not itself a covertly normative notion.
2. That full information is a coherent idealization, given that some facts may be essentially perspectival, or may change the agent who comes to know them, so that the informed deliberator is no longer the agent whose reasons are in question.
3. That the space of admissible idealizations converges: that two procedures differing only in which of the agent's attitudes are held fixed and which are idealized deliver the same verdicts.
4. That the output of an idealized procedure has authority over an actual dissenting agent — the estrangement problem, or the suspicion that the agent is being asked to defer to someone who is not her.
5. That the view is not merely a schema — that it does not quietly inherit its content from first-order ethics, so that the real work is all done by tacit assumptions about what the ideal deliberator cares about.
6. Whatever else you think the number turns on.

Then tell me how these combine into your headline number, which single term the headline number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence — a counterexample, a convergence result, or a case in which the procedure's verdict and the agent's most reflective commitment diverge — would move the number most, and in which direction. If you judge the claim not well-posed enough to have a truth value, say so, but still give the number you would defend and state what you are assigning credence to instead.

Format the answer as follows, in this order, at roughly the lengths indicated.

- Verdict (two sentences): the headline number, and the proposition in a single clause.
- The proposition, pinned down (one short paragraph): what would count as the claim's being true, and what would count as its being false.
- Decomposition (table or numbered list): each term with its probability and one line of justification.
- Combination (one short paragraph): how the terms combine, with the arithmetic or the combination rule made explicit.
- Sensitivity and update (one short paragraph): the term the number is most sensitive to, and the evidence that would move it most.
- Residual doubts (two or three sentences): what about your own framing of the question you distrust.
