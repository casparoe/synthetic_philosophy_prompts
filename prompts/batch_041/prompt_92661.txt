ok so im not a historian, i just read alot of history on my own and i've gotten kind of obsessed with this thing i keep running into called presentism, the worry that whenever you write about the past you're secretly just measuring it with a ruler from your own time. i finally watched the film of A Man for All Seasons (then went and got the Bolt play it's based on) and i can't stop chewing on it as a test case. on the surface it's a 16th century story about Thomas More resigning as Lord Chancellor and then refusing to swear the oath that made Henry VIII head of the church, and getting executed for it. but the More in the play feels like a 20th century hero to me, a guy whose conscience is basically the modern idea that you have to stay true to your own inner self even when the law, the king, your family, everyone is against you. my gut says the real More wouldnt have understood conscience that way at all, his conscience wouldve been God and the church speaking through him, not some authentic inner voice he has to protect. so is the play presentist, and if it is, is that a flaw that wrecks it or is it the whole point?

i want you to actually read the whole thing through that lens and not just answer yes its presentist. where does it get the history right, where does it bend it, and where does the bend tell us more about 1960 than about 1535? a couple of things that nag me. the play leaves out that the historical More went after heretics pretty hard, he wasnt just a gentle man with a conscience, he had people burned, and i only found that out by accident reading around. is leaving that out presentism, like we keep the bits that flatter our values and quietly drop the rest, or is that just what drama does when it needs a shape? and then theres the Common Man, the figure who keeps reappearing as different servants and boatmen and the jailer, hes obviously not a real person from the 1500s, hes a modern stage device. does that make him the play admitting out loud that it cant get out of its own time, or is it a cop out? same with the fact that they cast Henry as this young athletic guy when he was nearly 40 and already getting fat by then. does the play know what its doing or does it just do it and hope you dont notice?

the bigger thing i actually want out of this: does the play make any argument about whether you can EVER tell a story about the past without your own standards leaking in? because half of me thinks the honest answer is no and this play is a great example, and half of me thinks thats too easy, that a good historical drama is exactly the thing that lets you feel the distance between their world and ours instead of erasing it. and if the play does have something to say to the presentism argument that you cant get from the historians going back and forth about it, what is it? please be straight with me where my little theory breaks and the play resists the reading, i'd rather find out where im wrong than get a tidy essay that makes it all fit. no need to point me at books or sources, i wont be able to chase them down, i just want the reading itself.
