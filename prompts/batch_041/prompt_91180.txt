You are a philosopher of ancient economic thought who is also a close reader of Shakespeare, and I want a genuinely critical reading rather than a survey of received opinion. Before you write anything, work the scenes and the relevant Aristotelian passages through carefully in your own thinking, and do not pad the result.

I am writing an essay on The Merchant of Venice and want to read the play through Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange: his claim in Book I of the Politics that every possession has two uses, one proper to it and one for trade, and that money is a matter of convention rather than nature; his contrast between household management and acquisition pursued for its own sake, with usury as the clearest case of the latter because it makes money breed from money; and the account of exchange in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, where goods unlike in kind are made commensurable through need, with money as the conventional measure of that need, together with the discussion of equity at the end of the same book as the correction of legal justice. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.

Here is the reading I want tested, and I would rather be told it fails than have it flattered. The bond for a pound of flesh strikes me as Aristotle's anxiety about the conventionality of commensuration made literal: a contract that treats a man's body as exchangeable against a sum, and that can be enforced only by clinging to the letter of the words. On this view Portia's rescue is the opposite of Aristotelian equity; it is a literalism that happens to favour the defendant, and it would have destroyed him had Shylock chosen his words more carefully. I also want to know what to do with the rings: the two rings given as pledges and then demanded back, which circulate like money but are not priced, and the turquoise ring Shylock's wife Leah gave him, which he values beyond any sum and which Jessica trades away for a monkey. And I want the casket test brought in, since it asks suitors to choose among gold, silver and lead and thereby stages Aristotle's claim that wealth is useful rather than good, and that acquisition for its own sake has no natural limit.

Among the five points, please answer these. Does the play endorse the Aristotelian verdict on usury, or does it expose that verdict as a way of sorting people, given that the Venetians' own fortunes rest on the same commerce and on the inheritance that funds Belmont? Is the failure of the bond a demonstration of Aristotle's point that nature resists commensuration, or a legalism that shows convention can be turned any way a clever speaker likes? Does the ring plot present a kind of value that Aristotle's money-measure cannot capture, or does it merely sentimentalize what money already does? And in the last point, say what the play adds to the philosophical debate that Aristotle's argument on its own does not.
