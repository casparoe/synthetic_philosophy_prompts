I trained as an evolutionary biologist and work mostly on comparative methods. My everyday tools are these: reconstructing how traits change along a phylogeny; handling the fact that species are not independent data points because they inherit from shared ancestors — the problem that gets called Galton's problem, and the family of corrections, independent contrasts and their descendants, that try to strip the shared-history signal back out; separating homology (similarity due to common descent) from analogy (similarity due to independent evolution under similar selection); asking whether a trait is an adaptation, a by-product, or drift; and treating historical contingency — the sense that what happened was one of many things the tape could have produced — as a substantive part of an explanation rather than as noise around a law.

I read economic history as an amateur, and I keep having the impression that the debate over why some nations are rich is being conducted by people who have been forbidden to use the tools of comparative biology. As I understand it, there are three main camps. One says the deep cause is institutions: the rules that secure property, constrain rulers, and make exchange credible. One says it is geography and ecology: climate, disease environment, the wild plants and animals available for domestication, access to sea trade. One says it is culture: beliefs, trust, and attitudes toward work, thrift, and cooperation. All three lean heavily on comparing countries, and the more careful work leans on natural experiments or historical accidents that are supposed to break the confounding.

What I want from you is a systematic translation of that debate into my vocabulary — not a summary for a general reader, and not a survey of who said what. Assume I know the biology cold and the economic history only loosely. I don't need citations or a reading list; if a claim can't be made without a name attached to it, give me the claim instead of the name.

Do the translation claim by claim. For each of the three camps, tell me what its central explanatory claim becomes when it is stated as a claim about trait evolution across a phylogeny: is "institutions cause prosperity" an adaptationist claim, a claim about a selection pressure, a claim about a heritable trait, or something else — and does the translation preserve what the economists take themselves to be saying? Do the same for the methods: cross-country regression, instruments built on historical accidents, and "controlling for" geography or for institutions. What are those in my terms, and where do they come apart from independent contrasts or from reconstructing ancestral states? Do the same for the explanatory target itself: what is the analogue, in my vocabulary, of the question "why is this country rich?" Is it "why does this lineage have this trait," or is it something with no biological counterpart?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which parts of the debate pass through the translation intact, which arrive only after being distorted, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? I expect the interesting cases to be things like the economic notion of a cause as a marginal, in-principle-reversible parameter that could be varied while everything else is held fixed; the idea that a nation is a unit that can be counted; and the normative question of what a country ought to do, which I suspect has no biological analogue whatever. But don't just confirm my guesses — tell me what I have missed and where I have misidentified the mapping.

Keep the translation honest. The failure mode I am worried about is that you import my framework's assumptions — that every trait is there for a reason, that shared ancestry explains most similarity, that contingency means unpredictability — and then present them as if they were the economists' claims. If some piece of the debate only goes through because I have quietly changed what it was saying, say so.

Format the answer in four sections, in this order:

1. "The position to be translated" (~150 words): the shared question and the three camps, stated plainly enough that we agree on what is being translated.
2. "The translation" (~800 words): the claim-by-claim and method-by-method restatement in comparative-biology vocabulary.
3. "The residue report" (~450 words): what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all.
4. "What the residue tells us" (~250 words): for the most important residues, whether they point to a genuine blind spot in my vocabulary or to a part of the economic debate that was less well-defined than it looked.
