Ok so I need you to write something for me and my head is fried because I just spent two and a half hours arguing about this with my friend Nadia, who teaches philosophy of science, and she was annoyingly good at it.

Background: I sit on the commission our diocese convenes when someone proposes a healing as a miracle. We have a case in front of us and I'm definately the one drafting the opinion we'll vote on at Thursdays meeting, so I need it by then.

The case. The candidate is a laywoman who died in 1961; a scrap of cloth that touched her body is kept at a shrine up north. In 2011 a man, 41, was diagnosed with stage IV pancreatic cancer with liver mets -- biopsy-confirmed, scans and slides all on file. He was also enrolled in a phase I trial of an experimental immune agent, which he'd started nine days before the first scan. His aunt pinned the cloth to his gown and the family prayed a novena. Six weeks later the imaging was clean; the repeat biopsy found nothing; he's still fine thirteen years on. Our five-person medical panel: three say complete, durable, no known natural explanation; one says the remission is currently unexplained but that he was on a drug nobody really understands yet, and that this cancer does, very rarely, regress on its own; one abstained. The trial itself published later and reported two other complete responses in about sixty patients. Our handbook says we're looking for a cure that is complete, lasting, and 'not explicable by known medical science' -- it says nothing about a law of nature being violated. We also get roughly 900 healing claims a year and formally investigate maybe one, so we only ever see the hits.

Nadia's position: Hume wins. Testimony can only establish a miracle if it would be more miraculous for the testimony to be false than for the event to be true, and here that isn't close. 'Not explicable by known medical science' is a fact about us, not about the world. The family's story has already drifted (the aunt first said the cloth went on before the third scan, now she says before the first). And she says the other shrine's files are full of other faiths' healing claims, which she says is Hume's second point, the one everybody forgets -- that the testimonies cancel each other out.

My position, which I could not defend very well at 11pm: laws of nature are just descriptions of what usually happens, so 'violation of a law of nature' isn't even a testable claim, and Hume's standard makes the finding impossible by definition. And what we're actually being asked isn't a physics question, it's whether God acted -- a judgement about a sign, not a lab result.
