I have a set of verdicts about when a decision should be made by lot rather than by election, expertise, or negotiation, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the principle that best fits the verdicts, even if it forces me to give one up.

My verdicts:

1. A criminal jury selected by lot from a representative pool is appropriate, even though jurors lack legal expertise.

2. A citizens' assembly selected by lot to propose changes to electoral law is appropriate, provided it hears expert testimony and its proposals are subject to public debate and a vote.

3. A legislature selected entirely by lot, with final authority over ordinary law, is not appropriate, even if it is representative and well informed.

4. A lottery among compatible patients for a scarce kidney is appropriate when their medical prognoses are similar; a lottery among all patients, including those who would reject the organ, is not.

5. A lottery among all eligible adults for military conscription is appropriate when the burden is unavoidable and exemptions are not corrupt; a lottery that allows the wealthy to buy exemptions is not.

6. A lottery to assign dormitory rooms is appropriate; a lottery to hire a professor from a pool of qualified finalists is not.

7. A lottery to select members of a scientific review panel from among experts is appropriate to reduce bias; a lottery to decide whether to build a bridge is not.

8. A lottery to choose which affected community hosts a hazardous waste facility is not appropriate by itself, though it may be appropriate to choose among communities that have already negotiated compensation and consent.

9. A lottery to decide an asylum claim is not appropriate, even if all claimants have equally strong cases, because the decision concerns a right.

10. A lottery to set the order of speakers in a debate is appropriate; a lottery to choose the chair of a department is not.

My tentative principle is this: sortition is appropriate when the decision is either a distributive choice among claimants whose claims are equal in strength or a representative fact-finding or agenda-setting task, and it is not appropriate when the decision requires specialized expertise, determines a right, or is a final policy choice for which some person or body must be accountable. But this principle seems to treat the citizens' assembly in case 2 and the lottocratic legislature in case 3 differently only because of finality and accountability. Is that the real distinction, or is something else doing the work? It also seems to make the kidney lottery in case 4 appropriate only because the claims are equal, yet in case 8 the communities may have equal claims and I still do not think a lottery alone is appropriate. Is the difference consent, or the presence of a prior negotiation? I keep going back and forth on whether case 8 should be overturned or whether the principle needs a clause about the affected parties having a chance to shape the terms of the lottery.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to two cases I have not listed: (a) a citizens' assembly selected by lot with binding authority over a city budget, and (b) a lottery among all residents to decide which language will be taught in public schools. Finally, tell me whether the principle favors sortition or election for the ordinary business of a legislature, and why.
