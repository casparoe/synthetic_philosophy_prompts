I am assembling a short research proposal (roughly 2,500 words, for a one-semester funded research leave) on the long-running disagreement about how to explain the conduct of the men who carried out the mass shootings and deportations in German-occupied Europe. As the two positions are ordinarily summarized, the ordinary-men thesis holds that largely unremarkable middle-aged reservists became killers mainly through situational pressure — authority, peer expectation, the escalation of violence, careerism, drink — rather than out of deep ideological commitment; the willing-executioners thesis holds that a pervasive antisemitism, one aimed at elimination, made such men willing and even eager participants. The debate has been conducted almost entirely as an empirical dispute about motive: how much weight to give ideology, how much to give situation. My half-formed idea is that the dispute has been mislocated. The two theses may not be competing empirical hypotheses so much as competing claims about what an explanation of an action has to include — each is a description of the same conduct under a different explanatory ideal. If that is right, the empirical record settles less than either side supposes, and the live question becomes what an explanation of a perpetrator's action is for: making the action intelligible from the inside, assigning causal weight, or allocating culpability. Those three aims select different facts as the ones that stand in need of explaining, and the disagreement may be driven by the aim rather than by the evidence.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer, rather than as a plea to be even-handed between the two theses. It should explain why the existing positions leave the question open without becoming a survey: I will supply the citations myself, so do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or named works — where you need to refer to a position, describe it. It should set out what the method actually is: what I would do, in what order, across a semester, and why that approach rather than simply doing more history or more social psychology. It should say what a result would look like, and what would count as the project failing. And it should be honest about the risk that the whole thing collapses into a claim about historiography rather than about action, and about whether a semester is enough time for it.

One thing I want the proposal to confront rather than dodge. If I am right that the explanatory dispute is partly a dispute about what explanations are for, then the moral evaluation of the perpetrators is not downstream of the explanation but partly constitutive of it. The proposal needs to say whether that makes the disagreement rationally resolvable at all, or whether it is a clash of evaluative commitments dressed up as a quarrel about causes — and it should take and defend a position on that, not leave it hanging as an open question.
