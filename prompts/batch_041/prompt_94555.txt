I am a retired animal-behaviour researcher who has spent the past several years reading philosophy in earnest, and the department here has asked me to conduct the on-stage interview that will follow next month's public lecture. My guest has built a career defending the Kantian position on animals: that we have no duties to animals, only duties to ourselves, and to humanity in our own person, with regard to them; that cruelty to animals is wrong because it corrodes the natural disposition to sympathy on which morality depends; and that animals, lacking self-consciousness and reason, cannot be ends in themselves. I have read enough of her work to know that she can restate this position in her sleep, and I have no wish to hand her the opportunity. What I want are questions that test the two premises I am actually competent to evaluate — the claim about what animals are like, and the claim about what cruelty does to those who commit it — together with the internal architecture of the view.

Please draft ten questions, ordered as I should ask them, each followed by a brief note on what I am trying to draw out and a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. They must be speakable aloud and free of technical vocabulary I would fumble. Do not supply references, editions, or page numbers; neither of us needs them and I cannot check them. No opening questions about how she came to philosophy. At least one question must bear on each of the following:

- The load-bearing empirical premise. I want to put to her the case of an agent whose cruelty leaves no mark on his sympathetic dispositions — because of a drug, or because his habits have been engineered, or because he is simply made that way — and ask whether his cruelty is then not wrong at all; the case where the coarsening falls on other people rather than on the agent himself; and the case where one person's cruelty does no harm because everyone else's sympathy remains intact, so that the wrongness seems to depend on a ledger of aggregate psychological damage.
- The structure of a duty owed to oneself that nevertheless has an external object. In Kant's system the duties of virtue have no corresponding right in any other party; ask whether duties with regard to animals are a genuine category or a relabelling of a duty to oneself, and hand her the case of an animal made to suffer where no rational agent ever learns of it.
- The criterion that excludes animals from direct consideration. Ask whether the line is empirical — my own field keeps moving it, with tool use, caching, mirror self-recognition, consolation behaviour — or transcendental, and whether under Kant's own idealism the inner life of an animal is any less inaccessible to us than that of another person. Ask what finding would make her revise the view.
- The suspicion that the position collapses into virtue ethics, or into a merely pedagogical claim about habituation, and what the categorical imperative is doing if the wrongness of cruelty consists in its effect on the agent's character.
- Whether these duties fall on individuals or on humanity as a species, and what becomes of them if our sympathetic dispositions can be protected by other means — a moral prosthetic, as it were. Do the duties regarding animals then lapse?

Finish by telling me which question she is most likely to deflect, how she will deflect it, and which of my questions I am most likely to ask badly.

Begin your reply with the questions themselves; no preamble and no restatement of this request.
