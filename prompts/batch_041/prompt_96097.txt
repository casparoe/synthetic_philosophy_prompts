I teach a seminar on the ethics of refugees and asylum, and I want to build a session around Casablanca as a test case for the question of who counts as a refugee and who has a duty to admit them. Please give me a sustained reading of the film through that philosophical lens, written as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a long letter to a colleague.

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions. On the film's side, take the plot and the major scenes as given: the crowded café with its mix of Vichy officials, German officers, refugees, and profiteers; the murdered couriers and the letters of transit that everyone is chasing; Rick's professed neutrality and his refusal to stick his neck out for anyone; the Bulgarian couple who need money for exit visas and the roulette table where Rick lets them win; Victor Laszlo's status as a celebrated resistance leader and Ilsa's position as his wife; the corrupt Captain Renault and his habit of sweeping up the same suspects whenever he needs a show of order; the petty criminal Ugarte, who is killed and whose death the film treats as disposable; and the ending, in which the letters go to Laszlo and Ilsa while Rick and Renault walk off into the fog. On the philosophy's side, bring in the real distinctions: the 1951 Refugee Convention's persecution-based definition and the broader definitions that include people fleeing war, disaster, and general violence; the difference between a duty of non-refoulement and a duty of admission; the question whether asylum is a right or a discretionary gift; the distinction between a refugee, an asylum-seeker, and a displaced person who is simply trying to emigrate; and the tension between state sovereignty over borders and the moral claims of the displaced.

Then press on the film with those distinctions. Which characters in Casablanca count as refugees under a persecution-based definition, and which count only under a broader one? Is Laszlo the paradigm case, and does the film's focus on him and Ilsa push the ordinary displaced — the people in the café, the Bulgarian couple, Ugarte — into the background? What does the letters-of-transit plot suggest about the moral status of a scarce good that is supposed to be a right? Rick's intervention at the roulette table is an act of charity that bypasses the legal queue; is that a model of asylum as grace, or a confession that the institutional order cannot deliver justice? Renault's corruption and the visa black market make the border a commodity; what does the film think about the difference between a corrupt border and a just one? And who, in the film, has a duty to take refugees in — Portugal, America, the French colonial administration, or the city of Casablanca itself, and does the film even notice the Moroccan population on whose territory all of this happens?

I also want the honest critic's turn. Where does the film resist this reading? It is a wartime romance and a piece of Allied propaganda, and its ethics may be subordinated to Rick's redemption arc and to the sentimental priority of a few named individuals over the anonymous many. Does the film actually endorse a hierarchy of the deserving — the political hero over the economic migrant, the European over the colonial subject, the sympathetic over the criminal — or does it merely fail to see the hierarchy it assumes? Say plainly where the film is morally thin and where it is more perceptive than the philosophical literature.

Finally, end by saying what Casablanca adds to the debate that argument alone does not: whether it dramatizes something about moral luck, arbitrary mercy, and the emotional economy of asylum that the refugee-ethics literature tends to leave out.
