I am the taxonomic editor for a national biodiversity database, and our checklists are what regulators use to decide which populations receive legal protection, which recovery funds get spent where, and which land-use permits are granted. A few dozen times a year a researcher, a regional agency, or one of my own curators proposes that some population be recognized as a distinct species, and the proposals arrive with different kinds of evidence in different shapes. I have no fieldwork budget, my curators can read a phylogeny and a morphometric table but have no training in the philosophy of biology, and each proposal has to be resolved inside a fixed review window before the next regulatory cycle closes. I sign off on every one, so I am the person who eats the consequences of a procedure that quietly made a value judgment and dressed it up as a measurement.

I want you to write me a decision procedure the curators can actually run on a proposal. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to me because the remaining question is one of policy or value rather than evidence. It has to be explicit, at each step, about which species concept that step is presupposing, and about what to do when the lines of evidence point in different directions, for instance when the genetic data support splitting a lineage but the morphology does not, or the reverse. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and where a threshold I might reach for, such as a genetic distance cutoff, is doing work that no one has justified.

To make this concrete, here is the draft rule I have been using, which I want you to absorb, repair, or throw out, and to say which: recognize a population as a species if it forms a monophyletic group in the available genetic data and is diagnosably distinct in at least one character system. I already suspect this mishandles asexual lineages, hybrid zones, and lineages that diverged so recently that they have not yet sorted, but I want the procedure itself to tell me how it fails rather than me guessing.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, a cryptic frog lineage separated by deep genetic divergence and a distinct mating call but with no morphological difference, whose ranges overlap slightly at one contact zone where they do not interbreed. Second, a ring of salamander populations around a valley, where each adjacent pair interbreeds but the two terminal forms meet and do not. Third, an asexual parthenogenetic snail lineage that is genetically and ecologically distinct but offers no interbreeding test at all. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do.

One more constraint: our records must interoperate with a global checklist that openly uses a different and more inclusive concept than ours, so the procedure should say how to record and defend a decision that conflicts with that global list without pretending the conflict is a mere bookkeeping difference.

Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write me an email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. If you need to enumerate steps, do it in full sentences.
