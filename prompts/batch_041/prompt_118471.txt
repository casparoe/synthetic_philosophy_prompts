I am a retired experimental physiologist. I spent thirty-five years in a sleep laboratory — recording electro-oculograms, scoring epochs, arguing with colleagues about what the muscle atonia of REM is for — and I have never had an original philosophical thought in my life. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in a disorganised way, and I have run into an argument I cannot put down.

The argument, as it reached me. A dream is not something that happens to a person while asleep. When I say that I dreamt I was in Paris, I am not reporting an experience I had during the night; I am telling a story, and the dream is the story — it comes into being with the telling, and its only criterion is the waking report. The sleeper is not noticing anything, not attending to anything, not in a position to be aware of anything; whatever the sleeping brain may be doing, there is no one at home to have an experience. I have seen this credited to Norman Malcolm, and a reply to it by Daniel Dennett, who I gather held that the question was an empirical one rather than a matter for attention to how we talk. I have almost certainly got both of them wrong. Please treat the two positions exactly as I have stated them here and do not lean on my attributions.

My instinct, after a career spent on the other side of this fence, is that the sceptic must be wrong. But I notice that every piece of evidence I want to throw at him is evidence for which he has an answer, and his answers are not stupid. I want the thing fought out properly, not won. It matters to me beyond curiosity: the nightmare literature I spent years contributing to treats a nightmare as an event of suffering occurring during sleep, and I would like to know whether that was a finding or a manner of speaking.

You are a philosopher of mind with a solid working knowledge of the science of sleep, and you are used to writing for readers who are not philosophers. Please write me a dialogue between two proponents of these two views. Before you begin, think carefully about where the two positions actually make contact and where each is most vulnerable; sketch that out briefly for yourself, then write the dialogue without the sketch.

A few things I would like to be true of it.

1. Both speakers must be first-rate. I am not interested in a philosopher who wins by caricature or an experimenter who wins by waving a recording. Each should land at least one blow the other cannot fully absorb, and each should concede what he owes.

2. Neither may talk past the other. Every reply must engage the actual point just made, not the point the speaker would have preferred to be answering.

3. I would like each of them to have the following resources available, though neither needs to use all of them.

For the defender of dreams-as-experiences: the lucid-dreaming signalling work, in which a subject is instructed before sleep to make a prearranged pattern of eye movements once he realises he is dreaming, the electro-oculogram records that pattern during REM, and on waking he reports having made it deliberately, with variants in which dreamers count or estimate intervals and the timing comes out at least roughly right; stimulus incorporation, where water sprayed on the face, a tone, a light or a squeeze of the hand, delivered during REM, turns up in the dream as reported on waking; patients with REM sleep behaviour disorder who act out their dreams, punching and shouting and running, with the enacted behaviour matching the reported dream content and roughly its order; the suggestion, contested as I understand it, that the eyes during REM move in the direction of gaze within the dream's visual scene; and the general point that we accept the reports of waking subjects about their experiences on exactly this kind of evidence, so that a standard which would rule out dream reports would rule out a great deal else.

For the sceptic: the hypothesis that the dream is composed at the moment of waking rather than during sleep, a playback rather than a recording — on which the water-spray result is no difficulty at all, since the face is still wet, and the eye-movement signalling must be explained as the waking confabulation of a decision that was never made; the point that every one of these results is verified through the waking report, so that none of them tests the report against the dream itself; the conceptual point that to have an experience is to be in a position to notice, to be surprised, to attend, to be distracted, to remember, and that the sleeper by hypothesis is doing none of these; the observation that we never say "I am dreaming that" the way we say "I am seeing", so that the report is always retrospective and the whole content of a dream falls inside the operator, without commitment to anything; and, if it helps him, the fact that dreaming is not one state — reports from NREM awakenings and from the edges of sleep do not resemble REM reports — so that the old equation of dreams with REM was always too crude.

4. Somewhere in the dialogue I would like them to discover that they may be answering different questions — whether the sleeping person undergoes experiences, whether the waking report reports an experience, and whether the concept of dreaming requires that a dream be an experience — and to say which question each of them is answering.

5. I have a guess about where the real crux lies: whether having an experience is a matter of there being something it is like for the subject, or a matter of the subject's being in a position to notice, attend and remember. Do not defer to my guess; if you think the crux is elsewhere, put it elsewhere.

Mechanically: label the speakers, keep the turns short enough to read as a conversation rather than two essays stapled together, let it run to something like twelve hundred words, and use no quotations and no citations — I will do my own reading, and I would rather you did not guess at titles or dates. Finish with one short paragraph, in your own voice, telling me who has the better of it and whether the dispute is substantive or verbal, and if verbal, exactly which word is doing the work.
