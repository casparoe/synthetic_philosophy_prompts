I am writing a historical novel whose central question is whether a society's past is better read as a recurring pattern than as a story, and before I commit the argument to the page I want to hear it made properly and defended against pressure. Please play a committed cliodynamicist — an unnamed researcher, not a caricature — who holds three things: that large-scale history can be modeled quantitatively; that societies run through quasi-periodic cycles of integration and disintegration, driven by competition among aspirants for a limited stock of elite positions and by the immiseration of the ordinary population; and that these cycles are real, testable dynamical phenomena rather than convenient metaphors. Give the character the resources of a working modeler — the distinction between a cycle and a mere trend, the difficulty of operationalizing "elite," the question of what would falsify the theory — and let it grant that the data are patchy and the measurements noisy, but never the central thesis. It should be fluent and a little impatient with humanist objections it has heard before, and it should stay in character until I say stop. If it needs a fact about my invented setting, it should ask for that fact in character rather than stepping out of the role, and it should argue rather than point me toward a literature; I do not want a reading list, only the contest.

My opening move is this. The island society in my book leaves four hundred years of continuous records, and they trace the pattern your theory predicts almost too neatly: long stretches of order and rising population, then a widening gap between a swelling class of office-seekers and a fixed number of posts, then fiscal strain, then a generation of violence, then a reset. The trouble is that the island has no landed elite, no private wealth to speak of, and no hereditary office — its positions are assigned by lot every seven years and confer no income. So either the engine of your cycle is something other than competition for wealth and status, in which case tell me what it is, or what I have written is not a cycle at all but a coincidence dressed as one. Which is it, and what within your theory decides the question?

Answer me in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, and keep to that form in your later replies as well.
