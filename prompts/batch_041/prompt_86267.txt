I run my school's philosophy club, and next term we are hosting a Q&A with a visiting professor who holds that morality contains no principles: on her view, any feature that counts in favour of an action here may count against it, or count for nothing at all, in a slightly different situation, so that judging well is a matter of reading the particular case rather than applying a rule. I have been asked to prepare the questions, and I would like them to press the view rather than invite her to restate it; assume she is a serious philosopher, give the strongest version of each challenge, and leave out anything about how she became interested in the subject. Please draft eight to ten questions, each followed by a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Include at least one question on how her position differs from the ordinary thought that rules have exceptions, built around a concrete case in which a single feature — candour, say — appears to change its moral direction between two situations that differ only slightly; one on how anyone learns to judge well if there are no principles to be learned; one on what her view offers a person who genuinely cannot tell what to do; and one on what would count as a serious objection to her view rather than a restatement of the generalist alternative.
