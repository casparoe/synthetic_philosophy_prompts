I teach decision theory, and I have been trying to isolate the principle behind my own verdicts about tiny probabilities of enormous value, without success. I accept a 1-in-10^100 chance of saving 10^100 lives over a certain cure for one person. I reject paying one dollar for a 1-in-10^100 chance of the same. I accept a research program with a 1-in-10^6 chance of preventing a catastrophe ten times worse than the status quo when the only alternative is no progress at all. I reject a research program with the same chance and the same catastrophe when the certain alternative is a modest but genuine improvement. I accept a 1-in-10^100 chance of a finite but astronomically large good when the alternative is zero, yet reject it when the alternative is a small certain good. I reject any gamble that carries a positive probability of infinite value, even when its expected value is undefined or infinite. Is there a coherent principle here—perhaps a probability threshold, a lexical restriction, or a constraint on which probabilities may be taken up? I suspect the principle is that expected value is authoritative only above some probability threshold or only when the stakes are not of a different kind, but I can see that this either becomes ad hoc or collapses back into ordinary expected value. Please state the best principle or small set of candidates, say which case motivates each clause, what it implies for the case I keep going back and forth on—a 1-in-10^100 chance of 10^100 lives saved versus a certain cure for a rare disease that saves 10,000—and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
