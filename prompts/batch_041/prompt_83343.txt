I referee for a mid-tier analysis journal and every couple of months I hit the same thing: a proof says 'by Zorn's lemma', or 'for each n choose an x_n in A_n', and I have no routine way to tell whether that's a convenience I can wave through or a load-bearing use of choice, or whether the theorem could be proved without it at all. I'd like a referee's checklist I can actually run on a submission, in order: what to ask about each choice step, what counts as a satisfactory answer, when I can write 'the choice here is eliminable, ask for the revision', when the honest verdict is 'this needs some choice principle and the author should say which and why', and where I have to stop and hand it back, because the question (does this theorem imply a choice principle? is it independant of ZF?) is a research problem and not a referee's call. Assume I know what ZF, countable choice, and Zorn's lemma are and not a lot more, that I have an hour and not a week, and that my report has to be usable by an editor who knows less than I do. Then run it on three submissions: a linear algebra proof that every vector space has a basis via Zorn; a combinatorics paper that repeatedly says 'pick one element from each A_n' where the A_n are sets of naturals; and a functional analysis proof that calls on Hahn-Banach. I have a feeling at least one of these comes out badly for the author, and I want to know what I'm actually supposed to do then, since I can't reject everything that touches choice. And tell me straight what the checklist can't do.
