Hey, I'm TAing a course on early modern philosophy and I have alot of grading to do. I need you to grade a student's short essay on du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness. I definately need a committed grade here — pick a letter grade (with +/- if you want) and defend it. Don't just list pros and cons and tell me it's a mixed bag; I need you to make a call and then give me the specific comments that justify it. Which parts are right, which are wrong or shaky, and how much do the mistakes matter for the overall grade?

Here's the question I set:

Du Châtelet says a happy life requires us to free ourselves from prejudices. She calls them false and useless, especially religious ones, and says they make us vicious. But she also says we should be susceptible to illusions and preserve them, because they add charm to life. She even distinguishes prejudices from the social proprieties (bienséances), which she thinks have truth and virtue. These seem to pull in opposite directions: if prejudices are false beliefs we should reject, why are illusions, which also seem to be false, something we should cultivate? Explain how she can hold both, and say what work each does in her account of happiness.

Here's the student's answer:

Du Châtelet thinks happiness is something we can actively work at. In the Discourse she gives a list of things you need: good health, no prejudices, virtue, passions (or tastes, once you get older), and illusions. The last two are the interesting ones.

At first it looks like she's contradicting herself. She says prejudices are false and useless, especially religious ones, and that they make us vicious and miserable. But then she says we should be susceptible to illusions, and that illusions make life charming. If both are false beliefs, why keep one and ditch the other?

Her answer is that the difference is in where they come from and what they do. Prejudices are things society and religion drill into you; you didn't choose them, and they make you judgmental and unhappy. Illusions are things you choose to entertain, like getting swept up in a play or believing the best about someone you love. She thinks you should keep the ones that make you happy and drop the ones that don't.

She also says that as you get older you should replace your passions with tastes. Passions are too volatile and they can wreck you, so the wise thing is to trade them in for gentler pleasures like study or conversation. Study is her own favourite example — she says it's the most reliable source of happiness because it doesn't depend on other people.

So the two ideas aren't in conflict. Prejudices are false beliefs that hurt you, and illusions are false beliefs that help you. The test for whether to keep a belief is whether it contributes to your happiness.
