I run a monthly reading group for people who came to philosophy sideways — engineers, statisticians, longtime rationalist-blog readers — sharp and mathematically literate but with almost no exposure to twentieth-century epistemology, and next month I have ninety minutes on Moore's proof of an external world. I don't want you to explain the proof to me; I want you to plan how I should explain it. Assume nobody in the room has heard of Moore and that the only prior they bring is a general sympathy for the principle that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, which will predispose them to assume the skeptic wins by default. Lay out the order in which the components have to arrive for the hand-raising to look like a serious philosophical move rather than a stunt: what must already be on the table before the gesture even makes sense (the form of the skeptical argument, the closure assumption it leans on, and the fact that Moore grants that assumption rather than attacking it), where the general argumentative shape — that one person's modus ponens is another's modus tollens — should be introduced relative to the specific case, and which piece has to come last so that the group's instinctive reaction gets reframed rather than merely contradicted.

Then the pedagogical specifics. Tell me what should carry the whole session and why: whether the hands can bear that weight for this audience, or whether I should build the arc around a different case and let the hands arrive late as the historical punchline — and if the latter, what the substitute case must have and must not have (it should be something they already treat as more certain than any philosophical premise that could impugn it, and it must not be a case where they would happily say they merely believe it). Give me the misconceptions in the order this audience will actually form them. My guess at the list: that Moore is being cheeky, that he begs the question against the skeptic, that he is denying closure, that he confuses having a perceptual experience as of hands with knowing that one has hands, and that the whole thing is a verbal trick about the word know. For each, say exactly where in the sequence to head it off, and which ones I should let them voice first and dismantle themselves. I also want to fold in two threads I take to be load-bearing and that popular treatments usually flatten: first, that the real interest of the proof is not whether it works but what it reveals about which commitments we treat as bedrock and why; second, that asserting knowledge may be doing something different from reporting an inner state, so that relocating I know out of its ordinary use might be the actual error. Say which of those two belongs in the session, which I should cut for time and say out loud that I am cutting, and give me one closing question that would show whether they have grasped the shape of the disagreement rather than just memorized who said what.

Finally, be candid about the fragility of your own plan. Tell me where the sequencing is most likely to fail with this particular audience, what I would observe in the room that would signal it has failed, and which argument or consideration would make you revise the order or the choice of carrying example. I would rather have a plan with a visible weak point than a smooth one that pretends the audience arrives neutral.
