I'm a science journalist who has spent about a decade on the neuroscience and animal-cognition beat, and I'm applying for a nine-month fellowship that funds one substantial independent project. I need a two-page proposal. The project is conceptual rather than reportorial, and it's the proposal I want you to write, not the eventual essay.

Here is my half-formed idea, and the reason I think it is worth nine months. When people argue that brain size is morally relevant, they usually do it through a chain: some anatomical measure stands in for cognitive capacity, and cognitive capacity stands in for moral status. I want to pull that chain apart into three separate roles an anatomical measure might play, and ask whether any of them is defensible. First, an inclusion criterion: having a brain of at least some size or complexity is what puts a being in the class of things whose interests count at all. Second, a weighting criterion: bigger or more complex brains ground stronger or more extensive moral claims, which implies that moral status comes in degrees. Third, a purely epistemic role: the measure is not itself morally relevant but is a cheap proxy for whatever is, and may be used only when we can state the conditions under which it misfires. I suspect the third role is the only one anyone should want, and that it is harder to defend than it looks, because the candidate measures — absolute mass, size relative to body mass, neuron count, neuron density, connectivity — rank species differently, so any proxy's verdict depends on a choice that is almost never argued for. The fact that birds and cephalopods do impressive cognitive work with small, differently organized brains is what makes me think the choice is doing more work than the arguments admit.

What I need is the proposal itself: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the familiar treatments leave it open, in particular whether a hidden dispute about degrees of moral status is carrying the argument; say what I would actually do across nine months, and why conceptual work rather than more comparative neuroscience is the right instrument; say what a finished result looks like — I want something usable, ideally a short decision procedure for when a coarse anatomical proxy may stand in for a moral-status claim, plus an essay a general reader can follow; and be honest about what would count as failure. Two failure modes I already worry about: the question dissolves, because every measure turns out to be a proxy and the real disagreement is about something else entirely; or the project collapses into an empirical question about which animals are sentient, which is not mine to settle. I would also like the proposal to confront the within-species version of the weighting view — that a person with a larger brain has stronger claims than one with a smaller brain — and to say whether that is genuinely a reductio or whether the view can be held consistently while denying the implication.

Please leave out citations, a bibliography, and references to particular studies or authors; I'll gather those from the researchers I interview. Argue from the arguments themselves.
