I'm trying to get a handle on Adam Smith's account of sympathy. In the first chapter of The Theory of Moral Sentiments, he gives these examples where we see someone about to be hit and we shrink our own leg, or we see a beggar's sores and feel an itch in the same spot. Then he says that we can't directly experience what others feel, and that "it is by the imagination only that we can form any conception of what are his sensations." I'm confused about how these fit together. Is the bodily reaction supposed to be the sympathy itself, or just evidence of the imagination at work? And if sympathy is just me imagining what I'd feel in his situation, how does it ever give me insight into what he actually feels, rather than just my own projected feelings? I'm also wondering how this relates to the impartial spectator later on—is the spectator just this same imaginative mechanism, or something else? I feel like I'm missing something about what Smith means by "sympathy" here.
