My team at work got into an argument about whether a big enough computer could run an economy, and it turned into a rerun of the old socialist calculation debate, which I only half knew about. We never resolved it, and I've since realized I don't even know what kind of question we were arguing about. Can you diagnose it?

Here's my rough understanding, correct me where it's wrong. Mises said rational allocation is impossible under socialism because there's no market for capital goods, so no prices for them, so no way to compare alternative uses of steel or machine time against each other -- you're just guessing. Lange comes back with a model: keep markets for consumer goods and for labour, socialise the capital, and have a Central Planning Board set the prices of capital goods and productive resources and adjust them by trial and error until supply and demand clear, with managers instructed to expand output until marginal cost equals price. On that telling the impossibility claim looks refuted -- it's an algorithm, and algorithms get faster and cheaper. Then Hayek shifts things, or clarifies the real point depending on who you ask: the knowledge that needs to be used isn't sitting somewhere waiting to be computed over, it's dispersed, particular, often tacit, and the price system is how it gets discovered and communicated in the first place. And then there's a third strand about incentives and innovation -- even a perfect calculator doesn't tell you who bears the cost of being wrong or who has any reason to try something new.

So: is this a disagreement that's actually stalled, or one that keeps getting restated because the terms keep sliding? My hypothesis, and I'd like you to treat it as just a hypothesis, is that it's three or four different questions wearing one name.

1. A definitional fight about what counts as socialism. Lange's model keeps consumer markets, wages, and consumer choice, so an Austrian can say that isn't the thing under dispute, that's a market economy with a state-owned capital stock, and then the argument is partly verbal. Is that right, or does the definitional move do real work?

2. A genuine conceptual question about what economic knowledge is. Does the information a planner would need exist independently of the market process that generates it, or is it constituted by that process? If the latter, then no algorithm can compute over data that doesn't exist yet, and the whole debate isn't about compute at all -- which would make my framing wrong.

3. An engineering question about convergence. How fast does trial-and-error pricing actually converge in an economy with millions of distinct goods, strong complementarities, non-convex production, and constant structural change? Lange's setup assumes something like a Walrasian clearing process; real markets don't clear either, they just muddle through with inventory and queues. Is the residual disagreement here an empirical question that could in principle be settled by modelling, or is it not well posed?

4. A political-economy question about incentives, power, and who sets the objective function. This one is contaminated by the fact that the main historical test cases were poor authoritarian countries with bad data, so the evidence is nearly useless.

Here's my angle, which may just be me pattern-matching my job onto the 1930s. I used to work on a scheduling system. We had plenty of compute -- the thing that killed us was that nobody could agree on the objective function, and the teams whose numbers got worse under any proposed objective had both the power and the incentive to sandbag the inputs. So the hard part was never the solver. If that maps onto the calculation debate, then the real crux isn't solvability, it's aggregation and incentive compatibility, which is a different question from either Mises's or Hayek's. But I might be overreading my own scar tissue.

What I want from you: a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, which parts of it are the same question being answered differently and which are different questions, and where exactly does the sliding happen when someone says the debate is settled and someone else says it's still live? Then, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a formal result, a working demonstration on a real economy, a distinction that separates the strands, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed? And be honest about which parts of this are settled among people who actually know the area versus genuinely contested; I have the impression that everyone agrees the pure impossibility claim is dead and the live residue is about discovery and incentives, but I've also seen people say the whole thing is still open, and I don't want a confident consensus story that isn't one. Separate those out for me rather than smoothing them over.

Also, I'm a software engineer, I've never taken an econ or philosophy course, so if there's a distinction I'm fuzzing (impossibility vs. inefficiency, knowledge vs. information, planning vs. markets) please just say so directly rather than assuming I have it.
