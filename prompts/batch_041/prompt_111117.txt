You are a philosopher of history and a moral philosopher with a specialist's knowledge of Atlantic abolition and of the European duel. I want you to stress-test a position I hold, and to be willing to tell me that it is unfalsifiable rather than strain to defend it.

The position: the moral arguments against slavery and against dueling were available and widely known long before either practice ended — in some cases for centuries — and by themselves changed nothing. What ended them was a shift in the economy of honor among the elites who practiced them: once the practice came to read as a mark of dishonor rather than of honor, and once the material interests of the relevant elites had drifted far enough, the practice collapsed, and the old arguments were retroactively credited with the change. On this view moral argument is largely epiphenomenal — it ratifies a transition that honor and interest had already made. (The position is close to the economic reading of abolition often labeled the Williams thesis, and to recent honor-centered accounts of moral revolution.)

Start by brainstorming the candidate defeaters before you rank them. Then tell me what would count against the position — not only the famous cases in which moral argument appears to have done the work, but the harder ones: a practice that ended while honor and interest both still favored it; a documented case in which the arguments demonstrably preceded and caused the honor shift rather than trailing it; a ban imposed from above that stuck with no prior shift in either. Distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward, and say which a defender of the position would concede as counting against it rather than dismiss. Consider especially whether honor and interest are being drawn so broadly that any outcome can be accommodated after the fact, and whether, if so, that is a fatal defect for a view of this kind or merely the price of a historical explanation.

I would personally be most moved by a well-documented case of a practice that ended with neither its honor nor its interest standing changed.

End with a final line of exactly this form: \answer{...}, where the braces contain one of exposed, insulated, or mixed, judging how much the position actually risks.
