Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications, within the ethics of torture and the ticking-time-bomb problem, and then demonstrate that it satisfies each of them.

The scenario must be realistic — something an ordinary intelligence or police official could actually face — and must satisfy all of the following at once. First, the decision-maker has ordinary epistemic limits: no omniscience, no guaranteed success, no perfect lie detector. Second, the harm threatened, if it occurs, is catastrophic, but its occurrence is probable rather than certain. Third, the detainee is genuinely culpable and, on the best available evidence, probably holds the information that could avert the harm. Fourth, no other means of obtaining that information is available within the time available. Fifth — and this is the point of the exercise — the case must be one in which a committed act-consequentialist and a committed deontological absolutist reach the same verdict, while a committed rule-consequentialist reaches the opposite verdict, with the rule-consequentialist permitting the torture and the other two forbidding it. That pattern reverses the more familiar alignment, in which it is the consequentialist who permits and the deontologist who forbids. The verdicts must be generated by the theories as they are ordinarily stated; do not build them into the scenario by stipulation.

Give the scenario in a single paragraph. Then, in order:

1. For each of the three theories, set out the reasoning that produces its verdict, and show that the scenario meets the first four specifications without having smuggled the verdicts in.
2. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work — the feature such that changing it while holding everything else fixed would rearrange the verdicts — and explain why that feature, rather than the others, is load-bearing.
3. State what happens to each verdict when that feature is removed.
4. Explain how a defender of whichever theory fares worst would attack the case: what they would deny, and whether they can deny it without abandoning something central to their position.
5. State what the case is for. It is meant to determine whether the familiar permissibility intuition in ticking-bomb cases survives once realistic epistemic limits are imposed — that is, whether the intuition tracks a genuine moral difference or merely an idealized stipulation — and you should say whether the case you have built actually settles that question.

Take definite positions throughout: commit to the verdict pattern, commit to your identification of the load-bearing feature, and commit to a judgment about whether the case succeeds, giving your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. Write in American English.
