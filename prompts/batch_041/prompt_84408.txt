I'm running a section for a course on the concept of mental disorder, and next week we're pairing One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest with Szasz. Trouble is my students already have the canned reading (asylum bad, Ratched evil, psychiatry = social control), and I want the session to actually test Szasz against the book instead of nodding at it.

So: read the novel through The Myth of Mental Illness. Szasz's line, roughly, is that mental illness is a metaphor doing medical work it can't do, that illness means a deviation from a bodily norm, and that what gets called mental illness is really people with problems in living -- moral, legal and political trouble that gets relabelled as sickness so it can be managed coercively. He wasnt against therapy, he was against the coercion, and he thought a diagnosis should never get you off the hook for what you do.

Things I want taken seriously:

- McMurphy fakes insanity to dodge hard labour at the work farm, and the book ends with him lobotomised. Is that Szasz's story in miniature? And does the novel ever tell us whats actually wrong with him -- is that silence a Szaszian feature or a hole in the book?
- Nearly every non-chronic man on the ward has a story attached that has nothing to do with a lesion: Billy and his mother, Harding and his wife, the Chief and his father. Does that dramatise problems in living, or does the book cheat by only casting patients whose troubles are obviously social? I'm fairly sure there's one patient whose problem is plainly neurological -- what does he do to the argument?
- The Chief's vision of the Combine, the fog, the shock treatments used as punishment, Ratched's group meetings: does the novel bear out the claim that these symptoms are meaningful communications about a real world, or does it literalise the sickness metaphor in a way Szasz would hate?
- The ending. The Chief, sane and responsible, smothers McMurphy and goes out the window. Does that vindicate the insistence that these men are agents rather than patients? And where does the book let Szasz down -- if the ward makes people worse, that's iatrogenesis, which quietly concedes there was an illness there to worsen. I think students blur those two claims constantly; keep them apart.
- What does Szasz's own view forbid the book from saying? He opposed involuntary commitment. Does the novel leave room for any psychiatry he'd accept -- the men who are voluntary admissions and still won't leave, say?

Don't hand me a verdict that the book proves Szasz. I want a reading that's faithful in both directions: where the novel dramatises what his argument can only assert, and where it quietly assumes a rival picture of the mind. And tell me what to say when the discussion collapses into psychiatry is just a scam. No need to quote the book at me, I know it.
