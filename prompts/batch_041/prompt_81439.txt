I am in the lower sixth and I run a fortnightly philosophy club at my school; our next session is on whether ordinary social conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — are moral obligations at all, and whether a visitor has to follow the local ones. I would like to hand out a self-scoring quiz that sorts the club into positions rather than letting everyone settle on 'it depends'. Please write it.

Write nine questions. Each must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a member can answer it without knowing any labels or having read any philosophy. So no question may simply ask 'do you think tipping is a moral obligation?', and none may ask which named theory they prefer.

Three structural differences should do the work of the quiz, and I would like the questions built around them rather than around a list of famous positions:

- Who bears the cost of the deviation, and when. If I push into a queue, the person behind me loses a slot immediately and can object on the spot. If I do not tip, the cost lands on a third party, later and invisibly, and nobody present need notice. If I jaywalk across an empty road, there may be no cost at all. I want questions that force members to commit on whether this difference matters morally, or only to whether the act is likely to be caught.
- Whether participation is opt-in or opt-out. Tipping is something one can decline to do; queueing is something one is already in the moment one stands in the line; jaywalking is a legal prohibition that almost everyone breaches. The quiz should separate those who think obligation tracks what you have tacitly taken on from those who think it tracks what is expected of anyone present.
- Whether a visitor's ignorance bears on whether the act is wrong or only on whether the visitor is to blame. I suspect these come apart, and I want a question that separates them, plus one that separates 'the local convention gives locals no extra authority over visitors' from 'it gives them extra authority precisely because it is theirs'.

The scoring key must map answer patterns onto positions and must include at least these leaves: a coordination view (the convention binds only as a shared solution, hence only those already inside the practice); an assurance view (you are bound because by joining you gave others something to rely on); a harm-based sceptic (conventions as such generate no obligation, and only the downstream costs do); a localist view (visitors are bound by the local convention as a condition of taking part in that community, whether or not anyone is harmed); a duty-of-inquiry view (the visitor's obligation is to find out and accommodate, not to conform); a complicity view (some conventions should be refused, because complying sustains an injustice — for instance where the tip is quietly part of how the employer pays); and a standing-asymmetry view (deviating may be permissible while resentment at it is also permissible, because those are two separate questions). If two of these cannot be separated by any question a member could actually answer, say so plainly rather than inventing a question that only pretends to.

Please also note the combinations no position occupies — for instance, whether anyone would hold both that visitors must conform to local conventions and that locals have no standing to resent it — and say briefly whether each gap exists because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended. Two economics teachers sit in on the club and will ask whether any of this changes where the convention has real distributive effects, so let the harm-based questions be honest about that.

Set the scoring key out as a table, and end with a final line in exactly this form, with the leaf labels separated by slashes: \answer{<number of leaves>; <label>/<label>/...}
