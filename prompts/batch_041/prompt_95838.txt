For the past fourteen months I have been writing a long work of nonfiction on a technical subject. I mention this only because it explains why two puzzles that I had previously filed away as philosophy-department curiosities have recently become personally live for me.

Two weeks ago I finished the manuscript, and the last thing I wrote was the preface, in which I thank various people and then state, as is conventional, that errors undoubtedly remain. I signed off on that sentence without thinking, and then, on the train home, it caught up with me: I have spent a year believing every substantive claim in the book, and I also believe that the book contains at least one false claim. Around the same time I took a ticket in a workplace sweepstakes — a thousand tickets, one winner, drawn fairly — and found myself believing, of each ticket, that it would not win, and then noticing that this amounted to believing that no ticket would win, which is the one thing I knew to be false.

I tried to write my way out of this. The result is below, condensed. I am not asking to be told that these puzzles are famous; I know that they are, and I am not interested in a survey. I want comments on the essay itself.

Two paradoxes, one mistake

1. The sweepstakes. One thousand tickets, one winner, drawn fairly. For each ticket I hold the same evidence: exactly one of the thousand wins. That single body of evidence makes the loss of any given ticket 99.9% likely. If a likelihood that high licenses belief, I am entitled to believe, of every ticket, that it loses. And if belief is closed under conjunction — if believing P and believing Q commits me to believing that P and Q — then I am entitled to believe that all thousand tickets lose. But that is precisely what my evidence rules out.

2. The manuscript. The book contains, let us say, three hundred substantive claims. I believe each of them. They were established by different arguments and different sources; the evidence for claim 40 is not the evidence for claim 41. Closure then hands me the conjunction of all three hundred. And I also believe, on the strength of a well-earned estimate of my own error rate, that the conjunction is false. Here I cannot say that I have spent one piece of evidence many times over: each claim was paid for separately.

3. The asymmetry, and what it buys. These are not the same paradox. In the sweepstakes the trouble comes from a single body of evidence being spent a thousand times. The evidence says that one of these loses. It does not license a thousand separate beliefs; it licenses exactly one belief about the set, and the thousand individual beliefs are that one belief cut into slices. Conjunction is not the culprit; the culprit is treating a slice as a loaf. In the manuscript, by contrast, each belief has its own backing, and conjunction does exactly what it should do, which is to collect them. The paradox is genuine, and what it shows is that rational commitment is not closed under conjunction even if belief is.

4. A repair. What we commit to is not a threshold on credence but a standing policy: we assert, rely on, and act on a proposition. A commitment is rational when the cost of being wrong about it is worth the benefit of relying on it. Each of my three hundred claims earns its own commitment — separately asserted, separately revisable, each useful. The conjunction earns none: it adds no practical power over the three hundred taken individually, and the cost of being wrong about it is certain, since I am confident it is false. In the sweepstakes, by contrast, no commitment is earned at all, because committing to the loss of a given ticket buys me nothing I did not already have from the setup, and the thousand commitments taken together are just the denial of the setup.

Please comment on this. Specifically:

(a) Is the asymmetry in section 3 real, or have I merely restated the two cases in different words and attached a diagnosis to each? If it is real, say what the diagnosis actually turns on; if it is not, say which of the two cases I have misdescribed.

(b) Is the commitment-based repair in section 4 a solution or a relabeling? In particular, test it against the fact that I do sometimes rely on all three hundred claims at once — for instance when I invite a reader to trust the book as a whole, or when someone uses the book to justify a single practical decision that depends on many of its claims jointly. If the repair survives that, explain how; if it does not, say whether anything in section 3 survives with it.

(c) Have I conflated belief with acceptance anywhere, and if so, does the argument survive the distinction or does the distinction dissolve it?

(d) Is anything in sections 1 and 2 conceptually or probabilistically wrong — not merely debatable, but wrong?

Then tell me the single change you would make to the essay, and what that change costs me elsewhere in the argument. I would much rather be told that the argument fails at step 4 than be told that it is a pleasing illustration of a familiar puzzle.
