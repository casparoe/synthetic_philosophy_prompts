I'm trying to pin down my own view of cognitive penetration, and I'd rather have it extracted from my verdicts on cases than from a definition. Knowing that bananas are yellow makes a grayish banana look yellower to me, and I count that as penetration. A radiologist who has been told to look for a fracture sees a smudge on the film as a fracture; I count that as penetration too. But when someone points out the dog in the dappled photograph and I can then see it, I don't count that as penetration — it looks to me like perceptual learning rather than cognition reaching into perception. After a long hike a hill looks steeper, and I don't count that either, because nothing I believe seems to be doing the work. Knowing that the two lines in a Müller-Lyer display are equal doesn't stop them looking unequal, and I read that as perception resisting cognition rather than being penetrated by it. And when lip movements change which syllable I hear, I don't count it, since the influence comes from another sense rather than from anything cognitive. My tentative principle is that an effect is penetration only when the content of the thought matches the feature that looks different and the effect goes away when I drop the thought; but I notice this makes the dog case penetration after all, and it does nothing to separate the dog from the radiologist. So give me the principle, or small set of principles, that actually captures my verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; tell me which verdict it forces me to give up; and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — whether knowing a wine is expensive changes how it tastes, as opposed to how much I say I like it. Take a definite position on that last case and defend it rather than laying out the options.
