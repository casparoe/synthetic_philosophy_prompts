I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of history seminar, and the notes below are for a short essay due next week; I'd like your comments on them.

The essay's claim is that the dispute between the covering-law model and its narrativist critics is not a dispute about what an explanation must contain, but about how the explanandum is to be individuated. A covering-law explanation takes its target to be an event picked out in a description that is neutral with respect to the participants' own understanding of what they were doing; a narrative explanation takes its target to be a change of situation already described in the participants' terms. If that is right, the standard objections mostly miss their mark: the covering-law model is not refuted by the fact that narratives explain without laws, and narratives are not vindicated by the fact that historians seldom cite laws, because the two accounts are answering different questions about different objects.

My positive move is a distinction between two kinds of generality an explanation can have. Lawlike generality is the claim that a certain antecedent condition, however redescribed, yields the same outcome. What I call pattern generality is the weaker claim that the same story-form recurs — overreach, entrapment, the collapse of a coordinating expectation — with no claim that the antecedents were jointly sufficient. I argue that historical explanations typically deliver pattern generality, and that this is neither a covering law in disguise nor mere redescription.

The essay has four parts: (1) the distinction between the two explananda; (2) why the familiar objections slide past each other once that distinction is in place; (3) the argument that pattern generality is a genuine explanatory achievement, since it licenses some counterfactuals (had the coordinating expectation held, the cascade would not have run) while withholding others (no claim that this cascade was inevitable); (4) an unresolved worry — a critic might say pattern generality is just a covering law with the quantifier quietly dropped, so that I have described vacuity rather than a third option.

Please comment on whether the central distinction is a real one or only a verbal restatement of the familiar point that explanation is interest-relative; whether pattern generality can be made precise enough to do the work I assign it, or whether it collapses into the covering-law model on one side or into redescription on the other; whether the counterfactual test in part (3) is a fair test or begs the question; which single step is weakest and what would strengthen it; and anything in the notes that is simply mistaken about what the covering-law model claims. Concrete suggestions for revision are welcome — I would rather be told now that the distinction is unworkable than discover it in the seminar.
