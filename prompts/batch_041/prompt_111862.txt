I chair the constitutional review commission of Saint Aurelia, a Commonwealth realm of some 300,000 people whose head of state is the British monarch, represented there by a Governor-General. Two years ago the Governor-General refused a sitting prime minister's request for a dissolution after he lost a confidence vote, dismissed him, appointed the Leader of the Opposition, and then dissolved the House when that government too failed to win confidence. The reserve powers are written nowhere: they rest on Westminster conventions and on a single 1950 letter that a royal private secretary sent to a newspaper, which is the only text anyone can point to on when a dissolution may be refused. Parliament now has before it the Reserve Powers (Codification) Bill, under which the Governor-General would act only on the advice of a five-member Constitutional Council, publish reasons within twenty-four hours of exercising any reserve power, and dismiss a prime minister only on a two-thirds vote of the House. A rival amendment would adopt the Swedish arrangement, giving the Speaker the nomination of the prime minister; a third would make the head of state purely symbolic on the Japanese model, with every act in matters of state requiring the Cabinet's advice and approval. One complication: the Crown, not the state, is the named treaty partner in a land settlement with the indigenous Marawa, so any reform that empties the office of legal personality must reckon with that. Second reading is in six weeks and my commission's recommendation goes into the record. Draft the memo I will circulate to the other commissioners: a clear recommendation (one of these options, or a specified hybrid) with a timetable, and the strongest honest case for leaving the conventions uncodified. I care less about drafting language than about the underlying question — whether an unelected office can legitimately hold discretionary power in a democracy at all, or whether codification only relocates that problem — so tell me what would change my mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
