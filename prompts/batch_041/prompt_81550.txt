I cover science and ideas, and next month I have forty minutes, a whiteboard, and a room of generalist reporters and editors who will spend the coming year writing about behavioral economics. I want to use the time on the Allais paradox. Don't explain it to me — plan how I should explain it. Give me the order in which the pieces have to arrive so that the independence axiom ends up looking like something a thoughtful person might genuinely reject rather than a technicality (I assume lotteries and expected value come before utility, and utility before the axiom, but tell me where that chain actually breaks and what has to be in place before the axiom can even be stated). Then the one pair of choices that carries the whole session, and what it must have and lack: I want it to isolate a real independence violation from the certainty effect, since in the standard version the tempting option is a sure million and my colleagues will smell a framing trick from across the room. If that separation can't be made cleanly for a general audience, say so and tell me how to handle the objection live instead.

Then the misconceptions, and where in the sequence each one gets headed off. I expect them to hear it as proof that people are simply irrational; as a refutation of expected utility rather than a constraint on one of its axioms; as a species of risk aversion; as the same failure as intransitivity; and as an artifact of how the branches are drawn and split. Finish with the single question I should put at the end to see whether they can now catch an independence violation in a fresh pair I invent on the spot, and with what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the representation theorems, the parameterized alternatives to expected utility, and whatever else I will be tempted to name-drop in the last five minutes.
