Next Thursday I have to give the 20-minute presentation in my Archaeological Theory seminar — it's the format where you present your own working paper and then get grilled for forty minutes — and I'm trying to figure out in advance what I'm actually going to get hit with, because last time the questions came from angles I completely didn't see coming and I just flailed. My paper argues two things. First, that archaeologists should stop trying to say what past people believed and instead publish the whole set of belief-hypotheses that the material record plus our background knowledge is consistent with — report an equivalence class rather than a point estimate — because in most cases the record geniunely doesn't discriminate between them. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, that only beliefs which were costly to enact, or costly to signal, leave traces that survive into the record at all. So the equivalence classes we can even formulate are skewed towards expensive beliefs (burial treatment, monumental construction, anything involving killing animals) and the cheap everyday beliefs that probably made up most of what people actually thought are basically invisible, which means the whole exercise has a systematic bias baked into it before we start. I've been calling that the costly-trace principle, though I'm not fully sure it isn't a restatement of something obvious. My running example is a set of pits from the site I did field school at, with deliberately placed deposits — articulated animal parts, a couple of smashed querns — that three different people in my department have given three incompatible readings of.

The audience is the real problem. The seminar is basically two camps plus two outliers. Camp one is the processual/quantitative people: they do lithic use-wear, they think every interpretive claim has to be tied back to a warrant that's been independently validated against known cases, and they're broadly fine with probabilistic language but think "belief" is a mushy target that can't carry an inference. Camp two is the interpretive/post-processual people, who work on ritual and landscape and think material culture is something you read rather than something you measure, and who I strongly suspect are going to hear my whole framing as positivism with extra steps and a spreadsheet. Then there's my supervisor's collaborator, a philosopher of science who works on underdetermination and inference in the historical sciences — she's probably the most dangerous person in the room — and someone from the university museum who writes the public labels and is going to ask me the very practical question of what she's supposed to print on a label if I'm telling her to publish equivalence classes instead of an interpretation.
