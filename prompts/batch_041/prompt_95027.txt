I have been asked to give the commencement address at the college I graduated from, and I have said yes, which I am already regretting. The setting is the standard one: eight minutes, a hot morning, six hundred graduates who have been awake since five, and two thousand family members in folding chairs, few of whom have read any philosophy and none of whom came for it. I teach the subject for a living; the president knows this and will say so when she introduces me, and the audience will brace itself accordingly.

What I want to give them is the one thing I actually believe about what they have spent four years acquiring, and it has turned out to be unexpectedly hard to say in a gymnasium. The whole ceremony rests on a distinction I am not certain I can defend: that the graduates now know things, where before they would merely have believed them. Knowing and merely believing something true are not distinguished by the truth of what is believed, so the question is what the graduate has that a lucky guesser does not.

The easy answers fail. Knowledge is not more likely to be true — a true belief is already true. It is not more useful — a lucky true belief guides action just as well. That it is more admired is a fact about us, not about the belief. What is left is that knowledge is a true belief with a respectable pedigree, produced by a process that tends to produce truths: good eyes, good method, good teachers. This is where I get stuck. Reliability is like a good espresso machine. We value the machine for the coffee; once the coffee is in the cup, the machine's excellence adds nothing to the cup. If what we value in a belief is that it is true, then once the belief is true, the reliability of its production adds nothing to it. The diploma threatens to be a receipt for something whose value I cannot state.

The escape I keep trying is to say that knowledge is not a better belief but a better position. To know something is to be able to answer for it — to be someone who can be asked how they know and can say something. A lucky guess cannot be defended; you are right and have nothing to say, and that is a real loss even though the belief is true. But the answer has an obvious flaw: you can answer for a belief that is false, and you can answer for a belief that is true only because the world conspired to make your good reasons pay off by accident. What I have located, then, is a virtue of justification rather than of knowledge, which means my answer implies that knowledge is not the thing worth having. That may be the truth; I do not know that it is not.

What I want from you is the address itself, written as I would deliver it. Constraints:

- About 1,100 words, which is eight minutes at the pace I speak. Spoken register: short sentences, nothing that would need a slide or a diagram, nothing I would have to read twice.
- No names of philosophers, no -isms, no citations, and no announcement that a philosophical problem is being discussed. The audience should receive the difficulty without being told that it is a difficulty in philosophy. There is an old image I like — a true belief as a statue that will not stay in place unless it is tied down — and you may use it if it helps, but without saying where it comes from.
- Nothing I would consider false, and no resolution by rhetorical trick. If the honest ending is unresolved, let it be unresolved, but a shrug is not an ending. A commencement address owes the graduates a charge: something to do with the four years that survives the doubt.
- It must land for the families. They came to see their kid walk, not to hear a lecture, and the danger is that all of this arrives as a philosopher telling a tent full of people that their children's degrees might be worth nothing. It must not land that way, and it must not avoid landing that way by being dishonest.
- At least one concrete scene from ordinary life, not a thought experiment and not an example a philosopher would invent. Something the parents will recognize.

Write the address and nothing else.
