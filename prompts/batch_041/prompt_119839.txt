Okay so I've somehow ended up as the person who has to write the call for proposals for a small foundation that wants to sink about $15M over five years into "the deep determinants of development" — you know, the why-are-some-places-rich-and-others-poor question, and whether the answer is mostly institutions, mostly geography, or mostly culture. I have to decide what to actually ask people to work on and I keep flip-flopping. My fear is that the whole field is a treadmill: every year there's another cross-country regression or another clever historical natural experiment, and it always lands on "institutions matter, with caveats" or "geography matters, with caveats," and none of it accumlates into an answer. The instrument is always questionable (settler mortality, colonial borders, whatever), the outcome is always measured badly, and everybody's preferred cause turns out to be endogenous to everybody else's — geography shapes institutions, institutions shape culture, culture shapes which institutions stick. So I suspect the honest framing is less "which of the three wins" and more "what can we legitimately learn about causation from history at all," and if that's the real question then that's what I want to fund.

So here's what I want from you: not a lit review, an agenda. If you had my $15M and five years and maybe a dozen teams, which open questions in this area would you actually prioritize, and in what order? Tell me what's upstream of what — which questions, if settled, would make the others tractable — what's tractable with the tools that exist (quasi-experiments, border discontinuities, historical microdata, formal models, maybe some new data sources), what's neglected because it's unfashionable rather than because it's dead, and what's a dead end dressed up as a live one. And price the trade-offs for me: I'd rather fund one thing that could actually change how the field reasons than five things that produce publishable nulls.

A couple of half-formed ideas from my own notes that you should feel free to shoot down or build on. One colleague keeps pushing "switching cases" — forget decomposing the variance across all countries and just study the places that flipped within a generation or two, using within-pair contrasts like the two Koreas, the two Germanys, Botswana versus Zimbabwe, as the unit of analysis. I can't tell if that's a real research program or a parlor trick. Another idea: run a many-analysts-style exercise where a bunch of teams get handed the same historical dataset and we see how much their answers diverge, as a way of measuring how much of the disagreement is the world and how much is the analyst. And a third: stop asking whether institutions or geography or culture "matters" and instead ask about switching dynamics — under what conditions does a place escape a bad equilibrium, and is that even a causal question or a narrative one. Which of these, or something I'm not seeing, is worth the money — and what evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down your list?
