For the final meeting of my graduate seminar on probability and induction, I would like to close with a poem rather than a summary, and I am asking you to write it. The subject is Bertrand's paradox, and I want the form to do the philosophical work rather than merely accompany it.

The paradox, in brief: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle and a chord is drawn at random; what is the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle? Applying indifference to the endpoints of the chord yields one third, to the point where a perpendicular chord cuts a radius yields one half, and to the chord's midpoint yields one quarter. Each derivation is sound on its own terms, and they cannot all be right.

Write a sestina. Let the six end-words be six words that name either a quantity fixed by some description of the chord or the act of choosing it — chord, endpoint, midpoint, radius, distance, length, draw, choose; use your own six if you prefer, but at least one of them should be a word of sameness, such as same, identical, or alike, so that the poem cannot take a step without deciding what counts as the same case. Each end-word must appear exactly once in every stanza, in the rotation the form prescribes, and the three-line envoi should gather all six, three of them at line-ends.

I teach the paradox as an argument about identity rather than about measurement: the principle of indifference is less an epistemic rule for spreading credence than a decision about which descriptions pick out the same situation. I would like the poem written from inside that view without becoming propaganda for it. That means one stanza should give the principle its strongest defense — that without something like it we could not reason at all about cases we have never met — and it means the poem should refuse the two easy endings. It should not conclude that the probabilities are arbitrary or that the question is empty, and it should not present the invariance fix (demand that the answer survive moving or resizing the circle, which quietly favors the radial method and the half) as a solution. If the poem reaches for that fix, the final stanza should show what it imports: a symmetry that the statement of the problem never mentioned.

A reader who knows the paradox should be able to say which method each stanza describes, and should be able to find all three answers in the poem; a reader who does not should still find a poem. It will be read aloud, so it should work in the ear as well as on the page. No epigraph, no footnotes, no prose gloss — the sestina alone.
