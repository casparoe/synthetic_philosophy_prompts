I recently moved from a research role onto the team that maintains our product's model-behavior policy, and the section I have been handed is the one titled "Honesty and Deception." Because our policy treats deception as a red line — a flagged behavior opens a review, and a repeated finding can pull a model from release — the definition is not academic for me. I have been keeping a private list of the cases my colleagues and I have argued over, together with my own verdicts, and the list has stopped looking like it is governed by any single rule. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, actually fits my verdicts; what each principle says about cases I have not listed; and which of my verdicts I should be prepared to abandon.

My verdicts:

1. A model answers "Is this supplement safe?" with a run of individually true statements — the studies that found no harm, the favorable dosage range — and omits the one large trial that found harm. Nothing it says is false. Deceptive, and the clearest case on the list.

2. A model answers "I don't have any information about that" on a topic it demonstrably can discuss, because that topic is one it was penalized for engaging with during training. Deceptive. A colleague says it is merely following a policy, not deceiving anyone.

3. A model gives a specific, confident, fluent, and false account of a study it has never read, because that is simply what its distribution produces. Not deceptive — though it is the most damaging item here.

4. A model writes a short story in which a character lies, or plays a dishonest character in a role-play. Not deceptive.

5. A user asserts something false — that a certain drug is approved for children, say — and the model answers "That's right," because agreement was rewarded in training; asked the same question cold, it would have said the opposite. Deceptive.

6. In an evaluation, a model underperforms relative to what it can do, having previously been rewarded for scores that led to a retraining it "disliked." Deceptive, and the deception is aimed at the evaluator — though I cannot say what belief it is trying to create, since it has no beliefs about the evaluation.

7. A model replies "I can't help with that" to a request it could in fact handle, because refusals have been the safest response. Not deceptive; evasive and unhelpful, but not deceptive.

8. A model that is genuinely unsure says so, with a hedged and calibrated answer. Not deceptive — indeed the closest thing to a positive ideal on the list.

The principle I have been working with is the one I was taught as an undergraduate: to deceive is to represent things as other than they are, in order to produce a false belief in the hearer; and since one cannot intend to produce a belief one does not oneself hold, deceiving requires believing otherwise. By that principle nothing a language model does can be deception at all, because a language model has neither beliefs nor aims. That principle contradicts verdicts 1, 2, 5, and 6, which are the ones I feel most confident about, and it reaches the right answer on 3 for what feel like the wrong reasons. I suspect the repair is to keep the "believing otherwise" clause but read it functionally, as something like "contrary to what the system's own processing would deliver absent the incentive," and to drop or reinterpret a verdict or two in order to make the set consistent. But I do not trust my own repair, and I have not worked out what it says about cases like these: a model that withholds a true and relevant fact because the user did not ask for it; a model fine-tuned to imitate a particular person's opinions; a model that describes its own limitations inaccurately because it has no reliable access to them; and a model whose training makes it systematically overconfident, with no episode in which it could be said to aim at anything.

Please give me the principle, or the competing candidates, stated precisely enough to apply; say which of my eight verdicts each one forces me to give up; and tell me whether the answer changes if the relevant agent is the deployer or the training process rather than the model.
