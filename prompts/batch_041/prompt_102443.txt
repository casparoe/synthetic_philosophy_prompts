I keep a running list of my gut verdicts about when it is acceptable to impose a loss on one person for the sake of others, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle or only to a mess. A hundred people each gain a mild benefit if one person bears a slightly worse but still mild cost: permissible. One person is blinded so that a hundred others recover their sight: impermissible, and not merely because I might have the numbers wrong. Two outcomes with the same total suffering, one concentrated on a single person and one spread thinly across many, feel different to me even though the arithmetic matches, and the concentrated one feels worse. A person is made to endure hardship now for benefits that will fall to that same person later: that seems to me an ordinary piece of prudence, not a case of using anyone, and it puzzles me that separateness is supposed to block the second kind of weighing but not this one. When a person's interest is counted twice because they appear under two descriptions, as a worker and again as a consumer, the aggregate moves but my verdict does not, which makes me think the double-count is a defect rather than a reason. And one case I cannot settle: a person is conscripted into a small but non-trivial risk so that a large number of others each avoid a comparable risk, which sometimes strikes me as a fair sharing of a common burden and sometimes as plain exploitation.

My tentative principle is that aggregation is legitimate whenever every individual loss is small enough that its bearer could not reasonably complain, and illegitimate whenever someone is made to carry a loss that ruins the shape of their own life, however large the total benefit. Please give me the most precise principle that saves as many of these verdicts as possible, name the verdicts it forces me to abandon and say why, and tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed: one in which the sacrifice is voluntarily undertaken, but under a pressure that makes the voluntariness itself suspect.
