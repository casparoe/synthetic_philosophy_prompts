I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I guest-teach one session a year in a friend's upper-level seminar on minds and machines, and this term I've somehow been handed the final. The unit I cover is the one about reasons without reasoners — the idea usually credited to Dennett, that you can truly say a process is doing X in order to Y even though nothing in the system represents Y, so the rationale sort of floats free of any mind that has it. The problem is the students have read the usual material and can chant the phrase at me, which is exactly what I don't want on the exam. So I need you to draft one essay question that can't be answered by reciting the label, and then the model answer and a marking scheme to go with it. You're a philosopher of mind and of biology who's thought hard about teleology, about as-if explanation versus real explanation, and about the difference between redescribing a behaviour and explaining it — please think carefully before you write, and maybe sketch two or three rival readings of the free-floating rationale idea first, then build the question so it has to land somewhere between them rather than list them.

Here's the wrinkle I want to hang it on. In my last novel there's a machine — call it the Echo — trained on the complete surviving works of a composer who died mid-symphony, and it can extend the fragment in her style. Everyone in the book says it refuses to resolve the final cadence; the engineers say the refusal is an artifact, that the training corpus simply never contained a resolution of that particular cadence, so the model's next-token distribution just never puts much weight there. So we've got a behaviour that's reliably describable as avoiding the resolution in order to keep the piece open, no representation of that goal anywhere in the machine, and a training history that explains why the behaviour is there at all. I want the exam question to use that case, plus a contrast case where the rationale is represented — some system that carries an explicit model of its own goal — and to force students to say which reading of the free-floating rationale idea the Echo supports, what work the word free-floating is actually doing, and what would have to be true for it to be doing real explanatory work rather than just being a fancy redescription of the artifact story. If there's a genuine fork where two readings are both live, I'd rather the question expose it than paper over it.

Deliverable, in this order: (1) the essay question itself, worded so a student who only knows the vocabulary gets a C at best; (2) a model answer of around 700 words; (3) a marking scheme built from six must-hit moves, 25 marks total, each with the marks it's worth and a one-line note on how students typically blow that particular move — and make the scheme say explicitly what separates an A from a B, in terms of moves, not vibes. The students sit this closed-book with no internet, so nothing in the question or the model answer should lean on citations, reading lists, dates, or names of specific papers — argue from the cases, not from who said what where. Keep the model answer's prose clean; I'll be reading a lot of these.

Two things about how the answer should come at me. First, be upfront about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which are genuinely contested — I don't want a contested reading of the free-floating rationale dressed up as if it's the consensus, and I don't want the model answer pretending there's agreement where the whole point is that there isn't. Second, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning: no preamble, no restating my question back at me, no throat-clearing about what a rich and debated topic this is. Just the verdict, then the argument.
