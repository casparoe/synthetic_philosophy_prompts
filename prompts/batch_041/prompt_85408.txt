Three years ago I joined a food cooperative — a few hundred member households, a volunteer board, an annual general meeting, and a set of bylaws that have been amended by hand so many times that no two copies in circulation agree. This autumn the board asked me to chair the bylaws committee, and I have spent the last two months presiding over a dispute I do not know how to resolve.

The bylaws contain the ordinary clause: they may be amended at a general meeting by a two-thirds vote of the members present, notice having been given. At the November meeting two motions passed. The first was carried by exactly the required two-thirds and changed that very clause, so that amendments now require the unanimous consent of all members rather than two-thirds of those present. The second, carried later the same evening, added a sentence providing that the amendment article itself shall never be amended again. The chair ruled both valid, on the ground that each was passed under the rule in force at the moment it was passed. A minority holds the second motion void, on the ground that a body cannot use the power to amend in order to destroy the power to amend. A second faction holds the first void as well, on the ground that a rule which has been superseded cannot supply the authority by which it is superseded. The treasurer, who has sat on three previous boards and is unimpressed by all of us, maintains that the question is settled neither by the bylaws nor by any argument, but by whoever turns up at the next meeting and is willing to act as though the amendments hold.

I have come to see that we are rehearsing a problem much older and much more general than our co-op: the constitutional amendment clause that is amended by the procedure it prescribes; the provisions some constitutions place permanently beyond amendment; the doctrine that a constitution has a basic structure that even a unanimous parliament may not alter; the claim that no generation may bind its successors; and even the parlor game Nomic, invented by the philosopher Peter Suber, in which the players amend the rules — including the rule about how rules are amended — and must somehow keep playing. What I have not found is a usable map of the positions: a way of asking a member four or five questions and being able to say, with confidence, you are standing here, and here is what your answer commits you to.

That is what I would like from you: a decision tree — or, if it comes out cleaner that way, a taxonomy whose branches are labelled with the question that splits them — mapping the positions one can hold on whether and how a rule can validly amend itself.

Some constraints on the instrument:

- Every branching node should be a question a member could answer about a concrete case, not a question about a named theory. Use cases like ours: an amendment to the amendment clause carried under the old threshold; an amendment that purports to entrench the clause forever; an amendment that repeals the amendment procedure altogether and leaves none in its place; a rule that forbids its own repeal; a rule that lowers its own threshold; a rule that is repealed and then, years later, re-enacted by the very body that repealed it. Phrase each node so that answering it does not reveal which leaf it leads to.
- Each leaf should carry the name of the position if it has one in constitutional theory or the philosophy of law, and unnamed if nobody defends it. Where a position has a name, add a clause, not a paragraph, on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.
- Where a combination of answers leaves a node unoccupied, say so, and say briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to hold it.
- Keep it to roughly eight leaves. I want to use it at the February meeting, so it should be answerable by someone who has read nothing but our bylaws.
- No bibliography and no citations. I have no way to check them and would not know what to do with them.
