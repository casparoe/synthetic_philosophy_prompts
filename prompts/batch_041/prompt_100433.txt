I have just moved from a decade in the US technology sector to lead research at a small digital-rights foundation in Berlin, and the change in what the state is presumed entitled to know has become personally inescapable: the same routine forms now arrive in a jurisdiction where data minimization is the default rather than the exception, and I find myself filing them differently depending on which government is asking. My board has given me a three-year window to set a conceptual research agenda, and I want it to turn on questions rather than on commentary about whatever regulation is currently in the news. Which open questions about how far citizens should be able to hide from the state most deserve sustained attention, and in what order? I care far less about the list than about the reasoning: which questions are upstream of which — does the answer to what privacy is ultimately for actually constrain how one should think about data trusts, consent regimes, or scoring systems? — which are tractable for a small team with no empirical apparatus, and what evidence or argument over the next few years would move a question up or down the ranking. Please also name the questions you would deliberately deprioritize because settling them would not change much downstream, and treat the German, American, and Chinese arrangements as embodying three genuinely different premises about the citizen-state relation rather than as points on a single axis running from openness to control.
