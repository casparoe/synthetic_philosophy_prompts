So this is kind of a heavy one, sorry. My younger brother died last year, he was 34, and it was by his own hand, though there was some ambiguity and the coroner ended up leaving it open. He'd been writing a novel for years, mostly at night, and never showed it to a single person. After he died I found the whole thing on his laptop, about 90k words, and it's actually good. Really good. But near the end there's a chapter where a character — a guy who's clearly not him, different job, different childhood, he has a wife in the book and my brother never married — writes this long letter and then walks into the sea. And the letter reads a lot like things he said to me in the last few months. My mom has read it now and she's decided it's his suicide note. She wants to print the letter alone, on its own, in the memorial service program, and she's talking to a small press about bringing the whole book out as what she keeps calling his last word to us. I'm his executor, so technically it's my call, but I'm the one who has to look her in the eye.

My problem is I knew him. He was a fiction guy, read nothing but novels, and he used to get genuinely annoyed when people assumed everything a writer writes is secretly about the writer. If he were sitting here he would say flat out that the character isn't him and the letter isn't a note. But I also don't want to be the asshole who tells a grieving mother that her son's book doesn't mean what she thinks it means, and honestly I'm not even sure she's wrong. The letter only lands the way it lands because of the two hundred pages around it, is my feeling, but maybe that's just me being precious about the book as an object.

A friend of mine who's in a philosophy program said something that's been rattling around in my head. She said there are two different things we can mean by what a writer intended — the plan he had going in, the thing he set out to write, and then what he was actually doing in the writing itself, which can be something he only figures out along the way and maybe never admits to himself. That second one, she said, isn't any less real just because he'd deny it out loud. I don't totally understand it but it feels relevant.

So whose reading should win here, mine or my moms? Or is that the wrong question? And how do I decide what to do with the book without either steamrolling her or pretending I know what he meant better than he did? I'd rather have an actual argument than a platitude about how everyone gets their own reading of a thing.

One thing, and this matters to me right now: write it as plain prose, like you're writing me an email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I can't read that stuff when I'm upset.
