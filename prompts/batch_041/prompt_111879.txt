Hey, I need help with something for my reading group. We meet on Sundays and somebody always brings this terrible gas-station coffee, and honestly the coffee is not the worst part — the worst part is that for three weeks now we've been stuck re-fighting the same fight about reasons and reasoners, and I'm the one who has to write it up.

The fight is this. One of us, Marcus, thinks there can be reasons out there in the world with nobody around to have them. His favorite move is that natural selection can be said to discover solutions to problems, and that a wing is for flying in a sense that really is about what counts in favor of flying — even though no bird and no gene ever considers anything. He calls these free-floating rationales: the rationale is there, it's just not attached to any mind. The other one, Priya, thinks that's poetry. A reason, she says, is a consideration that counts in favor of something for someone; if there's no one who could be moved by it or even represent it, then all you've described is a causal process with a normative word bolted on afterward. They've both read the same books and are now just talking past each other, and I suspect a big chunk of it is them meaning different things by reason.

What I want is the document they should have produced instead of another shouting match: a joint statement for an adversarial collaboration between the two camps. Please give me (1) a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — about what selection and function-talk actually deliver, what neither is denying, and what would count as a reason under either view; (2) the real points of disagreement, each stated in wording its own side would accept as fair rather than a caricature; (3) the cruxes — I'd guess one is whether a merely statistical or tracking relationship (a thermometer tracks temperature, a bee tracks nectar) can amount to a reason, and another is whether calling something a rationale does any explanatory work or is just a redescription; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree would seperate them if it went one way or the other, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Make them test it against at least one hard case where the rationale seems to have gone missing or gone stale — a vestigial trait, or a behavior that used to pay off and no longer does. And if you decide part of their dispute is purely verbal, say which part and why. No citations needed, just the arguments — I'll be the one reading it aloud.
