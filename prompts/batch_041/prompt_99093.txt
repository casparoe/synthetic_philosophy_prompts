I have no philosophical training, but I have been stuck for weeks on a sentence I ran across and cannot stop turning over: "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining." What bothers me is that there seems to be nothing actually false or self-contradictory in it. It could be raining and I could fail to believe it; the world could be exactly as the sentence says. And yet I cannot picture sincerely saying it — the words seem to die in my mouth. I would like your honest credence about how deep the problem goes, with the reasoning broken out rather than handed to me as a verdict.

My question is this: what is your credence that the impossibility here is a matter of rationality rather than of manners — that someone who sincerely asserted that sentence would be committing a genuine irrationality, or violating a real requirement of reason, as opposed to merely producing something pointless, self-defeating, or socially awkward, with nothing genuinely irrational underneath? Please give me a number.

Then decompose it. I want a rough probability and one line of justification for each term you think the number rests on. My own guesses about the terms are these, but rearrange them if you see the question differently:

- That there is a genuine norm of assertion at all — a real requirement on what one may assert, distinct from the requirement to say only what is true and distinct from any norm governing what one may believe.
- That this particular sentence violates that norm, rather than being explained entirely by the fact that it expresses a contradictory pair of beliefs — that it is raining and that it is not raining — so that the trouble lies in the content believed and not in the act of saying it.
- That whatever goes wrong amounts to irrationality in the speaker, rather than to a defect in the sentence or in the occasion of its utterance.
- That the phenomenon is really about the expression of belief rather than about the content believed. The clue that pushes me this way is that the sentence stops being strange in the past tense — it was raining but I did not believe it — and in the second or third person — it is raining but you do not believe it. If the trouble were a contradiction in the content, I would expect the past-tense and third-person versions to be just as bad, and they are not. So the absurdity seems tied to the first-person present tense specifically.

Once you have given the terms, combine them into a single number and tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to — which one, if you moved it, would drag the whole thing with it — and what observation or argument would move that term most.

Finally, I keep trying to test my own grip on this with a variation I have not seen discussed anywhere: a sentence of the form "There is a seventy percent chance that it is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining." Does your account make that one absurd in the same way, or does it come apart from the original? What it says about that case will tell me a lot about whether I have understood you.
