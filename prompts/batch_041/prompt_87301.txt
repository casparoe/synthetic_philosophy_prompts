A ship in a storm jettisons cargo to save the vessel, or diverts to a port of refuge at great expense. Maritime law does not leave the unlucky cargo owner to bear that loss alone: the ship, the surviving cargo, and the freight all contribute pro rata according to the value each of them saved, so that what one interest gave up for the common safety is made good by all who benefited from it. The doctrine is ancient and is still written into modern bills of lading. Treat it as a lifeboat problem and ask what it presupposes about justice among parties thrown together in a common peril. Does it presuppose a pre-existing community of fate whose interests are pooled in emergencies, a duty of reciprocity owed to whoever bears the sacrifice, or merely an efficient risk-sharing arrangement that rational shippers would have contracted into in advance? The practice discriminates among these answers: contribution is owed only if the sacrifice succeeds. If the ship goes down anyway, the jettisoned cargo recovers nothing, though its sacrifice was just as real and just as voluntary. Tell me what that success condition reveals about the doctrine's actual grounding, whether the doctrine is coherent once we read it that way, which elements of the practice are strictly presupposed and which merely fit comfortably with it, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that rival theories would license it equally well. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
