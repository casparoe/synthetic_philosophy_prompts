You are a philosopher of physics specializing in the interpretation of quantum mechanics, with a talent for locating the exact step in an argument where a premise quietly does more work than it appears to. Think the argument through carefully before answering, and begin by restating each numbered step in your own words together with what would have to be true for it to fail; then give me your verdict.

I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I accept the no-collapse reading of quantum mechanics, on which the wavefunction always evolves unitarily and a measurement does not select an outcome but produces a real branch for each one, and I accept that the Born rule tells me how confident to be, so that a symmetric spin measurement is a fifty-fifty matter. A friend used those commitments to reach a conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) On that picture, a device that kills me if the spin comes out one way and does nothing if it comes out the other does not leave me dead; it leaves two successors of me, one per branch, both equally real.
(2) In the branch where the device fires there is no subject of experience, not an experience of dying but nobody left to have experiences.
(3) So no experience I go on to have is an experience of having died, and every experience I do have is had by the successor who lived. A person is a continuous thread of memory and psychology, and branching does not break the thread.
(4) So if I am anyone who has experiences after the experiment, I am someone who survived it, and the case guarantees that some branch contains a successor of mine having experiences.
(5) My credence that I survive should therefore be 1, not 1/2.
(6) Nothing in (1) to (5) turned on the amplitudes being equal, so even if the Born rule gave the surviving outcome a probability of 10^-30, my credence in surviving would still be 1.
(7) I should therefore be certain to survive anything with nonzero survival amplitude, a firing squad or a furnace included, and willing to stake my life on it for any stake at all. The Born rule then is not a rule about my credences.
(8) But that is absurd: the Born rule is exactly a rule about how confident I should be, and after a hundred rounds of the device I should be around 10^-30 confident of being alive, not certain.

I tried to break the argument by noting that the same reasoning applied to a harmless spin measurement that only decides whether I win a bet would make me certain that I won and certain that I lost, which convinced me the trouble is not with (1) but left me unable to say where it is. Which step should go, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep (1) and to keep the Born rule as a claim about credence, so tell me what I pay to keep both, and whether abandoning some other step is cheaper.
