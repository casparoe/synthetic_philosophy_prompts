My mate and I got into it last night about the Anthropocene and I want to think it through properly instead of just relitigating it the next time we drink.

Here's the shape of it, as I understand it. The stratigraphers spent about 15 years trying to make the Anthropocene official — an actual epoch in the geological time scale, with a golden spike you can walk up to and point at. They settled on Crawford Lake in Canada and a boundary around 1950, marked by the plutonium fallout from the bomb tests, on the theory that that's the first truly global, synchronous, permanent signal humans left in the record. Then in 2024 the vote went down, and hard — the subcommission, then the ICS and the IUGS, killed it, basically on the grounds that a few decades of sediment is too thin to call an epoch and that nobody's going to be mapping this as a rock unit. But the word didn't die, obviously; the IUGS even said as much, that everyone would keep using it. And meanwhile there's this other faction, some stratigraphers themselves, saying the whole exercise was misconcieved, that the Anthropocene isn't an epoch at all but an *event* — like the Great Oxidation Event — something that unfolds unevenly across the planet over time rather than switching on everywhere at one instant. Which is supposed to be exactly why no golden spike can work: the thing is diachronous, it spreads, it doesn't start at a point.

My friend's read is that this is basically a turf war about a word. Geologists won the vote so geologists get to say no, and the philosophers and historians and climate people who actually care about the concept just carry on using it, and nothing's really in dispute — they're fighting over who owns the term, not over anything true. My read is that there's something real underneath. And I've got a half-baked hypothesis I want you to stress-test: that the two camps aren't disagreeing about facts or even about authority, but are answering two different questions under one name. The stratigraphers are asking "is there a mappable, synchronous, formally definable unit here, meeting the rules a unit has to meet?" — and the answer might genuinely be no. Everyone else is asking "has human activity become the dominant force shaping the planet, such that we've crossed into a new chapter?" — and the answer might genuinely be yes. If that's right, the fight isn't a real disagreement at all, it's a category slip, and the vote settles the first question without touching the second.

But I'm not sure. So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on whether the Anthropocene is real. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, a dispute about which cases count as data (does the shallow sediment count as evidence of absence, or is the whole point that the record is still being written?), or a genuine disagreement about standards — i.e., do the two sides disagree about what a geological unit is *for*, where one side wants units for mapping and correlation and the other wants them for periodization and narrative? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — like, is the boundary actually synchronous or actually diachronous, and would resolving that settle it or not? And is the "event vs epoch" move a real conceptual distinction with teeth, or just a way of getting the word you want without meeting the rules?

Then tell me what would count as progress, given your diagnosis. A cleaner distinction? A ruling on who has the authority to define the word? Some new measurement showing the signal is or isn't globally synchronous? Or should I just accept that this one can't be resolved because it's half empirical and half about what we want the term to do? And if the disagreement really is two questions under one name, say whether that means both sides can be right at once — or whether calling it "two questions" is just a way of dodging the hard part, which is that the word is doing rhetorical work for alot of people and they don't want it carved up into a boring rock unit plus a vague vibe.
