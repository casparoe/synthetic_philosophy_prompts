I chair the records committee of a national truth commission. A coalition of community archives has petitioned us to append a register of silences to every official file: alongside the documents we release, the register would record what the file cannot show—events never documented, people never named, and the institutional reasons for those absences. Our legal counsel argues that the commission's archive must assert only what the documents support, and that such a register would put unverifiable claims into the historical record. The coalition replies that an archive reproducing only what power chose to write is itself a continuing silence. The immediate case is a file on a 1970s labor dispute whose official record contains only police logs, with workers' accounts surviving solely as oral testimony. Draft the committee's written opinion recommending a decision. It should set out a concrete policy for what the register may assert, how entries are marked and contested, what evidentiary weight they carry, and a timeline for implementation; and it should treat the strongest version of the losing side's argument fairly.
