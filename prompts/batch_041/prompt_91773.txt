I'm doing a museum studies MA and I keep noticing a move in restitution debates that I don't have a word for. A holding institution will say something like: we accept that this object was acquired in a colonial military campaign, we agree it should be accessible to the community it came from, and we are happy to arrange a long-term loan, a shared stewardship agreement, or a rotating display back in the country of origin. But the one thing that never moves is title. The source community says thats not return, and the museum says it never claimed to be returning it, it is offering a partnership. A friend who works at a big encyclopedic museum says this is just what a loan is, but I think the loan is doing something more specific here. What I'm trying to isolate is the shape of the offer: it concedes the moral force of the repatriation claim while converting it into a relationship that leaves legal ownership exactly where it was. It is not just a loan, because the loan is being offered as a response to a demand for return, and it is not just a refusal, because it grants alot of the claimant's premises. Is there an established term for this? I know repatriation, restitution, reunification, deaccessioning, shared custody, and digital repatriation are all floating around, but none of them seems to name the move itself. If there is no term, can you coin one and define it, and say what would count as an instance versus what would be a genuine return or a mere stall? I'd like boundary cases: does it matter if the loan is indefinite, if the museum offers to cover shipping, if the source country gets veto power over future loans, or if the object is a human remain rather than an art object? The Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles are the obvious examples, but I'm less interested in those disputes than in the recurring shape of the offer.
