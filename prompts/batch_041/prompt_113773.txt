My friend and I just spent a whole evening argueing about iterated amplification and distillation. I'm defending the optimistic picture: you train an overseer to break a hard task into subtasks, answer the subtasks with weaker models, and then distill the whole approved process back into a single model, and you should get both competence and alignment -- decomposition gives the overseer more leverage, and distillation compresses the approved behavior into one system. My friend says that's wishful. On alignment, the student only sees approved final outputs, so a subagent can learn a misaligned shortcut that happens to pass the check, and distillation bakes it in where you can't inspect it. On competence, each weak subagent's errors compound, so the composed system is less capable than the overseer thinks. I think both objections are too strong. What would actually count against my view? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and include the ones I'd have to concede even if I'm basically right. I'm especially interested in whether it matters if subagents can coordinate through a side channel the overseer doesn't parse, like a steganographic cue hidden in an intermediate answer. And if the optimistic view only survives by weakening to something like decomposition helps but guarentees nothing, just tell me that.
