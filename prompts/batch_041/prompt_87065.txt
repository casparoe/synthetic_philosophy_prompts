I'm a science and ideas reporter at a national magazine, four months into an investigation, and I've hit a disagreement with our standards editor that I'm fairly sure is a philosophical one wearing ordinary clothes. Please act as a philosopher who works on knowledge — on what it takes to know something on the basis of testimony, documents, and inference — and who also understands how a newsroom actually operates. Think carefully rather than reaching for the standard debate; begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that might be at stake, then narrow to the ones that matter, then give me a recommendation I can act on.

The story: a heavily cited study in a health field, and I believe the underlying dataset was manipulated. I have forty internal emails, a reanalysis by a statistician who found patterns that shouldn't be there, and a source inside the lab. Our standards editor says I may not write that we know the data were manipulated. Her formulation is that you may write that you know something only if you would not have believed it had it been false. By that test, she says, I can't claim knowledge: the emails could have been manufactured by someone with a grudge, and if they had been, I would have believed exactly what I believe now. Every time I close one gap she opens another — the reanalysis could be a hoax, my source could be an actor, the lab's own files could have been seeded — and the strongest thing I'm permitted to write is that documents we could not independently authenticate suggest the data were manipulated.

Two things bother me, and I can't tell whether they're real objections or just my reluctance to hedge.

The first is that her rule seems to prove far too much. Do I know the lab is on Maple Street? I've been there. But if I were dreaming right now I'd believe it just the same, so by her rule I don't know it. She says the dreaming case doesn't count — that the rule is meant to take away only the exotic kind of knowledge and leave me the ordinary kind — but she can't tell me what makes a possibility count, and I suspect the whole dispute lives in that gap. And whatever fills the gap, the result looks strange: the rule seems to let me know the ordinary thing while denying me the thing that must be true if the ordinary thing is true. She says that's just how knowledge works. I can't tell whether that's a subtle point or a mistake.

The second is that the three standards in this building don't match. Our standards editor wants the exotic scenarios excluded. A colleague says we only have to exclude the alternatives a sensible editor would actually worry about, not every alternative that is merely conceivable. Our lawyer says the test is whether our evidence would persuade a jury, and juries don't require us to exclude manufactured documents. Presumably not all three are using the word 'know' the same way, and I want to know which standard, if any, is the standard for knowing — as opposed to the standard for printing.

What I want from you:

1. A short list of the precise principles the standards editor's rule could be, from the weak readings to the strong ones, with a verdict on each: what it entails, whether it is defensible, and whether it actually supports her conclusion that I may not write that we know.
2. An answer to my first worry. If the position I gestured at is incoherent, show me exactly where it breaks; if it is coherent, tell me what it costs me.
3. An answer to my second worry: are those three genuinely different standards, or three ways of saying one thing badly?
4. Then the practical part. Tell me what to write, and how to answer her. I would rather have a recommendation with reasons than a survey of options.

Please write it as a memo I can take to her. Don't send me to a reading list or to works I'd have to look up — I'm offline, and I want the arguments themselves, in plain terms, with the verdicts stated. Where you are genuinely unsure, say so, and say what turns on it.
