In my civics class we studied the criminal justice system, and I have not been able to reconcile two things that the law does at the same time.

On the one hand, the law treats a person's past as permanently hers. A murder committed forty years ago can still be prosecuted; cold cases are reopened and solved by DNA; a man of eighty can be convicted for something he did at twenty, and the fact that he no longer remembers the act, no longer resembles the person who did it, and no longer endorses it is not by itself an excuse.

On the other hand, the same legal systems deliberately let the past expire. Under consumer-protection law in the United States, most adverse information on a credit report drops off after roughly seven years, and bankruptcy discharges most debts that were entered into knowingly. Juvenile criminal records are usually sealed once the offender reaches adulthood. Many jurisdictions provide expungement, by which old convictions are erased or hidden so that the person may, for most purposes, be treated as though the offence had never happened; in the United Kingdom, convictions become spent after a period and generally need not be disclosed; and European privacy law has gone further, recognizing a right to have old information about oneself removed from search results and treating the indefinite retention of old convictions as a possible violation of privacy. In many places, statutes of limitation bar prosecution for everything except the most serious offences.

I would like an essay answering the following. What view of personal identity over time do these practices presuppose? The two candidate views I have in mind are, roughly, that a person is wholly present at every moment of her life, so that the woman of eighty and the girl of twenty are literally the same thing, or that a life is more like an extended object spread out through time, of which the twenty-year-old and the eighty-year-old are distinct parts or stages (I believe these are called endurantism and perdurantism, though I may have the labels the wrong way round).

Please reconstruct the implicit theory rather than surveying what philosophers have written. Distinguish the parts of the practice that strictly require a view about identity from those that are merely explicable by other considerations, such as the decay of evidence, the aim of predicting future behaviour, rehabilitation, or administrative convenience. Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out, and whether its parts are consistent with one another — in particular, whether any single account of identity can underwrite both permanent liability for murder and a seven-year limit on credit history. Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, that is, where several incompatible theories would license it equally well. And finally, consider whether the law is implicitly committed to identity being a matter of degree, so that one is more nearly the same person as one's recent self than as one's distant self; if the implicit view were replaced by its rival, what would actually have to change in law or policy?
