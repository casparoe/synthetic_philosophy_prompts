Ok so I'm auditing an argument for a grad seminar and want to make sure I'm not missing a trick. A colleague in the anthropology dept keeps running this line: costly religious rituals — fasting, self-mortification, years of temple service, that sort of thing — are the peacock's tail. Only a genuinely committed believer can afford to burn all that time and pain, so the ritual is an unfakeable badge of commitment; that's why religions make you do hard pointless things, and why the groups that do it stick together better. He then concludes the whole business is adaptive, and that we should expect religions to get more demanding the more they want from you.

I think the analogy is doing more work then he admits. Can you give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the tail, the peahen, the cost, the "quality" being advertised — and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks? I care especially about (a) whether "costly" really gets you "honest" without the differential-cost condition, i.e. is the cost here even negatively correlated with the thing being signalled, or is it just a fee everyone pays, (b) whether this is a handicap in Zahavi's sense at all or the runaway / arbitrary-ornament story instead, since those seem to license very different conclusions about adaptiveness, and (c) what the analogy quietly imports from the peacock case that the ritual case doesn't have — a receiver with a real stake in the signaller's quality, a heritable quality worth reading, a cost borne by the signaller alone rather than spread over a congregation.

Then the money question: does the adaptiveness conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And if the peacock is the wrong template, whats a better one for the commitment-signalling idea, if there is one.
