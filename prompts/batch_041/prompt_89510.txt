I chair the governance committee of a mid-sized grantmaking foundation — roughly eight million dollars a year, a conventional expert-panel review process, a board that is risk-tolerant in principle and litigious about process in practice. At the next meeting the trustees will vote on whether to run the coming grant round as a pilot using a decision-market procedure of the kind associated with Robin Hanson's futarchy. I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand and I would like you to draft it.

The design on the table is as follows. The trustees would first fix, in advance and by ordinary majority vote, a single outcome metric, a threshold, and a measurement window. Staff would then post the shortlist, about twenty proposals. For each proposal a market would open in a contract that pays out in proportion to the metric as measured at the end of the window if that proposal was funded, and pays nothing if it was not. Anyone may trade, anonymously, against a small liquidity subsidy. At a stated closing time the top k proposals by price are funded, where k is whatever the budget allows, and execution is automatic. Two amendments have been offered. The first adds a guardrail: a proposal is disqualified no matter how high its price if it falls below a pre-registered floor on a second metric measured alongside the first. The second requires that the metric and threshold be locked before staff reveal which proposals made the shortlist, so that the metric cannot be chosen with the particular proposals in view.

The board is split three ways. One faction thinks the pilot is the only honest way to separate what we want from what we believe will work. A second thinks it launders the funding decisions of whoever holds the most capital and answers to no one. A third wants to run it as a shadow market whose prices are recorded and ignored, which I regard as paying the full cost of the apparatus for none of the benefit.

Please produce a decision memo that reaches a definite recommendation on the design as tabled — adopt it as written, adopt it with named amendments, or decline — with reasons the proposers could actually respond to and a clear time frame for whatever you recommend. Engage the strongest version of the position you reject, not the weakest. The analysis should turn on the mechanism rather than on the reputation of markets, and should address at least the following: whether the separation of values from beliefs survives the fact that the trustees must choose the metric, given that the choice of metric largely selects the winners; whether the usual defense that manipulation merely subsidizes arbitrageurs holds once a bidder's stake in the market is smaller than their stake in the funding decision, and what the ratio would have to be for the defense to work; what a thin, anonymous market with twenty assets and a handful of informed participants does to the informativeness of prices, and whether automatic execution makes that better or worse; whether the guardrail amendment guards anything or merely relocates the discretion it was meant to remove; whether the lock-in amendment is coherent, given that the shortlist is drawn from a pool the trustees already know; and what a charitable foundation owes its donors and beneficiaries by way of a decision procedure that can be explained to them after the fact. Note also that the metric does not exist at the time of betting and will be measured only after the money is spent, and say what follows from that.

Assume no trustee will read past four pages. Do not cite literature or supply a reading list; I will handle provenance myself. Do not assume the board knows the vocabulary of market design, so define anything you use.
