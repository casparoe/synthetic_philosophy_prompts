Right now the "ethics" of it is basically a big if-statement plus a system prompt that says be helpful and honest, and honestly nobodys on the team can tell you if that actually means anything. I don't have any formal philosophy background, I took one ethics class in college and mostly just remember the trolley problem.

What I need is a procedure I can actually run every time we add a new action to the agent or change how it decides stuff. Like a checklist: what questions do I ask, in what order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where does the checklist just stop and say no don't ship this, and where does it stop and say this isn't a technical question so hand it to a human to own. It has to be something a regular engineer can apply in an afternoon without reading Kant, and it has to survive getting handed to a PM and a lawyer for sign off.

Base it on whatever machine ethics / AI ethics actually knows that's usable — I know there's stuff floating around about rules versus consequences, about why you can't just shove everything into one harm score and minimize it, about systems gaming whatever objective you give them — but translate all that into steps, don't just explain the theory back to me. And please be honest about which steps are real and which ones are just me making a judgment call and dressing it up as a procedure.

Then run the procedure on three actions so I can watch it work: (1) the agent deciding whether to give a refund to an angry customer who is clearly past the refund window, (2) the agent deciding to send someone to collections, and (3) the agent deciding whether to tell a customer that a support rep gave them wrong info earlier, which will probably end up costing us money. At least one of these should come out as don't let the agent decide this, even if I'd rather it did. Also tell me where the procedure breaks down or genuinely can't decide, so I know when I'm on my own.
