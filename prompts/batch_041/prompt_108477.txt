I have no formal training in philosophy — I studied computer science and work as a backend engineer — but I've been reading G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica on my own, and I've gotten stuck in Chapter VI, "The Ideal," on the way Moore connects beauty and friendship to his principle of organic unities. I understand the individual sentences well enough, but I can't get the pieces to line up into a coherent picture, and I'd like help working through the passage rather than a general summary of the chapter.

Here is the stretch I keep returning to. From § 110, Moore distinguishes three senses of "ideal," and identifies the first with "the best state of things conceivable, the Summum Bonum or Absolute Good." In § 111 he introduces the principle he had earlier named the principle of organic unities, stating that "the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts." Two further remarks in the same section are what really trip me up. First, he says: "It is just possible that the Absolute Good may be entirely composed of qualities which we cannot even imagine." Second, in explaining why we can't simply reason our way to the Ideal, he writes: "We cannot judge of the comparative values of things, unless the things we judge are before our minds."

My difficulty is this. As I understand the rest of the book, Moore's two great goods turn out to be the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of human intercourse — friendship, roughly — and both are supposed to be cases of organic unities. The value of admiring a beautiful thing is not supposed to be the value of the object plus the value of the admiration, taken separately; the whole has a value of its own that isn't a sum. The same goes, apparently, for a friendship. But I can't work out what the "parts" of these wholes actually are, or in what sense they are parts. For the beautiful object: is the part the object itself, or my experience of it, or my emotion toward it? If the object is a part, then Moore seems to be saying that something outside a mind can be a constituent of a good that is nonetheless a state of consciousness — which I don't understand. And for friendship: if the parts are the two people, or their two minds, then the value of the friendship seems to be something over and above the value of each person, which makes me wonder whether Moore thinks the individuals have value in themselves at all, or only as ingredients in a relational whole.

There is a second, related knot. Somewhere earlier in the book — I don't have the section in front of me, so I'm paraphrasing — Moore proposes what I've seen described as a method of absolute isolation: to test whether something is good in itself, ask whether it would still be good if it existed entirely alone, stripped of every other thing. My instinct is that this test is unusable for exactly the cases Moore cares about most. A friendship cannot exist alone; it requires at least two people and a relation between them, so isolating it seems to destroy it rather than reveal its intrinsic value. A beautiful object can exist unperceived, but then there is no enjoyment of it, which is supposedly where the great good lies. So either the isolation test doesn't apply to these goods, or Moore is isolating something narrower than I think — perhaps a single person's state of consciousness, which could in principle exist alone even though as a matter of fact it is always caused by an object and another person.

What I want is an explanation of how the organic-unity principle is supposed to operate in the beauty and friendship cases specifically. Concretely: What are the parts, and what is the whole, in each case? Does the whole's value depend on the parts having value of their own, or can the parts be neutral or even bad while the whole is good? Is the relevant good a state of consciousness, a relation, or a fact about the world — and does Moore have a consistent answer, or does he shift between these? And how is the isolation test meant to be applied to goods that seem to require other things for their very existence? If my framing is confused — if, say, I'm importing a parts-and-wholes picture that Moore would reject — I'd rather be told where the confusion lies than have it politely worked around.
