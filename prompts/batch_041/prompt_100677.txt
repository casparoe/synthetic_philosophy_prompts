I am the standards editor at a fact-checking desk that grades the factual claims in our long-form journalism, and I want to replace our informal sourcing tiers with a decision procedure grounded in Nyāya epistemology — specifically its theory of the pramāṇas, the sources of valid cognition. I have an MA in philosophy and will lay the theory out below as I understand it; the translation into something my checkers can run without the theory is the part I need from you, and it is the whole difficulty, because the distinctions that make the theory do its work are exactly the ones a checklist tends to flatten.

The raw material. Nyāya recognizes four sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison (knowing a thing through its resemblance to something already known), and testimony — and holds that a cognition attains the status of knowledge only when it is true, free of doubt, not merely recalled from memory, and — on the account I want to use — not later overridden by a stronger cognition. Each source has its own entry conditions. Perception requires genuine contact between a sense faculty and its object and freedom from deviation, and it is worth separating bare perceptual contact from the conceptualized report that already says more than the contact delivered. Inference requires a reason that (i) is actually present in the case under consideration, (ii) is present in the cases already known to have the target property, and (iii) is absent from the cases known to lack it; it fails when the reason also turns up where the target is absent, when it supports the opposite, when it is itself as doubtful as the conclusion, or when it needs as much proof as the conclusion. Comparison works only when the resemblance appealed to is the relevant kind and not a surface one. Testimony requires a speaker who is both competent on the matter and truthful, whose words are actually understood, and the absence of defeating conditions. Two further points I want preserved: a clash between two sources, or between two deliverances of the same source, produces doubt, and doubt is not knowledge; and hypothetical or "what would have to be the case" reasoning can support a claim but never establish one on its own.

What I need. Write the procedure my checkers will run on each factual claim in a draft before publication: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops and refuses the claim the status of knowledge, and the points that must be escalated to me because they turn on what counts as competent, truthful, or relevant rather than on anything the checker can settle. The checkers work without a research budget and have roughly ten minutes per claim; they are not philosophers, so anything the procedure relies on has to survive being applied by someone following the steps without understanding why they are the steps. Mark plainly which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls wearing the costume of a test.

Then run the procedure, in full, on three claims: (1) a damaging claim resting entirely on a single anonymous source with an interest in the outcome; (2) a claim of a causal connection drawn from an observational dataset in which the exposure and the outcome are both common; (3) a claim our own desk published two years ago that a newly obtained document appears to contradict. At least one of the three should come out in a way the desk will hate — I would rather learn that now than in a correction. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do and where you expect it to misfire in practice.

Begin with a single sentence naming the one test the whole procedure ultimately turns on, then give the procedure itself, then the reasoning and the worked cases. No preamble, and do not restate this request.
