Last month my father was offered a choice between two operations for the same condition, and the surgeon handed me the hospital's outcome figures to help us decide. I am a graduate student in philosophy of science, so I did what one does: I read the table closely, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. The overall numbers clearly favored the older, open procedure, but when I broke the patients down by how advanced their disease was at the time of surgery, the newer, minimally invasive procedure came out ahead in both the early-stage group and the late-stage group, and not by a trivial margin. The reason, once you see it, is that the sicker patients were much more likely to be steered to the new procedure, so the aggregate is quietly comparing apples to oranges. A friend of mine in biostatistics has since built this into an argument that I find genuinely troubling, and I would like you to tell me where it goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all.

Here is his argument; the numbers are invented but the structure is faithful to what we saw. In the new procedure, 19 of 20 early-stage patients and 90 of 180 late-stage patients did well (95% and 50%). In the old procedure, 162 of 180 early-stage patients and 8 of 20 late-stage patients did well (90% and 40%). So the new procedure is ahead in both strata, yet the old one is far ahead overall (85% versus 54.5%). His argument runs: (1) Whether one treatment is better than another is a fact about the treatments and the patients, not about how we arrange the numbers. (2) For any complete dataset comparing two treatments, if the aggregate favors A, there is a partition of the very same patients into subgroups in which B is favored in every subgroup — and vice versa. (3) A claim that can be reversed simply by choosing a different partition of the same complete data is not a fact about the treatments; it is an artifact of the partition. (4) So no aggregate comparison is a fact about the treatments. (5) So there is no fact of the matter about which of two treatments is better, and every comparative-effectiveness ranking — including the ones in the guidelines my father's hospital follows — is an artifact of someone's bookkeeping. When I objected that in our case the subgroup comparison is plainly the meaningful one, he replied that this was just my preferring one partition to another, and that if I constructed a case in which the aggregate looked right, that would merely be one more partition, no better than his. I can see that the conclusion is absurd — it would mean the trial tells us nothing even about whether the operation reduces complications within a single homogeneous group of patients — but I cannot locate the misstep.

Please diagnose the argument rather than dismiss it. Which step should I reject — (2), (3), (4), or something earlier — and why did that step look so plausible in the first place? If you fault (2), tell me exactly what must be true of a dataset for the reversal he describes to be constructible at all, and whether his sweeping quantifier survives that. If you fault (3), tell me what distinguishes a partition that merely rearranges the same numbers from one that exposes or conceals a real feature of the world, and how I am meant to tell which is which without already knowing the answer I am trying to reach. If you fault (4), tell me what survives of (1) once the aggregate is demoted. And in each case, tell me what the repair costs: I would like to keep (1), because I do not want to say that "better" is a bookkeeping convenience, and I would like to keep the worry behind (5), because I genuinely distrust how guidelines flatten tables like this one. Show me what I have to give up to keep both.
