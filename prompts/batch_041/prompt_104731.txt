I'm a third-year PhD student in history, and my dissertation concerns a rural population that survives in the written record almost entirely through tax registers, a few parish disputes, and the occasional marginal note — a group that is everywhere present in the social life of the period and almost nowhere in the archive itself. Over the past year I've watched a single sentence do an enormous amount of work in my department, and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether the people repeating it agree with one another. In a seminar on archival methods, an instructor shut down a debate about whether the near-total silence of our sources on a particular seasonal practice amounts to evidence that the practice was uncommon, by announcing that "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" — and he clearly took the line to license reading the silences themselves as a kind of presence, as though what the record omits is still somehow recorded in its omissions. A week later, in a workshop run by someone trained in the social sciences, I heard the same sentence called obviously false: failing to find a thing, this person said, is exactly a reason to think it isn't there, and he pressed the point by asking how likely our documents would be to survive at all if the practice had been widespread. I've since heard the phrase used to mean everything from "we can't prove it didn't happen" to "the powerful wrote the record, so its gaps are testimony," and I genuinely cannot tell which of these the instructor meant, or whether any of them can bear the weight he put on it.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be making — from the ones that turn out to be trivial or even tautological, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, to the ones that are simply false — and for each say what it would actually commit its speaker to and whether it would support the instructor's move of treating archival silence as evidence of presence. I suspect one of the readings has to do with how probable the record would be to survive if the practice had existed, but I don't want to prejudge that; if some readings collapse into one another, or if the slogan as usually stated is the negation of a claim people actually mean, say so. I'd like to bring the list to my advisor, so please be explicit about which reading the instructor probably intended, which reading his opponents are attacking when they call the line false, and which of the readings, if any, would let me responsibly argue from a gap in the sources to something about the world the sources failed to capture.
