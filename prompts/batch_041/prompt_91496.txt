I am drafting the methodological preamble for a long-horizon endowment, and before we argue about which rate to apply I want the specification itself: the desiderata a theory of pure time preference must satisfy. By pure time preference I mean the discount attached to a future good merely because it is future, as distinct from discounting that falls out of growth, risk, uncertainty, or forgone alternatives. Give me the requirements such a theory has to meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp rather than features of the phenomenon itself.

Organize the requirements as the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the formal results it must be consistent with.

The cases, at minimum:

- Pure delay: two programs identical in goods, in recipients (the very same people), in risk, and in opportunity cost, differing only in the date of delivery. The theory must say whether the later one is worth less, and on what ground.
- A non-growing good: a fixed, irreplaceable stock, delivered now or later. Because it cannot grow, no growth-based discounting is available here, so whatever discounting survives must be pure.
- Guaranteed-richer recipients: the future recipients are certain to be wealthier than we are, so diminishing marginal utility already supplies a reason to prefer present consumption. The theory must isolate the pure component from that reason.
- An empty future: the benefit may fall to no one at all, because the recipients may never come to exist. The theory must classify the discount that follows, whether it counts as pure time preference or as a different kind of discount.
- Horizon stability: an identical benefit at a hundred years and at a million. The theory must either be stable under extending the horizon or explain why it need not be.

The distinctions it must not collapse: pure versus derivative discounting (growth, risk, opportunity cost); discounting one's own later utility versus discounting across persons who never coexist; the pure rate versus the overall social discount rate; discounting utility versus discounting consumption; and a rate applied to a flow versus the horizon over which it compounds.

The results it must be consistent with: that a constant positive pure rate drives the present value of any fixed future benefit toward zero as the horizon grows; that a constant pure rate yields exponential, time-consistent discounting, while any rate that declines with the horizon is time-inconsistent; and that pure and growth-based components compound rather than add in the present value.

Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I expect at least one such pair, and that is the part I most want, so give the argument rather than an assertion. Finally, name the single requirement that does the most to rule out the most popular positions, and say what it rules out. I do not need a survey of the literature or a scorecard of named authors; the list itself is the deliverable.
