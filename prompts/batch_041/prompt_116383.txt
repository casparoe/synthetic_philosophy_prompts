The idea. Whenever anyone drags the no-free-lunch theorems into the induction debate, the same thing happens: someone says the theorems show there's no a priori reason to prefer one inductive bias over another, someone else says yes, Hume wins, and then the reply comes that the uniform measure over all objective functions is absurd and nobody is actually uncertain that way, so the theorems are irrelevant. I think both moves are looking at the wrong part of the theorem. The sharpened versions of NFL, as I understand them, aren't really about uniform measures as such — they say the averaging result holds for a class of problems if and only if that class is closed under permutation, i.e. if relabelling inputs and outputs maps the class onto itself, then every method does equally well on average and there's no free lunch. So the theorem is a statement about which transformations leave your space of problems invariant, not about what probability you assign. And that looks like the shape of the grue problem to me (maybe I'm being sloppy here — grue and green aren't related by a pure permutation, there's a time transformation in there too, but you get what I mean): what makes 'green' the better bet is that we treat some transformations as meaning-preserving and others as gerrymandering, and the whole thing bottoms out in which symmetries we take to be constitutive of our concepts rather than discovered in the world. So what I actually want to ask is whether the induction debate is better reconstructed as a disagreement about symmetries, and whether that reconstruction buys us anything or just relocates the mystery one step back — from 'why these biases' to 'why these transformations', which sounds like the same problem wearing a nicer outfit.
