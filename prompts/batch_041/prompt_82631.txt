Ok, odd one. I'm a third-year in a history of science program, prepping for a seminar on science and religion, and I keep getting stuck on a counterfactual: what would Galileo actually have made of the 19th- and 20th-century fights over biblical inerrancy and historical criticism? The standard story makes him the patron saint of the accommodationist camp — scripture isn't a physics textbook, it talks the way ordinary people talk, so there's no real conflict — but I suspect he'd be uneasy with how far the modern accommodationists push that, since he never gave up on scripture being true and he thought the whole dispute was really about which propositions the text is asserting. So put him in the room with the debate over Genesis: is it just ancient Near Eastern cosmology that the writers happened to share, so simply wrong about the heavens, or does it use the language of appearances and come out true but non-scientific? Which side does he land on, or does he reject the choice — and what does his own account of why God accomodates (who the audience is, what the Spirit is and isn't trying to teach) commit him to? Make him pick; don't tell me he was ahead of his time.
