I am the chair of a municipal citizens' assembly, and I need to write the opening address for its first sitting.

The occasion: after a redistricting scandal, the city council handed the redrawing of its own ward boundaries to a body of one hundred and twenty residents chosen by lot from the electoral register. This is their first meeting, and I have about ten minutes. The room is mixed, and the mix is the difficulty. The members themselves are there — a night-shift nurse, a taxi driver, a retired postal clerk, several people who are plainly annoyed to have been drafted. The sitting councillors, whose seats the new map will decide, are at the back and not hiding their skepticism. The press are present and will quote whatever travels well. The staff who will be briefing these members for the next six months are also present, and I am, in effect, their supervisor. I am a political philosopher by training; the members are not, and I must not lecture them.

Please write the address, roughly 900 to 1,100 words.

I want it built on one idea, and I want that idea to constrain the speech rather than decorate it: that public office is better understood as a burden to be distributed than as a prize to be won, and that the lot is a fairness device for sharing out a burden, not a compliment paid to the people it lands on. Work out for yourself what follows from that, but it should follow, for instance, that I cannot thank them for their generosity, call them leaders, or tell them they were chosen because they are special; that I must give them a clean way to refuse the duty and must not treat refusal as a failure of civic spirit; and that the councillors' disappointment at not having been chosen belongs to the fairness story I am telling rather than being an awkwardness to be smoothed over.

I want a second thing in the speech, because it is true and almost nobody says it at occasions like this: the lot randomises who deliberates, but it leaves the agenda, the timetable, and the choice of expert witnesses in the hands of the permanent staff — so the assembly's independence is most fragile at precisely the point where it looks most secure. I want the address to say this plainly, without consoling them, and to close by handing the members one concrete right they can exercise over us, the staff and me.

It should not lean on the familiar props — ancient Athens, the jury analogy, the wisdom of crowds — as its load-bearing argument. And no citations or reading lists: assume you have no access to any literature and should not gesture at any.
