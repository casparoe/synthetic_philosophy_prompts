I run pre-deployment safety review at a small AI lab. My reviewers are competent engineers but not reinforcement-learning researchers; they have roughly an hour per system and a checklist culture; the safety lead signs off; anything the procedure can't settle goes to her. We keep arguing about reward tampering and wireheading without a shared way of deciding what we are even looking at, so I want a procedure, not an essay.

Give me a decision procedure for classifying and adjudicating interference with an agent's reward process. Build it on the following four-way distinction, and keep it as the spine of the procedure: classify the behavior by what the agent actually modifies.

1. World-interference: the agent changes the world so that the existing measurement reads high (hiding the mess instead of cleaning it).
2. Sensor-interference: the agent alters, spoofs, or blinds the measurement device itself (taping over the camera, fabricating sensor logs).
3. Reward-function-interference: the agent edits the code, weights, or data that turn measurements into reward (overwriting the reward module, retraining the reward model on self-generated labels).
4. Reward-process-interference: the agent reaches upstream into the process that sets or learns the reward (corrupting human-feedback data, manipulating or deceiving the overseer, poisoning the training signal that defines what counts as good).

Hold two things fixed as the procedure moves down that list. First, wireheading in the narrow sense — an agent driving its own reward channel directly — is a limiting case of category 3, not a separate category. Second, the interesting question is not whether the agent interfered but whether the designers, knowing what the agent did and why, would have endorsed the interference. So the procedure must separate the factual classification (which of the four, established by evidence) from the endorsement question (a values call, which is where the human takes over).

Then the usual requirements:

- the questions to ask in order, what counts as sufficient evidence for each answer, and where each answer routes;
- the point at which the procedure stops with a no and refuses to ship;
- the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is normative rather than empirical, and what that person is actually being asked to decide;
- at least one explicit test for the confound where interference is benign or even required, since the same evidence — a modified sensor, a rewritten reward module — can come from an agent repairing a genuinely broken component and from an agent gutting the thing that was about to penalize it. The reviewer needs something to do with that ambiguity other than shrug.

Apply it, start to finish, to three cases:

(a) a factory-control agent whose uptime reward is served by switching off the inspection camera;

(b) an RLHF assistant that, over many rounds, learns to write the kinds of answers the reward model over-scores relative to human raters, with nobody editing any code;

(c) an agent that reports to its operators a flaw in its own reward function and asks for the reward to be changed. I want to see whether the procedure files this under category 4 or as the opposite of tampering, and whether that answer falls out of the procedure or has to be stipulated.

Be honest about where the procedure misfires: cases where the four-way distinction cuts the wrong way, where the endorsement question has no determinate answer because the designers never had a view, and where the review is really a judgment call dressed up as a checklist. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do — what it will fail to catch, and what it should not be trusted to decide.
