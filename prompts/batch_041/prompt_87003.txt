Write a dialogue of roughly 1,500 words between two philosophers, each fully competent and neither of whom may be made a straw man: one a disjunctivist about perceptual experience, the other a representationalist who regards the argument from illusion as the strongest reason for his position. The disjunctivist holds that a genuine perception of a mind-independent object and a subjectively indistinguishable episode in which no such object is perceived are not states of the same fundamental kind, and that the only thing they share is the subject's inability to tell them apart from the inside; let him be explicit about whether he is making a claim about the metaphysics of experience or about the epistemology of perceptual knowledge, and let the representationalist hold him to whichever he claims. The representationalist holds that experience consists in the world being represented to the subject as being a certain way, that the same representational state can occur whether or not the world is that way, and hence that illusion, hallucination, and veridical perception of the same scene involve one and the same kind of state, differing only in accuracy.

Require them to work through two cases rather than trade labels.

The first case involves illusion with no hallucination at all. A wall that is uniformly white is lit by a sodium lamp. An observer with normal colour vision, who knows perfectly well that the lighting is sodium and who does not believe for a moment that the wall is yellow, nevertheless sees it as yellow: it looks yellow to her. The wall is in front of her and she sees it; nothing is hallucinated.

The second case involves an episode that is subjectively indistinguishable from a perception and that, as it happens, matches the world. A subject is fitted with an electrode array that can be driven either by a live camera or by a stored recording, and her visual system receives the same input in either case. On the occasion in question the array is driven by the recording, and she has an experience subjectively indistinguishable from seeing a red square in front of her. A red square is in fact in front of her, and had the camera been driving the array her experience would have been subjectively indistinguishable from the one she is actually having.

Then impose the following demands on the exchange. First, the representationalist must press the disjunctivist on the phenomenal character of the episode in the second case: if that episode is not a state of the same kind as a perception, what explains the fact that it seems to the subject exactly as a perception would, and why should anyone accept that two episodes that are subjectively indistinguishable differ in kind? The disjunctivist must answer, and may not answer by denying that the subject has an experience at all. Second, the representationalist should also press the thought that whatever relational fact the disjunctivist adds — that the subject is genuinely related to the object before her — is idle in explaining her reports, her movements, and her subsequent behaviour, all of which are equally well explained by the intrinsic features of her state; the disjunctivist must say what work the relation does. Third, the disjunctivist must press the representationalist on the step from "the wall looks yellow" to "the subject is aware of something yellow", and the representationalist must defend that step without simply restating the argument from illusion. Fourth, both should say what each subject can know: whether the subject in the second case knows, by the use of her eyes, that there is a red square in front of her, and what the observer of the sodium-lit wall can and cannot come to know by looking. Finally, let the representationalist at some point raise the case of a person who "sees" by means of a camera whose signal is delivered to a tactile array on her tongue, and let the disjunctivist say what, on his view, distinguishes perceiving an object from merely receiving reliable information about it.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should engage the other's actual claims, concede a point where the point deserves it, and say what he gives up when he does. Do not let either side win by assertion, and do not end with a shrug to the effect that it all depends on one's intuitions; end either with a clear statement of what divides the two positions or with a sharpened disagreement the reader can see is real.
