For years I have sat on a hospital ethics committee and in departmental seminars where the same argument about the definition of death recurs, and I still cannot say what the two sides are actually disagreeing about. The neurological position treats irreversible cessation of brain function as the death of the organism; the circulatory position treats the loss of circulation as the event, and reads the neurological criteria as a proxy whose adoption was shaped in part by the needs of transplantation. Each side knows the other's cases — patients ventilated long after a neurological determination, patients whose circulation stops and is restarted, the dead donor rule that makes the definition load-bearing for procurement, the fact that jurisdictions codify different criteria — and neither has moved in a decade.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and not a survey of positions. My working hypothesis is that the word 'death' is being made to answer three questions at once: when an organism ceases to be a living organism; when a person ceases to exist; and when an individual's institutional status changes so that certain permissions and obligations attach. If that is right, much of the impasse is verbal, and what remains is a disagreement about which question the concept ought to track. A colleague goes further: she proposes that death is a status conferred rather than an event discovered — closer to 'bankrupt' or 'married' than to 'frozen' — so that the criteria should be chosen for the function the status serves, and different practices may legitimately draw the line in different places. I cannot tell whether this dissolves the problem or merely relocates it.

So test the hypothesis against what each side actually argues. Which of the following is doing the work: a concealed empirical claim, for instance that the brainstem is what integrates the organism, or that circulatory cessation after the withdrawal of treatment is irreversible rather than merely unreversed; a normative claim about which lost capacity matters; a disagreement about the function the concept serves; or a genuine metaphysical disagreement about biological kinds? Separate the readings under which the debate is verbal from those under which it survives full specification of the question being asked. Then say what progress would look like given your diagnosis — a distinction, a criterion, an agreement about which practice the concept is for — or argue that none should be expected, and why. Finally, tell me which consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
