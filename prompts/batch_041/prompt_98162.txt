I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and in ten days I will deliver the keynote at a small interdisciplinary symposium on the science of consciousness. I have been asked to argue a position rather than report on one, and I have chosen to defend the claim that consciousness is a user illusion: that what we call phenomenal experience is not a window onto anything but a low-bandwidth self-report that the brain generates about its own processing, with the representational character of that report hidden from the system producing it.

Here is the argument I intend to make, so that you can aim the preparation squarely at it. First, the brain is a massively parallel, distributed set of processes with no central stage; the serialized, single-stream quality of experience is a summary, not a broadcast of an inner show. Second, the summary is transparent: it presents its outputs as direct acquaintance with the world and with ourselves rather than as constructed representations, which is why introspection, sent looking for the construction, finds only the finished product. Third, and this is the part I expect to be attacked hardest, the summary is self-covering: it includes a representation of the very act of representing, so that the illusion is not a mistake made by some inner observer but the shape the self-model simply takes. On my account there is no one being fooled. I also mean to claim that the standard charge that illusionism is self-undermining, on the grounds that if experience is an illusion then something must be undergoing it, presupposes exactly the inner subject my view rejects; the felt quality of what it is like is itself part of the model, so the question begs what is in dispute.

The audience is not a philosophy department. It will consist of roughly four groups, and I need the prep sheet keyed to each. There will be a block of computational neuroscientists who build predictive-processing and Bayesian models of perception; they are largely indifferent to phenomenology, regard phenomenal consciousness as a pre-theoretic notion to be quarantined rather than explained, and will want to know whether my view does any empirical work or merely redescribes theirs in more provocative language. There will be two or three philosophers of mind who are realists about phenomenal experience and are convinced that any denial of it is performatively incoherent; they know the transparency intuition and the self-refutation objection inside out and will press them with care. There will be a pair of clinical neurologists who work with patients in chronic pain and with disorders of consciousness; they care about whether my position implies that patients who report suffering are systematically mistaken about something, and whether that implication has any bearing on how we should treat them. And there will be one contemplative-studies scholar who will observe that Buddhist accounts of no-self reached a similar conclusion centuries ago but would never have framed the matter as an illusion, since an illusion implies a deceived party, and who will suspect that my framing smuggles the self back in through the word illusion.

Please draft my question-period preparation as a single prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from a room of philosophers of mind alone. Order them by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them, and for each one phrase it the way the person who raises it would phrase it, in that group's vocabulary, so that I can hear it coming. For each objection give me a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than fight, identify the single reply that has to land for the talk to be judged a success, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Write the whole thing in American English.
