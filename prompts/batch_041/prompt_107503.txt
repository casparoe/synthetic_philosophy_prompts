I am a philosopher of science who works on the foundations of machine learning, and in three weeks I am to give a keynote at a small interdisciplinary workshop on interpretability. The paper I shall present defends what I have been calling causal-role representational realism: the claim that when a mechanistic-interpretability technique — a sparse autoencoder trained on a residual stream, say, or an activation-patching experiment that isolates a particular sub-computation — yields a feature that is stable across seeds and interventionally robust under ablation, we are entitled to say that the network represents that feature, provided we construe 'represents' as picking out a counterfactual-supporting causal role in the network's downstream computation rather than any relation to a worldly referent. I argue that this reading survives the usual deflationist objections and vindicates the practitioners' talk of 'features' and 'circuits' as something more than metaphor, while conceding that the realism is thin, that the features are multiply realisable across architectures, that they are partly artefacts of the training distribution, and that the view buys us nothing on the question of what the network means. I take it to be a middle way between the eliminativists, for whom interpretability vocabulary is convenient fiction, and the strong realists, who want to read semantic content straight off the weights.

The room will not be uniform, which is why I need help. Roughly a third will be mechanistic-interpretability engineers — implicit realists, philosophically impatient, who will want to know whether any of this changes what they do at the bench. A quarter will be statistical learning theorists who are constructive empiricists in all but name and who will press me on identifiability: they will point out that sparse-autoencoder dictionaries are not unique, that a different random seed returns a different basis for the same activations, and that if the 'feature' depends on the basis I chose, my realism may be realism about my own choices. Two will be cognitive neuroscientists who hear 'representation' in the efficient-coding register and will accuse me of conflating representation with coding, or of mistaking a decodable correlate for a causal vehicle. And one will be a philosopher of mind who is an eliminativist about representation in general and will maintain that I have smuggled in the very notion I claim to be deflating, dressed in causal clothing.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet — not a defence, a prep sheet. I want the objections I shall actually get from this room, ordered both by how likely each is and by how much damage it does, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it (the engineer's version of a worry and the statistician's version are different questions and I want them kept distinct), with a short reply I can deliver in under two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should concede outright, which I must hold come what may, and which are really the same objection in two costumes. Include the question I am dreading. Please reason from the substance of the positions rather than pointing me at the literature — I shall not have time to chase references before the session, and I would far rather have the argument than the citation.
