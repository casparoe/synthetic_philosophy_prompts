I am trying to understand a passage in Keynes's discussion of long-term expectation in The General Theory, in the chapter where he introduces the state of confidence, and I would like a careful conceptual reconstruction rather than a summary. The passage runs:

"It would be foolish, in forming our expectations, to attach great weight to matters which are very uncertain. It is reasonable, therefore, to be guided to a considerable degree by the facts about which we feel somewhat confident, even though they may be less decisively relevant to the issue than other facts about which our knowledge is vague and scanty. For this reason the facts of the existing situation enter, in a sense disproportionately, into the formation of our long-term expectations; our usual practice being to take the existing situation and to project it into the future, modified only to the extent that we have more or less definite reasons for expecting a change."

A few sentences later he adds:

"The state of long-term expectation, upon which our decisions are based, does not solely depend, therefore, on the most probable forecast we can make. It also depends on the confidence with which we make this forecast — on how highly we rate the likelihood of our best forecast turning out quite wrong."

What I want help with is the following. First, what exactly is the distinction being drawn here between the probability of a forecast and the confidence with which it is held? I take it that this is continuous with the distinction in his earlier work on probability between the probability of a proposition and the amount of evidence bearing on it — the quantity he called the weight of the argument — but I would like you to explain how confidence so understood is supposed to combine with probability to govern rational choice, and whether it is best modeled as a second-order probability, as a non-probabilistic quantity, or as something else entirely. Second, the first quoted sentence appears to counsel the opposite of expected-value reasoning: it advises discounting considerations precisely because they are very uncertain, even when they may be momentous. Is this defensible as a norm, or is it a description of a psychological tendency that Keynes is half-endorsing? Reconstruct the strongest version of the advice and then say where you think it fails. Third, I want to know what work "confidence" does that a Bayesian second-order distribution over one's own estimates could not do; if I model confidence as dispersion across my probability estimates, does anything in the passage become redundant or false? Fourth, does the passage commit Keynes to a non-numerical, partially ordered conception of probability, in which some pairs of prospects are simply not comparable in probability or in rational preferability? If so, say what follows for the kind of decision rule he can and cannot endorse.

Finally, I would like you to work through a case: an agent facing a small-probability, enormous-magnitude risk whose probability she genuinely cannot estimate, alongside a set of well-evidenced but modest considerations pointing the other way. Does the reasoning in the passage tell her to act on the modest considerations, and if so, on what grounds? Lay the reasoning out step by step, mark the points where you are reconstructing rather than reporting, and note where the view becomes unstable under pressure from a thoroughgoing expected-value approach. I am not interested in the surrounding macroeconomic themes, only in the epistemology of the passage.
