I have spent a lot of time in rationalist comment sections, and there is one sentence that keeps surfacing whenever the existence of mathematical objects comes up: 'to be is to be the value of a variable.' The most recent use of it was by someone who treated the platonism–nominalism dispute as settled — mathematics quantifies over numbers, the slogan tells us what quantifying over something amounts to, so numbers exist and the nominalist is simply confused about the semantics of her own language. I am fairly sure the sentence is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I cannot say precisely which of several quite different claims it is being asked to stand for, and I would rather have the menu than a verdict.

Please separate the distinct theses that one-liner could be expressing, from the nearly trivial to the strongly metaphysical: what each reading actually commits you to, whether it is defensible on its own terms, and whether it would support the conclusion my interlocutor drew. I am particularly interested in the gap between a claim about how to regiment a theory — a methodological or normative point about the existential quantifier — and a claim about what existence is, and in whether the slogan, on any reading a careful nominalist could accept, forecloses the nominalist's option of paraphrasing the mathematics so that the apparent quantification over numbers disappears. Tell me which readings are platitudes, which are contentious, which are probably false, and which one the people who repeat the sentence in these arguments almost certainly mean. A note on whether the platonist conclusion survives the correction, and on which reading would actually license ending the argument rather than continuing it, would be appreciated.
