I run ops at a forty-person company and we keep having the same fight: someone said something in a sales call, or a Slack thread, or on their way out the door, that never made it into the contract, and now half the team says we promised and half says we didn't. I want a decision procedure my team leads can actually run, in order, for whether an informal commitment morally binds us — assume the lawyers have already told us we can legally walk away, that part's settled. Ground it in what the philosophy of promising actually establishes rather than vibes: the difference between a promise and a prediction or a threat, why breaking one wrongs the person instead of merely disappointing them, the Scanlon-style idea that what a promise hands the other side is assurance, the Shiffrin-style idea that it hands them a kind of authority over your future conduct, the older conventionalist line that it's all a social practice with rules we're playing by. I want it to also force the team to say whether the promisee has a right to demand performance or only to be compensated, and whether we can release ourselves unilaterally or need them to. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a 'not bound' and why that isn't just us being cheap, and where it hands off to a person because the question is values not facts. Then run it on three cases: a rep who told a customer we'd always support the old version and then quit; a founder who said at an all-hands that there'd be no layoffs this year and now needs to cut; and one where I want the procedure to tell me we're bound to something expensive I'd rather not do. Keep it to something a busy lead can run in twenty minutes, and be honest about which steps are just rationalization with a flowchart drawn on top.
