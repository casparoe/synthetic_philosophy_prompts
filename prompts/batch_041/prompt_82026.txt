I am writing a novel about a discrimination case — an admissions or hiring dispute that turns, in the end, on a single statistical table — and before I invent anything I want to understand a real episode properly. The episode is the graduate admissions figures at the University of California, Berkeley, for the fall of 1973, and the study that followed them. The facts as I have them are these: across the whole university, about 44 percent of the men who applied were admitted against about 35 percent of the women, a gap large enough that chance looked like a poor explanation; and yet within most individual departments the women's admission rate was equal to the men's or higher, so that the aggregate disparity appeared to come from where the applications went — women applying in disproportionate numbers to the departments with the lowest admission rates, men to the more forgiving ones. The study's authors concluded that once the departments were taken into account, whatever bias remained ran, if anything, slightly in the women's favor.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of Simpson's paradox — the phenomenon in which a trend present in each of several groups reverses when the groups are pooled — and give me the difficult version rather than the textbook one. I know the standard resolution; what I need is an account that runs in both directions, the concept illuminating the episode and the episode testing the concept.

Work through the following. First, state the reversal precisely: name the groups, name the lurking variable, and say exactly what is being aggregated and what is being held apart. Second, set out the two competing readings as two different questions rather than one right answer and one wrong one — what the pooled number answers, what the department-by-department numbers answer, and why the same data can honestly support both. Third, and this is the part I most need, the causal question underneath the statistics: is the department variable a cause that precedes the alleged discrimination or a consequence of it? If a woman's choice of which department to apply to was itself shaped by her expectations of how she would be treated, or by the advice she was given, or by the structure of the process, then the department sits downstream of the very thing under investigation, and adjusting for it would remove part of the effect we are trying to measure. Lay out the general rule — adjust for what comes before the treatment, do not adjust for what comes after — and then say which kind of variable the department is here, what each choice of adjustment would license me to conclude, and whether the study's authors were making a statistical decision or a causal one when they chose. Fourth, tell me where the paradox goes silent: as a bare statistical fact it cannot say which number to report, so what supplies the missing judgment, and what does the episode reveal that the standard classroom presentation conceals? Finally, give me your verdict on what the case does to the concept — whether it confirms Simpson's paradox as a useful warning against naive aggregation, or troubles it as a case the concept never really anticipated — and what the whole affair shows about how such tables actually get used when real interests are at stake.
