I have no background in medicine or philosophy, but I have spent a long time on my own with the question of how death should be defined, and especially with the split between the older circulatory standard and the brain-based criteria that the ventilator made possible — the odd situation in which a body can have a beating heart, warm skin, and working reflexes while everything that made it a person has stopped. To test my own thinking, I have been asking what a figure from before any of this would have said, and I have settled on Descartes as the case I most want worked out. What I would like is a reconstruction, as careful as you can make it, of the position he would most likely have taken on the modern debate.

Please build it from what he actually held rather than from his reputation. In particular: that mind and body are two different kinds of substance; that the mind is a thinking thing whose nature does not depend on the body; that a living body is a machine whose workings can in principle be described mechanically, without reference to a soul; that he singled out one small structure deep in the brain as the point of contact between mind and body; and that he denied minds to animals altogether. Say which of these commitments would actually drive a verdict on death, and which would turn out to be irrelevant once the question is posed sharply.

Then take the modern distinctions one at a time and give me his likely answer to each:

- the difference between the death of the organism and the death of the person;
- whole-brain versus brainstem criteria, and the fact that a body meeting either can still regulate its temperature, fight infection, and in rare cases sustain a pregnancy;
- the difference between brain death and a persistent vegetative state, in which the brainstem still works;
- the practice of declaring death by circulatory criteria only after a ventilator is switched off, in order to procure organs;
- and the argument, made by defenders of the brain criterion, that the brain is what integrates the body into a single organism, set against the reply that a brain-dead body on a ventilator is still plainly integrating itself.

For each, I want the reasoning shown, and I want you to mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything he addressed, as opposed to where his own doctrines really do decide the matter. Where his framework gives no answer, say so instead of manufacturing one; three confident predictions and two honest silences would be worth more to me than five hedged ones.

Two further things. First, is there anything in his position that would make him reject the question altogether — some reason to think that death is not the sort of thing a physician's criteria could settle? Second, and I am not sure this is even coherent: would his dualism have made him more willing to call a breathing body dead, since for him the mind is not the body and may be gone while the machine runs on, or would it have made him less willing, since he would not accept that the destruction of an organ is the destruction of the person?

Do not give me citations or a reading list; I cannot look anything up, and I would rather have your argument than a bibliography.
