I'm a high-school junior on the editorial board of my school's science-and-philosophy magazine, and I've been given the satire slot for our next issue. I have a fairly specific idea of what would make the piece work, and I'd rather not settle for a draft that just makes jokes about a little man with a door.

Write an Onion-style news report — headline, subhead, and roughly 500 words of body copy — set in a world where a research institute has unveiled a working Maxwell's demon and is promoting it as a clean-energy breakthrough. The comedy should come from the physics rather than from the demon bit itself: every gag ought to be something a physicist or a philosopher of physics would recognize as a genuine feature of the problem, so that the piece reads as an insider joke and a specialist laughs instead of wincing. The thing being satirized is the press-release genre in which some clever mechanism is announced to have beaten a law of nature, and, behind it, the habit of treating information as though it were free.

Please build the article around the following, and get the details right:

- The device does exactly what the thought experiment says: a trapdoor between two chambers, fast molecules let through in one direction and slow ones in the other, until one side is hot and the other cold.
- The comic engine is the demon's memory. He cannot sort indefinitely without keeping records of what he has let through, and a finite memory has to be wiped eventually. Make the erasure of those records the thing that destroys the business plan: the standard account of why the second law survives is that erasing information carries a minimum thermodynamic cost, about kT ln 2 per bit, which at room temperature comes to a few hundredths of an electronvolt of heat per bit and cancels the energy the demon appeared to extract.
- Do not get the accounting backwards. The classic resolution is not that measuring the molecules costs energy — the measurement can in principle be done with no minimum cost at all — and the bill comes due at the erasure. A reader who knows this literature should be able to tell that the piece knows it too.
- Include at least one deadpan quotation from a "concerned" outsider — a thermodynamicist, a rival demon, or a regulator — whose objection is a real one rather than a punchline.
- Include one line that acknowledges, without settling, that even this accounting is disputed: some argue the second law is rescued for reasons other than the erasure cost, and the assumptions behind the erasure bound are themselves contested.

Constraints: keep it legible to someone who has never taken a thermodynamics course, glossing any technical term in plain English within the same sentence; never wink at the reader or explain the joke; and invent the institute, the executives, and the experts rather than naming or quoting real people, living or dead. Our faculty adviser teaches physics and will read it closely, so it has to be funny to someone who has followed the debate and still make sense to someone who hasn't.
