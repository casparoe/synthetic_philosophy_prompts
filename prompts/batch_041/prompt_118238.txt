I teach an upper-level seminar on rationality — 16 students, 90 minutes, they've already done Newcomb's problem so they know the causal/evidential split. I want them to feel the Death in Damascus problem rather than hear me describe it: the vertigo of a doom that follows you to whichever room you pick, so that every reason you have for going left is also a reason the doom is on the left. Design me a runnable activity that does that — roles, rules, a payoff scheme in candy or points rather than money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the structure of the case. Three things I want built in, because they're what I actually care about and they're not the parts people usually mention. First, I want the group to find the escape hatch themselves — that if you can be genuinely unpredictable the doom can't follow you, which turns the problem from I die either way into a lottery at even odds — and then to feel why that escape is cheap, since their deliberation is itself one of the things being predicted, so deciding to flip a coin is also on the table for the predictor to read. Second, I want them to end up seeing that the case quietly assumes two things that can't both be true: that the predictor is perfect, and that their choice is still genuinely open to them. Third, I want them to catch the fatalist equivocation, where the prophecy is treated as a fixed fact about the world when that helps the argument and as an effect of their choice when that helps instead. Don't name the case until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight about what the activity can't reproduce — I assume it can't give them a real perfect predictor, only a staged one, and I want to know what that costs. Give me one activity, fully specified, with definite calls on the payoff structure and on how the thing ends; don't hand me three variants to choose between, and don't send me to any readings.
