I've got a half-formed idea and I need you to turn it into something submittable — tight question, why the obvious answers don't settle it, why my angle and not some other, what I actually do month to month, what a win looks like versus a wasted six months, and a scope one reporter can honestly pull off.

Heres the idea. Everyone uses "common knowledge" to mean "everybody knows the thing." Game theory means something much stricter — I know it, you know it, I know you know it, you know I know you know it, forever up the tower. And the eerie part of that literature is that a hairline crack in the tower (some small chance a message never arrives, or the other guy is a shade less than perfectly rational) can flip what the rational move is, all the way down. There's a old toy story about two generals trying to coordinate an attack through messengers who might get captured, where no finite number of confirmations ever quite closes the deal.

What I want to report on is AI agents that negotiate and trade with each other. The strange thing is these agents are more transparent than people ever were — the weights, the prompts, the transcripts can all be public — so you'd figure common knowledge would be easy to get. But when I've watched these setups the deals still fall through, and it doesn't look like lying, it looks like each side being unable to know that the other knows, even with everything on the table. My hunch: publishing the artifact isn't the same as making the reasoning common, and that gap is where a lot of the autonomy hype quietly dies.

So the question I want to pin down: does making an AI agent's policy and payoffs public create common knowledge of its rationality in the strict sense — and if not, what's missing, and can it be manufactured?

For the proposal, I want my own small experiment as the spine of the piece: two off-the-shelf LLM agents haggling over something divisible, in three conditions — (1) each can read and verify the other's system prompt; (2) each can read it but one gets to describe its own policy in words and may lie; (3) the messages have a small random drop rate, like the generals' messengers. Then the conceptual writing sits on top: what the results mean for the "common knowledge of rationality" assumption that a pile of AI-safety and mechanism-design talk quietly leans on.
