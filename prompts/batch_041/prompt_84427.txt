Hi, I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so please excuse any butchered terminology. I'm about 200 pages into a book where a woman is dying and her personality sort of seeps into the five people closest to her, so that by the end each of them is carrying a different chunk of who she was — one gets her sense of humor, one gets her way of picking a fight, one gets how she remembered her childhood, and so on. The whole book hinges on whether I'm actually writing her death or her survival, and honestly I can't tell anymore.

A while back I read Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, and his idea stuck with me — that the self is basically a self-referential pattern, and that when you die you're not entirely gone because the pattern lives on, to different degrees, in the heads of the people who really took you in. The more someone internalized you, the more of you survives in them. Then a friend told me Parfit got to something similar in Reasons and Persons, that personal identity isn't the thing that actually matters, that what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity, and so "survival" comes in degrees.

Those two sound almost identicle to me, which makes me suspicious. Can you lay out the real difference between Hofstadter's picture — the self as a pattern that can be spread across other people's brains — and Parfit's degrees-of-survival idea? Where do they genuinely come apart, and why does the disagreement matter? Then tell me which one is the better tool for my five-friends scenario. What's bugging me is this: if each friend only holds a shard, and the shards are all different and maybe even contradict each other, is there any real sense in which she survived — or did I just kill her off and leave five imperfect copies walking around? I want the honest answer, not the comforting one.
