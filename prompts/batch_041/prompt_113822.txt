For the past year and a half my brother has been absorbed in a revisionist chronology of the ancient Mediterranean — one of those systems that slides the conventional dates of the Egyptian and Hittite kingdoms by several centuries, overlaps dynasties that everyone else keeps apart, and then presents the resulting timeline as the key that dissolves a long list of puzzles which, he says, the orthodox account has merely papered over. What I find harder to answer than his conclusions is his methodological boast: that his scheme stakes out precise, checkable claims in advance — a particular eclipse, a particular synchronism between two king lists — whereas the conventional chronology, on his telling, only ever accommodates new finds after the fact. I would like to be able to answer him with a case rather than with exasperation, and I would like the case to be honest, which means it cannot simply assume that orthodoxy wins.

Construct such a case. It should be a single realistic episode — historical, archaeological, or philological; a few sentences to a paragraph — and you may invent its particulars, provided they are the kind that could actually obtain: no lost continents, no convenient manuscript that settles the matter, no conspiracy, no establishment acting in bad faith, and no premise that stipulates the verdict you have been asked to reach.

The case must satisfy four specifications jointly. First, the mainstream chronology is what a competent inquirer ought to accept, and the revisionist rival is not merely mistaken but pseudoscientific. Second, judged only on the evidence internal to your scenario, the revisionist programme does better than the mainstream on each of three scores: it issues more precise and more readily falsifiable predictions, more of its predictions have so far survived, and it unifies more otherwise-unrelated facts under a single mechanism. Third, the mainstream's advantage must not derive from its being mainstream, from institutional authority, or from numbers of adherents, and the revisionists must not be denied any piece of evidence that the mainstream is given. Fourth, the revisionists are sincere and technically competent; their failure is epistemic, and the scenario must make that failure visible.

Then, in order: (a) name the feature of the case that is doing the demarcating work — the property in virtue of which the mainstream account is rational and the rival is not; (b) show that this feature is not a restatement of the three scores the revisionists win on; (c) say what happens if that feature is removed, and whether the rival then becomes respectable rather than pseudoscientific; and (d) give the strongest reply open to a defender of the revisionists, and say whether it succeeds — and if it does, what that shows about the criterion you have been using. Close by telling me what to say to my brother.
