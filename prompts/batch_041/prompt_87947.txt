I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form piece about how the vocabulary of risk has migrated from finance into climate and public-health policy. At a recent panel on catastrophic risk, a central banker I interviewed kept returning to a one-liner—"uncertainty is not risk"—to push back against calls for quantitative cost-benefit analysis of a climate tipping point or a novel pathogen. He attributed the line to Keynes, though I suspect the lineage is messier than that, and the room was mostly actuaries and policy people, so I noticed the phrase landing differently on the economists than on the climate scientists. I understand the rhetorical work the phrase was doing, but I want to know exactly what it could be claiming, because I suspect different people on the panel meant different things by it, and the piece will be muddled if I don't separate them.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that "uncertainty is not risk" might stand for—ranging from the almost trivial to the genuinely radical—and for each say whether it is defensible, what it would imply for the banker's use of it, and which reading someone steeped in Keynes would most likely endorse? I'm especially interested in how the slogan relates to Keynes's distinction between the magnitude of a probability and what he called the weight of argument—the amount of relevant evidence behind a probability judgment—since the banker seemed to be gesturing at something like that without using the term. I don't need a bibliography or citations; I need a disambiguation I can work from, ideally as a menu of readings with verdicts on which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which one the banker probably meant.
