I administer a state arts council's Enduring Traditions grant, and my two program officers review roughly forty applications a cycle. Applicants must show that their practice — a dance, a craft, a foodway, a festival — is transmitted within a community, but our eligibility rule, which asks for 'documented continuous practice for at least a century,' is doing more harm than good: it excludes practices that lapsed for a generation and were later revived, and it rewards anyone who can attach a single old newspaper clipping. I want a decision procedure my officers can actually run on an application, grounded in the scholarship on invented tradition and imagined communities — on how nations, folkways, and their pedigrees are made, and how recently. It should say what to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, when a claim of antiquity should be treated as a social fact about the applicants rather than a historical one, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to me because it turns on values rather than evidence. Include the questions that separate a genuine revival from a fabricated pedigree, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three applications: a midwinter house-visiting play documented in nineteenth-century sources but not performed between 1932 and 1974, now staged annually by a neighborhood group; a 'centuries-old' harvest festival that a chamber of commerce created in 1998 to draw tourists and that has since become the town's central civic event; and a boat-blessing ceremony a fishing community says it has held without interruption since the 1880s, with the same two families supplying the officiants. State plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where it will return an answer I will not like. Write in American English.
