Last night a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about chapter 25 of Machiavelli's *The Prince*, and we came away disagreeing about what the chapter even says, so I would rather have a careful reading of the passage than another round of the two of us talking past each other. Here is the stretch we kept coming back to (in the translation I've been reading):

"It is not unknown to me how many men have had, and still have, the opinion that the affairs of the world are in such wise governed by fortune and by God that men with their wisdom cannot direct them and that no one can even help them; and because of this they would have us believe that it is not necessary to labour much in affairs, but to let chance govern them. ... Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.

I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her. ...

I believe also that he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful. ... a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed.

Pope Julius II went to work impetuously in all his affairs, and found the times and circumstances conform so well to that line of action that he always met with success. ... but if circumstances had arisen which required him to go cautiously, his ruin would have followed, because he would never have deviated from those ways to which nature inclined him."

My friend's contention is that the chapter holds two accounts of fortune that cannot both be true. On the first, fortune is a natural hazard on the model of a flood: the damage it does is a function of what one has built in advance, so its share in human affairs is not fixed at half but varies with one's foresight, and virtù amounts to nothing more than the prudence to raise dikes in peacetime. On the second, what decides success is whether a man's temperament happens to suit the times, and since a man "cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to," no one can arrange that fit; on that account virtù cannot be foresight at all, and Julius II is an example not of skill but of luck — Machiavelli himself concedes that the Pope prospered only because the times suited his impetuosity, and that he would have been ruined had they called for caution.

What I want is a reading that takes a stand. Are these two doctrines genuinely incompatible, or one doctrine that I am mis-describing? In particular: (i) what does the river analogy commit him to — if the dikes work, in what sense is fortune still "the arbiter of one-half of our actions," and what is the force of the qualification "or perhaps a little less"; (ii) is the virtù that builds dikes the same capacity as the virtù he praises in the earlier chapters on new princes who rise by their own arms, or a thinner notion that has shrunk to mean little more than preparedness; and (iii) can the two be reconciled by reading the "spirit of the times" claim as an epistemic point — that we cannot foresee which times are coming — rather than a point about temperament, and if they cannot be reconciled that way, what view of free will the chapter is actually left with. Argue for one reading rather than surveying the options.
