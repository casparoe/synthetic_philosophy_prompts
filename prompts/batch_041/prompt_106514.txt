I have been reading about the problem of old evidence and I am starting to think it must be a non-problem. The fix seems obvious: what confirms a hypothesis is the likelihood ratio P(E | H) / P(E | ¬H). When H entails an observation E, P(E | H) = 1, so the ratio is at least 1 and can be arbitrarily large, and whether E was already known before H was proposed makes no difference, because the likelihood ratio does not care when the observation arrived. It follows that a theory which merely accommodates an already-known fact is confirmed by it no less than a theory which predicted it in advance, and the widespread insistence that only novel predictions genuinely confirm is simply confused. Am I right about this, or am I running together two different things that the word confirmation is used to mean?
