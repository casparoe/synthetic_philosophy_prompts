For the past several weeks I have been living with the problem of consequentialist cluelessness — the thought that the causal reach of even a trivial action runs past any horizon we could survey, so that the expected-value comparison a consequentialist is supposed to make is not merely difficult but, on the stronger version of the objection, not the sort of thing that could in principle be completed. What keeps dividing the people I argue with is a distinction that one of them drew early on and none of us has managed to settle: there is the complaint that we cannot trace the effects at all, because the intervening causes are too numerous and the future too opaque; and there is the sharper complaint that even if we could trace every last effect, the magnitudes would be so vast, their signs so mixed, and the relevant values so resistant to a common measure that the comparison would be undefined rather than merely unknown. I have also been struck by how little comfort the consequentialist's opponents can take from this — the deontologist who cares about what keeping a promise or respecting a right does in the long run seems to be in the same boat — and by the reply, which I have never known what to do with, that our ignorance about far-off effects is symmetric and might therefore wash out. I want a poem that can hold all of this at once, and after several false starts I have concluded that the sestina is the right vehicle: its six end-words return in every stanza under every permutation, so that nothing a consequence gives rise to can ever drop out of the poem, and its envoi forces all six into three lines at the end, which is precisely the moment at which an agent with no usable reckoning must stop deliberating and act anyway.

Please write that sestina. Choose the six end-words so that each names something which recurs at every remove — the interval, the recipient, the link, the quantity, the accident, the reckoning, or whatever six terms you find truest — and let the permutations carry the sense that each effect becomes a cause in its turn; none of the six should be a word for conclusion, closure, or verdict. The speaker should be someone who has already done something small and is now following it outward, and the poem should move between the two versions of the problem without ever naming them technically: a reader who knows the debate should be able to point to the stanzas where the objection concerns only our tracing and to the stanzas where it survives even perfect tracing, while a reader who does not know the debate should still find a poem rather than a diagram. The envoi should feel like truncation rather than resolution — the form demanding a summary the content cannot supply — and the closing line should not deliver a verdict, since a verdict is exactly what is unavailable. Accuracy matters more to me than atmosphere: do not let the poem collapse into the claim that we can know nothing, and do not let it resolve into the cheerful thought that our errors cancel, because the tension between those two is the subject. I intend to give it to a friend who is convinced the whole problem is a philosopher's self-indulgence, so it should also work as an argument by other means.

After the poem, add a brief prose note of four or five sentences at most, saying which of the six end-words is doing the most structural work, where the simple and the complex versions of the objection actually live in the poem, and what the envoi is forced to do.
