I've been going back and forth with a friend who defends the Chinese and Singaporean systems, and I keep running into the same knot. His line is that a government's right to rule comes from what it delivers -- growth, safety, order, a roof over people's heads -- and that this is what makes it legitimate, not whether anyone voted for it. Fine so far. But then look at the actual machinery, because it doesn't stop there. Ministers' pay and bonuses are pegged to what top private-sector people earn and get cut when the economy sags, so the government literally pays itself by results. There's an annual report-card exercise where it grades itself on growth, jobs, cleanliness and some vague "harmony" number. And he also insists the people running the thing are the best ones, picked by exams and track records, so it isn't luck that it works -- as if they'd earned the right to rule. Yet the same government holds elections it swears are real, talks constantly about what "the people" want, runs consultation exercises, and then keeps laws letting someone be locked up for a couple of years without trial on a minister's say-so if it's "necessary for order" -- which has nothing to do with whether the economy is going well. He says it's all consistent, it's just "good government." I dont see it.

So what does this whole bundle actually presuppose about political legitimacy? Does the pay-for-results part commit them to a purely outcome-based view, where consent and process don't matter at all -- and if so, why bother with the elections and the popular-support talk, are those just information-gathering, or is input-legitimacy sneaking back in through the side door? What about the merit-selection strand: is "we're the qualified ones" a claim about results, or a separate procedural claim about who gets to rule? And the detention power -- does that presuppose something beyond outcomes altogether, like the state's standing authority to define and enforce order no matter how well things are going? Reconstruct the theory of legitimacy the practice only makes sense under, tell me which rival theories it genuinely rules out versus which it's merely compatible with, and say where it's underdetermined so that several theories would license the same behavior. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if we swapped the presupposed view for a straightforwardly democratic one, or for a rights-based one that says some things aren't up for trade against prosperity. I'd rather you separate what's actually settled among people who study this from what's genuinely contested, and not dress up a contested view as the consensus. And write it as continuous prose please -- no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, just the way you'd write it in an email.
