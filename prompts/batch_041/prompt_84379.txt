I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years — the old Overcoming Bias archives, LessWrong, Slate Star Codex and everything downstream of it — and one habit I've absorbed from them is a standing suspicion of intuition pumps. The house line is that a thought experiment is not an argument: it's a machine for making a conclusion feel obvious, and the feeling of obviousness is precisely what a careful person should discount. I've come to think that line is true but useless — it tells you to distrust pumps without telling you, mechanically, what they're doing — and I'm trying to write an essay that replaces it with something sharper. I got into a pointless argument about this at a meetup last month, which is what finally pushed me to write it up. What I have is a sketch, not a draft, and I'd like your comments on the argument rather than the prose. I should say up front that I'm working without a library: don't send me a reading list, and if you think I've reinvented a wheel, name the wheel and argue that mine is worse.

Here is the sketch. I want to distinguish two sorts of feature in any thought experiment: load-bearing features and decorative ones. A feature is load-bearing if varying it, and nothing else, flips the verdict that most readers reach; everything else is decoration, however vivid. The thesis is that pumps work by disguising which is which — either by dressing a load-bearing feature as incidental scenery (the footbridge case's insistence that you push the man with your own hands reads like a staging detail but is carrying the whole verdict), or by piling on decoration until the reader mistakes vividness for argument. From that I want to extract a method I'm calling unpacking the pump: take the case, vary one feature at a time until the verdict flips, identify the feature that does the flipping, and then ask the only question that matters — whether that feature tracks something morally or metaphysically relevant, or whether it's a framing artifact that moves us without giving us a reason to move. I want to run this on the footbridge variant of the trolley problem, Nozick's experience machine, Searle's Chinese room, and Parfit's teletransporter, and to claim that the method sorts the cases that are arguments in disguise from the ones that are merely vivid. Where the trolley cases are concerned, I'd like the method to adjudicate among the usual suspects — personal force, using someone merely as a means, harm intended versus foreseen — rather than letting me pick whichever is easiest to name.

Four things, roughly in order of how much they worry me. First: is load-bearing versus decorative anything more than the truism that some details matter and some don't? If it is, state the difference in a form that survives a hostile reader; if it isn't, tell me now, because the whole essay rests on it. Second: the method looks circular to me and I can't repair it — to know which feature flips the verdict I have to know the verdicts, and the verdicts are the thing under suspicion, so I can't see that unpacking the pump does any work beyond restating my priors in a new vocabulary. Is there a non-circular version, or is the move doomed? Third: actually run the analysis on two of my four cases and tell me whether it illuminates them or merely redescribes them. I would rather be told that the experience machine resists the treatment — that its pull comes from something my distinction can't see — than watch it get forced. Fourth: if this is a well-worn idea in new clothes, say so plainly, and then tell me what the standard version gets right that mine doesn't. I want a genuine steelman of the opposing view, not a courtesy nod.
