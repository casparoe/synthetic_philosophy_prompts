I run a monthly game night at which we play a self-amending rule game of the kind where the rulebook contains rules for changing the rulebook. I write software for a living and have no training in law or philosophy, so I am asking for help with an argument that two of the regular players had after our most recent session. I was present but not a party to it, and I genuinely cannot see who has the better of it.

Our rulebook is short. Numbered here for reference:

Rule 1 — A rule is added, amended or repealed when a motion to that effect is carried by two-thirds of those present and voting.
Rule 2 — Rule 1 may be amended or repealed by a simple majority of those present and voting.
Rule 3 — A rule takes effect at the moment the vote carrying it is closed.
Rule 4 — Rule 4 may be repealed by a simple majority of those present and voting.
Rule 5 — A rule that has not been cited in support of a decision for twelve consecutive sessions lapses at the close of the twelfth such session; a lapsed rule ceases to be in force.
Rule 6 — No rule is applied retrospectively: an act is judged by the rules in force when it was done.

Rules 7 and 9 are ordinary rules with nothing self-referential about them. Twelve of us vote.

What happened:

Session 14 — a motion to repeal Rule 7 fails, 7 for and 5 against, short of two-thirds under Rule 1.
Session 15 — a motion to amend Rule 1, replacing two-thirds with a simple majority, carries 7 to 5 under Rule 2.
Session 16 — the motion to repeal Rule 7 is put again and carries 7 to 5 under Rule 1 as amended.
Session 17 — a motion to repeal Rule 2 carries 7 to 5, and then a motion to amend Rule 1 back to two-thirds carries 7 to 5, both under Rule 1 as it stood at that moment.
Session 18 — a motion to repeal Rule 4 carries 8 to 4, which clears two-thirds anyway.
Separately — Rule 5 was never cited in any decision in sessions 1 to 12 and nobody noticed; from session 13 onwards we cited it to strike out two rules, one of them Rule 9. A penalty imposed under Rule 9 is now being appealed, so it matters whether Rule 9 is still in force.

The argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marek: every one of those votes was carried by a rule that was in force when it was carried, and Rule 2 expressly says Rule 1 can be changed by a simple majority. Entrenchment lasts exactly as long as the rulebook says it lasts, and a purpose is not a rule. Rule 4's repeal is the same story: Rule 4 was in force when the session 18 vote closed, so the repeal is valid. Rule 5, by contrast, lapsed itself at the close of session 12, so the citations from session 13 onwards were made under a rule no longer in force; Rule 9 therefore never lapsed and the penalty must go.

(2) Ines: a rulebook cannot lift itself by its own bootstraps. Rule 2 cannot authorise a majority to dismantle the protection in Rule 1, because Rule 1 sets the threshold for changing rules and its protection covers itself; where the two conflict the protective provision must govern, otherwise entrenchment means nothing, since every entrenched rule could be removed by the very majority it was written to restrain. The same objection disposes of Rule 4: at the moment the repeal takes effect the authority for it is gone, so the act has no author. And on Rule 5 she says a rule cannot bring about its own lapse, so Rule 5 is still in force, the citations from session 13 were valid, and Rule 9 has lapsed, penalty and all.

(3) Marek: that proves too much. If any rule can be defeated by pointing at the purpose it was meant to serve, then nothing is settled by the rulebook and every vote can be challenged by whoever dislikes its result. Rule 2 may well have been put there deliberately, as a safety valve; if the players did not want it they should not have written it. He adds that Ines's two objections cannot both stand: she says the session 15 amendment is void because the amending rule conflicted with the rule it changed, yet she says Rule 4's repeal is void because the authority for it disappeared — but Rule 4 was in force when the vote closed, exactly as Rule 2 was, so presence at the time cannot be enough in one case and not the other. And in any event she is drawing two different principles, one about conflict and one about self-application, neither of which is written down anywhere.

(4) Ines: they are one principle in two guises. The rule that authorises a change must stand apart from the change and must not be contradicted by it. Rule 2 is at the same level as Rule 1 and contradicts it, so it cannot do the work; Rule 4 is the object of its own act, so it cannot do the work either. She concedes that this means the rulebook is not self-sufficient, and says that is simply true of rulebooks: the players stand behind them and must patch them by agreement when they contradict themselves.

Please adjudicate this point by point rather than overall. Specifically:

(i) Rate each of the four turns on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and within each turn say which claims carry the weight and which are decoration.
(ii) Give verdicts, with reasons, on: whether the session 15 to 17 sequence is valid; whether the repeal of Rule 4 stands; whether Rule 9 is in force; and whether Rule 5 lapses itself, and if it cannot, whether and when it lapses now that it has been cited.
(iii) Say what each of them is missing, and identify the one distinction or principle that would settle most of the rest if both accepted it.
(iv) Say which of the two is closer to right overall.
(v) Where the rulebook is silent or contradictory, say so explicitly, and distinguish the questions that have a determinate answer from those that are a choice.
(vi) If you bring in a distinction from legal theory or philosophy, state it in your own words and explain what work it does here. I have no background in this and cannot look anything up, so please give no reading list, citations or references, and please do not tell me the paradox dissolves without saying what the dissolution is and what it costs. Write in British English.
