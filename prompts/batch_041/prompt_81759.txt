I am applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy and need a two-page research proposal on a question in moral theory. My half-formed idea is this. In the debate between moral particularists and moral generalists, the central disagreement is usually put as a disagreement about valence: particularists hold that a consideration that counts in favor of an action in one situation can count against a relevantly similar action in another, while generalists hold that when a consideration appears to switch sides, it is either a different consideration or a different situation. What neither side tends to do is say what makes two appearances count as one and the same consideration in the first place, and that looks load-bearing to me. Holism about reasons is a substantive thesis only if the consideration that favors in one case and opposes in another is genuinely one and the same consideration. A generalist can absorb almost any apparent switch by individuating reasons finely, so that this kindness and that kindness become two reasons rather than one; a particularist can resist by individuating coarsely. Unless individuation is constrained, holism comes out trivially true or trivially false depending on how we count, and the debate threatens to be verbal.

Please turn this into a proposal. The question I want made precise and then pursued is this: is there a non-arbitrary way to individuate moral reasons that leaves the holism of reasons a substantive, contestable claim rather than an artifact of how finely we slice? I suspect the pressure point is the requirement that a reason be able to recur. Defeat and switching only make sense if the reason that is defeated or that changes sides is the same reason across the cases, so any individuation criterion has to preserve sameness while still permitting genuine switches. Coarse criteria make switching too easy and holism vacuous; fine criteria make switching impossible and holism false; the interesting work is in whether there is a defensible middle and where it sits.

The proposal should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open, given that both sides tend to assume an account of individuation rather than argue for one; and say why this angle is better than the obvious alternatives, such as adjudicating more of the stock cases or simply defending one side on intuitive grounds. Give me a concrete method I could actually follow, roughly month by month across the year. I would want to assemble a stock of the valence-switch cases both sides rely on and, for each, catalog which individuations make it a switch and which do not; formulate several candidate criteria, for instance individuating reasons by the feature they cite, by the practical response they recommend, by the argument they figure in, or by their role in a defeasible inference; and test each candidate against the stock, asking whether it yields the verdicts its proponents need without collapsing holism or making it vacuous. Then say what would count as the project succeeding and what would count as it failing. I take success to require a criterion that is independently motivated, that makes holism a thesis which could turn out false, and that preserves the recurrence of reasons so that defeat and switching stay intelligible; I would want to treat as a genuine result the deflationary outcome in which no such criterion can be found and the debate as usually posed rests on an equivocation. Be honest about scope: the project is conceptual and needs no empirical work, but the case stock could balloon, and there is a real risk of gerrymandering the criterion to fit the verdicts, so say in advance how I would guard against that and whether the project is too large for a year.
