A friend of mine keeps running this argument at me and I can't tell if it's airtight or if he's cheating somewhere. Here it is:

(1) Every probability claim about a particular case -- that this coin will come up heads, that this patient will recover, that it'll rain tomorrow -- has to rest on how often outcomes like this happen in some reference class of similar cases.

(2) Any particular case belongs to endlessly many such classes (coins / coins I've tossed / coins tossed on a Tuesday / coins of this exact weight), and they give different frequencies.

(3) Nothing non-arbitrary singles out the one right class. Narrowest class, most causally homogeneous class, class containing all the relevant evidence -- each either has obvious counterexamples or just quietly assumes we already know which facts are relevant.

(4) So the probability isn't a fact about the case; its an artifact of which class you happened to pick.

(5) And none of this is special to messy cases, since a coin toss is itself a particular case, and "fair coins" is just one class you could file it under.

(6) So there's no fact of the matter even about a fair coin being 50/50. As far as probability goes it's on a par with a double-headed coin.

(7) (6) is obviously false, so one of (1)-(5) is broken.

I'd honestly rather keep (1) and dump something else -- I think (1) is basically right and that most of what we say about probability is looser than we like to admit -- but I can't swallow (6). So which step goes, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? And tell me what would change your mind about your diagnosis: if there's a version of the argument that survives, or a case where the step you're defending clearly fails, that's the part I most want to hear.
