hey, i've been trying to write a short piece for my reading group that pulls apart two ways of stating the alignment problem, and i'd like your comments before i send it round. dont worry about style, i mostly want to know if the argument holds up and where it's sloppy. here's the draft:

---

Everyone agrees the alignment problem is about making AI systems do what we want. The slogan hides a fork.

On one reading, alignment is a relation between a system and a principal's intentions: the system is aligned if it's trying to do what its operator actually meant to ask for, as opposed to what the operator literally typed, or what the reward model happened to reward. Call this intent alignment. On the other reading, alignment is a relation between a system's behavior and what is actually good or valuable for people — the system is aligned if it does the right thing, whether or not anybody intended that. Call this value alignment.

I want to defend the claim that only the first deserves to be called the alignment problem, and that the second is either a different problem or a way of hiding a moral theory inside an engineering term.

Three reasons. First, tractability. Intent alignment is a relation you can check against a principal who exists. You can ask the operator what they meant; you can study the cases where the intent and the specifcation come apart (someone asks for the dishes done and gets teh plates thrown out). Value alignment requires a determinate answer to what's good, which we don't have and which no amount of ML progress will supply.

Second, the narrow problem is where the interesting failure modes live. Deceptive alignment, specification gaming, reward tampering — these are all cases of a system not doing what the principal intended. They're diagnoses of a gap between intent and behavior, not of a gap between behavior and The Good.

Third, the broad reading tends to smuggle in a particular moral view while sounding neutral. If you say the AI should do what's valuable, you've already picked a side in every dispute about what's valuable, but you get to keep the word alignment, which sounds like it's only about not being broken.

The objection I keep running into: an intent-aligned system can still kill everyone, if the operator intended something catastrophic or just didn't think it through. True — but I think that's a feature of the distinction, not a bug. It shows catastrophic risk isn't the same thing as misalignment, and that a lot of what gets called alignment is really about the wisdom of the people giving the orders.

So: narrow is the engineering problem, broad is the ethics problem, and conflating them makes both harder.

---

ok, tear it apart. in particular:

(1) is the intent/value fork a real distinction or am i just redividing the same thing with new labels?

(2) is the tractability argument doing any work, or is it just "intent is easier to pin down because we get to make it up"?

(3) does the third reason beg the question against anyone who thinks alignment is just irreducibly a normative notion?

(4) is the closing move — saying catastrophic risk isn't misalignment — too cute? if the narrow problem is the only one we can state precisely, does that actually mean it's the one that matters?

and if you think the whole framing collapses, say so plainly and tell me which of the two readings you'd keep.
