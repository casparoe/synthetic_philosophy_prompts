My reading group has spent two sessions on the Great Divergence, and a friend there has convinced me that the coal explanation is dead on arrival. Her argument is simple. An explanation of why industrialisation took hold in Britain and not in China has to identify something that Britain had and China lacked; coal cannot serve, because China had coal too, and a great deal of it. Coal is therefore at best a background condition, present on both sides, and the real cause of the divergence has to be sought elsewhere. I find the principle behind her objection compelling, and I have been trying to follow it where it leads.

Two things now trouble me, and I would like them settled before I commit to a position. The first is what a correct account of the divergence looks like once coal is off the table: whether the work is done by some institutional or cultural difference, or whether the form of answer I am looking for is itself misconceived, and if so in what respect. The second is whether the principle, taken seriously, collapses two literatures into one. If the explanation of a difference must be a factor present in one case and absent in the other, then whatever explains why Britain industrialised also explains why China did not, and the Industrial Revolution and the Great Divergence are one question asked twice; I would like to know whether that is right, and if it is not, precisely where the inference fails. I am not after a survey of the literature; I want to know what shape a good answer could take.
