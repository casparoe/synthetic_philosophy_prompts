So I was at dinner with my old flatmates last weekend and got stuck in the middle of an argument I still can't settle, and it's been bothering me all week. The story: our friend Dev used to manage a guy called Ali. Ali is genuinely brilliant technically, but about a year ago he had a blow-up with a client on a call, and after that two teammates refused to work with him, so he got quietly moved off the project and then left. Ali is now interviewing elsewhere and the hiring manager phoned Dev for a reference and asked him straight out, 'Is there anything I should know?' Dev said Ali's code was always clean, that he hit every date, and that he was one of the sharpest people he'd worked with. All true. He didn't mention the client thing or the teammates. The manager said that was really helpful, and Ali got the offer. Dev told us the story like it was a mildly funny thing that happened to him. Anyways, Nadia and Marcus went at it for about forty minutes and I couldnt work out who was winning, so I'm handing it to you.

Here's how it went. (1) Nadia says Dev lied. Her point: the question he was asked was 'is there anything I should know', and he answered it with a true sentence chosen precisely because it would make the manager believe something false -- that there was nothing to know. She says making someone believe what isn't so is what lying is for, and if the vehicle happens to be a true sentence, that's a detail about the vehicle, not about what he did. She also says it's worse than a plain lie, not better, because he can now say 'I never said he was easy to get along with', and that built-in escape hatch is exactly the trick. (2) Marcus says that's far too loose. Lying is saying something false; what Dev did was decline to volunteer something, and the manager drew an inference off her own question. He says if implicatures count as lies then all sorts of innocent stuff becomes lying -- 'I'd rather not get into it' when you're getting divorced, a doctor saying 'let's keep an eye on it', answering 'how are you' with 'fine' when you're miserable. He also brought up something he'd read about, an old Catholic casuistry idea called mental reservation: you say words that are true and silently add a qualification in your head, and the argument was that since the sentence you mean is true, you haven't lied. He says he's not defending it -- the Jesuits got a reputation for that sort of thing and Pascal spent a whole series of letters making fun of the whole business -- but that even that doctrine required the words themselves to be true in some sense, whereas Dev's words were true outright, so Dev's position is stronger than the casuists', not weaker. (3) Nadia said that just shows the doctrine was a machine for having it both ways, a lie with a receipt. (4) Marcus came back with: fine, Dev behaved shabbily, but calling it a lie flattens the difference between Dev and someone who simply says 'Ali is great with clients, no issues at all', which is flatly false. He says that difference is real and any theory that erases it is broken. (5) Nadia said she'll grant the difference exists but asked which way it cuts: the guy who says the false sentence is at least playing the game straight, you know he's selling you something and you can check. Dev built himself a defence. And she said Marcus's fallback -- that the manager should have asked a better question -- amounts to saying that the more skilfully you misdirect someone, the less it counts against you, which is backwards. Marcus said the manager had a phone and two other references to call, so she wasn't exactly defenceless, and Nadia said great, so now it's her fault for taking a direct answer to a direct question at face value.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right on each of the five, not just who wins overall? Then the thing I actually care about: is there a real moral difference between lying and misleading someone with a true sentence, or is that distinction just somewhere for people like Dev to hide? If you think there is a difference, say what it rests on and whether it makes what Dev did better or worse. And tell me what both of them are missing, because I have a suspicion the arguement they're having isn't the arguement they should be having.
