I've been chewing on this for a while — the argument below is one I assembled partly from a seminar discussion and partly on my own, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a good argument that I should accept, or a bad one with a step I'm not seeing. That's the whole reason I'm writing.

Here's the argument. (1) For an agent to be rational just is for her credences to be coherent — to obey the probability axioms — because a credence function that violates them is vulnerable to a Dutch book, and being exploitable like that is what irrationality amounts to. (2) Coherence requires logically equivalent propositions to receive the same credence; you can't coherently be more confident that P than that P-or-Q-where-that's-trivially-true, and you can't coherently be more confident of P than of something logically equivalent to P. (3) Now take any open problem — say some conjecture in number theory nobody's settled. The conjecture, its double negation, its conjunction with an unrelated tautology, the disjunction of it with a contradiction: these are all logically equivalent. By (2), a coherent agent must give them all the same credence. (4) Extending the same point, coherence requires probability 1 in every logical truth and 0 in every contradiction, and equal credences across every equivalence class of logically equivalent sentences. (5) That is just logical omniscience. (6) But no real agent is logically omniscient — working mathematicians are uncertain whether the conjecture is true, and that uncertainty is not a defect. (7) So either every real agent is incoherent and hence irrational, or something in the run from (1) to (5) is wrong. I don't want to give up (1) — the Dutch book story is the best thing anyone's offered for what makes credences rational, and I'm not ready to say coherence isn't the standard. But (7)'s first horn is absurd: if being unsure whether an open conjecture is true makes you irrational, then rationality is impossible for anyone who doesn't already know all the mathematics, and the entire project of modeling an agent who reasons its way toward the truth over time as *rational* — updating credences as it proves things, growing more confident as evidence accumulates — would be incoherent from the start, which can't be right, since that's exactly the kind of agent we are. So which step do I reject? Name the premise, say why it looked compelling in the first place, and then tell me what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether I can keep the Dutch book justification for coherence in some form, or whether the fix forces me to weaken what "rational" means for credences, and if it does, whether there's anything left of the claim that coherence is *the* requirement. I care more about keeping (1) than about keeping the strong reading of (2), but I want to see the price tag either way.
