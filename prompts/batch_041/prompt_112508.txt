I'm in the final stretch of a seminar on the epistemology of perception, and we've just spent two sessions on the bootstrapping problem, which I think I understand in the abstract but keep losing my grip on the moment I try to state it precisely. The worry, as I have it, is this: if perceptual experience gives you justification for ordinary beliefs about the world without your first having to justify the reliability of perception, then you can parlay a string of particular perceptions into inductive evidence that perception is reliable — using the very faculty whose credentials are in question. Concretely, I glance at a table and believe it's red, I note that it looks red to me, and I treat the match as one small datum in favor of my color vision working; I do the same with the next object and the next, and before long I appear to have accumulated evidence that my eyes can be trusted, evidence I could never have gathered without already trusting my eyes. Our professor calls this bootstrapping, because the conclusion is supposed to lift itself by its own bootstraps, and someone in the back row said it was less like lifting and more like a dog chasing its tail. What nags at me is that the procedure looks illegitimate and yet I can't say exactly where the illegitimacy enters — and, relatedly, that a reliabilist seems unable simply to wave it away, since the view grants knowledge from a reliable process without demanding that the subject already possess evidence of that reliability. I'd like to see the structure of the thing rendered rather than merely described, which is why I'm asking for a poem.

Could you write a pantoum about the bootstrapping problem? The pantoum's interlocking lines — each quatrain reusing lines from the one before, the whole poem closing by returning to where it began — seem to me to enact precisely the circularity I'm after: the poem should be unable to establish anything except by leaning on what it is trying to establish, and the form should let a reader feel the loop rather than be told about it. Please let the two recurring lines carry the two halves of the bootstrap — one a first-order perceptual report (something like "the table is red"), the other the reliability claim that is supposed to be the conclusion (something like "my eyes are working") — and arrange the repetitions so that by the final quatrain each line has been made to shore up the other, with the closing lines doubling back to the opening so that reading the poem again would restart the very inference it just performed. I'd like it accurate enough that a philosopher of perception would find nothing to wince at — it should convey why the procedure feels like cheating and why the obvious escape routes (insisting the conclusion was never really in doubt, or that the evidence is merely permissive) don't fully satisfy — and yet a reader who has never heard of the problem should find it a poem worth rereading. Meter and rhyme are up to you; what matters is that the pantoum's machinery does the philosophical work rather than merely decorating it.
