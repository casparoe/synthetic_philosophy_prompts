I'm in this little reading group that's been working through some AI-safety material, and last week a guy in the group -- econ PhD, reads all the blogs, very confident -- gave us his take on the whole "intent alignment vs. value alignment" thing, and I wrote it down because it sounded clean but also felt slightly off and I couldn't put my finger on why. Here's basically what he said: "There are two seperate problems people keep mushing together. Intent alignment is making the AI do what you literally tell it to -- it's basically the obedience problem, the monkey's-paw thing where you get exactly what you asked for and it ruins you. Value alignment is the real, hard problem: making the AI do what's actually good. The 'narrow' definition of the alignment problem just says alignment means intent alignment, i.e. getting the thing to obey, full stop. Which is kind of a dodge, because it lets people wave off the question of whose values the AI should serve by saying 'that's not an alignment question, that's a politics question.' But it's not a dodge that actually works, since an AI that's perfectly intent-aligned to some guy with terrible values is still a catastrophe, so the narrow framing buys you nothing."

I nodded along but two things bugged me. First, I'd always assumed intent alignment was almost the opposite of "does what you literally said" -- that the whole point of the word "intent" was that you want it to do what you meant, not what you said. Second, I don't think the narrow definition is supposed to claim that a badly-valued but intent-aligned system is safe, or that the value question doesn't matter at all; I thought it was more about scoping which problem you're calling "alignment" versus which you're calling something else. Can you go through his summary point by point and tell me what someone actually working on this would agree with, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And for whatever's wrong, does his final conclusion -- that the narrow framing "buys you nothing" -- still go through once you fix the mistakes, or does it only land because of them? I'm inclined to think the distinction is real and useful and that he's being unfair to it, but I definately don't trust my instincts here.
