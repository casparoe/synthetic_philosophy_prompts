You are a philosopher of history and of historical explanation, with a particular feel for the unglamorous work of locating the exact joint at which a plausible argument dislocates. I have an argument that a colleague in my historiography seminar has been pressing on us, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a deep result or a trick of phrasing. Help me diagnose it.

Start by restating the argument in your own words and marking which steps are load-bearing. Then, before you commit to any verdict, brainstorm the two or three most plausible places a flaw might hide, and say for each what would have to be true for that to be the real trouble. Only after that should you tell me which step you would reject, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. Think slowly, resist the first reading that comes to mind, and if the argument turns out to be valid, say so plainly and identify the least costly premise to give up.

The argument, as he states it:

(1) Every historical explanation must select, from an indefinitely rich past, the conditions that actually produced the outcome to be explained.
(2) To explain an outcome is to show how it arose from its antecedents; explanation therefore runs forward in time, from earlier conditions to the later outcome.
(3) When a historian narrates the past by running the story forward from conditions to outcome, she is treating the outcome as the point toward which the story was moving.
(4) A history that treats a later outcome as the point toward which earlier events were moving is a Whig history: it reads the past as the road to the present.
(5) Therefore all history is Whig history.
(6) The standing objection to Whig history is that it distorts — that it evaluates the past by present-day standards and imposes a narrative of inevitable progress.
(7) But a distortion that follows from the very nature of explanation, and that no historian can avoid, is not really a distortion.
(8) So the standing objection to Whig history is empty, and no historian need feel the guilt it was designed to induce.

He calls the conclusion 'pan-Whiggism' and treats (1), (2), and (7) as truisms. I, for my part, want to keep (6): I believe Whig history is a genuine vice, and I am unwilling to accept (8). Almost everything else I would trade away, but I want to know the price. Which step should I reject — is it invalid, does some term quietly change meaning between steps, or is a premise simply false? If several repairs are open to me, lay them out and tell me which one best preserves the claims I care about.
