I've somehow been volunteered to give the after-dinner speech at our land trust's twenty-fifth anniversary dinner next month — ten minutes, maybe twelve, to a room of about sixty people — and I want to spend it defending the weird clause we stuck in our charter a couple of years back, because its been quietly causing fights on the board and I'd rather air it in public than sit through another three-hour meeting about it. The clause says, more or less, that trustees may discount a future benefit for the ordinary reasons — we don't know whether the people will be there, we don't know whether our projects will pan out, and money we don't spend now can be invested and grow — but they may not discount a benefit merely because it lands later in time. A unit of good done in 2225 counts the same as a unit of good done this year; no pure time preference, no impatience, nothing. I'm pretty sure I think that's right. The trouble is the audience. Half of them are our bigger donors, several of them economists, and the moment they hear 'no pure time preference' they start explaining the Ramsey formula at me — the social discount rate is pure impatience plus a growth term, they say, and you can't just toss out the first part, and anyway the split between 'pure' and 'impure' discounting is a fiction you learn in grad school and then stop believing. The other half are the volunteers and the folks whose families have farmed here for generations; they've never heard of any of this and mostly want to know why on earth anyone would ever think the future counts for less in the first place.

So what I want from you is the actual speech. Make the philosophy do the work — really take apart what discounting is, say honestly whether caring less about later goods is a defensible preference or more like the bias we have toward our own near future over our far future, and show why the charter's clean division between the two kinds of discounting is or isn't something an economist can just wave away. It has to be genuinely good for both halves of the room: the economists shouldn't feel condescended to or fobbed off, and the volunteers shouldn't feel like they wandered into a seminar. And please don't just be diplomatic with me. If the distinction the charter rests on doesn't actually hold up, or if there's no honest version of this speech that also keeps the peace at a birthday party, tell me straight and tell me what I should say instead — I'd much rather find out from you now than in front of everyone.
