hey, so i'm a high school student and i run the philosophy club at my school (we're small, like seven people, and mostly we argue about free will and whether we actually have it). We've been doing a unit on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and our teacher gave us a practice essay that was literally just, explain Frankfurt cases and whether they refute PAP. Everyone wrote the same thing about the guy with the chip in his brain and the counterfactual intervener, and it was super boring. I definately want to write a better exam question for our end-of-unit mock, one that actually makes people think and can't be answered by reciting the standard story.

Here's the twist I want to use: instead of the usual neuroscientist who flips a switch, imagine a case where a person is choosing between two options, and there's a device that only kicks in if they're about to choose the second option. But the device doesn't make them choose the first option -- it just makes the second option feel slightly nauseating, so they drift back to the first. The person chooses the first option and says afterward that they chose it because they wanted to. I want the exam question to force students to seperate two versions of PAP: (a) you could have done otherwise, and (b) you could have chosen otherwise, and to say whether the case refutes either, both, or neither. It should also make them address the objection that the person still has some alternative -- like, they could have kept trying to choose the second thing even if it felt gross. Please write the exam question (for a 45-minute in-class essay, 20 marks), a model answer of about 450-500 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically get each wrong. Keep the whole thing under 900 words. And don't make it about memorizing names or citations -- we don't have internet in the club room, and anyway the point is the argument.
