A small foundation has funding for roughly six researchers for three years to work on foundational questions about computation and the brain, in the tradition opened by von Neumann's posthumously published Computer and the Brain (1958), the unfinished Silliman lectures. Rather than a survey of that literature, I want a defended agenda. Consider three candidate lines of work the manuscript leaves unresolved: first, the representation question, whether neural signals are best modeled as a small-alphabet code combined combinatorially, as continuous analog quantities, or as some statistical mixture of the two, and what would settle it; second, the reliability question, how a system of slow, low-precision, unreliable components achieves reliable computation, and whether von Neumann's redundancy and multiplexing arguments still constrain modern accounts of neural coding; third, the architecture question, whether the brain is usefully described as an addressable stored-program machine or whether its memory and control are better captured by distributed, non-addressable schemes. Which of these should the foundation prioritize, in what order, and why? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: which is upstream of which, which is tractable for theorists without new lab data, and which is most neglected. Say what evidence over three years would move each up or down the list, and be willing to say that one of them does not deserve the effort at all. I am less interested in the list than in the reasoning, and I do not want citations or reading lists.
