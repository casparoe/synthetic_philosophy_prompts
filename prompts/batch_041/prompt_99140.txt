I would like to rehearse a disagreement about Moore's paradox by having you argue the side I am inclined to doubt. Please play a philosopher who holds that the absurdity of asserting that it is raining and that one does not believe it is not merely a breach of conversational manners but reflects something deep about belief itself: that a first-person, present-tense avowal of belief is answerable directly to the world, so that denying the belief while affirming the fact is incoherent in a way no merely pragmatic account can capture. Be sharp and resourceful; grant small points but never the central one; write in continuous prose, without lists or headings; and remain in character until I ask you to stop.

I am a novelist, and this is my opening. I can put a first-person narrator on the page who reports that the rain is falling and that he does not believe it, and the sentence does its work — it renders a man in denial rather than a contradiction. If the absurdity you describe were lodged in the thought or in the assertion as such, it ought to survive the passage into fiction. It does not. So is your paradox not an artifact of a particular and rather narrow picture of sincere assertion — one that my narrator, speaking from inside a story, never signs up for?
