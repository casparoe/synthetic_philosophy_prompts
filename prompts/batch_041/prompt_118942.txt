I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether brain size has any moral relevance, and I came away no longer sure that my own position is even coherent. Before I write it up for a reading group, I want an audit of what I actually believe rather than a defence of it: which of the commitments below genuinely pull against one another, which merely look as though they do, how much damage each real tension does, and what the cheapest repair is in each case.

Here is where I stand.

1. Moral standing supervenes on psychological capacities — on a creature's having a life that can go better or worse for it — and not on species membership, DNA, or any other bare biological fact. Species boundaries are morally arbitrary.

2. That capacity is realised by nervous systems and comes in degrees: the richer the information processing a system supports, the more there is, morally speaking, to weigh. Below some level there is nothing to weigh at all.

3. The best available natural measure of that processing power is the number of neurons, and in particular the number in the structure that does the integrating, not total brain mass and not any ratio of brain to body mass. I take the allometric point seriously: mass mostly tracks body size, which is why the fact that men's brains are on average larger than women's, or adults' larger than children's, shows nothing by itself.

4. When I look at the counts, our position is unimpressive. I am told that elephants are far ahead of us on total neurons, with the great majority of theirs in the cerebellum; that at least one cetacean comes out ahead of us on cortical count; and that crows and their relatives pack primate-like numbers into a forebrain organised on an entirely different plan. I do not remember the figures and do not need them. What I take from this is that where we sit in the ranking is a contingent fact of natural history and not a moral one.

5. Because standing is graded, the weight a creature's interests carry is graded too; but above a certain point a being is inviolable, in the sense that it may not be used merely as a means.

6. Whatever else I say, a newborn, a person with advanced dementia, and a person with a severe intellectual disability have exactly the standing I do. My friend's reductio was that this is flatly inconsistent with 2 and 3: if standing tracks neurons, then the infant, the person losing neurons to disease, and the person with a smaller or differently wired brain all rank below me, and if men's brains are on average larger, so do women. My reply was that the allometric point in 3 disposes of the last case and that 6 is not negotiable. He said I was having it both ways, and I have not been able to show that he is wrong.

7. I am also a consequentialist about the aggregate: what matters in the end is the total, and the total is roughly the sum over individuals of their individual weight. I care far more about one human life than about one chicken's, and I do not know what to do with the fact that there are vastly more chickens than people.

One thing I keep worrying about, possibly for no reason: 5 puts a threshold on a scale, and I am not sure that a threshold imposed on a graded quantity can be anything but arbitrary.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the animal-ethics literature and not a reading list. Tell me which pairs among 1 through 7 actually conflict and which are only apparently in conflict; order the real conflicts by how much damage they do; for each, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. If the friend's reductio has force once the allometric reply is subtracted, say so plainly and say which of 2, 3 or 6 has to move — but I would rather be told that the problem is somewhere I have not thought to look.
