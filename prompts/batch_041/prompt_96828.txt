Last night I had a long argument with a friend about higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views on which a mental state counts as conscious just in case a suitable higher-order representation of it is present — and we reached an impasse over a case neither of us could settle. Imagine a creature whose first-order state is a state of hearing a bell, while its higher-order representation represents that state as one of seeing red; consider also the variant in which the higher-order representation is merely indeterminate, representing the creature as being in some mental state or other without getting the kind right. If the higher-order representation is what confers consciousness, what does the creature consciously experience? Does the answer depend on whether the higher-order representation is occurrent or merely dispositional, and on whether it is itself conscious? My friend takes the case to force a dilemma: either the higher-order representation must itself be conscious, in which case the account is threatened by a regress, or it need not be, in which case it is unclear what it adds to a first-order account. I suspect the deeper difficulty is that the view has no adequate account of how a higher-order representation fixes the fine-grained phenomenal character of the state it targets. Write an essay adjudicating this. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, and defend a verdict — either take one horn of the dilemma, or argue that it is false and say what the theory should maintain instead. I don't want a survey of the standard objections; I want to know which ones actually land, and why the others don't. Please write in British English.
