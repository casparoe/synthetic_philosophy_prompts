I'm a teaching fellow for a graduate seminar in epistemology, and next week I'm running the session on the lottery and preface paradoxes. The instructor wants to spend the time on candidate solutions, but before I can judge any of them I want a clean statement of what a theory in this area is supposed to deliver. So please write me the desiderata: the cases an adequate account of rational acceptance has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the structural results it has to live with, each one accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it. Two of the cases, so we're on the same page: in a fair lottery with a very large number of tickets it seems rational to accept of each ticket that it will lose, yet not rational to accept that every ticket loses; and an author who has carefully checked a long manuscript may rationally accept each of its claims while also rationally accepting, on inductive grounds, that at least one of them is false. I want more than those two puzzles, though — the list should include whatever else a theory of acceptance turns out to be answerable to, and my instructor's view is that every proposed solution buys off one requirement in order to save another, so I'd like the requirements laid out before the bargaining starts.

I've been floating candidate requirements of my own and I'd like you to sort them. (1) The theory must preserve the rationality of accepting each member of a large body of independently probable claims while refusing their conjunction. (2) Likewise for the lottery case. (3) Acceptance must be closed under single-premise entailment: accepting A, and accepting that A entails B, yields acceptance of B. (4) Belief must not turn on a sharp numerical cutoff — or if there is one, the theory must explain why its exact location isn't an embarrassment. (5) Acceptance must be the sort of thing one can adopt or abandon voluntarily, unlike a credence. (6) The theory must locate the trouble in one of the puzzle's premises rather than declaring the puzzle a pseudo-problem. (7) Whatever one accepts may serve as a premise in practical deliberation, even when the stakes are high. (8) The account must extend to groups — a jury, a research team — whose members each accept consistently while the union of their acceptances is inconsistent. For each of these, tell me whether it is a genuine requirement or a parochial demand of one camp, and why. Most of all I want the incompatibilities: if some subset of the requirements cannot be satisfied together, say which subset, and what forces the choice.

Then, briefly, how do three broad families fare against the list: the view that belief just is credence above a threshold, the view that we should give up binary acceptance in favor of graded credence, and the view that acceptance is a distinct, voluntary, context-sensitive attitude that is not simply high probability?
