I'm in a political philosophy reading group — we've been working through distributive justice, mostly the standard stuff, and this week a friend circulated a short write-up on prioritarianism to get us going. She's sharp and usually reliable, but reading it I got this nagging sense that something's off, and I don't know the literature well enough to put my finger on what. Here's the gist of what she wrote, roughly as she put it:

"Prioritarianism says we should give priority to the worse off. It's basically a compromise between utilitarianism and egalitarianism: like the utilitarian, it thinks only how much wellbeing people have matters; like the egalitarian, it thinks how that wellbeing is spread around matters too, so a benefit to someone badly off counts for more than the identical benefit to someone already comfortable. Formally it just is utilitarianism with a concave transformation of everyone's wellbeing — diminishing marginal utility — so whether you call yourself a prioritarian or a utilitarian is a matter of taste. The view was invented to escape the leveling-down objection that sinks egalitarianism, but it doesn't escape it: because it weights the worse off more heavily, it ends up caring about the gap, and so it still tells you to pull the better off down even when nobody benefits. Take one person at 10 units of wellbeing and another at 100: prioritarianism says cutting the second down to 50 — where nobody gains a thing — makes the world better, because the gap shrinks. So it collapses back into egalitarianism, and both are refuted by the plain fact that leveling down helps no one."

I'd like an audit of this rather than a general essay on prioritarianism. Which bits would a careful prioritarian accept, which are defensible simplifications of a real position, and which are just wrong or attribute to the view something nobody actually holds? Two spots bother me in particular. First, the claim that it's really just utilitarianism with a concave utility function and that the labels are a matter of taste — I feel like I've heard that line before and it sounds too quick, but I can't say why. Second, the leveling-down example: my instinct is that no prioritarian would rank cutting the better-off person from 100 to 50 as an improvement, but if that's right then I don't understand why the leveling-down objection is supposed to trouble anyone at all, or who it's actually aimed at. And if prioritarianism does dodge it, does that make it the better view, or does it just move the trouble somewhere else? Go through the summary claim by claim, say what a defender would say in reply, and tell me which parts I can safely repeat in group without getting corrected. (The group meets in a cafe that's always too loud, so I'd rather not be the one shouting something false.)
