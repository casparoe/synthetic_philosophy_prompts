I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have only lately begun reading philosophy, so I hope you will bear with a question that may betray my inexperience. I have been puzzling over a result in the literature on imprecise probabilities that is usually called dilation. As I understand the example: two quantities, X and Y, are each standard normal with mean zero, and their correlation is unknown to me, though I am certain it lies somewhere between −1 and 1. Because Y has mean zero no matter what the correlation turns out to be, my imprecise expectation for Y is exactly 0. Now suppose I learn that X equals 2. The conditional expectation of Y given X = 2 is twice the correlation, and since the correlation could be anything from −1 to 1, my imprecise conditional expectation for Y becomes the interval from −2 to 2. Observing X has thus turned a single point into a broad interval; learning appears to have made me less certain about Y. Please explain, as intuitively as you can, what is actually going on in this example, why it is not an arithmetic slip or a misuse of the formalism, and whether it genuinely counts against the interpretation of imprecise credences as a measure of an agent's ignorance. If you believe that interpretation survives, explain how. Please answer in American English.
