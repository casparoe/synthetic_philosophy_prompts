I'm writing a piece for a collection on the doctrine of double effect, and I've decided it should be a satire rather than an essay. Please draft it for me.

The piece is a letter. Its author is the chair of the clinical ethics committee of an invented hospital trust, writing to the trust's clinical directors to introduce a new mandatory declaration that must be completed before any procedure in which a patient's death is a foreseeable consequence. Invent the trust, the committee, the declaration, and the in-house name by which the trust refers to the doctrine itself — its own documents would not use the familiar phrase. The letter is entirely deadpan. The chair is neither a fool nor a hypocrite; he or she genuinely believes the new paperwork is a moral advance, and the humor should arise from that belief rather than from any editorial nudging. Nothing in the letter should signal to a reader that it is meant to be funny, and the chair must never come close to contradicting himself or herself.

Assume the reader knows the doctrine well — the conditions it lays down, the difference between what an agent intends as a means and what the agent merely foresees, the contrast between a case in which the one person is used and a case in which the one person is merely in the path of the harm — and so never explain or summarize it, and never cite anything. Everything comic should come from the doctrine itself, applied honestly, at the level of detail a hospital would really apply it.

The letter should cover at least the following, in whatever order suits it. First, which outcomes require the declaration, with the threshold drawn at certainty rather than at risk, and an account of what a clinician must do when the good outcome cannot be reached except through the patient's death: the honest answer there would place the death in the column reserved for means, and the letter therefore instructs the clinician to stop and consult the committee before proceeding, so that the cases the form exists to catch are precisely the cases it cannot record. Second, the trust's experience with the form so far, offered as evidence that it is working: some number of declarations reviewed and not one intended harm found. Third, the compulsory training workshop and the quiz it ends with, built on the familiar contrast cases, together with a statistic the chair finds reassuring. Fourth, the committee's ruling on the anaesthetists' objection about sedation at the end of life, which lets them continue on the condition that the intention be recorded as the relief of suffering and nothing else. Fifth, a closing reassurance that no clinician who has completed the declaration correctly has ever been found to have done anything wrong.

It should run to about nine hundred words. Write it as continuous prose, the way a long internal letter is actually written: no headings, no numbered clauses, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Do not name any real hospital, committee, philosopher, or publication, and include no references of any kind. When the letter is finished, a reader should be left with the real question of whether the line between what a person intends and what a person merely foresees is a fact about the person or a description that an institution applies afterwards — but the letter itself must never raise that question, or any question, in its own voice.
