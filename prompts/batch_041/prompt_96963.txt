I just watched Another Round for the first time since I stopped drinking, and it hit me way harder than I expected. I'm definately not a philosophy person, but I read the Laws in college and the wine stuff in Book I and II keeps coming back to me now. Plato seems to think drinking parties, if they're run right, are a kind of training ground for virtue -- the sober commander who keeps order, the idea that wine softens the soul like heated iron so you can mold it, the whole thing about letting people act shameless for a bit and then teaching them shame. He even says the lawgiver should use drinking to test and educate people, not just ban it. The movie is almost a natural experiment in that idea, except nobody's actually running it. Four teachers decide to keep their blood alcohol at 0.05% all day as a creativity hack, then push it to 0.10%, and the wheels come off -- Tommy drowns, Martin's marriage blows up, and the ending is this weird mix of grief and joy with Martin dancing and jumping into the harbor. So my question is: read the film through Plato's account of drunkenness in the Laws. Does it dramatize his claim that wine is a tool for testing and shaping character, or does it show the limits of that idea? I'm especially interested in the fact that there's no symposiarch here, no sober older person in charge -- the experiment is self-run, and the rules (no drinking after 8, none on weekends) are supposed to do that job. Does Plato's framework predict that this would fail, and if so, is the film agreeing with him or just telling a modern cautionary tale? And what about the scenes where Martin drinks with his students, or the final scene with the graduates? Plato thinks older people should drink alongside the young to model shame and self-control. Does the film show anything like that, or does it invert it? Where does the film resist the Platonic reading -- e.g. is Tommy's death a refutation of the whole idea, or is it exactly the danger Plato says the sober commander is there to prevent? I want a reading that takes both the film and the Laws seriously, points to specific scenes, and ends by saying whether the film adds anything to Plato's view that argument alone doesn't.
