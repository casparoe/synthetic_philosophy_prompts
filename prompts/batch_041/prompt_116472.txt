I am a novelist, and for the last four years I have served as the lay member of the collections board of a small maritime heritage trust. Our board meets a week from Thursday to settle a question we have been avoiding for thirty years, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance of the vote.

The facts, as our curator has assembled them. The trust owns and exhibits a wooden working vessel built in the 1830s. After a century and a half of repair, every original hull plank, deck beam, and ceiling timber has been replaced; what remains of the original fabric is the keel, the stem and sternpost, and roughly a dozen frames. This spring's restoration removed the last surviving original plank. Separately, a private boatbuilding school has spent twenty years acquiring the timbers taken off our hull over the decades — the actual removed originals — and has fastened them, with new nails and some new timber, into a second hull, which it now exhibits as the original vessel and offers for loan. It has asked us to endorse its claim and to hand over the vessel's name and marks.

Two consequences hang on what we decide. A donor's endowment is payable annually to the trust "that keeps the vessel on public view," and the two of us cannot both hold it. And a national exhibition has invited "the vessel" to travel next spring; only one hull can go, and the lender's insurer requires us to certify which object is the vessel. Our founding deed is silent on what makes the vessel the vessel, so the board is split and the chair wants a reasoned recommendation rather than another round of anecdotes.

Please draft that opinion. It should adopt and state explicit criteria for the vessel's identity, defend those criteria rather than merely assert them, and apply them to our facts; recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — which hull we recognise, what we say publicly about the other, and how we resolve the endowment and the exhibition; and give the strongest case for the claimant I am recommending against, stated in its own terms rather than as a caricature.

A few constraints. Everyone keeps calling this the Ship of Theseus, and I do not want an essay about that puzzle; assume the reader knows it and spend your words on our particulars. Do not cite statutes, cases, or scholarship, and do not give me a reading list — I cannot check any of it and would rather you reason from the facts as I have given them. Do not lean on any real vessel, trust, or precedent by name. Keep the tone fit for a board minute: firm, unhurried, and free of mystification.

Finally, I want the bottom line extractable. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one label from this closed set — CONTINUITY (the hull we have continuously maintained is the vessel), MATERIAL (the hull reassembled from the original removed timbers is the vessel), or NEITHER (the vessel is not identical with either physical hull, and we should treat it as a status that passes with the name) — then a dash and a phrase of no more than fifteen words stating the action the board should take.
