I am the speechwriter for the chair of the National Commission on Truth and Amnesty of the Republic of Marovia — an invented country, and an invented commission, in a scenario I built for a graduate seminar on transitional justice; the scenario is fictional on purpose, so please do not name, quote, or allude to any real commission, country, statute, or public figure, and do not cite anything. Here is the setting. A twelve-year internal conflict (1993–2005) ended in a negotiated settlement rather than a victory, so no side was ever in a position to try the other's fighters. The peace accord created a commission with two powers: to establish the facts of gross violations committed by all parties, and to grant amnesty for politically motivated offenses to any applicant who disclosed those offenses fully at a public hearing. The statute is explicit that the commission shall make no finding as to the moral culpability of any applicant — remorse is neither required nor recorded. The amnesty window closed fourteen months ago: about 2,300 people received amnesty, and about 400 applicants were refused, almost all for withholding material facts. One feature of the design has turned out to matter more than anyone expected. A designated family member of a victim has the right to put up to three questions to an applicant on the record, and an applicant who refuses to answer without the panel's leave forfeits the amnesty. In practice the panels granted leave generously, and a great many applicants used it to decline the questions that were not about what they did but about why this house, this son, this day. The result is a record that is extraordinarily thick on facts and almost silent on answers. The closing ceremony is next week: the report is handed to the president, the amnesty list becomes final and unappealable, and the chair speaks last. I am writing that address.

The room will be hard. Present are the survivors' association, which fought for the bargain and believes the record itself is the victory; a victims' caucus that has spent a decade calling the amnesty a pardon issued over their heads, on their behalf, by a state that was not the one harmed; the former combatants' association, relieved and defensive; and foreign donors. Three languages are in play and the sound system outdoors is poor, so it has to be speakable, not readable. What I want the speech to do — and this is the part I care about — is refuse the two easy moves. The easy move for the survivors' association is to say that the commission has given the victims the truth; the easy move for the caucus is to say that nothing has been given and the bargain was a theft. I want the address to hold both of the following at once: first, that a record of what happened and an acknowledgment by the person who did it are different goods, and that only the second one is a relation — something one person owes another — which is why a disclosure regime that permits silence on the question of why can produce a mountain of information and still leave the asker empty-handed; and second, that when the state waives prosecution it is disposing of a claim that arguably was never its to dispose of, and that this is a real objection, not a complaint, and cannot be answered by pointing out that the alternative was a war that did not end. If your speech implies that the state owned the claim and generously gave it up, you have failed the brief; if it implies the survivors were somehow consulted, you have lied on the record and failed worse.

Write the address. 800–1,000 words, in the voice of the chair, addressed to the room as described. The philosophical distinction above must do structural work: it should shape what the chair can promise, what she can thank people for, and what she must concede, rather than appearing as a paragraph of theory. Constraints: the words "closure" and "healing" are forbidden — the survivors' association has formally objected to both as consoling language that is not owed to them; the phrase "the truth has been established" is also forbidden, and you must say something more precise in its place; no naming of any real case, and no invented statistics beyond the ones I gave you. The speech must end with a concrete undertaking that the commission or the state could actually be held to, not with an aspiration. After the address, on its own final line, give a one-sentence summary of the central claim the speech makes, in exactly this form: THESIS: <one sentence>. If you conclude that no single coherent claim can be made from this podium to this audience, say that in the thesis line rather than smoothing it over in the speech.
