You are a political philosopher specializing in the ethics of immigration and territorial rights, and I want you to help me recover the principle behind my own judgments rather than summarize a familiar position. Begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could underwrite the pattern below, then work toward the most precise formulation you can and test it case by case.

Here are my verdicts, offered as gut reactions I would defend if pressed.

- A state turns back at its border a person fleeing a war zone. Wrong, and not merely regrettable.
- A state turns back a healthy adult who wants to move there to work and has no claim of persecution. Also wrong, though I feel it less sharply.
- A state turns back someone who has already been offered a job and housing by residents there. Wrong, and more clearly so than the previous case.
- A state turns back a citizen of its own who has lived abroad for years. Wrong, and the worst of these four.
- A state turns back a well-armed group that intends to seize its territory. Permissible.
- A small tribal nation, holding a recognized territory, turns away outsiders in order to preserve its language and land. Permissible, and not a close call for me.
- A town inside a liberal state passes a bylaw barring anyone not born there from taking up residence. Wrong.

The pair I cannot square is the tribal nation and the town: both are communities excluding peaceful newcomers in order to preserve a way of life, yet I want opposite verdicts. I suspect the difference is that the tribal nation is itself exposed to a history and structure of dispossession while the town is not, but I do not know how to state that without making the principle hostage to whatever facts I happen to know about each case.

A tentative principle I would like you to test: exclusion of a would-be entrant is permissible only when necessary to protect the members' basic rights or the community's continued capacity to govern itself, and only if the community is not itself implicated in the injustice that generates the entrant's need to move. Does this deliver all seven verdicts? Where exactly does it fail?

Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not listed -- one in which a democratic majority restricts entry precisely in order to prevent the newcomers from voting to change the restriction, and one in which a poor, weak state rather than a wealthy one turns away a would-be migrant worker -- and say which of my verdicts the best version of the principle forces me to abandon. State the principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you invent.

Be self-critical throughout. If my pattern does not admit of a single principle, say so instead of manufacturing one, and if my framing rests on a confusion -- for instance, if the community is being treated as an agent in a way no collection of people can be -- tell me plainly rather than accommodating it.
