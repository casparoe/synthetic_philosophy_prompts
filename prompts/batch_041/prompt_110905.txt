I hold the following view: in translating a work whose identity is bound up with a formal constraint—a lipogram, a palindrome, a poem in a fixed metre and rhyme scheme, a text written under some comparable restriction—the constraint is not an ornamental feature but part of the work's content. On this view, a translation that abandons the constraint, however semantically faithful and however elegant, is an adaptation rather than a translation; and a translation that preserves the constraint at the cost of some semantic drift may be more faithful to the work than one that preserves sense at the cost of form. Tell me what would count against this view. I am interested not only in the obvious objection that constraints can be untranslatable—consider, for instance, a lipogram forbidding a letter that does not exist in the target language, or a rhyme scheme in a language with almost no rhymes—but in the cases that should make me revise the position. Which counterexamples, intuitions, or consequences would a defender have to concede as genuine defeaters, and which could be dismissed as merely awkward? I would particularly like to know whether the view can be qualified so as to survive every apparent counterexample, and if so, whether that is a defect or simply the price of a defensible account of translation. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they are, and give an honest verdict on whether the position risks anything at all.
