Re-reading Charlotte's Web with my kid and it's turned into an ethics problem I can't put down. Everyone treats it as the story where the pig gets saved, but the farm is a place that turns animals into meat, and Wilbur only makes it because Charlotte spins a whole campaign and Zuckerman decides a famous pig is worth more alive than butchered. Charlotte herself is a predator who eats flies and insects by the score, and the book adores her, and I cant square that with the argument that we shouldn't eat animals because they can suffer. So is the book for eating animals or against it? What does it do with all the animals nobody saves — the flies, Templeton and his relatives, whatever's getting eaten out in the woods — and does making us cry over one pig actually help those animals or just make them easier to forget? I want a real reading, not a book report. Where does it line up with the thinking about meat and about suffering out in nature, where does it quietly dodge, and is there anything in it the philosophers miss — like why we'll weep for the pig and shrug at the swarm? One more thing: there's an old argument that a farm animal is better off existing to be eaten than never existing at all, and the book seems to live inside that idea; does it ever push back on it? And does the grain and the crops in the background matter here, since I've heard even growing plants kills mice and bugs?
