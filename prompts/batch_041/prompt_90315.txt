I've been sitting in on a reading group on AI governance, and one member — a former competition-policy economist — has a framing he returns to every session, and I want to know how much weight it will actually bear. His claim is that safety and alignment work is best understood as a pollution-control cost in a competitive industry: a firm that installs scrubbers pays more per unit of output than its rivals do, so unless the rules bind everyone at once the firm that moves first is simply priced out, and if one jurisdiction regulates while another doesn't, the activity migrates to the lax jurisdiction and total emissions barely move. He calls the resulting bind the competitiveness constraint: no lab and no country can afford to be the one that slows down, not because anyone wants unsafe systems but because the field is a race and the laggard loses it. From this he concludes that unilateral safety commitments are not merely costly but self-defeating — that the only moves that matter are ones that bind rivals simultaneously — and he reaches for carbon leakage and the race to the bottom in corporate taxation as the worked examples. I find the shape of the argument compelling and its conclusion demoralizing in roughly equal measure, and I would rather have it audited than argued about.

So please audit it. Start by laying out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the taxed good, the tax base, the tax rate, the taxed firm's rivals, the regulator, the jurisdiction, the mobility of the tax base, the consumers whose demand is being served, the externality that justifies the tax in the first place, and the revenue and what is done with it. Then tell me which of those slots has no counterpart in the AI case, and which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports without argument: for instance that the taxed activity is a pure cost to the firm with no offsetting benefit, that demand for the product is fixed, that the tax base can pick up and leave, that some authority has jurisdiction over everyone, or that a race to the bottom is symmetric and collectively self-defeating. Say which of these imports are load-bearing for his conclusion and which are mere decoration.

Finally, and most important, tell me whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies. In particular: does the leakage argument go through without both mobility of the activity and a fixed demand for it, and does frontier capability work actually have both? Can an alignment tax be negative — safety work that makes a system deployable, or that produces understanding which is itself capability-relevant — and if so, what does that do to the whole frame? And when he says the field is a race, which race is it: a prisoner's dilemma in which mutual restraint would leave both sides better off, a patent race in which the winner takes the market, an arms race, a tragedy of the commons over some shared resource, or a public-good problem in which safe systems are non-excludable and the laggard free-rides on everyone else's safety work? The conclusion should change with the game, so tell me which model fits best, how much of his conclusion each model can support, and whether in the end the analogy is carrying the conclusion or merely illustrating one he holds on independent grounds. If there is a better analogy for the competitive pressure on safety work, I would like that as well.
