I am trying to recover the decision rule that my own verdicts on a family of cases commit me to, and I would like you to state it precisely, test it against those verdicts, and tell me where it fails. I am not asking for a survey of the positions that have been taken on these problems; I want the rule itself, together with its consequences, and I am prepared to be told that one of my verdicts has to be surrendered.

1. A predictor of high but imperfect reliability has placed $1,000,000 in one box if and only if it forecast that I would take that box alone; a second box contains $1,000 in any case. I cannot see into either box. I take the first box alone.

2. A coin has been tossed. If it came up heads, I am to be asked for $100. If it came up tails, I am to be given $10,000 if and only if I would have paid had it come up heads. It came up heads. I pay the $100.

3. I am stranded in a desert. A passing driver will stop for me only if he is persuaded that I will pay him $100 on reaching the town, by which point he will have no means of recovering the money and I will have no further need of him. I cannot conceal my intentions. I persuade him, and I pay.

4. A man writes to me. He holds information that would ruin me, and will release it in a week unless I send him $1,000. He is a good judge of character and says he selected me because he was confident I would pay. He has never been known to bluff. I do not pay.

5. A machine may be built in the future that will identify, by simulating what each person would have done, everyone who knew of the possibility of its coming to exist and did nothing to bring it about, and will punish them; those who worked for it will be rewarded. (The case is usually called the basilisk.) I know of the possibility. I do nothing, and I would do nothing even if I thought the machine's arrival fairly likely.

My working hypothesis has been that an action is to be judged as an instance of a policy, and that a policy is to be judged by the expected value of the world in which it is the policy I follow — counting among the consequences of following it the fact that other agents who are able to predict my behaviour will behave differently depending on which policy they take me to follow. As far as I can see, this hypothesis reproduces my verdicts in the first four cases. In the fifth it appears to tell me to work for the machine, on the ground that a machine which punishes those who do nothing and rewards those who work for it will treat me better if I am the sort of person who works for it; and that verdict I will not accept. Either the hypothesis needs a clause I have not found, or my fifth verdict is the one to give up. I notice that the fourth and fifth cases have much the same shape and opposite verdicts, and I cannot say what the difference between them is supposed to be.

Please do the following. State the rule, or the smallest set of rules, that reproduces my verdicts, and for each verdict identify the clause that forces it and the case that motivates that clause. Say what the rule implies about two cases I have not decided. (a) An agent in the future is built not by forecasting me but by running my own deliberative procedure — the procedure I am using now — on different information and different payoffs, so that its choice is linked to mine more tightly than any forecast could link it. Does the rule require me to weigh its payoffs in my choice, and how does that requirement differ from the one generated by the predictor's forecast? (b) Two rival machines might be built, each of which identifies its targets by simulation: the first rewards those who worked for its existence and punishes those who did not; the second rewards those who opposed the first and punishes those who helped it. I have no idea which is more likely. If the rule must treat the fifth case differently from the first three, say exactly where the line falls — between offers and threats, between correlations that run through an exogenous event and those that run through another agent's chosen strategy, between threats that are credible and threats that are not, or somewhere else — and give me a case on each side of the line you draw. Finally, tell me plainly which of my five verdicts the rule cannot accommodate, and whether the cost of surrendering that verdict is greater than the cost of the clause that would save it.
