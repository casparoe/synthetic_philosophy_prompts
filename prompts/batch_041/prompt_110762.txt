I am preparing a session for an undergraduate seminar on fiction, games, and moral standing, and I want to build the discussion around Undertale, because it seems to me to be the rare work that does not merely thematize the question of whether video-game characters matter morally but builds that question into its mechanics. A colleague who teaches game design thinks the whole question is sentimental—that the characters are just code and any guilt the player feels is a trick the designer plays—and I would like a reading that can answer him without cheating. For those who have not played it: you are a human child who has fallen into an underground world of monsters; every encounter can be ended either by fighting or by sparing; the game remembers what you do; if you kill everything you can, the game slides into a route in which the world empties out and the ending is bleak; if you spare and befriend everyone, you get a warmer resolution. Crucially, the power to save and reload is not just a user interface but part of the fiction: you can undo deaths, the monsters mostly do not remember, but the game itself does, and a genocidal run leaves a mark that a later pacifist run cannot wholly erase. I would like a reading that takes both the game and the philosophy seriously, and that is honest about where the game resists the reading.

I want you to work with a distinction that I find useful, and that I suspect the game will complicate. There are at least three ways one might say that video-game characters matter morally. First, they might be moral patients in their own right, beings whose simulated or fictional suffering counts directly. Second, they might not be patients at all, but our treatment of them might still be assessable because it expresses or cultivates traits in us—cruelty, care, curiosity, a taste for domination—so that the moral question is about the player's character rather than the character's status. Third, the game's design might itself make a moral demand on the player, so that the relevant question is not what the characters are but what the game, as a made thing, is asking of us. Which of these does Undertale dramatize, and where does it resist the reading? Does the game's punishment of the genocidal route—the sense that the world has been permanently altered—constitute an argument that the characters matter, or only an argument that the player's choices matter? Does the save-and-reset mechanic vindicate the thought that fictional suffering is not real and therefore not morally weighty, or does it show the opposite, that the characters' inability to consent to being reset is exactly what makes the player's power over them troubling?

Finally, I would like the reading to say something about the paradox of fiction. We know the monsters are not real, yet the game solicits real guilt, real grief, and real affection; the paradox of fiction asks whether such responses are rational, and the moral question is whether they can ground anything like an obligation. Does Undertale treat the player's emotional engagement as evidence of moral status, or as a sentiment the game can manipulate precisely because it controls the fiction? And what, if anything, does the game add to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not—about the moral significance of interactivity, about the difference between a reader's complicity and a player's, or about whether the moral standing of a fictional being could be constituted by the practices of the community that engages with it? I am less interested in a verdict on whether the characters really matter than in a careful account of what the game shows, what it merely stipulates, and what it leaves unresolved.
