I write about ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for an issue on the theme of responsibility we are running fiction rather than essays. So what follows is a commission, not a question, though the commission is shaped by a philosophical problem and I care about getting the problem right.

The problem is moral luck, but I want to avoid the version everyone knows. The standard illustration is a pair of drunk drivers, one of whom happens to hit a child, and the observation that we blame them very differently although the difference between them lies outside anything they chose. I would rather have a story set after a jurisdiction has tried to fix that.

The premise: a legislature has rewritten its law of negligence so that the sanction for a careless act no longer depends on what came of it. Two drivers who both run the same red light receive the same penalty whether the intersection was empty or a pedestrian was crossing; the sentencing schedule is keyed entirely to the act and to what the driver knew or intended at the time. The reform is defended in the obvious way: no one deserves more punishment merely for having been unlucky, and the gap between the two drivers is a fact about the world, not about their culpability.

My interest is in what follows, and here I want the fiction to do something more specific than rehearse the familiar debate. My suspicion, which I would like the story to test rather than illustrate, is that abolishing one kind of luck does not abolish luck. It relocates it, and then disguises the remainder as merit. Once outcomes are struck from the ledger, the person who never in fact drove carelessly begins to look like someone of better judgment, even though whether one ends up at the wheel at two in the morning after two drinks is itself a matter of circumstance, upbringing, exhaustion and chance. The person who did drive carelessly is now treated as though the carelessness were a standing feature of her character rather than a thing she did once. And where there was a victim, the family may experience the equal sentence as a second injury, since it tells them that what they lost is, in the eyes of the law, immaterial.

I am looking for roughly two thousand words that take one of these lives seriously. Possible vantage points, though you should feel free to find a better one: a magistrate who must read the new sentencing schedule aloud and finds that the statute obliges her to describe the act and forbids her to describe the consequences; a defendant punished identically to a stranger whose identical act killed a child, who cannot manage to feel the equality as a mercy; a public defender who notices that the reform has made her clients' histories do all the work that outcomes used to do.

Two constraints. First, no character may articulate the philosophy. The idea should live in institutional furniture, in the sentencing worksheet with its checkbox for knowledge and its blank space where the outcome used to be, in the categories on a form, in the way a courtroom talks. If someone has to explain the problem, the story has failed. Second, do not resolve the story into a verdict for or against the reform. What interests me is that the reform is both plainly just and plainly incomplete, and I would like the ending to leave that tension standing rather than dissolving it into a defence or a satire. The reader should close the story with a concrete sense of how a system can succeed at removing one form of unfairness and, in the same motion, convert another into something that looks like desert.
