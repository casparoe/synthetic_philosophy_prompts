I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a course on ancient ethics and political thought, and a seminar discussion last week turned into a two-person argument I genuinely couldn't referee. I'd like you to adjudicate it for me.

The context was Aristotle on money and exchange — the account of reciprocal justice in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics and the treatment of wealth-getting and usury in the opening book of the Politics. Two classmates, Priya and Dominic, went four rounds.

(1) Priya: Aristotle's objection to interest is really an objection to money being treated as if it breeds. He thinks coins are barren, so treating them as fruitful is a category mistake dressed up as a moral one. It's an analogy, not an argument, and a poor analogy at that.

(2) Dominic: The breeding image is just rhetorical packaging. The real target is the structure of the end. Household acquisition is bounded because need is bounded; wealth-getting for its own sake is unbounded because it has no external end, and interest is the clearest case of a means being turned into an end and treated as self-generating. So the objection is about the inversion of means and end, not about the biology of metal.

(3) Priya: But that just pushes the problem back. If money's whole function is to be a conventional measure and store, then using it to earn is continuous with its function, not opposed to it. And there's a deeper difficulty: Aristotle says just exchange must be proportionate, but if the two goods really are equal in value, why would either party exchange at all? Money has to be doing the equalising, which means the equality is conventional through and through, so the appeal to what is natural is doing no work. Either money is natural, and then earning on it is natural too, or it is conventional, and then interest is no worse than any other conventional use.

(4) Dominic: That conflates a thing's function with a particular use of it. Aristotle can hold that money is natural as the measure that completes exchange within a community, while a particular use of it — treating the medium as itself productive — is unnatural. It's the difference between walking to the market and walking in circles. Natural here is about whether the practice serves the end that justifies it, not about whether the stuff is a natural kind.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is mistaken, partly right, or right, and justify briefly. Then say which points, if any, both of them are missing. End with where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong — the consideration or argument that would lead you to a different verdict.
