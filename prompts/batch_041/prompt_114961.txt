I'm putting together the final for a small upper-level seminar I run, officially called Formal Systems and Creativity, unofficially a GEB fan club with alot of LessWrong-era baggage. One essay question, and I want it to be unanswerable by anyone who just remembers the punchline of the MU puzzle, i.e. that the number of I's is never divisible by 3.

So here's the shape I want. Hand them the standard setup: start from MI, four rules — append U after a final I, double everything after the M, replace III with U, delete UU — and the usual argument that MU is unreachable. Then give them two variants and ask what happens to the jootsing move in each.

(a) MIU-plus: keep everything and add the reverse of the III-to-U rule, so a U can also be rewritten as III. Does MU become derivable now? Why or why not?

(b) MIU-minus: drop the III-to-U rule, keep the rest, and add a rule that deletes a single I anywhere. Does MU become derivable? And what does the contrast between (a) and (b) actually show about what jootsing is — is it finding an invariant, is it finding the right level of description, or is it just a name for try stuff till something works?

Then the part I actually care about. Is jootsing something you can do systematically, or is the whole point that it can't be mechanized? Hofstadter makes it sound like a creative leap, but in the MU case the leap is look for a quantity preserved by every rule, which is a textbook move you'd teach in week three. And if the meta-system where you prove MU unreachable is itself a formal system, have you really jumped out of anything, or just moved into a bigger room? I want them to take a position and defend it, and to say what would count as a counterexample to their position.

Please write the question as I'd hand it out, a model answer around 600-700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — not just topic labels. Level is upper-level undergrads who've done a bit of logic but aren't mathematicians. Keep the question self-contained, since they won't have the book in front of them and I don't want them guessing at page numbers.
