In a graduate seminar on the interpretation of quantum mechanics, a classmate disposed of an hour's discussion with a single line: Bell's theorem shows that the world is nonlocal. He used that line to argue that quantum mechanics is in tension with special relativity, that some genuine faster-than-light influence must be real, and that the rest of us were in the grip of an outdated prejudice. I have heard the same sentence do other work: to mean only that local hidden-variable theories are dead; to mean that a measurement here instantly alters the state of a particle there; to mean something vaguely metaphysical about the universe being one indivisible whole; and to mean that the theorem is really about contextuality or about measurement dependence rather than about locality at all. I would like the sentence taken apart, since I suspect the disagreement in the seminar was not about physics but about which of several claims that one line was carrying.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, as a menu, ordered from the nearly trivial or merely verbal up to the strongest, and then give a definite verdict on each. For every reading I want to know what exactly it asserts; whether it is a claim about the world or only about how we describe the world; and whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, contentious, or simply false. Two things matter to me especially. The first is the several senses of "nonlocal": the sense in which it means only that a joint state fails to factorize; the sense in which it means statistical dependence that no local account can reproduce; and the sense in which it means a genuine causal influence propagating faster than light. I want to know which of these the theorem actually establishes, and what the popular phrase for instantaneous action at a distance commits one to that the theorem does not. The second is the assumption set the theorem really needs, and what becomes of each assumption under the usual escape routes: denying definite measurement outcomes, denying the independence of the measurement settings from the state, admitting backwards causation, or retreating to a purely operational reading on which the theorem constrains only a certain class of models and says nothing about the world. For each route, say which assumption it sacrifices, whether it is a serious position or a cost that only looks tolerable from a distance, and whether it leaves the classmate's conclusion standing.

Take a definite position throughout. I would much rather have your verdicts and your reasons for them than an even-handed survey of camps, and if one reading is the one a careful person should have in mind, say so. Where you are genuinely unsure, say so, and say what turns on it. No citations, references, or reading lists: I want the claims themselves, and I know you have nothing to consult.

Please structure the answer as follows. "The slogan and its setting" — one short paragraph restating what the one-liner was being made to do in the seminar and what would have to be true for it to do that. "The menu of readings" — the bulk of the answer, roughly six to eight readings, each with a name, the precise claim it makes, and a note on whether it is a claim about physics or about language. "Verdicts" — a definite verdict on each reading, with your reasoning compressed but explicit. "Which reading the argument needed" — a short section naming the reading the classmate's conclusion requires and stating plainly whether he has it. "What would settle it" — a brief closing section on the kind of case, experiment, or argument that would separate the readings that survive, and on which of them a reasonable person should end up holding.
