I'm the vice chair of the historical society in a small mill town in New England, and I've been drafted to give the keynote at this year's Founders' Day, which the banner already printed as the one hundred and fiftieth. The trouble is that I spent this past winter in the town archives for an unrelated project, and I now know that Founders' Day as we celebrate it was assembled in 1961 by the chamber of commerce: the date is off by about forty years, the founder is a composite of two unrelated men stitched together for a newspaper series, the colonial-style costume was designed by a department-store window dresser, and the little ritual everyone loves — the oldest child in town turning the first spade of soil — shows up in no record before that year. The audience will include three of the founder's actual descendants, the boosters who sell the commemorative plates, and plenty of people for whom this day is simply the day the town is itself. I can't stand up and recite the legend as though it were true, and I don't want to stand up and just take it apart in front of them, either. Please write the speech.

I've been reading about invented tradition and imagined communities, and it has left me more confused than armed. If nations are imagined and traditions are invented, it starts to seem as though everything is, and then the word stops doing any work at all — which can't be right, since I feel a real difference between the ceremony we hold every year and, say, the town's habit of putting out sand barrels in winter, which nobody decided and nobody remembers choosing. So I want the speech to think that through out loud, for a room that has not read any of this: what actually separates a tradition deliberately manufactured in 1961 from one that accreted slowly and that no one remembers inventing? Is the difference in origin, in function, in how it's worn, or only in how we feel about it? Does the 1961 date make the spade ceremony less real, or merely less old — and does that distinction survive contact with the fact that a lot of the town's older customs were probably invented just as deliberately, only further back and by people who are now dead? Don't let me off with a platitude about how all traditions are invented; I want the speech to land on a specific account of what, if anything, the manufactured version lacks.

It should run about six to eight minutes spoken, in a register that a retired millworker and a high-school civics class can both sit through. It needs to end with a position rather than a shrug: either we keep the day and are honest about its age, or we let it go, and you should argue for one of them and give me a line I can actually say to the descendants in the second row. Do not name scholars or cite books — nobody in that hall will know or care who wrote what, and I'd rather the thinking be visible in the argument than in the footnotes.
