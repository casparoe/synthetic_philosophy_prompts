Examination question:

"Weakness of will (akrasia) is sometimes said to be explained by hyperbolic discounting. Set out the explanation as clearly as you can, and assess whether it succeeds — in particular, whether it captures what is distinctive about weakness of will, and what the empirical evidence for hyperbolic discounting does and does not establish."

Student's answer:

"1. Akrasia. Weakness of will is acting against one's own better judgement: an agent judges that, all things considered, she ought to do A, and yet does B. The phenomenon is puzzling because it seems to require that she both believes A to be best and is not moved to do A. Aristotle denied that this is possible: on his view no one willingly does what he believes to be bad, and the apparent cases must really be cases of ignorance. I shall suggest that the empirical work on hyperbolic discounting shows the denial to be false.

2. Hyperbolic discounting. Ainslie's work on choice between rewards at different delays showed that the value of a reward does not decline by a constant proportion per unit of delay, as it does under exponential discounting, but falls steeply for near delays and gently for distant ones. The consequence is preference reversal: I may prefer £100 in six months to £50 today, and yet, once the six months have passed and only the present choice remains, prefer £50 today to £100 in six months. The same reversals are found in rats and pigeons, so the pattern is not a peculiarity of human culture.

3. Why this looks like weakness of will. The planner-doer, or multiple-selves, framework reads the reversal as a conflict between a long-run self that endorses the plan and a short-run self that is moved by the immediate reward. The agent really does endorse the diet, the savings plan or the early night — and then the short-run self takes over, and she eats, spends or stays up. This is exactly weakness of will: the agent knows what is best and does otherwise. Hyperbolic discounting therefore not only explains akrasia but explains why it is so common and so predictable.

4. What the evidence establishes. Because the discounting pattern is robust and species-general, it shows that weakness of will is not a special failure of human rationality. It is a by-product of the shape of the discount function, and so the denial of akrasia is refuted by the data rather than by argument.

5. Limits. Two qualifications. First, the model is descriptive: it tells us how choices are made, not what the agent ought to do, and so it cannot by itself license the charge of irrationality. Second, it has been argued that a declining discount rate can itself be rational, for instance when the future is uncertain, in which case present bias need not be a mistake at all. These qualifications show that the explanation is at most partial."
