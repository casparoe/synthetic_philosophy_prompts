I run a reading group for machine-learning researchers and engineers — about fifteen people, no philosophical training, no advance reading — and next week's session, seventy-five minutes with a whiteboard, is on two objections to utilitarianism that come from Bernard Williams. The first is the integrity objection: the charge that a theory which makes rightness a matter of maximizing agent-neutral value has to treat the agent's own deepest commitments as so much more weight in a calculation the agent does not own, that it must count what the agent merely allows among the things the agent does, and that an agent who took this seriously would be alienated from the projects and actions that make them the person they are; the case normally used to fix it is one in which an agent is asked to kill a single person in order to prevent the killing of many, and the objection is about what the theory would make of the agent, not only about what it would tell them to do. The second is the one-thought-too-many objection: in the familiar case where an agent can rescue only one of two drowning people and one of them is their spouse, the complaint is not that saving the spouse is forbidden but that the theory locates the agent's reason in a general permission — that this is my spouse figures as an exception clause in an impartial rule rather than as the reason the agent acts on.

I want the plan for the session, not the argument. What has to be in place before each idea can land, in what order, and why that order matters — in particular, what must already be true before the group can see that the second objection is not the first one restated, since the two are usually introduced in the same breath and sound like a single complaint about coldness? The pivot has to be the case where the theory's verdict is uncontroversial — the agent is permitted to act, nothing is sacrificed, no calculation is violated — and the objection nevertheless survives; I mean to build the session around the drowning-spouse case for that pivot, so tell me whether it can bear the weight, what it must be paired with if the first objection needs a different case to carry it, and what the carrying example has to have and has to lack.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface. This audience optimizes for a living, and I expect them to read the integrity objection as a point about conflicting objectives or about a reward function that fails to capture what the agent cares about, to read the one-thought-too-many objection as a point about the cost of deliberating at the moment of action, to hear the word integrity as consistency of an agent's values across contexts, and to treat the two-level reply — that the theory is a criterion of rightness rather than a decision procedure — as having disposed of both. Tell me where in the sequence each of these has to be headed off rather than corrected after the fact, which one cannot be headed off at all and should instead be used, and what I should refuse to settle in seventy-five minutes, saying so explicitly, since the group takes up consequentialism in general the following week. Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end whose answers would show whether they can hold the two objections apart rather than merely recite that they differ, and say what a bad answer to it would look like.
