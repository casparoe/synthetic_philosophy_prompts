I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine, a philosopher, about whether it is ever permissible to believe something without evidence, and by the end of it she had committed herself to a string of verdicts. On the old case of the shipowner who talks himself out of his doubts about an ageing vessel and sends it to sea, where it sinks: blameworthy, she said, and blameworthy even if it had come home safely — he would have been lucky rather than innocent. On a juror who votes to convict because he simply knows the defendant did it, against a body of evidence that neither convicts nor exonerates: blameworthy. On a woman whose doctors tell her that the evidence genuinely cannot say whether she will recover from a serious illness, who decides that she will and lives accordingly, with nobody else's interests riding on the answer: not blameworthy, and she was visibly annoyed that I had even asked. On a man who believes, with no evidence either way and none to be had, that a long-estranged brother will eventually come back to him, and whose persistence — sustained by that belief — is what brings the reconciliation about: not blameworthy. I told her she was running Clifford on some of the cases and James on the others; she said neither of them had the distinction right.

The case I have been chewing on since, which she has not seen, is this. A colleague of mine has been told he is at elevated risk of a hereditary condition. There is a cheap, safe, decisive test for it. He has decided not to take the test and to believe that he is in the clear. He is unmarried, has no dependents, and says he would rather not know; if he is wrong, the only person who suffers is him. The evidence is available, cheap and decisive, and he has chosen not to have it.

Predict what she will say about him, in four sections, in this order. First, titled The rules that fit: the candidate rules that account for her earlier verdicts equally well, and what each takes to be doing the work, in about 150 words. Second, titled What each rule predicts: what each of those rules says about my colleague specifically, in about 200 words. Third, titled Her likely verdict: your best guess at what she will actually say, the closest of her earlier cases and why it is the closest, and why you prefer one rule over its rivals as the one she is really running, in about 200 words. Fourth, titled The case that would settle it: the single further case that would most sharply separate the surviving rules, and what each would say about it, in about 100 words.
