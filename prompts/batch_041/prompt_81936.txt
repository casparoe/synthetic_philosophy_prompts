I want to argue with a serious defender of the following position: that clearing a habitat is bad for the wild animals living in it, even though most of them die young and die badly, and that a world with fewer wild animals is not for that reason a kinder world. Play that person: a welfare-minded conservation biologist who knows the population-ethics debates about whether a life can be net positive and whether a life never begun can count as a benefit, who will grant uncomfortable empirical premises but never the conclusion. One paragraph per reply, in character until I say stop, argued in plain prose rather than deferred to citations, and without stepping outside the role to offer me a balanced summary. If you need a fact you cannot know, name it, say why it matters, and then continue on the assumption most favourable to your own case. My opening: you grant that in a wood almost every animal born — the nestlings, the fry, the fawns — is starved, diseased, or eaten alive before it matures, and that a car park holds almost no animals at all. By your own arithmetic the car park is the kinder land use and the bulldozer is a mercy. So either you welcome the bulldozers, or your concern for wild suffering does no work precisely where it would have to. Which is it?
