I have been constructing a case for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and the argument below now strikes me as too quick, though I cannot say exactly where. I would like you to diagnose it: identify the step or steps you would reject, explain why they initially appeared compelling, and set out what rejecting them costs. I am attached to the idea that a rational agent should evaluate her options from an ex ante standpoint—that is, as a policy chosen before she knows which branch of a gamble she occupies—and I am also attached to the thought that counterfactual mugging is a case in which that standpoint requires paying. What I cannot accept is the conclusion that we are therefore obliged to pay off every clever blackmailer. But I want to know what I am giving up if I resist that conclusion.

The argument:

(1) A rational agent should choose the policy that maximizes expected utility, evaluated from the ex ante standpoint—before she knows which branch of the thought experiment she occupies.

(2) In counterfactual mugging, the policy of paying when asked has higher ex ante expected utility than the policy of refusing. If a fair coin lands heads, Omega has already given you $10,000 on the condition that you would have paid $100 had the coin landed tails; if it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100. Ex ante, paying on tails yields the $10,000 in the heads branch at a cost of $100 in the tails branch, while refusing yields nothing in either branch. So paying is better.

(3) Therefore, a rational agent pays in counterfactual mugging.

(4) XOR blackmail has the same formal structure. Omega sends you a letter if and only if your house is infested with termites xor you pay Omega $1,000. The letter's arrival is predicted from your decision, and the decision is predicted from the letter's arrival, just as in counterfactual mugging.

(5) By the same ex ante reasoning, the policy of paying when the letter arrives has higher expected utility than the policy of refusing. If you pay, the letter's arrival tells you that your house is free of termites; if you refuse, the letter's arrival tells you that it is infested. Since a termite infestation would cost $1,000,000 and the payment is only $1,000, paying is the better policy.

(6) Therefore, a rational agent pays in XOR blackmail.

(7) Moreover, the XOR trick can be iterated. An adversary can construct any number of independent XOR threats, each of which, considered alone, is such that paying is recommended by the reasoning in (5). An agent who accepts that reasoning will pay each one.

(8) A decision theory that licenses an adversary to extract unlimited sums from the agent is not a correct theory of practical rationality.

(9) Therefore, premise (1) is false, and paying in counterfactual mugging is not required.

Where does this go wrong? If I must choose, I would rather retain (1) and (3) than accept (9). Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked fine, and what the rejection costs me. In particular, if the answer turns on a difference between the two cases, I want to know whether that difference is principled or merely a patch.
