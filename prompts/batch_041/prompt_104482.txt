Below are my gut calls about copying other people's stuff, and I'd like to know what rule, if any, is actually behind them.

- A standup does another comic's bit word for word on a late-night show, and even says it's Dana's joke. Not okay. The bit only really works once, and now it's his.
- A food blogger makes the exact recipe from a restaurant's cookbook and posts it with a link back. Fine. She still had to cook it, and the restaurant still sells the dish.
- A perfume house reverse-engineers the top-selling scent and sells it for a third of the price. Not okay. Feels just like the joke.
- A fast-fashion brand knocks of a small designer's dress and sells it for £20. Jerk move, but I can't get worked up -- it's a dress.
- A pharma company sells a copy of a drug who's patent just expired. Fine.
- The same company tweaks one atom of a drug that's about to go off patent so it can keep charging brand prices. Not fine, even though they aren't copying anything.
- A producer lifts a three-second drum break off an old record and it becomes the hook of his hit. Not fine. The drummer got nothing, and everybody knows that break.
- A magazine compiles every phone number in a town from public records, and a rival just reprints the whole list. Not fine, even though nobody invented the numbers.

My half-baked theory: it's about whether your copy stands in for the original. If people buy yours instead of theirs, not fine; if they'd still want the original anyway, fine. But the generic drug clearly stands in for the brand and I still say fine, so that can't be the whole thing.

Which of my verdicts has to go? The perfume and the dress are the same situation and I've got opposite calls, so one of those probably does. Is there a single principle that saves all eight, or close? Then tell me what it says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a model trained on a novelist's books that writes in her style without reusing a single one of her sentences. And one I haven't considered: a magician publishes the secret to a trick another magician has performed for forty years.

End with the principle stated in one plain sentence, then a last line of the form \answer{...} giving a short label for the principle and which one of my verdicts you'd overturn (or none).
