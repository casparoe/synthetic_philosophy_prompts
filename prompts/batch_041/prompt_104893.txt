EXAM QUESTION (Q3): The Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing — that is, when is there a whole of which those things are the parts? In lecture I introduced an answer I called the *functional-integration answer*: some things compose a whole just in case they jointly realize a single function, i.e. they're organized so as to operate together as one system performing some task or sustaining some process. (a) Situate this answer relative to the standard options we discussed (nihilism, universalism, contact, fastening, organicism) — is it a version of one of them, or a new option? (b) Apply it to: a table, a cloud, a committee, a flame. (c) What does it imply about the statue and the lump of clay, and does that implication help it or hurt it? (d) Is "functional integration" precise enough a condition to do the work the SCQ needs? One page, no outside reading.

STUDENT ANSWER: Van Inwagen's SCQ asks when some things compose a further thing. The two extreme answers are nihilism, on which composition never occurs and there are only mereological simples, and universalism, on which composition always occurs, so that any things you like compose something. Both are motivated by not having to draw a line, so neither is stuck with vagueness. Contact says things compose iff they're touching; fastening says iff they're fastened together; and organicism, van Inwagen's own view, says iff their activity constitutes a life — which is why he says there are no tables, only things arranged tablewise.

The functional-integration answer sits closest to organicism, since both make composition turn on a process rather than on spatial relations. In fact I think it just is organicism under a different name: a life is a function, so anything functionally integrated is alive. On the cases: the table is functionally integrated — its parts are organized to hold things up — so the table exists. The cloud is not, since the droplets aren't working together toward anything, so strictly speaking there's no cloud. The committee is functionally integrated, since the members coordinate to produce decisions, so the committee exists as a composite object. The flame is the awkward one: it does look like a self-sustaining process, so the answer would say the flame is a composite object — but I take that to be a mark against it, since a flame is a process and processes aren't objects.

On the statue and the lump: the statue is functionally integrated, so it exists; the lump is just a mass of clay with no function, so it doesn't. I think this actually *dissolves* the statue/lump puzzle rather than taking a side in it — if the lump doesn't exist, there's only one object there, so there's no mystery about two things occupying the same place at once. As for (d), I'd say the condition is precise enough: either a bunch of things are working together as one system or they aren't, and that's a yes/no matter, so functional integration doesn't smuggle in the vagueness the SCQ was trying to avoid.
