I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory — my own stuff is on the Frankfurt School and ideology critique, so the Grundrisse is honestly a bit outside my lane — and I've somehow ended up in a reading group this term that's going through the "Fragment on Machines" from Notebook VII. I've been volunteered to open the discussion on Wednesday, and there's one paragraph I keep rereading without being able to pin down what it's actually claiming, and I'd rather not bluff my way through it in front of people who know this text cold. Here it is:

"The exchange of living labour for objectified labour – i.e. the positing of social labour in the form of the contradiction of capital and wage labour – is the ultimate development of the value-relation and of production resting on value. Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

What's tripping me up is the middle bit. He says the presupposition of the value-relation "is – and remains" the mass of direct labour time as the determinant of wealth, and then in the very next breath says that as large industry develops, real wealth comes to depend less and less on labour time. Those two sentences seem to be pulling in opposite directions and I can't tell whether that's the whole point or whether I'm just misreading one of them. Is he saying the labour theory of value is false, or that it's still true but the system is now running up against its own conditions? And is "real wealth" supposed to be the same thing as "value" here — because if it is, this looks like a flat contradiction, and if it isn't, I genuinely don't see what the difference is meant to be. I also don't get why he calls this the "ultimate development" of production resting on value: ultimate as in most complete, or most self-undermining, or something else entirely?

So what I'd really like is a patient walk-through of this one paragraph — what each of the key terms is doing (value, wealth, labour time, the "agencies set in motion," the value-relation), how the argument actually moves from the first sentence to the last, and what the passage is committed to, since our convenor keeps calling the whole thing "dialectical" and that isn't helping me much. If it's useful, also tell me what a good opening question for the group would be — i.e. what the genuinely hard thing to decide about this passage is, as opposed to the easy "Marx predicted the robots would free us" reading that I suspect is what half the room is going to say.
