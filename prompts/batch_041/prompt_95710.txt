I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-division seminar on civilizations in crisis — the term is built around the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui, and the question running underneath everything is whether "collapse" names a real phenomenon or is just a dramatic word we paste over ordinary political change. I've just come out of a long argument with my co-instructor about how to grade one student's final exam, and we ended on opposite sides — one of us reads the answer as strong, the other as merely competent — so I'd like an assessment that doesn't just split the difference or quietly side with either of us. The exam question was: 'Choose two of the three cases we studied and assess whether "collapse" is the right description of what happened in each. In each case, say what work the word is doing — what it captures about the evidence and what it obscures — and then say whether the two cases should be described in the same terms at all.' We grade on four roughly equal criteria: accuracy of the case material, handling of the concept itself, quality of argument, and clarity of writing; letter grades with plus/minus.

The student wrote: 'Collapse is a word we bring to the past, not one the past hands us, but the two cases I've chosen bring it out differently. Rapa Nui is the clearest case, and the one where the word is least metaphorical. The islanders felled their palms to roll and raise the moai, the deforestation stripped the soil, the seabird colonies failed, and the society turned on itself in warfare and, by some accounts, cannibalism — all of it before any European arrived. It's the textbook case of a people destroying the environment they depended on, and it fits Tainter's model of a society that keeps investing in complexity past the point where the investment pays off; when we say Rapa Nui "collapsed," we're naming a real event, an ecological overshoot the islanders inflicted on themselves. The Maya are harder, because the mechanism was different — drought, most likely, a run of dry decades the divine kings couldn't pray their way out of — but the outcome looks the same on the ground: Tikal, Copán, and Palenque are abandoned one after another in the ninth century, the stelae stop being carved, the jungle closes over the plazas. Here I'd say "collapse" is not just defensible but accurate, because unlike Rapa Nui there was no successor to pick up the culture — the Maya didn't move their civilization north, they simply ended, and what the Spanish found centuries later was a landscape of ruins rather than a living state. So the two cases do belong together, but only if we hold on to what the word is doing: in both it names a real process — diminishing returns on complexity, in Tainter's terms — and that's why I'd keep the term rather than replace it with something softer like "transformation."'

Please give the answer a grade and, more usefully, a point-by-point assessment: what it gets right, and every place where a claim is wrong, unsupported, or doesn't follow — with enough precision that I can tell whether each objection is fatal or merely a matter of emphasis. I especially want the specific errors of fact or inference named, since the hasty reading is exactly the one I'm trying to avoid. Tell me how confident you are in the grade and in each of the specific criticisms, and single out the one point in your assessment you're least sure of — the place where a reasonable grader might go the other way.
