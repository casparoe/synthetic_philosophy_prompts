Hegel's remarks on war in the Philosophy of Right — the state's individuality, the condition of states as individuals with no judge above them, war as the moment in which the ideality of finite goods is actualized, the citizen's willingness to sacrifice life and property as the ethical moment, the claim in a remark that war preserves a people's ethical health by making it indifferent to the stabilization of finite institutions, the warning that prolonged peace would produce corruption, and the closing idea of world history as the tribunal over nations — are compressed, embedded in remarks and lecture additions, and have been read both as a sober account of international politics and as a romantic glorification of war. I want them tested against the Concert of Europe: the 1815 settlement and the congress system, the long stretches without a general great-power war, broken by the Crimean War and finally by 1914, the limited wars and interventions that punctuated the period, the colonial violence that accompanied the European peace, the domestic repression and the revolutions of 1848, and the fact that Hegel died in 1831 and saw only the first phase.

Map the theory onto the episode, then the episode back onto the theory. What does Hegel's account say the actors faced and were obligated to do, and what would count as success or failure? Which features of the Concert does it explain — the absence of an authoritative judge, the balance of power as a substitute for adjudication, the internal consolidation produced by successful wars, the treatment of treaties and institutions as stabilizations of finite things? Which does it miss — the longevity of the peace, the role of congresses and shared norms, the peripheral violence, and the possibility that a peace punctuated by limited wars is not the prolonged calm Hegel warns against at all, so that the Concert vindicates the winds-and-sea image rather than refuting it, while exposing that Hegel gives no criterion for distinguishing a wind from a storm? Then reverse the direction: is the Concert a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly it cannot accommodate, or a case it never anticipated — a system of states that is neither a sovereign league nor a mere state of nature? Test the corruption thesis directly: did the prolonged absence of general war produce the corruption Hegel predicts, and if the form it took was nationalism, revolution, or bureaucratic repression rather than the decay of civic virtue, does the concept stretch to cover it? Say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak — on whether the ethical moment of war can be detached from war's suffering, on whether world history as tribunal amounts to more than the outcome of force, and on whether Hegel's framework can address diplomacy and the institutional management of conflict at all. No citations or reading lists; use the facts above and common knowledge, and flag where an interpretation of Hegel, rather than the text's surface, is doing the work.
