Write a parody of an institutional compliance manual — approximately 750 to 900 words, organised as numbered sections with short headings — issued by the invented Office of Truthful Communication of a religious body that has adopted Augustine of Hippo's teaching on lying as binding policy for its employees and clergy. The piece should be written entirely straight: the voice is that of a real, faintly weary compliance department, with no winking at the reader, no narratorial nudges, and no modern irony of tone. The humour must come from the doctrine itself, worked out consistently; a reader who knows the De Mendacio material should recognise that you understand the position and find the parody fair, while a reader who does not should still follow it and laugh.

The manual should get the following right, and should let the comedy arise from taking each point seriously:

1. The definition. A lie is not a false statement as such but a falsehood uttered with the intention to deceive. State the consequences precisely: someone who sincerely believes a falsehood has not lied (he has only erred), whereas someone who states a truth but believes it false has lied. Draw out the consequences for ordinary institutional life — self-appraisals, customer correspondence, apologies, and the annual newsletter's joke issue, which the manual must certify as compliant on the ground that jokes carry no intent to deceive.

2. The schedule of offences. Organise the disciplinary appendix around Augustine's eight grades of lying, running from the gravest (false teaching in matters of doctrine) down to the least grave (a lie that harms no one and is told to protect someone's chastity), and make explicit that the schedule grades the gravity of the sin and the penance due, not the permission granted: every grade remains prohibited, and the eighth is the smallest sin, never a licence. The department should sound quietly proud of the precision of this table.

3. The rejection of the helpful lie. The manual must explicitly abolish the charitable or well-meant lie — the falsehood told to spare someone's feelings, to protect a colleague, or to save a life — and must state that no exception exists. In its place it should offer only the permitted alternatives: silence, a refusal to answer, a change of subject, a true statement the questioner did not ask for, and the acceptance of consequences.

4. The pressure cases. It should address the scriptural and everyday precedents that critics press against the policy — the Hebrew midwives, Rahab, Jacob's deception of his father, Abraham's description of Sarah — and defuse them without licensing imitation, and it should reproduce Augustine's reading of Paul's claim to have become all things to all men as a matter of sympathy rather than of lying.

5. A worked example. Include a section in which a caller asks where a colleague is, and walk the reader through the permissible and impermissible replies with bureaucratic care.

Do not name living people, and do not depend on citations, dates, or reading lists. Then append a short note, 150 to 250 words, in your own voice and outside the parody, identifying where the piece takes liberties with Augustine's actual position or leans on a contested reading — for instance, whether the eighth grade is a grudging concession or a flat prohibition, and how far he allows concealment that stops short of assertion. End that note by saying what would change your mind: which passage or argument would show that the manual has mischaracterised the doctrine, or that the satire is unfair to it.
