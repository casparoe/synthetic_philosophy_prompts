I'm in a punishment & society seminar and we keep butting heads with the law-and-econ crowd. My friend Maya — very Becker-pilled, reads everything as an optimization problem — keeps saying that punishment is just a price. The sentence is the price you pay for the crime, the offender is a rational buyer deciding whether the offense is worth it at that price, the state is the seller/shopkeeper, and the whole criminal law is basically a market where we set prices to get the quantity of crime down to where we want it. She uses this to argue that all the hand-wringing about desert is a distraction: just tune the numbers, make the expected penalty do the deterrent work, and note that since deterrence depends on severity times probability, the two are interchangeable levers — so if a big fine and a short prison term produce the same drop in offending, insisting on prison is just waste. Some of this feels obviously right to me and some of it makes my skin crawl, but I cant put my finger on where the picture goes wrong or whether it does.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the price, the buyer, the seller, the market, the demand curve, and what's supposed to be the "good" being exchanged — and say who in the actual debate runs with this picture (no citations needed, I just want to know whose argument I'm really arguing with). Then tell me where the mapping breaks: which features of a real price the criminal sanction doesn't have, and which features of punishment the price picture simply can't represent — I'm thinking things like censure, the fact that a price is a permission, incommensurability of certain wrongs, offenders who aren't choosing at all, and the fact that "the market" here has no independent supply side. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten her conclusion from the ones that are just colorful differences. And then the payoff question: once the breaks are on the table, does "desert-talk is a distraction, just optimize the expected penalty" survive, or was the price metaphor carrying that conclusion the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what punishment is doing, tell me what it is and why it does the work hers can't.
