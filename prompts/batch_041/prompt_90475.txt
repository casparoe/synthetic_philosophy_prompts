I am a novelist at work on a book set in the Warring States period, and its spine is a single long argument between two of its characters: an older teacher who holds that the moral life is the unfolding of something we already carry within us, and a younger one who holds that whatever goodness we achieve is the artifact of an external discipline — ritual, habituation, instruction — that we would never have generated on our own. I have given them, in essence, the positions conventionally associated with Mencius and with Xunzi, and I have read enough of the commentary to suspect that the interesting question is not which of them is right, but whether they are answering the same question at all. I need your credence on that, decomposed, because I want to know which parts of their quarrel my characters can press in good faith and which are artifacts of my own staging.

The proposition is this: that the Mencius–Xunzi dispute over human nature is substantially verbal — that is, that there is an interpretation of each thinker, defensible from the received texts, on which their central claims about human nature can both be true at once, and on which the surviving disagreement concerns the meaning of the word 'nature' and the practical necessity of ritual and deliberate effort, rather than any contested psychological or normative fact.

Give me a number, and then the terms it rests on, with a number and one line of justification for each. I would expect the following to matter, though you should add, split, or discard as you judge best:

- whether Mencius's claim that human nature is good is best read as a claim about innate incipient motivations — the alarm and distress anyone feels at the sight of a child about to fall into a well — rather than about the finished virtues or about a normative ideal;
- whether Xunzi's claim that human nature is bad is best read as the claim that our innate desires, left unshaped, cannot produce and will erode social order, rather than that we are innately malicious;
- whether Xunzi's account of how ritual and deliberate effort transform a person must presuppose an innate capacity for that very transformation — a susceptibility to being shaped, or an appetite for order — which Mencius would count as part of nature, and which would render Xunzi's denial of innate goodness unstable; both thinkers, after all, appear to hold that every person has the capacity to become good, and it is unclear from what, on Xunzi's view, that capacity is made;
- whether the two are even using one word, if Mencius extends 'nature' over the whole developmental endowment — what a creature is expected to become under the right conditions — while Xunzi confines it to what is present at birth, so that their sentences never contradict each other directly;
- and how much substantive disagreement survives once the preceding terms are settled: the trustworthiness of spontaneous moral feeling, the necessity of external ritual, the direction of dependence between nature and cultivation.

Then combine the terms into your final number, and tell me which one the number is most sensitive to. I would also like to know what would move you most — a passage, a reconstruction of one of the two arguments, or nothing at all — and what would count as settling the question rather than merely shifting the interpretation. Two further things, if you can: whether your number would change if we asked not what the historical Mencius and Xunzi thought but what the strongest versions of their two positions come to, and whether the number depends more on the texts or on the interpretive literature that has grown around them. For what it is worth, I find myself near 0.5, and I suspect I am hedging because I cannot tell whether the instability in Xunzi is real or only an artifact of reading him through Mencian lenses.
