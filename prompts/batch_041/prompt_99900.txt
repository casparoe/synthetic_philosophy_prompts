I convene a final-year undergraduate module in applied ethics and environmental philosophy, and I am setting the paper for its final examination. I need a single essay question on the ethics of insecticides and pest control — one that cannot be answered by reciting the stock positions on animal welfare, speciesism, or environmental holism. To force genuine reasoning rather than recall, the question should make students apply a decision procedure of my own devising, which I have built into the course and which I set out below. Treat the procedure as stipulated for the purposes of the question; students are to take its rules as given and argue about what follows from them.

The procedure — call it the Prospective Welfare Ledger — has three rules.

(1) Weighting. An intervention is scored by summing, over every individual it may affect, the expected change in that individual's welfare multiplied by the credence that the individual is sentient. Species membership carries no independent weight; the credence term does all the work.

(2) Attribution. Only harms that the intervention makes more likely than a no-intervention baseline count against it; harms that would have occurred regardless do not. A harm counts in full whether it is intended or merely foreseen.

(3) No Credit for Replacement. Where suppressing a pest population predictably triggers density-dependent rebound, so that the suppressed individuals are replaced by others, the suppressed lives are not credited as lives spared; lives that would not exist but for the suppression count as a cost against the intervention, not a benefit.

The question should stipulate a single arable field and a choice between two options: (A) a broad-spectrum insecticide that also kills large numbers of non-target invertebrates, including pollinators; and (B) an introduced parasitoid wasp that targets the pest narrowly but is itself an insect whose sentience credence is unknown and which will itself suffer and die in large numbers. Students must apply the Ledger to this choice, identify a case in which rules (1) and (3) pull against rule (2), and then reach a verdict on whether the resulting conflict exposes a defect in the Ledger or registers a genuine moral insight. The question should be answerable in about fifty minutes, should demand a verdict rather than a survey, and should be self-contained — nothing may depend on set reading.

Please supply three things: the examination question itself; a model answer of roughly 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer must make, the specific moves that distinguish an A from a B answer, and the characteristic errors that should cost marks, with a one-line note on each. Write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme in British English.
