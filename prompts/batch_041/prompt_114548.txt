This has become personal for me. I recently sat on a small committee that had to decide whether to commit a scarce resource to one person who would suffer greatly without it, or to spread the same resource thinly across many people who would each benefit only a little. I argued against the aggregative answer, and the reason I kept giving was that persons are separate — that a large benefit to some cannot simply be converted into a license to burden one. A colleague replied that this was a slogan, not an argument. Since then I have tried to state the thought more precisely, and this is what I have arrived at:

The separateness of persons is a decisive objection to utilitarianism. The core of it is not that utilitarianism is cruel or that it ignores rights; it is that utilitarianism treats the good of distinct persons as commensurable and summable, and thereby treats a benefit to A as a reason that can be transferred across persons to justify a harm to B. But a reason must be a reason for someone. The fact that some good would accrue to A is not, by itself, a reason for B to accept a burden, because A's good is not a constituent of B's. Any theory that licenses such cross-personal transfer has misdescribed the structure of practical reason.
