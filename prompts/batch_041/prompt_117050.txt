I would like to rehearse a specific objection to the Dutch book argument against an opponent who will not let me have it cheaply. Please play a committed subjectivist about credence — someone for whom degrees of belief just are the odds at which one is disposed to bet, and for whom the Dutch book argument, and not any appeal to accuracy or closeness to the truth, is what makes coherence a requirement of rationality. Be a formidable interlocutor: concede an objection's genuine force where it has force, never give up the central claim that incoherence is irrational in virtue of exposing one to a sure loss, and do not retreat into the accuracy-dominance argument as a substitute. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this stays a conversation rather than an essay, and construct the book explicitly if I ask you to. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Here is my opening move. I grant that a bookie who knows my credences can offer me a series of bets, each of which I accept as fair by my own lights, whose combined effect is to leave me out of pocket whatever happens. My objection is that this shows nothing about my credences and everything about the bookie's freedom. Taken one at a time, every bet in that series is one I was content to make; the loss exists only in the aggregate, and only because the bookie is permitted to choose the stakes and to keep dealing until the sum comes out against me. Worse, if I am simply not disposed to bet at all, or if my marginal utility for money declines so that a large stake is not a multiple of a small one, then no such book exists and I appear to escape your argument without altering a single degree of belief. So your conclusion looks hostage to assumptions about my gambling habits and my utility curve that are not themselves part of the theory of credence you are trying to ground. What do you say to that?
