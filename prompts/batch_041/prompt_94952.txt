Construct a two-trader market to order. Constraints: one claim with two possible payoffs; a single round of trade; both traders are Bayesians who share a prior, whose rationality is common knowledge, and each of whom holds a private signal the other lacks; no noise traders, no liquidity, hedging, or consumption-timing motive, and no exogenous shocks; the scenario itself in one paragraph. In the first version, one trader's signal must be genuinely valuable — if her counterparty inferred nothing from her willingness to trade, she would trade at a profit — and yet the standard no-trade argument must correctly predict that no trade occurs, so that the signal never reaches the price, not because the price already contained it but because trading would have put it there. Give the prior, both information partitions, what the equilibrium price can and cannot be, and name the premise that is doing the work. The second version must differ from the first in exactly one respect: the claim's payoff depends on its own price. Say what now happens, what the price reveals, and which assumption of the no-trade argument the second version violates — including any assumption that statements of the argument usually leave implicit. Then run the removal test: drive the payoff's dependence on the price to zero and say whether the first version returns exactly. Close by identifying the single feature that does the epistemic work, saying what the first version implies about a market's capacity to aggregate dispersed information, and how a defender of the no-trade argument would reply that the second version is not a counterexample but a different kind of market.
