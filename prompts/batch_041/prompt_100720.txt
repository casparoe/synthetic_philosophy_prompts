I sit on the board of a small housing cooperative, and we have to decide whether to build a rooftop garden. Because the proposal has two separate prerequisites, we agreed to vote on each on its own: first, whether the roof can safely bear the added load; second, whether the gardener's insurance would cover water damage to the flats below. Our bylaws then say that if both pass, the garden goes ahead. On the load question the vote was five to four in favor, and on the insurance question it was five to four in favor as well — so by the rule, we approved it. But when I asked everyone afterwards how they felt about the garden itself, a clear majority of the same nine people said they were against it, each because they doubted one or the other prerequisite. So we ended up with a majority behind each premise and a majority against the conclusion, and the outcome is being treated as the board's decision anyway. One member now wants to scrap the whole thing and simply re-vote on the garden as a single yes-or-no question, since that is what people actually care about. Another says that would throw away the only votes that were truly informed, because no one can judge the garden except by judging the two prerequisites. Which procedure should we have used, was it a mistake to fix the rule before we knew how the votes would split, and is there a principled way to say what the board actually decided?
