I've spent most of my career on population ethics -- totalism, the non-identity problem, mere addition, all that -- and I keep running into the decision-theory crowd's counterfactual mugging business at workshops. I want to know wether my home apparatus already has this debate inside it, or whether they're onto something my vocabularly can't say.

The setup I mean, roughly: a perfect predictor flips a coin. Tails, it asks you for $100. Heads, it hands you $10,000 if and only if it predicted you'd have paid had it come up tails. Everyone agrees you should have precommitted if you could; the fight is purely about what to do when you're standing in the tails branch with no precommitment in place. Then the elaborations. There's a symmetric version where an agent who reasons updatelessly comes out ahead regardless of how the coin lands, while an agent who reasons only from its actual branch gets nothing either way. There's a version where the coin is replaced by a fact you don't know offhand, so the two branches are related by logic rather than by chance. And there are the blackmail variants people have built on top of it, where whether the threat fires depends on some combination of what several recipients do rather than on a single flip.

Restate all of this in the vocabulary I actually use. Branch, possible world, expected utility, precommitment, updatelessness -- put that into the language of possible people, welfare summed across a population, the non-identity relation, totalism against its rivals, the supposed asymmetry between adding good lives and adding bad ones. Do it one idea at a time and as faithfully as you can. I want the translation, not a verdict.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What carries over cleanly? What carries over only if you fudge something -- for instance, if you treat the branch-self who pays and the branch-self who collects as distinct people, when the decision theory insists they're the same person under two suppositions? And what has no expression at all in my vocabulary: is there anything the decision theorists are arguing about that population ethics simply cannot state, and is that because my framework is missing something or because their disagreement is thinner than it looks? The reverse question matters just as much. Is there anything my literature agonizes over -- the repugnant conclusion, the non-identity complaint that the party who gets sacrificed is a different person from the one who benefits, whether numbers can outweigh quality -- that has no counterpart on their side, and if so, is the decision theory impoverished or is my debate doing work theirs doesn't need?

I'm fluent in this stuff, so don't dumb it down or explain what totalism is. Assume I know the terrain and push on the joints.
