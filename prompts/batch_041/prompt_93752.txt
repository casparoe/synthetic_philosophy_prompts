I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough to know how a certain kind of audience will hear Dennett's four grades of creature—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—and I want to design a session that gets ahead of that. I'm facilitating a ninety-minute reading-group discussion for smart non-specialists, many of them from a rationalist or effective-altruism background, comfortable with reinforcement learning, Bayesian updating, and large language models, but with no training in philosophy of mind. They will have read a short summary of the four grades beforehand. I want you to plan how I should teach the framework, not explain it to me.

Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and why—what depends on what, and where the sequence would break if I swapped two steps. Then give me the one carrying example that should run through the whole session. It has to be able to be pushed through all four levels without changing the subject, and it has to make the Gregorian step feel like a genuine discontinuity rather than just more of the same. Tell me what the example must have and lack, and why the obvious candidates—the bird and the cuckoo, the rat in the Skinner box, the human with a hammer—either work or fail. I have considered using a person deciding whether to step onto a frozen lake, but I am not attached to it.

Next, map the misconceptions the group will bring or form, in the order they will arise, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. I predict the following, and I want you to tell me which of these I am wrong about and what I have missed: first, the ladder-of-intelligence reading, where the four grades are just increasing amounts of cleverness; second, the replacement reading, where later levels supersede earlier ones rather than stacking on top of them; third, the thin reading of Gregorian as merely using tools or being cultural; fourth, the assumption that Gregorian is just Popperian plus language; fifth, the mapping of the four levels onto evolution, operant conditioning, model-based planning, and tool use, with the assumption that this mapping is the same thing as Dennett's distinction. I especially want the plan to resist that last flattening. As I understand it, the interesting claim is that the Gregorian creature's cognitive architecture gets partly outsourced to tools and representations that are inherited from other minds, and that this changes what kind of thing the creature is, not just what it can do.

Then give me one check for understanding I can use at the end—a question or task that would show the idea has landed rather than that they can recite the four names. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and say so explicitly, and advise me on whether to use a large language model as a running example: if yes, where in the sequence it belongs and what it would clarify or distort; if no, what I should use instead.
