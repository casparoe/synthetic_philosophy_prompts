I'm in a comparative constitutional politics seminar and we spent two whole sessions on the 1975 Australian dismissal and then the 2019 prorogation mess, and both times somebody eventually said some version of "the monarch reigns but does not rule" — in the prorogation case to say the Queen simply had no choice but to do what the PM advised, so there was nothing to get worked up about, and in the Australian case to say that the Governor-General was just standing in for the Crown and that's how the machinery is supposed to work anyway. I definately nodded along both times and then realised on the walk home that I can't actually say what the sentence claims. Because it looks like it could be making at least five quite different claims: that the monarch is legally bound to act on ministerial advice; that she has real discretion but never uses it; that she has neither discretion nor influence; that she has plenty of influence (the weekly audience, the warnings, the thing Bagehot says about the dignified part of the constitution) but that influence just isn't ruling; or that "rule" means "make law" and since the Queen-in-Parliament is the sovereign legislator she does rule, in exactly the sense at issue. And I suspect that the reading people reach for when they want to shut a controversy down is the strongest-sounding one, while the reading that's actually true is the boring one.

Can you lay these out for me as a menu rather than picking one and explaining it — ordered from the trivial or near-tautological readings up to the ones that would be genuinely surprising if they were true — and for each say what kind of claim it is (a legal rule, a constitutional convention, an empirical/political prediction, a conceptual point about what "rule" means), whether it's plausible as applied to the UK, to the Scandinavian monarchies (where I gather at least one of them wrote the monarch out of government formation altogether), and to postwar Japan (where the Emperor is constitutionally a symbol but still performs a list of formal acts on cabinet advice), and — this is the bit I actually care about — whether that reading is strong enough to support the use it was being put to, namely "the monarch had no choice, so there's nothing to argue about." Somewhere in there, please say whether the slogan is even the right shape for the question, since neither "reign" nor "rule" is a technical term and one of them might be doing no work at all; and whether it transfers to a Governor-General at all, or whether that's a different claim wearing the same clothes.

I don't need reading lists or case citations, I can look those up myself — I want the distinctions, and I want to know which of them the seminar was actually relying on. Flag where you're unsure or where the answer genuinely depends on which country's constitution we're talking about, and if the sentence turns out to be too vague to be worth precisifying, or if my five readings collapse into two, say so plainly instead of humoring me.
