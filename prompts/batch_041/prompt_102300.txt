So I'm in this little reading group on social epistemology — five of us, we meet Thursdays, there's usually wine — and two members have been circling each other for about three months now over testimony. One is a hardline reductionist: testimony gets no special epistemic status, any trust we place in what we're told has to be earned by tracking the source's track record, basically the Hume move. The other is an anti-reductionist who thinks we arrive with a default entitlement to believe what people tell us unless something defeats it, the Reid move, and that the reductionist picture is both psychologically false and quietly circular. The rest of us are tired of it. Could you write the document they should have produced: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, in their two voices but signed by both. I want the numbered list of claims they can both actually sign (about what testimony is, about what needs explaining, about what the psychological and developmental facts do and don't show), then the exact remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept as fair to itself, and then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things would move at least one of them. For each crux, name a case or argument that both of them would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about that case in advance, so it can't be fudged later. Finish with a disagreements table. If you decide part of their fight is just verbal — e.g. whether "presumption" and "prima facie justification" are the same thing, or whether an LLM counts as a "speaker" — flag that part and say why, don't paper over it.

One more thing, because otherwise it'll stay abstract. In the group we eventually agreed on a fixed set of cases the two views both have to run, and I'd like the statement to include them so the disagreement gets concrete. (1) Someone reads a proof of a theorem and checks every single step herself — is she relying on testimony at all by the end, or has it been fully discharged? (2) A source you have good reason to think is a liar or hopelessly biased, but whose claims, for boring structural reasons, turn out to be reliable anyway. (3) A child picking up language, and the broader claim that almost everything any of us believes rests somewhere on what we've been told. (4) The one that started the whole thing: a language model produces a fluent, confident answer with no identifiable person behind it — is that testimony at all, and does either side's default of trust extend to it? (5) A community whose entire knowledge of some distant place is inherited from generations nobody can now trace, and which turns out to be accurate. For each case I want both of them on record: what their view predicts, and what outcome would embarrass them. And if it turns out they'd both update in the same direction on some of these, say so — I'd like to know which of the five is actually load-bearing and which are just scenery.
