I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to understand what the cogito argument actually establishes. I would like you to rank the following readings of it by how much they establish about the existence and nature of the self, from strongest to weakest, and to give a one-sentence justification for each placement. Treat the rankings as a tier list if that is more natural, but make the ordering explicit.

The readings are:

1. The inference reading: the argument moves from the premise that thinking is occurring to the conclusion that a thinker exists, relying on a suppressed premise that whatever thinks exists. It is a valid argument whose force depends entirely on that suppressed premise.

2. The performative reading: the act of doubting or thinking cannot be carried out without a thinker; the conclusion is not inferred but enacted, so any attempt to doubt it reinstates it.

3. The presupposition reading: any doubt, including doubt about whether the thinker exists, presupposes a subject who doubts; the argument makes explicit a condition of doubting rather than adding a new piece of knowledge.

4. The indexical reading: the first-person existence claim is guaranteed by the semantics of the first-person pronoun and the present tense; it proves something about the use of a word rather than a substantive fact about a persisting person.

5. The episodic reading: what is proved is only that a thought occurs now; it does not prove that the same thinker existed a moment ago or will exist a moment hence. The argument concerns a momentary event, not a substance.

6. The transcendental reading: the argument identifies the minimal structure any experience must have, including a first-person perspective and a subject for whom things appear. It proves a framework condition rather than a particular existence claim.

7. The immunity-to-error reading: when a subject thinks, it cannot be mistaken about who is doing the thinking, even if it can be mistaken about what it is thinking or whether the thought is veridical. The argument proves a special kind of first-person certainty about ownership, not the existence of a persisting ego.

8. The therapeutic reading: the argument is not a proof at all but a move that dissolves the question; the doubt about whether the thinker exists is not a coherent doubt because it cannot be formulated without the very subject whose existence is in question.

9. The runtime-invariant reading, which is my own attempt to put the point in software terms: the execution of the doubt routine requires a non-null self pointer; the assertion cannot fail without aborting the process that would observe the failure. I am not sure whether this is a genuine reading or just a restatement of one of the others, so please say which reading, if any, it collapses into.

After ranking them, tell me which readings are merely verbal variants of one another, which one you would defend if you had to pick just one, and what the cogito does not prove even on the strongest reading. I am not looking for citations, reading recommendations, or historical attributions; I want your own analysis of the arguments.
