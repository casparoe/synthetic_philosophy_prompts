Next month our local astronomical society holds its annual dinner, and I am to give the speech marking the retirement of the man who has been its president for eighteen years and who first taught me to use a telescope. He is also, unapologetically, an astrologer: he has cast charts for friends and neighbours for decades, and last spring, when my daughter was born, he gave me a hand-drawn nativity that I have kept and can neither dismiss nor accept. The room will be split — most of the society regards astrology as nonsense and will flinch at any hint of endorsement, while his family and the many people he has charted for will flinch at any hint of mockery.

Please write that speech, about four minutes long, and let Kepler give it its structure rather than merely ornamenting it. Kepler earned his living from horoscopes and almanacs and defended a serious astrology against those who wished to reject it wholesale; yet he held that the heavens do not act on us unmediated, that what matters in an aspect is the geometrical configuration as received here on earth rather than any power residing in the planets themselves, and that the soul meets it with a vital power of its own. He was nonetheless scathing about the machinery that purported to forecast particular events, and refused to play the marketplace entertainer. Use that division between a defensible core and an indefensible superstructure to decide what may and may not be said, so that the sceptics do not think I have gone soft and his family do not think I have patronised him. Paraphrase Kepler's views rather than quoting him, and do not invent quotations. British English, please.
