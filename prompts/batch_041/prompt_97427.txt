I have no formal training in history or philosophy, and the question below is one I have arrived at on my own rather than taken from any book. I have a friend — equally self-taught, but a voracious reader of history — who likes to pronounce on whether this or that historical outcome was inevitable, and I have been trying to work out the rule behind his verdicts. His verdicts so far, in his own terms:

1. The dissolution of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century: inevitable.
2. The survival of the Reformation after Luther's break with Rome: not inevitable; he insists it was nearly extinguished more than once and need not have lasted.
3. The outbreak of the First World War in 1914: inevitable, given the alliance commitments and the mobilization timetables.
4. The collapse of the Soviet bloc in 1989: not inevitable.
5. The French Revolution's descent into the radical Republic: inevitable.
6. The success of the American Revolution: not inevitable.

The new case I want him to rule on is the beginning of the Industrial Revolution in Britain, rather than in the Netherlands or in China. I am less interested in his verdict than in the rule he is running, so please tell me:

(a) what he will say about the new case;

(b) two or three rules that fit all six earlier verdicts equally well. One candidate, I suspect, is that an outcome counts as inevitable only when several independent causes had already made it overdetermined; another is that it counts as inevitable only when the agents involved had no workable alternative open to them. State these and any others precisely enough to apply, and say what each implies;

(c) what each candidate rule predicts about the Industrial Revolution case, where those predictions diverge, and which rule you judge him to be actually running, with your reasons;

(d) the further case that would best separate the rules that survive — that is, the kind of verdict that would force him to choose between them — and what verdict each surviving rule would give in that case.

Where his six verdicts genuinely underdetermine the rule, say so rather than manufacturing a decision, and explain what extra commitment he would be taking on if he settled the new case one way rather than the other.
