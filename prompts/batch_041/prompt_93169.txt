I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, thirty-odd years of lab work, and since I gave up writing grant applications I've been teaching myself philosophy — mostly the bits that lean on physics, since that's where I can smell a bluff. Something's been nagging me about Gödel. Not the incompleteness stuff; the other thing. In 1949 he cooked up a rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations, the one with the closed timelike curves, and around the same time he argued that relativity actually supports idealism about time — that the passage of time, the moving now, isn't a feature of the world at all but something about us. As I understand it his case runs like this: for time to genuinely pass you need a world-wide objective present, a global split of events into past and future; but relativity makes simultaneity frame-dependent, and in his rotating universe there's no consistent global ordering of events at all, so the theory simply has nothing to play the role of the objective present. Fine — except I keep feeling this proves too much. If no privileged reference frame kills objective passage, why doesn't the equally unprivileged here kill space, or kill the objectivity of causal order? And I'm honestly not sure the rotating universe is doing any real work in the arguement rather than being a vivid illustration. Write me an essay on whether Gödel's argument really shows that time, as opposed to the spacetime manifold, is unreal — or whether it only shows that a Newtonian picture of time is dead. Give both sides a proper hearing, then commit: I want a verdict and your reasons, not a survey. Go easy on the jargon, I'm a phycisist playing catch-up.
