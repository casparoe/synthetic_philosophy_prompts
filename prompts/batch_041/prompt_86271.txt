I retired a few years back after 30-odd years as a planetary geologist, and since then I've been reading philosophy — political stuff mostly, some metaphysics — which my wife finds alarming. Somehow this landed me on the external advisory board of a Mars settlement venture, and they've just handed us their draft charter to sign off on before the next funding tranche closes, so I have to actually say something.

Here's what's eating at me. The charter locks in the founding crew's decisions about the settlement's basic setup — life support architecture, whether it ever terraforms, the ban on refitting the return vehicles — and says nothing can be revisited until the resident population passes 10,000, at which point they get a constitutional convention. Everyone else on the board thinks this is fine: the founders are the ones taking the risk, and they'll mostly be dead by then anyway. But "they'll be dead" doesn't seem to me to do the work they think it does. My worry is that they've written a political promise about things that are really just physics — no vote in eighty years can undo a sealed atmosphere or a gene pool of forty people. There's a rival draft floating around where every clause expires after 25 years unless the people actually living there re-ratify it, and I genuinely can't tell if that's utopian nonsense or the only honest option.

Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursdays vote. Tell me which version to recommend, whether to attach conditions and a review date, and give the strongest case for the version I'm against, so I'm not blindsided when the chair says I'm overthinking it. Don't be too gentle with the founders, but don't be unfair to them either.
