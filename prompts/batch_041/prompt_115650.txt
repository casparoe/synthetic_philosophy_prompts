I'm a philosophy professor here and I've somehow ended up as the department's rep on our new Learning Analytics and Advising Advisory Group (the acronym is LAAG, which nobody likes). We meet next Wednesday and I have to bring a written recommendation on the item that's been eating our meetings: the advising office wants to pilot a vendor's preference-aware course recommendation engine with the incoming class, starting at spring registration. The system watches what students actually do -- which courses they browse and add and drop, which sections they switch into, how long they sit on a page in the LMS, whether they come back to a listing after abandoning it -- and fits a model that is supposed to recover each student's underlying preferences over majors, departments, and course attributes. Then it nudges: students whose inferred profile resembles yours tend to do well in X, and it flags anyone whose registration pattern looks out of line with their declared major so an advisor can reach out. The vendor's technical appendix describes the inference as inverse reinforcement learning, and the sales pitch is basically that this only makes explicit what students have been revealing all along by choosing, and that a preferences survey is worse because self-reports are noisy and biased by whatever the student thinks we want to hear. Here's the wrinkle that has people stuck: when the model's inferred profile conflicts with what a student says she wants, the vendor says advisors should trust the model, since the behavior is the honest signal. Two of my colleagues are ready to approve the whole thing as harmless personalization; the advising director says if we don't approve it she'll buy a cheaper product from another vendor that does the same work with less oversight, which I think is a real argument and not just a threat.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. I want a concrete recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions and a date for revisiting -- plus reasons the advising office and the vendor could actually respond to, and a fair statement of the strongest thing the other side has going for it, since I don't want to be the guy who just says no to technology in a memo nobody can answer. And here is the part I care about most: the philosophical content shouldn't be decoration on a risk-management document. I want the memo to be honest about what is actually settled in the literature on learning preferences from behavior versus what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and not to present a contested position as the consensus. If the argument turns on a specific claim about what this kind of inference can and cannot identify from observed choices, state the claim plainly and mark it as a claim rather than asserting it as established, and where you think reasonable people would divide, say where the line is.

Also, no reading list and no citations, please -- I don't want the memo padded with references, and I don't want us to be the committee that hides behind a bibliography. Short enough that people actually read it before Wednesday. American English. And no, I can't just recuse myself, I already tried.
