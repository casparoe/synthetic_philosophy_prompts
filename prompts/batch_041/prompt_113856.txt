I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a mixed reading group of about fifteen people — two graduate students in philosophy, a working economist, a retired GP, a secondary-school teacher, and several interested laypeople who will have read nothing in advance — and I would like you to plan how I should teach it rather than to teach it to me. I have a whiteboard and no handouts.

The subject is the levelling-down objection, and the claim of the priority view (prioritarianism) to be immune to it. I have come to suspect that the immunity is less secure than it is usually presented as being. The objection is normally aimed at views that treat equality as something that makes outcomes better; the priority view escapes it because it never holds that making the better off worse off is, by itself, an improvement. Yet the priority view's distinctive content seems to depend on our taking a particular scale of well-being as given, since any concavity in the way we aggregate can be re-described as a concavity in the well-being scores themselves. I do not want the session to be a defence of that suspicion; I want the group to see the shape of the problem for themselves, and to see why it is a problem at all.

Set out the order in which the components of the session must come, and for each say what it depends on and what will fail to land if it is skipped. Tell me in particular what must be established before the levelling-down objection can be stated as an objection rather than as a puzzle about arithmetic, and what must be in place before the prioritarian's reply can be heard as an answer rather than as a dodge. I am also unsure where to place, or whether to draw at all, the distinction between a view that locates the value of equality in the betterness of outcomes, a view that locates it in how the worse off fare in absolute terms, and a view that locates it in the relations between people; tell me where in the sequence that distinction belongs, and what goes wrong if it is drawn too early.

Then choose the one example that should carry the session, and say why it should be that one rather than either of the two I am currently tempted by: the case in which everyone's well-being is lowered to a common level and nobody is made better off; and the case in which a benefit must be allocated either by a fair lottery or by an equal division, so that what is at stake is not whether anyone ends up worse off but how the ex ante and the ex post perspectives come apart. Say what the example you choose must contain and what it must be free of.

Give me the misconceptions the group will arrive with or form — I expect at least that the priority view is merely utilitarianism with a concave transformation of utility built in and so adds nothing, and that the levelling-down objection is a complaint about waste — and mark exactly where in the sequence each must be headed off, and which of them I should instead let surface and dismantle in the room. Finish with one question I can put to the group at the end that would show whether they have understood why the objection is an objection, and tell me what I should refuse to open at this level, saying so out loud — in particular, whether the move to variable populations should be ruled out of order for the evening.
