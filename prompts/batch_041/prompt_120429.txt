So Pascal's Wager has this modern descendant that people call Pascal's mugging: some guy stops you on the street and says, hand over your wallet and I'll set you up with an infinitely good afterlife — and if you dont, infinite torture. Yeah, its wildly unlikely, but it's not zero, and infinity times any nonzero probability is still infinity, so by the Wager's own logic you should pay up. And he can pull the same move with any payoff at all, which is kind of the whole problem: the argument suddenly seems to prove way too much.

I want you to predict what the actual Pascal would have made of this. Not the cartoon Pascal — the real one: the guy who helped invent the mathematics of probability, who was deeply religious and honestly a bit grim about it, and who thought Christianity came with receipts: miracles, prophecies, the whole evidence apparatus he leans on in his religious notes.

A few things I specifically want him to weigh in on:

- Does he think the mugging is a fair generalization of his Wager, or a cheap gotcha? Where exactly does he claim the analogy breaks — and is that break principled, or is he just protecting his pet argument?
- The modern defusers say: cap the payoffs, refuse to multiply by infinities, and treat any reasoning that would make you hand your wallet to a stranger as a reductio rather than a proof. What does Pascal say to that? Does he accept any of it, and on what grounds?
- Now the nasty version. The mugger doesn't just promise — he shows up with credentials: claims of miracles, fulfilled prophecy, the same dossier Pascal himself cited. If the mugger can match the evidence, does Pascal's escape hatch still work, or is he forced to pay?
- And the one I actually care about: if he can't cleanly separate his Wager from the mugging, does he bite the bullet and become a mugging-maximizer, or does he ditch the Wager? Which one does he give up, and why?

Put it in his voice where you can — he should sound like a 17th-century guy thinking out loud, not a modern analytic philosopher with a slide deck. Real arguments, not vibes.
