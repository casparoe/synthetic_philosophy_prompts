It's about conventions, the Lewis-style picture: a convention is a regularity that solves a coordination problem — we all do X because we all expect everyone else to do X, and any one of us would be worse off doing something else. My friend has read the evolutionary stuff on top of that and thinks the case is closed. His line: conventions start as accidents, the useful ones get copied and the groups that have them outcompete the groups that don't, the useless ones die out. So a convention is just a regularity that survived, and the reason you feel you have to follow one is that deviating is a bad move — you lose the payoff, you stick out, people look at you funny.

I think he's missing something. Argue my side: the coordination-plus-survival story can't explain the authority conventions have, and it especially can't explain the cases where the payoff is gone, or was never there, and the thing still binds. Build the argument on these three cases:

1. A practice everyone still keeps even though whatever it was coordinating — a technology, a schedule, a shared danger — isn't around anymore. You can pick the example, but it has to be a real kind of case, not a made up one.
2. A "convention" with no payoff to coordinate on. Empty movie theater, everyone spreads out, you leave a seat or two between yourself and the next person, and sitting right down next to a stranger is wrong — but nobody loses anything if you do, no arrangement is better than any other, and you aren't giving up a payoff by breaking it. Same family of cases: standing in a line, the way you're expected to acknowledge a gift. If you think these are secretly coordination problems after all, good — then your job is to say why, and why that doesn't rescue him.
3. A convention two groups share but that means different things in each, so doing the thing isn't the same move on both sides.

So: (a) argue that these are real counterexamples and not just edge cases, (b) tell me what the story is missing — I think it's something about the convention becoming the standard other people now hold you to, so what makes it stick is that they've started expecting you to be the kind of person who does X, not any payoff you'd lose, but if there's a better mechanism, use it, and (c) argue that the evolutionary add-on makes things worse rather than better, because selection only sees payoffs and the thing we're trying to explain doesn't track payoffs.
