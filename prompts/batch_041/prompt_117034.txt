**Question-Drift: What Cross-Cultural Variation in Intuition Reports Is Evidence About**

**1. The setup.** Experimental probes of philosophical intuition present a case and ask a question. Where answers have been found to differ across cultural groups, two opposite conclusions have been drawn. The restrictionist says: intuitions vary, so they cannot be treated as data about knowledge, reference, causation, or responsibility, and the armchair method that treats them as data is parochial. The pluralist says: different groups operate with different concepts, so there is no single correct answer to be had, and philosophy must become comparative. I want to argue that both conclusions rest on a mistake about what the subjects in these studies are doing.

**2. Three loci of variation.** When a subject answers a question like "Does the agent know that p?" after reading a vignette, variation in the answer can originate in three different places. First, case-comprehension: the subject does not represent the case as the experimenter intended — she reads it as a story about a mistake, or about luck, or fails to register that the evidence was misleading. Second, question-selection: the subject represents the case correctly but takes a different question to be at issue — whether the agent is justified, whether she is blameworthy, whether it would be reasonable to rely on her, whether she is certain, whether her belief is knowledge in the sense of a claim she is socially licensed to make. Third, normative output: the subject represents the case correctly and identifies the same question, but applies a different standard to it, weighting luck or error differently.

Call variation of the second kind *question-drift*. My thesis is that the cross-cultural variation that has been reported is, in the main, variation of the first and second kinds rather than the third, and that this makes the data philosophically far less interesting than either camp needs it to be.

**3. Why question-drift is invisible.** The instrument fixes the question by fiat — the words on the page. But the words underdetermine the question, because the same sentence can be used to raise different questions, and which one a subject hears it as raising depends on what she takes the point of the exchange to be. Is this a test? A request for advice? An invitation to moralise? An exercise in applying a rule? The vignette supplies no information about its own purpose, because it is presented without one. The subject must supply a purpose, and supplying one is saturated with local norms about what questionnaires are for, what strangers asking about beliefs want, and when a claim to knowledge is the kind of thing one is entitled to make. So the instrument measures an answer to a question the experimenter did not control.

Note also that the standard remedy does not address this. Adding detail to the vignette fixes the case, not the question. If anything, more detail gives the subject more material from which to infer an unstated purpose.

**4. What follows.** If the variation is mostly of the first two kinds, then two things follow. The restrictionist inference fails: from the fact that answers vary it does not follow that the underlying competence varies, since the variation may be variance in the measurement rather than in the thing measured. The pluralist inference fails as well, and for a symmetrical reason: you cannot infer concept-variation from answer-variation when the question is uncontrolled, because to infer that two groups have different concepts of knowledge you need evidence that they answered the same question differently, and the data do not establish that they were answering the same question. So the empirical work is neither the refutation of armchair method that one side wants nor the vindication that the other wants. It is evidence about the pragmatics of questionnaires.

**5. Two concessions.** First, I do not claim that all reported variation is artifact. Some differences plausibly survive any amount of tidying, and my argument says nothing about how much variation would remain. Second, question-drift is itself a cultural fact of some interest: it tells us that what is being asked is not culturally invariant. But that is a fact about conversational norms, not about knowledge or reference or free will, and it is the sort of fact that belongs to survey methodology.

**6. Upshot.** Both camps resist this reading, because it makes the empirical literature inert with respect to the philosophical questions that motivated it. I take that resistance to be a reason for taking the reading seriously.
