Background you'll want: I spent 34 years as a dryland ecologist, most of it running field stations and collaborating with institutes in places where the customs office and the permitting office have alot of discretion over your life, and I've only gotten serious about philosophy since I retired — I sit in on a graduate political theory seminar these days and I keep noticing that the seminar's puzzles look exactly like the messes we used to argue about in grant meetings. For the last four years I've been on the integrity panel of a small international research funder: we make maybe forty grants a year, mostly to teams working where the grant is worth several years of a local salary, and when something gets flagged the panel has to say whether what happened counts as corruption and whether we sanction. So we have a track record, and I've started to suspect we dont have a rule, we have reflexes.

Here's the record. (1) A team in West Africa paid a customs officer about $300 in cash, in an envelope, to get a container of equipment released; the shipment was legal, it would have cleared eventually, the money came from the local institution rather than the PI, and nobody told us. We called it corruption and put the institution on probation for a year. (2) A PI in South Asia hired his wife's cousin as a field technician; no advertisement, no competition; the cousin turned out competent and is still there eight years later, and the PI mentioned it in his annual report. We said it was nepotism, noted it, and made no finding — no corruption. (3) A PI in Southeast Asia gave the ministry officer handling his permit a box of tea and a hamper at New Year, maybe $40 worth, while the permit was pending, and wrote it into the report. Not corruption: we said that's how the office works, and no decision was bought. (4) A US-based PI took around $15,000 in consulting fees from a company whose product was the subject of the grant and didn't disclose it; the chair called that 'corruption-adjacent misconduct' and we froze the grant. So two findings, two noes, and I genuinely can't tell you what they have in common.

Now the case in front of us. A team working where the permitting office has broad discretion and where everyone local agrees that hosting is simply how business is done: the PI's institution hosted the whole permitting office — nine people — at a banquet, and paid for three of them to fly out to the field site for two days to see the work. About $1,100, no cash to any individual, done openly, receipts attached to the report, and the permit would have been issued anyway, but this cut roughly two months off the wait. One panel member, a retired diplomat, says that finding against them is imposing a Western standard on a gift economy and that the real scandal is the 14-month wait; another says a benefit is a benefit, cash or canapés, and we're fooling ourselves if we pretend otherwise. I want to know what our own record commits us to. So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which earlier case is the closest analog, and which rule are we actually running? My hunch is that we've got three rules tangled up — the familiar one where corruption is any abuse of a position of trust for private gain, a functionalist one where only conduct that distorts the decision or degrades the institution counts, and a local-norms one where a benefit the officials themselves treat as ordinary etiquette isn't corruption — and that (1) and (2) each fit more than one of them while (3) is the one quietly doing the work. Tell me what each rule predicts about the banquet, which one our record best supports and which we should adopt, and — the part I'd most want — what the next case would have to look like for the rules to come apart, so that next time we're deciding instead of guessing.
