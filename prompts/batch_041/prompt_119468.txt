Ok, so I need a dialogue about Descartes and animals, and please, not the version everyone trots out — animals are machines, just clockwork, no souls, the end. The bit I actually find hard is the seam between sensation and thought. On one reading Descartes is happy to let animals have bodies that hurt and senses that register, while flatly denying them thought, reason, language and any sort of self-awareness. I want two people really going at that split. One of them thinks it holds up: there's a definate difference between feeling something and thinking about it, animals sit on the feeling side, and the Cartesian isn't being daft. The other thinks it collapses: a sensation that nobody has, that isn't anyone's experience, isn't really a sensation at all — it's just a mechanism, and calling it a sensation is wishful dressing-up, so Descartes is in fact denying animals any inner life whatsoever. Neither should be a strawman, and they should answer each other's actual moves rather than talking past one another. The defender needs to say what makes a sensation a sensation if it isn't its being thought; the critic needs to say what's supposedly missing in a creature that feels but never reflects. Have them test it on a concrete case or two — your pick, maybe an animal that seems to grieve or to scheme, or the modern parallel of a chatbot that talks a good game with nothing behind it — and then say where it leaves us on how we may treat them. I'd like it in British English, and as continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just the dialogue, the way you'd write it in an email.
