I am putting together the closing materials for a graduate seminar on memory, myth, and political identity, and the final session is on lost causes — the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk, and the like. I want to end with a poem that does not merely describe a lost cause but enacts the mechanism by which one is kept alive. The idea I want it to embody is what I have been calling the retrospective muster: a defeat is first suffered on the battlefield, and then suffered again — or rather, enlisted again — by the generations that inherit it. The retrospective muster is not simply a lie; it is a grammar of remembrance, a set of repeated words and gestures that allow the dead to be recruited into a story they never lived. The poem should make that grammar audible.

I would like a sestina, in the voice of a stonemason who is repairing a monument to a lost cause, perhaps in a town that no longer remembers which side it was on. The six end-words should be: stone, name, debt, field, flag, again. Let those words drift across the stanzas so that by the envoi they are no longer quite the words the mason started with; the repetitions should be the retrospective muster happening in front of us. Please avoid proper nouns and dates — I want the poem to work for any of the cases we have discussed, and I do not want it to depend on facts the reader would have to look up. The mason should be neither a debunker nor a sentimentalist; he should be someone who knows the historical record and still feels the pull of the myth, and the poem should leave that tension unresolved. A reader who knows the seminar material should be able to see the distinction between history and heritage at work in the form, while a reader who does not should still find a poem about a man and a stone.
