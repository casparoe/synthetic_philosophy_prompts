I have no academic training in either philosophy or history — I work as an incident investigator for an industrial insurer — but I have spent two years reading narrative history in the evenings, and I have begun to notice that historians seem to talk about causes in a way that my job does not permit, and I want to know whether the difference is real or whether one of us is confused.

My working vocabulary is narrow and I am fluent in it, since I use it daily: proximate cause, root cause, contributing factor, latent condition, safety barrier, single point of failure, the but-for test, fault trees, and the five whys. The whole apparatus is built to answer one question — what, had it been otherwise, would have kept this from happening — because that is the question the file has to answer: what to fix, and whom to hold responsible.

Here is the position I would like you to restate in those terms, as faithfully as you can: the claim, which I take to be common among philosophers of history, that a single event can have several causes each of which was on its own sufficient to produce it, so that no one of them was necessary; and that citing such a cause remains legitimate even though the but-for test fails for it. If a concrete case helps, use one of mine: a warehouse that burned after a pallet was left against a heater, where separately the sprinkler main had been valved off — either alone would have been enough.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of that position translate cleanly into my vocabulary? Which translate only with distortion — for instance, when I am forced to enter one chain as the root cause and the other as a contributing factor, which is what my report templates demand? And is there anything in the historians' position that my vocabulary cannot express at all: a way of explaining an event that is not aimed at control, or a notion of cause that does not answer to what could have been changed?

I would like you to take a view on the residue itself — is it a defect in my framework, or does it show that the historians' claim is vaguer than it sounds? Finish by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration would push you toward the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail, in particular whether the translation has quietly imported my framework's assumptions and then handed them back to me as facts about history.
