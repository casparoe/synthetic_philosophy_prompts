You are a philosopher of logic and epistemology with a specialization in classical Indian philosophy, especially Jain logic, and a second competence in the contemporary literature on truth-relativism, perspectival semantics, and indexical accounts of truth. Think carefully and reason in stages rather than delivering a verdict immediately.

I am preparing for a reading group on Jain anekāntavāda, the doctrine of many-sidedness, together with its two technical companions: nayavāda, the theory of partial standpoints or nayas, and syādvāda, the scheme of standpoint-qualified predication ordinarily marked by the qualifier syāt. To keep the discussion concrete, our group tried to reconstruct the doctrine as a standpoint-indexed semantics. Let S be the set of admissible standpoints; each standpoint assigns truth values to assertions. We then asked how an assertion could be true simpliciter, and generated three candidates: (A) true at every standpoint in S; (B) true at some standpoint in S; (C) true at the standpoint of the omniscient knower, which the tradition treats as the limit case of cognition.

The reconstruction then produced this argument, and I have not been able to see my way out of it.

(1) On (B), an assertion is true if any standpoint endorses it. Standpoints are cheap to generate: for any assertion, some perspective can be described from which it holds. So (B) makes every assertion and its negation true. Truth becomes trivial, and many-sidedness loses its critical edge, since it can no longer mark anything as mistaken — precisely the edge the tradition uses against one-sided views (ekānta).
(2) On (A), an assertion about a many-sided object is true only if every standpoint endorses it. But standpoints disagree about exactly such objects; that is the point of many-sidedness. So almost nothing is true, and the many-sidedness thesis itself is not true, since the one-sided theorist rejects it.
(3) On (C), truth is whatever the omniscient standpoint delivers. But we have no independent route to that standpoint, so the criterion cannot be applied; and if it could be, every ordinary naya would be a mere approximation to a single correct total view — the very one-sidedness the doctrine was meant to reject.
(4) So no standpoint-indexed semantics captures many-sided truth: (B) trivializes it, (A) falsifies it, (C) abandons it.
(5) Therefore anekāntavāda cannot be given a coherent semantics, and its apparent tolerance of competing views is either vacuous or covertly dogmatic.

If I have to give something up, I would rather keep the metaphysical half — the claim that reality has indefinitely many aspects, so that no single description exhausts it — than the semantic half according to which every assertion must be standpoint-qualified. But I want to know what I am paying.

Your task: diagnose the argument. Open with a brief brainstorm listing the two or three places where it might be thought to fail, without committing to one yet. Then name the step or assumption you would reject, explain why it looked compelling, and price the alternatives, including what the rejection costs the doctrine elsewhere. I expect the following considerations to bear on the diagnosis, though I cannot tell which is decisive: whether the dilemma depends on treating standpoints as unstructured and freely generable rather than as answerable to aspects of the object; whether the distinction between a partial standpoint and comprehensive valid knowledge, together with the treatment of an absolutized partial view as error rather than as one more legitimate perspective, blocks premise (1) or (3); whether folding nayavāda and syādvāda into a single semantics is itself the mistake; and whether the omniscient standpoint is being asked to serve as a definition of truth when its role should be only regulative. Finally, say what follows for the wider family: if the argument worked, would it also sink other standpoint-relative or perspectival accounts, and if so, is that a reductio of the argument or of the family?
