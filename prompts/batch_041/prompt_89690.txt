I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, and I am writing the essay section of the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on political concepts and their histories. I need one question on the transatlantic divergence in the meaning of 'liberal' — American usage picking out a left-of-centre, social-democratic position, continental usage picking out a market-oriented, limited-government one — that cannot be answered by reciting that contrast, and that forces students to make analytic choices rather than catalogue examples.

The question should make them decide what kind of divergence this is: one word with two senses, two words that merely look alike, or a single inherited label that different political coalitions have captured at different times, so that the interesting dispute is about ownership of the word rather than about its meaning. It should ask what follows for a case in which an American who calls herself a liberal and a German who calls himself a liberal each assert that liberalism is under threat: are they saying the same thing, are they contradicting one another, and is the shared word doing any work at all or is it a historical accident? It should require them to accommodate three awkward items: the Australian Liberal Party, which sits on the centre-right; the German Free Democratic Party, which is liberal in the market sense; and the asymmetry by which 'neoliberal' circulates in European and Latin American political speech almost exclusively as an accusation, while 'liberal' in American political speech functions as an epithet far more often than as a banner. It should also require them to say whether the term's career in international relations theory, where 'liberalism' names a research programme about cooperation and institutions rather than a domestic political family, is a fourth sense, a mere homonym, or evidence that the whole question is badly posed.

The examination is in class, seventy-five minutes, closed book, and the question is worth 40 marks. Give me the question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Present the model answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and key the marking scheme to those same five points: allocate the 40 marks across them, and for each point name the error that should cost a student marks and the move that separates a first-class answer from a mid-range one.
