Last night a friend and I had a long argument about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I have been stewing about it ever since. She reads widely and is usually reliable on this sort of thing, so I am reluctant to dismiss her, but something in her account struck me as too tidy and I could not articulate why.

Her summary, as best I can reconstruct it, was this. "The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are two versions of one and the same problem, and what they show is that it is impossible to be fully rational, because anyone who thinks carefully about her own beliefs will end up believing a contradiction. In the lottery case, you are justified in believing of each of a thousand tickets that it will lose, since each has a 99.9 percent chance of losing; but you are equally justified in believing that one ticket will win. So a perfectly rational person believes that ticket 1 loses, that ticket 2 loses, and so on through ticket 1000, and also believes that one of them wins. In the preface case, the author of a book believes every claim in it, and also believes, since no book is perfect, that at least one of those claims is false. Same structure, same contradiction. The lesson philosophers have drawn is that the conjunction principle — if you are rational in believing A and rational in believing B, then you are rational in believing A and B — simply has to be abandoned. That is why the sophisticated response is to stop talking about belief altogether and assign probabilities instead, since probabilities are not closed under conjunction and so are immune to the paradox."

I would like to know how much of this I should trust. Would you go through it essentially claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on these paradoxes would accept, what she would wince at as an oversimplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong? I am especially interested in whether the two paradoxes really are "one and the same problem," whether the diagnosis in terms of the conjunction principle is the one philosophers actually settle on or merely one contested option among several, and whether the closing recommendation — give up belief-talk, use probabilities — follows from anything established earlier. I am inclined to think she is right that there is a genuine and deep problem here, but I suspect she has collapsed a puzzle into a slogan, and I do not know enough to say where. Please be specific about which premise each paradox actually puts under pressure, and flag anything I should have pushed back on but did not.
