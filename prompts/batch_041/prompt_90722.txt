I was in a reading group on value theory last week, and a friend — a graduate student in economics who has been thinking about population ethics — laid out an argument he claims shows that "better than" cannot be transitive. I followed each step on its own, but the conclusion is one I can't bring myself to accept, and I can't tell whether he has made a mistake or whether I simply have to give something up. Here is the argument in the form he gave it.

(1) "Better than" is transitive: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. This is normally treated as part of what the phrase means; abandoning it seems to leave us unable to compare options that are not presented side by side. (2) Take a life that is extremely good overall — call it L0 — and a second life, L1, identical to L0 except that it contains one fewer mildly pleasant experience, or one additional moment of mild boredom, whichever you prefer. L0 is better than L1; a single such unit cannot outweigh everything else. (3) The same step can be repeated: L1 is better than L2, L2 is better than L3, and so on, each successive life being just one unit worse than its predecessor in the same respect. (4) Repeat the step enough times — say, a few million — and you arrive at L_n, a life containing almost nothing but mild discomfort and no genuine goods: a life most of us would call barely worth living, and clearly worse than L0. (5) By repeated application of transitivity in (1), L0 is better than L_n. (6) But that is absurd; a life of near-unrelieved mild misery is not better than a life of great flourishing. So one of the premises is false, and since (2) through (4) look like arithmetic, the culprit must be transitivity, (1). The conclusion, then, is that "better than" is not transitive.

That seems to prove far too much: if better-than is not transitive, a great deal of ordinary and theoretical reasoning about value, choice, and welfare falls apart. Yet I don't see how to keep transitivity without either swallowing (5) or insisting that there is some particular unit at which the comparison flips, which looks like drawing an arbitrary line in a sorites series. Tell me which premise or step I should reject, why it looked fine before I looked closely, and what rejecting it costs me. If you think the argument is valid and I simply have to choose which plausible claim to abandon, say which abandonment is least costly and why; if you think it contains a flaw, name the step and the precise nature of the error — an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a step that holds only under a reading the premises don't support. I would rather keep transitivity than accept the conclusion, but I want to know what I am paying for that. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or counterargument would move you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
