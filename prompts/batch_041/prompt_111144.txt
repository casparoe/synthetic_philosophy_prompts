Having spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, I have retired and begun reading philosophy in earnest, and there is a pattern of institutional behaviour that I lived inside for decades without ever inspecting. At the institution where I worked, a protocol involving fifty mice could not proceed until a committee had reviewed it and I had justified the number of animals, the severity of what would be done to them, and the method by which they would be killed. A protocol in the neighbouring building, which reared and destroyed several hundred thousand insects for an equivalent piece of work, required no review whatever. The statutory schemes under which I worked follow the same contour: they reach vertebrates, and in a few places octopuses and their relatives, and there they stop. When the committee did undertake to put a number on welfare, it used a scoring sheet on which the cost of a protocol was the number of individuals multiplied by a weight assigned to the species, with a further column for the probability that the species is sentient. Vertebrates received weights and a probability of one; taxa that were not listed were entered at zero, and a footnote described that entry as provisional pending further evidence. The institution's sustainability office, meanwhile, recommends insect protein in its catering as the low-suffering alternative to meat, on the ground that insects are probably not sentient.

I would like a reconstruction of the philosophical commitments this pattern presupposes. I am new enough to the subject that I would rather you name the positions than assume I already know them. First, what theory of moral status does the categorical line presuppose? Does it require a threshold view, on which sentience is all-or-nothing and gated on a certain kind of nervous system, or is it equally consistent with a graded view of sentience together with a decision rule that treats an unknown probability as zero? Second, which of the three elements — the categorical exemption, the zero entry on the scoring sheet, the catering recommendation — strictly presupposes that theory, and which are explained by something else entirely, such as administrative tractability, or the fact that a committee can only regulate what it can count and inspect? Third, is the package internally consistent? In particular, does describing the zero entry as provisional commit the institution to the claim that the probability is nil, and can it be coherent at one moment to discount a small probability of sentience by an astronomical number of individuals and at another to treat a small probability as decisive? Fourth, where is the practice underdetermined — that is, which rival theories of moral status would license exactly the same pattern, so that the pattern cannot tell us which one the institution actually holds?

Finally, I would like to know what the practice would have to become if the implicit theory were replaced by a precautionary expected-value view, on which a small probability of sentience multiplied by a vast number of individuals generates an enormous claim. Since the machinery for detecting and avoiding damage appears to be present well below the vertebrate line, is there any way of drawing the boundary that a biologist would not regard as arbitrary? And if the replacement view implies that very nearly everything human beings do to the insect world is a catastrophe, I would like to be told whether that is a reason to reject the view or a reason to change the practice.
