I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year on a book that moves between three cities: one in which the municipal administration has built an apparatus of continuous data collection of the kind one associates with contemporary China; one in which a constitutional court has made informational self-determination the organising principle of the state's relationship with the citizen; and one in which a newspaper would publish the contents of a mayor's inbox as a matter of routine. The research has made me insufferable at parties, but at a literary festival last month I met my match. A panellist shut down a question about whether a city ought to be permitted to correlate its residents' travel records with their medical records by saying, as though it settled the matter, that people who have done nothing wrong have nothing to hide and therefore nothing to fear. I could see the sentence doing work in the room — it shifted the burden of proof onto the person who had asked — but I could not say precisely what it was claiming, and I have been turning it over ever since.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a refutation. The slogan 'nothing to hide, nothing to fear' can be read as any number of distinct theses, and I should like the menu laid out: from the reading on which it is a bare tautology about guilt and punishment, through the readings on which it is a psychological prediction about how the innocent actually behave, a normative claim about what the innocent have reason to fear, a claim about the proper scope of privacy (that privacy protects only the concealment of wrongdoing), and a claim about where the burden of justification lies, up to the readings on which it is a substantive and rather sinister doctrine about the state's entitlement to look wherever it pleases. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, contestable but defensible, or simply false, and — this is the part I care most about — whether that reading would actually support the use the panellist made of it, namely treating the question as closed. I should also like each reading marked for what it presupposes about the relationship between citizen and state, and whether the three political traditions in my novel would each accept that presupposition or quietly reject it: the German one that treats informational self-determination as a right, the American one that has often preferred openness and publicity to secrecy, and the Chinese one in which the collection of data is bound up with a system of social credit. Where a reading turns out to be available in one tradition and unavailable in another, say so, and say what the difference consists in.

Please give me the result as a table rather than an essay. One row per reading, with these columns: 'reading', a one-sentence precisification of what the slogan is claiming; 'type', whether the claim is empirical, conceptual, normative or definitional; 'status', one of platitude, contestable or false; 'supports the panellist?', yes or no with half a line of justification; 'presupposes', what it assumes about the citizen-state relation; and 'tradition', which of the three traditions could sincerely assert it and which could not. After the table, add a short paragraph saying which reading you take the panellist most likely to have intended, and — since I would rather not build a novel on a misreading — what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to assign a reading a different status, or to judge that it does after all support the conclusion drawn from it, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be weakest. Please write in British English.
