I have been trying to understand why the scholarly and public argument over democratic backsliding has stalled, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another tour of the cases. Begin with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.

The debate I have in mind runs across comparative politics, constitutional law, and political commentary. One side treats democracy as a set of electoral procedures—free and fair elections, alternation in power, universal suffrage—and therefore asks whether a given country has crossed a threshold into competitive authoritarianism or full authoritarianism. On that view, Hungary after 2010, Poland under Law and Justice, Turkey after the failed coup, and the United States during and after the Trump presidency differ enormously: some are authoritarian, some are not, and calling all of them backsliding flattens the differences. Another side treats liberal rights, judicial independence, media pluralism, and minority protection as constitutive of democracy rather than as optional constraints on it, and therefore sees a common pattern of erosion in all four cases even where elections remain competitive. A third group suspects that the concept has become a partisan label: that it is applied to right-wing populists but not to comparable abuses by other governments, and that its apparent empirical content is doing less work than its rhetorical function. A fourth group thinks the real issue is measurement and authority: the indices disagree, the baselines are arbitrary, and we cannot say whether a country is backsliding without first fixing what democracy is, when it was healthy, and who gets to decide—comparativists, courts, international monitors, or domestic political actors. A fifth group, which I find hardest to place, says the concept is fine but the temporal frame is wrong—that what looks like backsliding is sometimes a failed attempt, sometimes a reversion after a previous deepening, and sometimes a normal oscillation that only looks linear when you pick the wrong starting point.

My hypothesis—offered as a hypothesis, not as a conclusion—is that the impasse is not primarily empirical. The sides agree on many of the events but disagree about what those events are events of: whether democracy is a threshold concept, a graded property, or a family of institutional arrangements; whether liberal rights are part of the definition or external constraints; whether the unit of analysis is the regime, the government, or the constitutional order; and whether the point of the concept is explanation or condemnation. If that is right, then the disagreement is partly verbal and partly substantive: verbal because different sides attach different senses to democracy and backsliding, substantive because those different senses generate different predictions and different practical recommendations. But I am not confident. It may instead be a hidden empirical dispute about whether democratic erosion actually has a common mechanism—executive aggrandizement, media capture, electoral manipulation—or a dispute about which cases count as data, or a normative dispute about whether a democratically elected government that violates liberal rights is failing democracy or fulfilling a majoritarian conception of it.

What I want from you is a diagnosis with reasons. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is: a verbal dispute about words, a substantive disagreement about facts, a disagreement about standards of evidence or about which cases are data, a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, a case of two sides answering different questions under one name, or an impasse that shows the question is ill-posed. Use what the sides actually say about Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States to support the diagnosis, and be concrete about where they part company. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a distinction, a measurement convention, a formal result, a change of question, a decision to split the concept into several—or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement and an honest answer about whether it can be resolved.
