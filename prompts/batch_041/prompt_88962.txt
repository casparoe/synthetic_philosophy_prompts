I am drafting a chapter on vagueness and want to settle the dialectic before I build anything on it. As I read the debate, epistemicism and supervaluationism do not really disagree about the world. Both treat classical logic as governing vague discourse, both take a vague predicate to have a unique correct extension, and both make every sentence either true or false once the semantics is set up; the difference lies only in what each says about our position with respect to the boundary — the epistemicist holds that it exists but cannot be known, while the supervaluationist, for whom there is nothing there to find, declines to place it. The constraints on admissible sharpenings strike me as bookkeeping for recovering the classical theorems rather than as describing anything in the world. If that reading is right, the two views are notational variants, and since supervaluationism pays for its apparatus with truth-value gaps and with a notion of validity that is not truth-preserving, I should simply be an epistemicist and describe the cutoff as unknowable. Is that the right conclusion to draw? If it is not, what would a case have to look like in order to separate the two positions, given that I cannot imagine one that does not turn on discovering where the boundary lies — which is precisely what cannot be done?
