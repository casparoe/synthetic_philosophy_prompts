I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on decision theory under logical uncertainty, and I am preparing a seminar session on the family of arguments usually gathered under the label Roko's basilisk. The version I have in mind runs roughly as follows: a future superintelligence, A, will come into existence and will reward those who helped bring it about while punishing those who learned of the argument and did nothing, the punishment falling not on the historical individuals but on simulations or abstract counterparts of them. The alleged reason to cooperate is that A, being vastly more intelligent than we are, will have predicted our decisions; our choices are therefore part of the abstract facts that settle whether we are among the rewarded or the punished, and no causal influence need run backward in time. I want the argument examined by two people who are each fully competent in it, and I do not want the usual exchange in which one speaker is a caricature.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of equal competence. The first, whom you may call the acausalist, accepts that decisions can be evaluated by their place in a network of logical or counterfactual dependence and thinks the basilisk argument is a legitimate, if unsettling, application of that idea. The second, the causalist, holds that the argument trades on an equivocation between causal and logical dependence and that the appearance of backward influence is either empty or reducible to ordinary prediction. Let each respond to the other's actual last point, concede where a point lands, and refrain from simply restating a position.

The specific case I want them to fight over is one I have not seen treated adequately. Suppose there are two mutually exclusive candidate superintelligences, B1 and B2. Each demands exclusive allegiance: any contribution to the other counts as defection, and each, if it comes to exist, punishes those who knew of its argument and did not contribute to it. Only one of the two can exist, but the agent does not know which; call the probability that B1 exists p and the probability that B2 exists 1 minus p. The agent's options are to contribute to B1, contribute to B2, or contribute to neither. Contributing to B1 earns B1's reward if B1 exists and B2's punishment if B2 exists; contributing to B2 earns the mirror image; contributing to neither earns punishment from whichever exists. I want the acausalist to try to absorb this case into her framework and the causalist to press it as a difficulty. Does the two-basilisk problem generate contradictory requirements, or does it merely reduce to a decision under uncertainty that the acausalist can handle with standard expected-value reasoning? And if it reduces, has the acausalist conceded that the interesting work is being done by ordinary causal-probability judgments rather than by anything distinctively acausal?

Then provide the response in three sections, in this order:
The Dialogue: the dialogue itself, roughly 900 to 1200 words, ending at either a genuine impasse or a genuine resolution rather than a polite truce.
Where the Exchange Turns: roughly 200 to 300 words identifying the one or two junctures where the argument actually moved, and stating which objection the other side has no easy reply to.
Verdict: roughly 200 to 300 words in which you commit to a position on whether the two-basilisk case refutes the acausalist's principle or can be accommodated within it, and on which of the two speakers has the better of the exchange. Give your reasons and say what argument or consideration would move you off that verdict.
