Ok so I've been down a rabbit hole on whether "better than" is transitive and I want to actually build a case instead of just reading about them. Heres the spec.

I want a thought experiment with exactly three options, A, B and C, where A is better than B, B is better than C, and C is better than A. The catch: it has to be robustly intransitive, not sorites-flavored. No vagueness, no heaps, no "a tiny improvement cant make a difference", no chain of a thousand steps where each link is a near-tie. Each of the three pairwise comparisons has to be a clear win, the kind where someone who shrugged and said "eh, close call" would look like they were being difficult on purpose.

And no cheating by stipulating the ranking. The intransitivty has to fall out of a particular account of what makes one option better, stated up front and independently motivated. Heres the account I want you to try, because I think it's underexplored: treat each option as a bundle that scores on several seperate dimensions of welfare (say health, relationships, meaningful work, security, whatever — you pick, and justify the picks), and say that option X is better than option Y just when X wins on more of the dimensions. Majority rule over dimensions, nothing fancier. If that turns out to be too easy or too obviously broken, fine, tell me and swap in the nearest thing that does work, but say what you swapped and why.

Other constraints: the three options have to be things a real person could actually choose between — a job, a city, a treatment plan, a place to live — not three abstract vectors. One paragraph for the scenario itself, then the analysis. The dimensions all have to be ones where more is genuinely better, no "the optimal amount of friendship is three" tricks, and all three options have to be evaluated from one standpoint at one time, so no preference change over the years and no ignorance doing the work.

What it's for: I'm arguing with a friend who says any allegedly intransitive set of preferences just proves you didn't specify the options properly, and that transitivity is basically analytic for "better than". So the case has to be one where all three options are fully determinate and every pairwise comparison is something a reasonable person would accept on the merits, not something I've engineered by rigging a definition.

After the scenario: give the pairwise verdicts with the dimension-by-dimension breakdown, then tell me which structural feature of the setup is actually doing the work. My hunch is it's the aggregation across dimensions rather than anything about the dimensions themselves, but I want to know if that's right, and whether you can get the same cycle with a weaker assumption — like just requiring that the dimensions not be commensurable, without the majority rule. Then the collapse tests: whats the minimal change that makes it transitive again (one common currency? letting a single dimension dominate lexically? averaging instead of voting? giving the agent a discount rate?), and does the case survive if the agent is uncertain about how much each dimension matters to them.

Last thing, steelman the other side. If you think betterness just has to be transitive, give me the best line of attack on my case — e.g. that one of the three "clear wins" is really a parity or incommensurability judgment dressed up as a ranking, or that majority-over-dimensions isn't a theory of betterness at all but of something else (preference, choiceworthiness, overall desirability) — and then say whether the attack lands or just relocates the problem.
