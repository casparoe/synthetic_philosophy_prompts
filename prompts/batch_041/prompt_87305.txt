"Explain how money-pump (coherence) arguments are supposed to show that an agent who violates the axioms of expected utility theory is irrational, and assess whether they really succeed in showing this."

Here's what my mate handed in:

"The money-pump argument is meant to show that if your preferences don't satisfy the expected-utility axioms, you can be led to give away money for nothing. The clearest case is intransitivity. Suppose I prefer tea to coffee, coffee to water, and water to tea. Since I prefer tea to coffee, I'll pay 10p to swap my coffee for tea. Since I prefer coffee to water, I'll pay 10p to swap my water for coffee. Since I prefer water to tea, I'll pay 10p to swap my tea for water. I'm back where I started and 30p down. Each trade was one I preferred at the time, so by my own lights I've ended up worse off, which is supposed to be impossible for a rational agent. So intransitive preferences are irrational. The same trick generalises: the other axioms are supposed to be necessary in the same way, because violating any of them — completeness, say, where you simply can't compare two options — leaves you open to a similar series of trades.

So the axioms aren't just a tidy mathematical set-up, they're constraints a rational agent has to obey on pain of being exploitable. And von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that an agent who obeys all of them can be represented as maximising expected utility, so the argument gets you all the way to expected utility maximisation being the uniquely rational policy.

The obvious objection is that all this shows is that certain preferences are exploitable, not that they're irrational. But I think that's a distinction without a difference: if you can be made worse off by your own lights through trades you yourself accept, then something has gone wrong with your preferences, and 'exploitable' is just the name we give to that. The only real gap is that the pump assumes you don't see it coming. A rational agent would look ahead, notice where the sequence ends up, and refuse to start — but that can't be right, because then the argument would also condemn perfectly ordinary preferences that change as you go along."
