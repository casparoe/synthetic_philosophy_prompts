I am a philosopher of mind and an amateur composer, and next week I am giving a forty-minute talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on creativity. The audience is mostly cognitive psychologists who study analogy, conceptual combination, and transfer; a few music theorists who work on variation form and thematic transformation; and two AI researchers who build generative models and will think in terms of latent spaces. I am defending a thesis about the knob-twiddling picture of creativity associated with Hofstadter. The picture says that creativity is largely a matter of taking a theme—a melody, a concept, a proof, a game—and varying it along dimensions, turning knobs, exploring a space of possibilities. I agree that this captures a lot of creative work, but I argue it mistakes a symptom for the mechanism. The mechanism I want to defend is representational fluidity: the capacity to treat any feature of a representation as a dimension along which to vary, including features that were not dimensions before. Knob-twiddling presupposes a space of knobs; creativity sometimes consists in building the space. On my view, this explains why creative variation is domain-general without being domain-neutral, and why the knob-twiddling model fails for radical novelty: when the dimensions themselves are created, there are no knobs to twiddle yet. I also want to distinguish representational fluidity from mere analogical mapping and from latent-space interpolation, because I know the AI people in the room will collapse the distinction if I let them.

I need a Q&A prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the thesis. Please act as a seasoned interdisciplinary chair who has watched many such talks go badly. First, brainstorm the objections that each constituency will actually raise, in their own vocabulary: the cognitive psychologists will say X, the music theorists will say Y, the AI researchers will say Z. Then order the objections by how much damage they would do to my position if I fumble them, and for each give me a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, and a sentence on what I should concede up front rather than wait to be forced. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the load-bearing assumption in my distinction between twiddling knobs and building the space—and tell me why it is dangerous. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or bibliography; I cannot look anything up and the room does not care about names. Phrase each objection the way the relevant audience member would phrase it, not in generic philosophy-speak.

Think carefully and start with a short brainstorming section before the prep sheet. Assume the audience is smart, skeptical, and not hostile to the general idea; they are hostile to being told that their own specialty is a special case of someone else's. The psychologists think creativity is analogy and combination, the music theorists think variation is a historically specific practice, and the AI researchers think a sufficiently rich latent space already does everything I am describing. I need to know where each of them is right, where representational fluidity is doing real explanatory work rather than relabeling, and which of the three objections is most likely to be fatal. Be concrete: use examples from music, from conceptual combination, and from generative modeling, but do not rely on any example whose details I would have to look up. End with a one-page summary titled If You Only Remember Three Things, for the five minutes before I walk in.
