I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, working on theory change, and I keep meeting a comparison in my reading group that I cannot decide whether to trust. A colleague defends structural realism — the view that we should be realists about the mathematical or relational structure that successive theories share while suspending belief in the unobservable entities those theories posit — by likening it to the Ship of Theseus. On the Theseus picture, the planks of the old theory are its entities: the luminiferous ether, the caloric fluid, phlogiston, each pulled out and replaced by the entities of the successor theory. What remains, and what makes the ship still the same ship, is its form — the set of structural relations that the old and the new theory have in common — and it is this surviving form, rather than the discarded planks, that is supposed to explain both the older theory's predictive successes and the realist's lack of embarrassment about the graveyard of discarded entities. My colleague treats the analogy as doing real work: the conclusion — realism about structure, agnosticism about entities — is said to follow from the mere fact that form persists while matter does not.

I would like an audit rather than a defence or a refutation. Please set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the planks, of the ship's form, of the shipwright, of the voyage and the harbour, and of the question 'is it the same ship?'. Then say where the analogy breaks in ways that matter for the work it is being asked to do. I have three suspicions of my own: first, that the ship's form is not a given but something we can always abstract after the fact, so that any two theories can be described as sharing some structure if we are free to choose the description, in which case the surviving form may be doing no explanatory work at all; second, that theory change is seldom the gradual replacement of one plank at a time but is often a wholesale rebuilding, with the form itself revised; and third, that identity for a ship is a matter of spatiotemporal continuity, whereas the realist's question is about reference and the retention of content, which may be a quite different relation. Please separate the disanalogies that are fatal to the inference from those that are merely picturesque, and tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is used to license survives once they are on the table. If a better comparison exists, or if the analogy should simply be abandoned, say so, and say why.

Please write in British English and keep the whole answer to at most 650 words; a correspondence table followed by the discussion is welcome.
