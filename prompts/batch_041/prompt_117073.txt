I'm a third-year student in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of biology and mind, and one of our assignments is to draft a candidate final-exam question, along with a model answer and a marking scheme, that the instructor may adopt for the real exam. My assigned topic is the notion — associated with Dennett's work on natural selection — of a free-floating rationale: the idea that there can be reasons in nature before there is anything around to represent them. What I want, and can't seem to write myself, is a question that can't be answered by reciting what the notion is supposed to be. The marks should turn on whether a candidate can take a defensible position on whether the notion earns its explanatory keep.

Please write three things.

First, one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes, built around the following invented case, which the candidate should be told is hypothetical. The lamp shrimp carries a patch of reflective tissue on its tail. In Population A the patch's reflectance is a fixed byproduct of the tissue's structure; it happens to flash light into a predator's eyes in a way that, on average, buys the shrimp time to escape, but the shrimp has no sensor for predators and no internal state that varies with the patch's effect. In Population B a lineage of the same animal has acquired a photoreceptor that detects an approaching predator and drives the patch's reflectance up and down accordingly, so that the flashing now occurs just when a predator is near. In both populations the trait is stably maintained, and in both it is the sort of thing we are tempted to explain by saying that the flashing is for escaping predators.

The question should require the candidate to: (i) say exactly what is supposed to make the rationale free-floating in Population A, as distinct from the behavior merely having a cause; (ii) decide whether the arrival of the sensor in Population B dissolves the free-floatingness of the rationale, and if so for whom — the shrimp, the predator, or nobody; (iii) consider a variant in which the deterrence in A arose once, in a single shrimp, as a developmental accident, and say whether there is still a free-floating rationale there, or whether the notion presupposes a history of selection; and (iv) take a stand on whether the notion does explanatory work or merely redescribes causal facts in teleological language. It's a closed-book exam and no citations are expected; the course has covered teleosemantics (proper functions, consumers) and the intentional stance, so the question may presuppose those resources without requiring any particular text.

Second, a model answer of about 700 words that argues a definite line rather than surveying the options — it should be the answer I'd have written if I understood the topic better than I do.

Third, a marking scheme out of 30 marks, broken into named must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates get it wrong — including at least one distractor move that looks sophisticated but should cost marks, such as sliding from the predator's detecting the signal to the shrimp's representing a reason.
