Ok so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm writing a book where one of my characters is a logician who is insufferable at dinner parties. I want her rants to actually be right, and I keep tripping over one thing.

I get the Liar — the sentence that says of itself that it's false, and how it explodes if you insist every sentence is either true or false. Fine, I get it. What I don't get is why its twin gets treated as a joke. You know the one: the sentence that says of itself only that it is true. Everyone I ask shrugs and says oh, that one's harmless, it's just underdetermined, it could be either way. But its the same move, isn't it — a sentence whose truth value is fixed by nothing outside itself? So compare the two for me. Which one is actually the deeper problem for a theory of truth? And the bit I really care about: if you build a theory that handles the Liar, does the Truth-teller come along for free, or do the two need completely different machinery? It seems like the Liar at least slams into a wall when you flip it, while the Truth-teller just flips forever and never hits anything, and I want to know whether that difference is doing real work or whether it's just aesthetic.

Is that pair a Liar in disguise or its own animal? And what about the cheerful version, where each card says the other one is true?

Be straight with me about what's settled here among the people who actually work on this and what's genuinely still up for grabs.
