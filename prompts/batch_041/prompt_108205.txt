I've come around to the worst-case framing of AI alignment — a system counts as aligned only if it is safe against the worst admissible environment, not merely on the distribution it was trained and evaluated on — but I want to hold it the way I'd hold any view I actually care about, by knowing what would kill it. So: what would count against it? Please disaggregate by which sense of "worst case" is doing the work — worst case over inputs at deployment, over training distributions, or over the space of possible agents, goals, and contexts — because I suspect the position borrows its air of rigor from the first two while actually resting on the third, where it becomes far less clear what a guarantee even is. For each sense, tell me what would have to be true of the world for the demand to be satisfiable, and what should instead convince me it is vacuous (nothing we can build meets it) or irrelevant (the worst cases are measure-zero corners no real deployment visits — the way adversarial robustness began as a worst-case program and drifted empirical). I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, an honest account of which of them a committed worst-case theorist would themselves concede, and a verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, for a position that deliberately aims at guarantees rather than probabilities, whether that insulation is a feature or the entire problem.
