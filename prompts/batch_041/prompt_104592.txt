For several years now I've been trying to convince myself that the boundary problem in democratic theory -- the question of who gets to be in the demos that decides -- is not just analogous to but actually a special case of the indexical/self-locating problem in decision theory, and that treating it that way does real work rather than being a cute reframing. I'm presenting the current version of that argument at a two-day political theory workshop next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet built for the exact room I'll be walking into, not for the internet.

The room: about a dozen political theorists. Most are deliberative democrats who think legitimacy runs through actual or hypothetical consent among participants; two are neo-republicans who care about non-domination and are suspicious of anything that sounds like it lets a demos define its own membership; a couple work on the all-affected principle and will press me on why "affected" needs a standpoint at all; and there is one decision theorist from the local formal epistemology group who does anthropic and self-locating reasoning and will be by far the most dangerous questioner. None of them read rationalist blogs and none of them will be flattered by the vocabulary I use in my own notes.

Here is the argument they'll be attacking, so the objections have something to bite on:

1. A boundary rule is a function from a description of a situation to the set of persons who constitute the demos for a given decision.
2. Non-indexical boundary rules -- rules that pick out the demos without reference to any particular standpoint -- face a trilemma. Territorial rules presuppose the very demos they claim to generate. All-affected rules are underdetermined, because "affected," in kind and degree, is itself standpoint-relative. And any rule that looks neutral either smuggles in an unacknowledged standpoint or collapses into the first horn.
3. So boundary rules must be indexical: they answer "who is the demos, from where I stand?" rather than "who is the demos?"
4. Indexical boundary rules are exactly the object of study in the self-locating problem: what an agent should do depends on which position she occupies, and there is no standpoint-neutral fact about which demos she is in.
5. Therefore the boundary problem should be handled with the tools of indexical decision theory, and any legitimacy claim that presupposes a determinate, already-given demos is confused.
