Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session for a reading group of philosophy graduate students who have had a standard course in modal logic but have never met epistemic logic. The topic is the problem of logical omniscience: on the usual possible-worlds treatment an agent knows every logical truth and everything entailed by what she knows, which no finite reasoner does. I know the problem; what I want is a plan for teaching it. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and why — in particular, whether to build the semantics first and let the idealisation fall out of it, or to open with a case of someone plainly failing to know an obvious consequence of what she knows and only then construct the semantics that cannot represent her.

Choose the single carrying example to build the session on, and say what it must have and what it must lack: it should be one my students can work through with nothing beyond propositional logic, and it should yield a case where the agent knows the premises, the inference is valid but long or non-obvious, and she plainly does not know the conclusion, so that they feel the pull of the semantics and of the intuition at once. Say why the textbook illustrations — knowing all the axioms of arithmetic, knowing every theorem of a formal system — are the wrong choice here.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect: that logical omniscience is a harmless idealisation like a frictionless plane; that the repair is simply to restrict the worlds the agent cannot distinguish; that an agent who fails closure must have inconsistent beliefs; that impossible worlds are just possible worlds with a different accessibility relation. For each, say where in the sequence it will surface, whether it is a misreading of the formalism or a substantive position worth taking seriously, and exactly where I should head it off — and where I should instead let it stand and flag it as a live option.

I also want to introduce one thing they will not have met: that a reasoner can fail to know a consequence in two quite different ways — through a failure of awareness (she has never entertained the proposition) and through a failure of computation (she has entertained it and has the resources, but has not yet derived it). They should leave able to say which standard repairs address which failure, and to see that a repair aimed at awareness can leave computation untouched.

Finish by giving me the one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the shape of the problem rather than memorised a menu of solutions, and say what a good and a bad answer would look like; and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes.
