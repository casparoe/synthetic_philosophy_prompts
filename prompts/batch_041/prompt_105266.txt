Ok so I've somehow ended up running the "logic and reasoning" track of our local rationality reading group, and next month its my turn to set the written exercise. I want to do Carroll's tortoise, but every write-up of it I can find is the same three paragraphs about how rules of inference aren't premises followed by a hand-wave, and I don't want people just regurgitating that. I need the exercise to actually seperate the people who get it from the people who skimmed the wiki page.

Here's the setup I want the question built around. Keep it self-contained — I don't want it leaning on any real published positions, so don't have it demand names or citations; I want moves, not name-dropping. The tortoise accepts "P", accepts "P→Q", and won't accept "Q", and whenever you hand it the conditional that would license the step it just asks for that one as a premise too, forever. That's the standard bit. What I want to add is a second character — call her the mechanic — who says: fine, the tortoise is just a machine that prints "accept P", "accept P→Q", and never prints "accept Q", and if that's all "accepting" means then no mistake is being made, the tortoise is just a different kind of machine. And a third, the judge, who says no — "accepting P and P→Q" isn't a *description* of an internal state, it's a commitment, and the tortoise is violating it.

I want one essay question, ~65 minutes, upper-undergrad / smart-reading-group level, that forces the student to (i) say precisely what the tortoise is demanding, and why the usual "rules aren't premises" line answers only one version of that demand and not the other; (ii) adjudicate between the mechanic and the judge — is "accepting a rule of inference" a descriptive fact about a system or a normative commitment, and can a creature that behaves like the tortoise be *wrong* rather than merely different; (iii) say whether the regress is vicious; and (iv) not punt with "modus ponens is just self-evident" or "we're just wired that way" without saying what work those lines are supposed to do. The whole point is to make them commit to a reading of what the demand *is* before they answer it — if they answer some version of the demand they never named, they should lose marks even if the answer is fine on its own terms.

Then give me a model answer, about 600 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, 4 marks each (20 total), plus for each point one line on how a student typically botches it. Keep the model answer honest about where its stipulating rather than deriving — I'd rather it say "I'm reading the tortoise as X, and on that reading..." than pretend there's a consensus on this.

And end the whole response with a last line \answer{...} containing just the single distinguishing move — a short phrase, under 15 words — that a top answer makes and a mediocre one misses.
