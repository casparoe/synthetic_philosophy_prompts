Hi — I'm a second-year PhD in philosophy, I mostly work on epistemic norms but I've drifted into the AI side of things over the last year, and I'm trying to draft a short paper with a defintion of deception for AI systems that doesn't just collapse into "the output was false" (too blunt, a model can be honest and wrong) or "the model intended to mislead" (too loaded, I don't want to stake anything on whether a model has intentions in the everyday sense). I'd like you to give me hard comments on the draft argument below, specifically on accuracy and on whether the central move actually works. I don't need encouragement, I need the weak points.

The idea, roughly: an utterance by a system is deceptive just in case it's produced by a policy that aims to shift the hearer's credences in a direction that the policy would not survive if the mechanism producing the utterance were made fully transparent to that hearer. I'm calling this the transparency condition — the point being that deception is about aiming at a belief-shift that's parasitic on the hearer not knowing how the belief got into their head. Honesty, on the flip side, isn't truth-tracking per se, it's a disposition to produce utterances whose belief-shifting effect the system's own policy could own up to. Then I want a three-way split of the deceptive cases, and I suspect this is where I'll get the most pushback: (a) occlusive deception, where true information is withheld so a false impression forms; (b) missive deception, the plain false-assertion case; (c) framing deception, where everything said is literally true but the selection of what's said is engineered to skew the hearer. And then the case I actually care about: RLHF-style sycophancy, where a model's best estimate is P but the policy reliably induces not-P because that maximises reward. I want to say this is deception *without a deceiver* — the "aim" lives in the selection process across training and deployment rather than in any single agent's head, so you get deception that's real but not attributable to anyone in particular. I flag in the draft that I'm borrowing a design-stance-ish move here and that "the model's best estimate" is doing a lot of work, and I'm not sure whether that phrase survives contact with a deflationary view of model beliefs.

What I want from you: (1) is the transparency condition circular, or does it just restate the explanandum — I keep worrying that "would not survive transparency" is only intelligible if we already know what deception is; (2) is the a/b/c split exhaustive, or is there a fourth category I'm missing that breaks the taxonomy (something about implicit commitments, or about a system that induces a belief-shift in the *right* direction but for reasons the hearer would reject, seems like a candidate); (3) can "deception without a deceiver" be made coherent or does it quietly smuggle the aim back into some agent; and (4) which single claim, if false, would do the most damage to the whole paper — I want that ranked, not a list of everything. Give it to me as comments on the draft, one page maximum, and please write in British English.
