I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and every year I get roped into giving the faculty address at our department's graduation. I've been thinking alot about how to actually use it for something this year. The hook I keep coming back to is vagueness: whether "ready" — ready to be a philosopher, ready for whatever's next — works the supervaluationist way or the epistemicist way. On the first, there's no fact of the matter about the kids in the borderline zone, and every admissible precisification agrees they're fine; on the second, there is a fact of the matter and they'll simply never learn where the line fell. I want the speech to commit to one of those and let it constrain what I'm allowed to tell them, rather than just name-dropping the distinction as decoration. And whatever I say has to survive a careful philosopher's scrutiny on both views — including the higher-order stuff about the boundary between the borderline and the definitely-not being itself vague, which is exactly where a graduation speech would be tempted to cheat.

You're a philosopher of language who also writes speeches for a living. Before you draft, spend a few lines thinking through what each of the two views actually licenses me to say to a room of twenty-two-year-olds, then write the address. Five to seven minutes. The audience is about two hundred people: mostly parents and grandparents who've never heard of a sorites series, plus four or five majors who will catch every word, so it has to land for both at once. Warm, not a lecture, no footnotes, and please don't wriggle out of it by hedging — pick a side and let it shape the whole thing.
