so I'm a third-year PhD student in phil of physics and I've been roped into running a film night plus discussion for the intro phil sci undergrads. I picked Tenet because I wanted an excuse to make them think about the arrow of time, and now I'm worried I've picked something that'll teach them alot of stuff that isn't true. Help me work out the reading before I stand in front of forty undergrads.

The frame I want to use: the micro-laws are basically time-reversal invariant (Loschmidt's old objection to Boltzmann — reverse all the velocities and you get another legal solution), so the arrow can't live in the laws, it has to come from a boundary condition, i.e. the low-entropy past hypothesis, and everything we care about — records, memory, causation, the sense that the future is open — is downstream of that rather than being a seperate arrow of its own. I also want them to get suspicious of the double standard where we explain why the past is fixed and the future isn't by appeal to traces and records that only exist given the very asymmetry we're trying to explain.

What does the film do with this? A machine, the turnstile, inverts a thing's entropy: inverted bullets fly backwards, inverted people live through their own time forwards while moving backwards relative to us, and a dying future sends inverted objects back into our present, eventually aiming to invert the whole world's entropy with the Algorithm. My questions:

Is inverting a thing's entropy even coherent, or is the film treating entropy as a kind of charge you can flip on an object? A subsystem can spontaneously drop in entropy, but that's a fluctuation, and it doesn't give you a local arrow pointing the other way. Where does the film slide between (a) a worldline running the other way in time, (b) reversed entropy, and (c) reversed subjective experience — and are those even separable, or does the plot quietly need all three at once?

The turnstile is a machine, so someone is imposing a low-entropy boundary condition on a subsystem from outside. Is that a decent intuition pump for the past-hypothesis picture, or does it cheat by making the arrow locally engineerable and controllable, which the statistical story doesn't obviously license?

Interaction: if your entropy runs backwards, can you breathe our air, talk to us, remember what we'd call the future? (I think there's some masks-and-inverted-air business in the film, which suggests Nolan was at least half-aware of the problem.) And if inverted people form records at all, those records run the wrong way relative to ours — shouldn't cross-inversion communication be incoherent? This feels like the deepest place the film notices a problem and papers over it, but tell me if I'm wrong.

The film is hard determinist about the past — nothing anyone does changes anything, and the temporal pincer is built on that. Is that a consequence of its physics or an extra premise? It smells like the Lewis point that counterfactual asymmetry comes from overdetermination, which is itself downstream of the entropy gradient, so the film might be right for reasons it never states.

Then give me the actual reading, in both directions: which clips to show and what to say about each, where the film dramatizes something the past-hypothesis literature has a hard time making intuitive, and where I have to stop it and say no, that isn't what a local entropy reversal would be. Don't worry about quoting lines, I just want the argument. If the honest verdict is that it's too muddled to teach with, say so — but I'd rather find a way to use it, because they'll actually watch it.
