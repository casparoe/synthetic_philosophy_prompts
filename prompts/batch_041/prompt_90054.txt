I'm a third-year student taking a seminar on rational choice and decision theory, and the last few weeks have been devoted to money-pump arguments and the diachronic dimension of rationality — a topic my flatmates and I have been arguing about past midnight, which is where this request comes from. The puzzle, as I understand it, is that if your preferences cycle (you would trade A for B, B for C, and C for A, each swap sweetened with a small side-payment), then a patient counterparty can walk you around the loop and set you back down where you began, a little poorer, with every individual step being one you would endorse at the moment you take it. What interests me is less the arithmetic than the temporal structure: a synchronic version, in which the whole package is offered at once, is easy to refuse, whereas the diachronic version, in which the trades arrive one at a time and each is defensible on its own, is far harder to resist even when you can see the loop coming. Then there is the menu of responses people have proposed — being naive and never anticipating the trap; being sophisticated and working backward from where the chain ends; being resolute and holding to a plan even when your present self would rather defect; or refusing to rank certain options against one another at all, so that there is no loop to walk in the first place, which seems to block the pump only by conceding that some things cannot be compared. I am also drawn to the neighbouring difficulty, of the kind raised by Kavka's toxin puzzle, of intending an act you know you will not end up performing.

Could you write me a short story that treats this material as fiction rather than as proof — something set over a single night in a mid-sized city, centred on one person, and built around a pump that is diachronic and social rather than formal? I would like whoever leads her around the loop to be a plausible human being with plausible motives — a regular customer, a neighbour, someone she is fond of — so that the pressure comes from each step being genuinely reasonable rather than from a trick. If you can, make the trades souring ones: each should leave her worse off in every respect she cares about, not merely in some currency she is indifferent to, since a pump that trades on a dimension she does not value would be too easy to wave away. Give her one of the escape routes — resolve, precommitment, or a refusal to compare — and then let the story test it honestly: show whether it actually saves her or merely relocates the cost, and leave it genuinely open whether the fix that blocks the pump is one she can live with. I do not want the story to end by announcing a moral; I would rather the philosophy live in the mechanics of the exchanges and in what she notices, or fails to notice, about her own preferences along the way.
