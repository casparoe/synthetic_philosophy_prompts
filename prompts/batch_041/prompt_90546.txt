In our jurisprudence seminar a colleague has been ruling, case by case, on whether a morally defective norm still counts as law, and I want to predict what she will say about the next one. Her verdicts so far: an unpublished decree issued by a subordinate body beyond its authority and retroactively stripping a religious minority of its property — not law; a properly enacted, public, prospective statute creating a public health scheme — law; a validly enacted but hopelessly vague statute that in practice licensed arbitrary detention — not law; a clear, prospective, properly enacted tax increase — law. The case she takes up next is formally impeccable — validly enacted, published, prospective, general, and perfectly clear — but it revokes the citizenship of a named minority and authorizes their indefinite detention. This is the old positivism-versus-natural-law question, and I want more than a label. At least two rules fit everything she has said so far: that legal validity tracks the formal virtues of legality (generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity), and that it tracks a substantive moral floor. Say what each predicts about the new case, which you would bet on and why, whether a third rule fits her record equally well, and which further case would tell the surviving rules apart.
