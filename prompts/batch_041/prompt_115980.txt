I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar that puts demographers and philosophers of social science in the same room, and I need one classification instrument for a question that both groups keep talking past: are the generations we name — boomers, millennials, Gen Z, and whoever comes next — real kinds, or artifacts of our labeling practices? Please build me a decision tree, not a list of positions. The branch nodes should be the questions on which the views actually divide, each phrased so a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to; the leaves should be named positions, each carrying one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

The tree should run through at least the following disputes, though you should add, cut, or reorder nodes if the logical space demands it:

- whether a generation-term is doing causal work or interpretive work;
- whether a generation is constituted by a shared formative experience, by mere co-membership in a birth-year band, or by the members' own self-ascription;
- whether the birth-year boundaries must be non-arbitrary for the kind to be real, and what non-arbitrary could even mean here;
- whether an observed generational difference can be decomposed into age, period, and cohort effects, and what follows for the reality of the kind if it cannot be;
- whether a label that people internalize and then act on is real in virtue of being self-fulfilling, and whether that is the same sense of real the other branches are trading in.

Three distinctions I want the tree to incorporate explicitly, because the usual surveys skate past them:

1. The difference between what I will call upstream and downstream generations: whether the kind is defined by what happens to a cohort (a shared imprint) or by what the cohort does (a shared response). Ask whether these can come apart — a cohort that shares an imprint but responds in scattered ways, or responds in concert without sharing an imprint — and let positions be located by which one they treat as canonical.

2. So-called indexical generations: terms whose extension is fixed by the speaker's own birth date, as in my generation, and whether they function differently in kind from names like Gen Z that purport to pick out a determinate slice of the population. Include a branch that asks what the referent of my generation is supposed to be.

3. Self-fulfilling generational labels, and whether a kind that exists only because it is believed in belongs on the same tree as kinds that would exist unlabeled. Treat this as a genuine branch rather than a footnote to the self-ascription question.

One constraint on the leaves: since my readers will not be looking anything up, no leaf may depend on a specific empirical claim about how actual birth cohorts differ. Keep every leaf about what a generation would have to be, not about which ones there are. Name positions by their commitment rather than by an author where you can; where a position is standardly associated with someone, the name is optional.

For each leaf, give: the position's name, or unnamed if nobody defends it; one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and, where a leaf corresponds to a combination of branch answers that looks logically available but unoccupied, a note saying so plus a brief diagnosis of whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered. Flag any two leaves that none of the questions above can separate, and say what further question would be needed to pry them apart.

Audience: graduate students in sociology, demography, and philosophy of social science, none of whom know the philosophy of kinds literature well. Size: six to eight branch nodes and seven to ten leaves. The instrument should sort readers by what they are willing to say about the cases and the commitments, not by which label they already like.
