Hey, so I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science on the philosophy of mind side, and I've somehow been volunteered to write the lead piece for our department's annual satire issue, which goes out to alot of people who actually know this stuff, so I can't phone it in.

What I want is a deadpan parody of an external audit / accreditation report. An inspection body visits a fictional logistics company and has to decide whether the company understands what it does. The auditor's problem is that every test it devises either turns out to be a competence test (which the company passes easily) or an inner-state test (which nothing passes, including the auditors). The target is the habit of demanding an inner certificate of understanding over and above performance — but write it fair enough that someone who thinks comprehension really is something special doesn't feel strawmanned.

The jokes have to come from the philosophy, not from buzzwords. So work in: the competence/comprehension distinction and the fact that it's a matter of degree, not a switch; the inversion where competence comes first and comprehension is a late internalization of a skill already being exercised (the company trains new hires by giving them a quota and no explanation, the Turing move of building something that learns like a child instead of simulating an adult); Darwin's version of the same thing — the company's routing system was evolved by a process nobody there can read or explain, so it's competent and opaque and no designer ever comprehended it; the as-if business, i.e. at what point it becomes fair to say the organization knows something; and the regress — who audits the auditors, and does the audit body itself pass its own standard. Include one finding where the standard gets applied to a single human employee and it goes badly, e.g. the one guy who does genuinely understand everything and is therefore useless, or the janitor who turns out to be the only person whose comprehension is verifiable.

Around 700-800 words, formatted like a real report: methodology, standard applied, findings, nonconformities, and a recommendation at the end that takes the logic one step too far. No citations or references to actual papers — you don't need them and they'd be weird in an audit anyway; invented names and a made-up agency are fine. Stay deadpan the whole way through, no winking at the reader. American English please.
