The question on the problem set:

A teacher announces on Friday that there will be a surprise exam next week (Monday–Friday), where "surprise" means the students cannot know on the morning of the exam that it will be that day. A student argues: it can't be Friday, because by Thursday night we'd know Friday was the only day left, so it wouldn't be a surprise. But then it can't be Thursday either, by the same reasoning, and so on back to Monday — so there will be no exam. The exam is then held on Wednesday, and it is a surprise. Explain what is going on: is the student's reasoning sound, is the teacher's announcement true, and where exactly, if anywhere, does the reasoning go wrong?

The student's answer:

The student is running a backward induction, and it's valid at each step, so if you grant the premises the conclusion that there will be no exam does follow. What's wrong is that the premises can't all be true together. The teacher's announcement says there will be a surprise exam, but if the students reason correctly from that very announcement they arrive at "no exam" — so the announcement is self-contradictory. It's a bit like the liar paradox: the announcement undercuts itself. That is also why the Wednesday exam is unexpected: the students had talked themselves into thinking the announcement was impossible, so when the exam comes they're caught completely off guard. So in the end the teacher's announcement was false — given the announcement, no exam could have been a surprise — even though in practice the students were surprised. The general lesson is that you can't publicly announce a future surprise, because the announcement itself destroys the surprise. And it shows that knowledge can't be iterated: facts that everyone knows that everyone knows can't include a surprise.
