I have taught the mind–body problem for twenty years and the exchange between epiphenomenalists and their critics — the argument usually called the paradox of phenomenal judgment — has not moved. The critics' case looks like a single clean argument: if qualia cause nothing, then the state that causes me to report that something hurts is a physical state, and my belief that I am in pain is produced by whatever physical state produces it; so that belief would have occurred whether or not the quale did, which is to say it is not a response to the quale, and the epiphenomenalist's own testimony about experience gives her no evidence for it. The epiphenomenalist's reply is equally settled: the belief is reliably correlated with the quale, and reliable correlation is what makes a state about something. Each side then restates its premise, and we adjourn where we began.

My hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about consciousness or about causation but about the theory of content — the critics helping themselves to a causal constraint on representation, the epiphenomenalists to a purely informational one, neither arguing for its constraint except by the verdict it yields on this case — so that the impasse is a standoff between two commitments, each of which could be given up and neither of which either side will give up. If that diagnosis is right, say what kind of disagreement the underlying one is — substantive, verbal, or two questions under one heading — and what would count as progress: a theory of content that earns the causal constraint rather than assuming it, an account of first-person warrant for experience that does not run through causation, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed. If it is wrong, tell me what is actually doing the work, and whether the recent reframing of the issue as a question about why creatures like us make the judgments we make counts as progress or as a change of subject.
