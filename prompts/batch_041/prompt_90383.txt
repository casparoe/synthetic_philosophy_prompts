Read Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove through the lens of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical idea seriously: where the film dramatizes something the literature on self-binding tends to miss, where the idea illuminates a scene or a character's choice, and where the film resists the reading and the honest critic has to concede it.

The film is full of devices that bind future action. General Ripper's Wing Attack Plan R delegates retaliatory launch authority down the chain of command so that a decapitated United States can still strike; the CRM-114 discriminator accepts only messages carrying a secret three-letter code, so that the Soviets cannot spoof a recall; the Soviet doomsday machine detonates automatically if the USSR is attacked, cannot be deactivated, and is itself rigged to detonate if anyone attempts to deactivate it. Treat the machine as a philosophical object rather than a plot device.

Work with two distinctions, and push back on either if it turns out to be less useful than it looks. First, separate three binding relations: one temporal stage of a single agent binding a later stage of that same agent (the standard Ulysses contract); a corporate agent entrenching its own future decision procedure (a constitution in the thin sense); and a generation binding all successors, including those who never consented and who may have no amendment path at all (entrenchment without a sunset or repeal clause). Which of these is the doomsday machine actually an instance of, and does the film's comedy depend on its being the third rather than the first?

Second, distinguish a precommitment that removes an option from one that relocates it. A Ulysses contract removes the option to eat the cake. The doomsday machine does not remove the option to disarm; it relocates the decision to the moment of attempted disarmament and makes that moment fatal to the decider. Call that a second-order precommitment: a commitment whose content is that the commitment shall not be revised. Is there a general reason such commitments are unstable, or is the machine's instability merely the film's satirical exaggeration?

Then bring the analysis to the scenes. Where does the film dramatize the difference between a commitment that is credible and one that is merely rigid? The doomsday machine deters only if everyone knows about it, yet the Soviets kept it secret until the Party Congress, so its deterrent value and its political survivability pull in opposite directions. Is that a structural problem for precommitment, or a contingent fact about Soviet politics? Consider the asymmetry between the superpowers: the United States precommits to retaliation by delegating authority, and then cannot recall the planes because the recall channel is itself a precommitment to communications security. Does the film show a case in which the precommitment that protects the principal is the same precommitment that disables him?

Where the film resists the reading, say so plainly. It may be that Kubrick is less interested in the logic of commitment than in the men who make them, and that the War Room is a place where everyone has already bound himself and can now only talk. If the film's real subject is the evasion of responsibility through machinery, then the philosophical literature may be too generous: it treats self-binding as a solution to weakness of will, whereas the film treats it as a way of institutionalizing weakness and then disowning it. Dr. Strangelove's closing proposal, to preserve a chosen remnant in mine shafts, is itself a precommitment to a future the precommitter will not have to share with the unbound.

End with what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not: the comedy of the gap between the solemnity of the binding and the absurdity of the bound, the way a commitment device can become a character in its own right, and whether the doomsday machine is a reductio of constitutional precommitment as such or only of one pathological form of it. I am teaching this alongside standard material on Ulysses contracts and constitutional entrenchment, so the reading needs to be usable without either reducing the film to an illustration or pretending it is doing analytic philosophy. Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I want the argument.
