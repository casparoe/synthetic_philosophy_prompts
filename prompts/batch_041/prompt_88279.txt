I've been chewing on the lying vs. misleading thing for years and I think I've finally got a position, so I want you to act like a very sharp philosopher of language + ethicist who knows this literature cold and has zero interest in being nice to me. My veiw: lying is worse than merely misleading, and the reason isnt the size of the harm or even the fact that a false belief gets installed. It's that a lie recruits the hearer's trust. When you assert something false you hand them your word and ask them to take it as a warrant, and they can't check it — you've made yourself a premise in their reasoning. When you merely mislead, you gave them true material and they did the inferential work themselves. So the wrong is something like using someone's trust as an instrument, which is a different and greater wrong than engineering a false belief.

What would count against this? Give me the full map, sorted by how decisive each item would be — fatal at one end, merely awkward at the other. Not the generic "well, some people think there's no difference" stuff. In particular:

- Is there a case where misleading is clearly worse than a comparable lie, rather than just equally bad?
- What happens to my view on bald-faced lies — the coerced confession, the "I didn't do it" that everyone in the room knows is false? Does my account predict these are barely wrong, and is that a bullet I can bite or a reductio?
- What about misleading that runs through presupposition, or through little words like "even" and "but"? The hearer basically can't help drawing the inference there. Does that break my "they did the work themselves" story?
- And the case where the true statements are selected so carefully that nobody could reasonably notice what was left out (I think there's a name for this — paltering?): is that morally identical to a lie, or is the "well, they could have thought harder" line actually doing real work for me?
- The complicity angle: misleading gets the hearer to co-author their own false belief. Is that aggravating or mitigating? I honestly can't tell, and it feels like it should matter to my ranking.
- Is the difference even moral, or is it really about who has standing to complain about what? If a lie just hands the hearer a cleaner grievance, does my grounding survive, or does it turn into a fact about social practice?

Also be honest about what someone who holds my view has to concede, and whether there's a version of it so hedged that nothing could count against it — and if so, whether that's actually a problem. I'd alot rather find out my position risks something than keep a version of it that's safe and empty.
