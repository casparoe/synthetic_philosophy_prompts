I am preparing a graduate seminar on historical explanation, and before we read any account of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation I want the specification on the table. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history must meet.

The purpose fixes what adequacy means here: the theory has to be good enough (a) to arbitrate disputes in which historians disagree about which of several admitted conditions was the real cause of an event, and (b) to license a narrative that cites several causes at once without ranking them and without the narrative collapsing into "everything mattered equally." A theory that only works for toy cases, or that only tells us what causation is without telling us what explanatory work it can do in a historical narrative, fails on this purpose.

Build the list as follows. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. State which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. Flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories.

Include, at minimum, requirements drawn from these cases, and add whatever others you think are genuine:

1. A regime falls, and historians cite three conditions: a ruinous war, a harvest failure, and a defection of an army faction. Remove any one and the fall still occurs on the strength of the others; none is a difference-maker against the actual outcome, yet we resist saying only one caused it, and we resist saying none did.
2. Two messengers carry the same decisive order; either alone would have produced the action; the first arrives and is acted upon, the second is intercepted. We want the first to count as the cause, and we are unsure what to say about the second.
3. A subordinate receives a valid order from a colonel, then, moments later, an identical order from a general who outranks him and whose order would have been obeyed had it come first. Both are sufficient; only one seems to have made the difference, and the counterfactual test and the authority-based test point at different orders.
4. A cause and its effect run through a chain of intermediaries, and the historian must decide whether to credit the distant cause, the proximate one, or both, without double-counting the same causal contribution.

Also make the list address the distinctions a theory must not collapse: between causation and explanation; between token and type; between redundancy and genuine joint production; and between selecting a cause for pragmatic narrative reasons and selecting it as the objectively privileged one. And make it state which results from the metaphysics of causation and from probabilistic accounts of causation it must remain consistent with, given that some of those results pull toward difference-making and others pull toward sufficiency.

When the list is done, score it briefly against the counterfactual or difference-making family, the probabilistic family, the mechanism or causal-process family, and the family that treats a cause as a necessary component of a sufficient set. Keep the scoring to a paragraph each; the weight should be on the criteria, not the scorecard.
