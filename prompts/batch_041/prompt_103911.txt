For some years I have been trying to name a phenomenon that I can describe clearly and have never seen named to my satisfaction, and I would like to know whether it has an established name — and, more importantly, how it is to be carved away from the notions that surround it.

The setting is a system whose laws are fully deterministic and known exactly: fix the state at one time and the state at every later time is fixed with it. Nothing is random and nothing is hidden. And yet I keep finding, in discussions of such systems, that two quite different obstacles to prediction are run together under a single heading.

The first obstacle is the familiar one. Nearby initial states separate, so a finite error in specifying the present state grows, and prediction is limited by how precisely one can measure. This is a matter of degree. With finer measurement and more computation one predicts further, and in the limit of perfect precision the obstacle dissolves.

The second is the one I actually care about, and it does not dissolve. For certain deterministic systems there appears to be no shortcut whatsoever: to learn what the system does later you must run it, step by step, and no cleverer procedure will serve — the system is its own fastest simulator. For some of these, moreover, the question one most wants answered about the long run — whether this cell ever takes that value, whether this trajectory ever crosses that surface — is undecidable in the strict sense: no algorithm settles it for all initial conditions. Here the failure of prediction is not a shortage of precision or of computing power but a fact of logic; a perfect measurement and an infinite machine would not help.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell how many things I am describing. Is the absence of a shortcut one phenomenon and the undecidability of the long-run question another, or are they a single phenomenon at two strengths? Is either of them a species of the first obstacle, or is the contrast precisely that the first is epistemic and quantitative while these are structural and absolute? I have met the phrase "computationally irreducible" and the phrase "undecidable dynamics," and I cannot tell whether they name the same thing, different things, or one of them the general case and the other a special one.

So please do not stop at a label. What I want is the carving. For each obstacle I have described, say what it consists in and what it does not; distinguish it from sensitive dependence, from mere computational expense, from the non-computability of the state as a function of time, and from the undecidability of a decision problem about the orbit; and tell me whether the divisions I am drawing are ones the field actually makes, or whether I am manufacturing distinctions where there is only a single concept. If no single name fits the whole, I would rather have the phenomenon named and defined so that its boundaries are marked than have a familiar term applied loosely to it.
