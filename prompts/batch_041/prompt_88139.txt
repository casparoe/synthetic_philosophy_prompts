I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, a moral philosopher, writes a column on duties of rescue. I have already fixed her verdicts on six cases and need her next column to cohere with them. She holds that a child drowning in a shallow pond must be saved even if it ruins one's shoes; that a distant child dying of a preventable disease must be saved by a small donation, though one need not donate to the point of ruin; that a promise to meet a friend must be broken to save a drowning stranger; that one may not let one's own child drown in order to save two stranger children; that a rescue requiring one to risk one's life is not obligatory; and that a rescue requiring a trivial trespass is obligatory.

The new case I have to write: she is walking home with the only copy of her novel in a watertight case. A child is drowning. The case is the only floatable object within reach; throwing it in will save the child, but the case will be lost and the manuscript destroyed. The loss would devastate her career and, she says, her reason for living, though it would not kill her or leave her destitute. What would she say, and what should she say if she is to be consistent with herself? Identify at least two plausible rules that fit her earlier verdicts and diverge on this case, say what each predicts, give your best guess with your reasons, and name a further case that would tell the rules apart. Finally, tell me what argument or consideration would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
