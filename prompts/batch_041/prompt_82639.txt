I am a high school student preparing for a philosophy club discussion, and I recently read an article that summarized T. M. Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem. The article said: 'Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. To determine whether a principle can be reasonably rejected, we look at the objections that individuals could make against it. The theory is individualistic: it does not sum up objections across different people. Instead, it compares the strongest objection that any single person could make under one principle with the strongest objection that any single person could make under the alternative. So if one principle imposes a severe burden on one person, and another principle imposes a mild burden on many people, contractualism will favor the first, because the one person's objection is stronger than any individual's objection to the second. This means contractualism cannot account for the moral importance of numbers. For example, we can either save one person from permanent blindness or save ten million people from a temporary headache. Contractualism would save the blind person, because the blind person's objection is stronger than any single headache sufferer's objection. But intuitively we should save the ten million. Scanlon tries to avoid this conclusion by allowing numbers to count when the claims are exactly equal, as in a choice between saving one person and saving another. But this is inconsistent: if numbers can break a tie when claims are equal, then they should also be able to add weight when claims are unequal. So either contractualism is committed to the absurd conclusion that we should save one blind person over ten million headache sufferers, or it secretly relies on aggregation and thus collapses into a form of utilitarianism. Contractualism is therefore refuted.' I am inclined to think the article is right that contractualism has a problem with aggregation, but I am not sure about the claim that it collapses into utilitarianism. Could you please go through the article's summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful contractualist would accept, what they would object to, and whether the article's conclusion follows from the accurate parts? I am particularly interested in whether the article correctly characterizes the role of reasonable rejection and the tie-breaking argument, and whether Scanlon's view really implies that we should save one person from a serious injury rather than many from minor injuries. Please be precise and explain any distinctions I might be missing, especially regarding the difference between aggregation and tie-breaking.
