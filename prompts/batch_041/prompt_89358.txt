Right, so I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy training, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole with these infinite-task puzzles — the lamp that gets flicked on and off at the 1/2 minute mark, then 1/4, then 1/8, and so on — and I've ended up with a pile of gut reactions that I can't square with each other. I'd like you to tell me what principle (if any) they add up to, and which of them I have to give up. Please answer in British English, and don't send me a reading list — I want the actual reasoning, not pointers.

Here's what I think, going purely on gut:

1. Plain flicking lamp, a toggle at every halving, no other rules. At t=1 there is no fact about whether it's on. Not "we can't know" — there's nothing there to know, like asking what's north of the North Pole.
2. Same timings, but the switch is a ratchet that can only go from off to on, never back. Then it's on at t=1. No wobble on this one at all.
3. A dimmer instead of a switch, where the brightness after n steps is 1 − 1/2^n. At t=1 it's at full brightness. The limit is the answer.
4. A dimmer whose brightness after n steps is sin(n), so it oscillates faster and faster between fixed values, amplitude unchanged. No fact at t=1, same defect as case 1. So for me it isn't discrete vs continuous — it's whether the sequence settles down.
5. The urn one. At step n, balls 2n−1 and 2n go in and one ball comes out, at the same halving times. If you always pull out the lowest-numbered ball, the urn is empty at the end, definately. If you always pull out the ball you just put in, there's infinitely many left at the end. Both feel definite to me, even though at every finite stage the two processes look identical, and I'll admit I don't love that.
6. Zeno's runner reaches the wall. Obviously. Infinite steps, no problem.
7. Now the one that breaks me. Suppose the toggles aren't on a fixed schedule but are driven by the binary digits of some particular real number: toggle at the n-th halving iff the n-th digit is 1. I keep feeling there's always a fact here — the number's digits are what they are, so the lamp is either on or off at t=1 — even when the digits never settle and it oscillates forever. But that's exactly case 1, where I said there's no fact. Same if I put a fair coin in charge of the toggles instead of a number: I feel there's a fact, just one I can't find out.
8. Two lamps wired so they're always in opposite states. No fact for either at t=1, same as case 1.

So: is there a principle that gets 1–6 and 8, and what does it say about 7 — do I have to drop "always a fact" there, or drop "no fact" back in case 1? State the principle as precisely as you can, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep and why, and then run it on a couple of cases I haven't thought of — ideally ones where the state isn't a simple on/off oscillation. I'd rather have a slightly ugly principle that saves the lot than a tidy one that throws out half my intuitions, but be honest if that trade isn't on offer.
