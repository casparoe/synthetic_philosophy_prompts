I teach the biography session in a 300-level ethics seminar — 14 students, 90 minutes, mostly juniors plus two grad students sitting in who never talk — and it's the session about whether you can actually learn how to live from reading about other people's lives. Every year it goes the same way: someone says obviously you can, that's what biographies are for; someone else says biography is just flattery with dates; everyone nods; we go home. I want them to feel the problem in the room instead of describing it. Here's what I've been turning over. I hand out a one-page profile of a person — a composite I write myself, so we don't burn twenty minutes arguing about whether my facts are right — written in the admiring register you'd get in a memorial tribute, all courage and quiet generosity. They read it, and I ask for one sentence: the lesson you'd take from this life, plus one thing you'll do differently tomorrow. Then page two: same person, same events, told straight, with the vanity, the convenient omissions, the people who got used. They revise the sentence. After that I want a third beat where the whole thing turns over on them, but I can't decide what it should be and I'm definately not sure beats one and two will even land with this group. What I'm chasing is what Plutarch is up to when he pairs a Greek with a Roman and then, in most of the pairs, makes them face each other at the end — the comparison is the moral instruction and it's also where the idealizing gets baked in — plus what the saints' lives did to biography for a thousand years, where the whole point of the genre is that you're meant to imitate the subject, so everything idiosyncratic or unrepeatable about them has to go. I want the debrief to land them on the question of whether admiration is a way of knowing anything, or just a feeling that a well-built story produces in you.

So build me the activity properly. Rules, what I hand out and in what order, what I say at each stage and what I deliberately refuse to say, a 90-minute timeline, and especially how to stage the third beat so they walk into the turn themselves instead of me announcing it — they've mostly never heard the word hagiography and I'd like to keep it that way till the end. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong with 14 people in a seminar room and what I do when it does, and be honest about which part of this the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
