When a prediction market puts the chance of some event at seventy per cent, news reports present that figure as the market's probability, and treat it as a better estimate than the judgement of any individual trader. What theory of disagreement does that practice presuppose? It seems to require that traders differ only in their information while sharing a background view of how likely things are in the first place — a common prior — so that the price can be read as everyone's pooled judgement. Reconstruct that implicit theory, and say which rival accounts of disagreement it quietly rules out, in particular the view that people hold irreducibly different prior commitments, so that no exchange of information would ever reconcile them. Then say whether the practice is internally consistent, and distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely suits it. The same markets are full of speculative trading, in which each side believes it knows better than the price; and there is a standard result to the effect that, where the way traders come by their information is common knowledge and no one is irrational, no one can profit from private information, because an offer to trade would itself reveal it. Is the common prior what makes the price readable as a probability, or does it only underwrite the result that makes speculative trading mysterious? And what would have to change in the way we speak of market prices if we gave it up?
