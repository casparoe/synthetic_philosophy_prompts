I have spent a long time in the overlap between rationalist blogs and academic epistemology, and there is one exchange I keep failing to settle in my own head. I am a vision scientist by training, so my instinctive vocabulary for anything about confidence is signal detection theory: a noisy internal channel, a decision variable, a criterion, hit and false alarm rates, d′, ROC curves, the equal-variance Gaussian idealization, and then the calibration machinery I picked up from the blogs — confidence as a likelihood ratio, calibration curves, Brier scores, prediction bookmarks, all of it. The philosophical side of me, meanwhile, keeps chewing on the anti-luminosity argument, the one that runs on margin-for-error reasoning about a series of cases in which you feel slightly less cold than in the one before, and on the KK principle that it is supposed to sink. Every time I read it I find myself quietly rewriting it in my own notation, and I cannot tell whether I am translating the argument or dissolving it.

So here is what I want, and I want it done properly rather than gestured at. Take the KK principle and Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and restate them inside the framework I actually think in — the detection-theoretic one above, plus the metacognition literature's estimates of how much of a first-order d′ a second-order confidence judgment recovers (meta-d′ and its relatives). I have been toying with a concrete two-stage model I would like you to use as the target, because I suspect its shape is doing more work than I notice: a first-order detector that classifies whether I am in some condition C (feeling cold, say, or a low-contrast grating being present), and a second-order detector whose only input is the first stage's output, which is supposed to decide whether the first stage knew. Restate both the KK principle and the anti-luminosity argument as faithfully as you can within that model. Where does the margin-for-error requirement on knowledge live — as a bias term, as a gap between the criterion and the point at which the second stage can be confident, as a constraint on the second stage's d′? And what exactly is the claim that no nontrivial condition is luminous, once it has been put that way?

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Tell me which components translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary, and be explicit about where the distortion goes. My suspicion, which I would like you either to confirm or to kill, is that anti-luminosity is nearly trivial in these terms — obviously a detector with finite d′ cannot read its own state off noiselessly, obviously a thresholded likelihood ratio is not itself a likelihood ratio you get for free — and that the philosophical version must be adding something the statistical version cannot express. If that is right, say what the addition is; if it is wrong, say precisely where the trivializing move cheats. Either way, do not smuggle the framework's assumptions in and then present the conclusion as a translation of the original argument: flag every step where the two-stage picture is doing work that the philosophical argument never licensed, tell me which moves are available only because I built the machinery in advance, and finish by saying whether the leftover is a defect in signal detection theory, a sign that the original claim was vaguer than it looked, or the honest boundary between a statistical model of confidence and a factive notion of knowledge.
