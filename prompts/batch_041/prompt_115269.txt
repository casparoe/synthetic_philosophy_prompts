I'm in a weird spot. I've been reading a bunch of Einstein's popular essays for a seminar -- the ones where he talks about religion and about why he doesn't believe the will is free -- and it's messing with my head, because I agree with him in the abstract and then my gut keeps doing something else. His religiosity, as I understand it, isn't belief in a God who intervenes or hands out rewards; it's more like awe at the fact that the world is lawful through and through, a Spinoza-ish reverence for necessity. And he says the thought that we don't will our own wills should make us gentler, less inclined to take ourselves and each other seriously as little authors of our deeds. Fine. But here's what I actually feel, case by case, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind it. A guy who grew up in a brutal household, took a bad head injury as a kid, and ends up doing something violent: I don't feel resentment toward him, and locking him up feels like quarantine rather than him getting what he deserves. A con artist from a comfortable family who coldly planned how to ruin an elderly client: full-blown resentment, and I think he deserves what he gets. My friend who misses my wedding because of a freak accident on the highway: no blame at all. My other friend who misses it because he's chronically disorganized and always has been: I blame him, even though if you pressed me I'd admit he's just built that way. A man whose personality changes after a tumor and starts behaving in ways he'd previously have found horrifying: no blame, and somehow it feels different from the head-injury guy even though I can't say why. Someone who pulls himself out of a terrible situation by sheer persistence: I think he deserves credit, and not just because praising him is useful. My dog bites a stranger: nothing there to blame. My own past self doing something I now find embarrassing: I blame him, sort of, even though he's me and I know exactly how the sausage got made.

The pair that won't sit still is the careless friend versus the tumor guy. If determinism is true, neither of them could have done otherwise in any deep sense, and by Einstein's lights that should deflate my blaming attitudes across the board. But the careless-friend verdict and the con-artist verdict feel like they're tracking something real, and it isn't the availability of alternatives. My best guess at a principle is something like: blame tracks whether the person's will was responsive to the reasons that were available to them, not whether they could have done otherwise. That seems to let the tumor guy back in, though, and I'm not sure it can handle the credit and praise cases either -- if the persistence guy is just wired to persist, where does the desert come in?

So: is there a principled way to keep the verdicts I want, or do I have to give some up? If I keep them, does that mean Einstein was wrong that denying free will should reconcile us to other people's actions, or was he making a practical suggestion about attitude rather than a claim about desert? And does the religious side of his picture -- the lawful-order awe, the sense that everything including our choices is necessity all the way down -- actually do any work in his anti-free-will stance, or is it just decoration on top of a straight determinist argument? Also, tell me what your favored principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone who returns a lost wallet only because he's worried about cameras. I keep flip-flopping on that one and I'd like to know whether my flip-flopping is a sign the principle is wrong or a sign I'm just inconsistently applying it.
