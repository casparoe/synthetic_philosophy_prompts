I'm trying to write something for a reading group on the Chinese Room and I keep producing an essay when what I actually want is a dialogue. Can you write me one?

Setup: two people, both sharp, neither of them a punching bag. One is a Searle-style sceptic — the guy in the room follows rules for manipulating Chinese characters, doesn't understand a word of Chinese, and no amount of rule-following is ever going to add up to understanding, so symbol manipulation isn't sufficient for thought, full stop. The other has a different story about where meaning comes from: a state means what it does because of the history that shaped it, the way a heart's thumping counts as pumping because pumping is what hearts were selected to do. Her line is that if you change the setup so the room's rulebook wasn't written by a programmer but evolved — thousands of generations of rooms, the ones that gave good Chinese answers got copied and the rest got scrapped — then the room's inner states inherit content from that history, and there's genuine understanding somewhere in the system even though the man has none. You can call that a teleosemantic view of content if you want a label for it, but don't lean on the label, just make the idea do work.

Things I want them to actually fight about:

- Whose understanding is it supposed to be? The sceptic should press hard here — point to the thing that understands. The man doesn't. So what does?
- Whether selection history can give you content without anyone grasping anything. Sceptic: a thermostat was selected to track temperature and it doesn't understand temperature. The other side should either say something real about why the room is different, or bite the bullet and own the thermostat.
- The objection that the selection story is just a description we impose from the outside, and the reply that the same is true of the sceptic's own brain.
- At some point one of them should raise the variant where the rulebook gets replaced by an enormous precomputed lookup table — no rule-following at all, you just index the whole input history and read off the answer. Does that help the system or wreck it?
- I'd like a moment where they notice they might just mean different things by 'understanding' and have to decide whether they're talking past eachother.

Don't wrap it up neatly. If they can agree on what would settle it, great; if they can't, fine too, but then they should be able to say what they'd each have to seperately concede. Roughly 8-12 exchanges, real back-and-forth, and no citations or name-dropping — I just want the argument.
