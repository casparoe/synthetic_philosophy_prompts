A friend and I have been arguing, over several long conversations, about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and our disagreement has narrowed to a single question that I would like you to address directly. I am not asking for a survey of what has been written about either paradox; I want the underlying conceptual issue set out clearly enough that I can tell which of us is under pressure.

The two cases run as follows. In the lottery case, an agent knows that a fair lottery of one thousand tickets has been drawn and that exactly one ticket has won. Each ticket's chance of having lost is 0.999, and she believes, of every ticket, that it lost. Each of her thousand beliefs is therefore supported by overwhelming probability, yet she also knows that at least one of them is false, so they cannot all be true together. In the preface case, an author has checked a thousand claims and believes each of them; from long experience she also believes that a book of that length will contain at least one error, and she says so in her preface. Again each belief is highly probable on her evidence, and again she believes something — that at least one claim is false — which cannot be true alongside all the others.

The orthodox line, which my friend rejects, is that the two cases warrant different verdicts: the ticket holder is irrational if she believes of her own ticket that it lost, whereas the author is rational in believing every claim she has checked. The alleged difference is that the lottery agent knows the propositions partition the possibilities and therefore has a deductive guarantee that one of her beliefs is false, while the author's guarantee is merely inductive, a matter of her track record.

What I want from you is an explanation of the conceptual issue that this contrast is meant to capture — the issue of what distinguishes a body of beliefs that is rationally held from a mere collection of individually probable propositions, and what work the requirements of consistency and of closure under conjunction are doing on each side — followed by a considered verdict on whether the alleged disanalogy survives scrutiny. Please let the verdict turn on the two cases below, which my friend devised to erode the difference, and say of each whether the intuitive verdict travels with the structure of the case or stays where it was.

First, a preface in which the author's ground for thinking that not all of her claims are true is not her track record but a proof: she has discovered that her thousand claims jointly entail a contradiction, though she cannot tell which claim is at fault. Her guarantee that at least one claim is false is now deductive, and her credence in each individual claim is unchanged. Is she still rational in believing each of them? If the verdict changes, what precisely has changed?

Second, a lottery in which the agent knows how many tickets were sold and that the draw was fair, but not that a winner is guaranteed: she takes a no-winner outcome to be possible, though she is sure there cannot be two winners. She still gives her ticket a chance of one in a thousand of winning and still believes of every ticket that it lost, but she now has no deductive guarantee that any of those beliefs is false — only that at most one of them is. Does her belief that her own ticket lost become rational? And if the verdict changes here, does it change for the same reason as in the first case?

Finally, tell me which of the two cases does more damage to the orthodox line, and whether there is a principled way to keep the original verdicts apart without leaning on the deductive-versus-inductive contrast that my friend is attacking. I am particularly interested in whether the real difference between the cases lies in the structure of the agent's evidence at all, or whether it lies elsewhere — for instance, in whether her beliefs answer to a single standard of correctness, or in whether her thousand beliefs are the sort of thing that can be assessed one at a time or only as a whole.
