I chair the behavioral-science review panel at a national agency whose interventions span tax compliance, benefit enrollment, health reminders, and household energy use. Our rules vet every nudge as a discrete item: each default, reminder, social-norm message, or scrap of added friction in a cancellation flow gets its own ethics review, its own trial, and its own line in the public register. I have come to think this is the wrong unit of analysis. What a person actually encounters is a single layered choice environment in which dozens of these interventions operate at once, most of them unnoticed, some of them reinforcing one another, and some jointly closing off options that any one of them would have left open—a portal where the default, the follow-up message, and the friction on the way out all point the same direction. I want to propose that we evaluate choice environments rather than individual nudges, and before I take that to the panel I need to understand what the shift commits us to.

Please describe the most important implications of treating the choice environment, rather than the individual nudge, as the unit of ethical evaluation, for how the agency should design, approve, monitor, and publicly defend its interventions. I am most interested in the implications that are non-obvious, and especially in those that cut against the proposal: where it would demand more of us than we can deliver, where it would license interventions that item-by-item review rightly blocked, what it would do to the burden of proof, and how it would handle the fact that much of the surrounding environment is built by private actors we do not regulate. Where an implication rests on a contested empirical assumption, name the assumption and say what turns on it. Close with the one implication that, if I cannot answer it, should keep me from proposing this at all.
