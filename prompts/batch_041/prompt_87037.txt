I'm writing the take-home final for a metaphysics course — 3rd-years plus a couple of MA students — and I need one essay question on laws of nature, Humean vs anti-Humean. Everything I draft turns into something they can answer by reciting: Humeans think laws are just the regularities in the best system, anti-Humeans think laws govern, here's Armstrong, here's Bird, here's Maudlin, the end. I want a question that makes them do the mechanics of the best-system account instead of the vibe of it.

The pressure point I want them on is the axiom/theorem thing, which I dont think anyone has ever made them think about. Here's the case I use in lecture, I've been calling it Two Calendars. Take a possible world W whose total mosaic of particular facts can be axiomatised in two ways, A and B, exactly tied on simplicity and strength — not roughly as good, exactly tied. A states a certain regularity R as an axiom, R being something like "every X is Y" with X and Y perfectly respectable predicates of W, so on A, R is a law of W. B gets the same total simplicity and strength a different way: it doesn't state R, it derives R as a theorem from axioms about other matters. Both systems axiomatise the same mosaic, so they have all the same theorems — the only difference is what they take as axioms. And the tie can't be broken by piling on more of the same criteria.

What I want them to work out is what the best-system account says about R in W, and I want them to notice that the answer turns on something the account usually doesn't say out loud. If laws are the axioms, then whether R is a law depends on which equally-simple packaging you pick, which makes lawhood look like an artefact of descriptive economy rather than a feature of W. If instead anything that follows from the best system counts as a law, the tie stops mattering for R — but then you have to say why the consequences don't include things like "all the gold spheres are less than a mile across," and the usual answer is that only the axioms are laws, which walks you back onto the first horn. So they have to pick a horn or find a way between them, and then say whether the horn is a problem for Humeanism as such or just for this particular Humean option. Theres a lot riding on that last bit and I don't want them collapsing the two.

Then the symmetry move, which is the part I most want in the model answer: does the anti-Humean face the same dilemma in a different costume? Armstrong-style necessitation between universals and Bird-style dispositional essentialism both look like they give a principled answer — only the fundamental ones are laws, derived regularities are downstream — but that answer costs them things we ordinarily call laws. And Maudlin-style primitivism looks like it just relocates the tie: if laws are primitive posits, what in W picks out R?

Deliverables: (1) the exam question as the students will see it, self-contained — spell out Two Calendars in the question itself rather than assuming they've got my lecture — closed-book, 1200 words, 90 minutes, and make clear they're marked on the argument and not on the reading. (2) a model answer, around 700 words. (3) a marking scheme out of 20, with the must-hit moves named one by one and the marks for each, plus a list of specific moves that should lose marks. I want to be able to dock someone for "Humeans are instrumentalists about laws" (just false), for treating the tie as an epistemic inconvenience — we just can't tell which system is best — when the question is whether it's a metaphysical one, and for waving the anti-Humean through as though only Humeans have this problem.

Two constraints on the answer and the scheme. It's closed book and they can't bring notes, so don't let the scheme reward name-dropping; marks are for moves, not citations. And no quotations in the model answer, I don't want them memorising sentences. Also keep the model answer honest about the bits that are genuinely contested rather than pretending there's a received view, because on the axiom/theorem point there isn't one.
