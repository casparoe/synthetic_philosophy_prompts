I want a careful explanation of a conceptual issue that I have been trying to state clearly, and that I have not seen stated in quite this form. It concerns what I shall call expository slack.

Definitions, so that we are talking about the same thing:

- A pump's description is the text of the case as presented. Its content is the case as the reader must imagine it in order for the intended verdict to follow. Expository slack is the gap between the two: the unstated details — about the agents' knowledge, the causal history, the salience of background conditions, what the narrator is assuming — that a reader must supply before the case yields any verdict at all.
- A pump runs in 'mirror mode' when the reader supplies so much of the content that the verdict reports the reader's prior commitments rather than anything about the case. It runs in 'stipulation mode' when the author supplies so much of the content that the verdict is entailed by the description and so carries no information beyond the author's choices.
- The 'specification dilemma' is the suggestion that these are not two avoidable vices but the two horns of a forced choice: every detail added to escape mirror mode pushes the case towards stipulation, and every detail left out to escape stipulation pushes it back towards mirroring. On this suggestion there is no description that is neither a mirror nor a stipulation, and therefore no pump that supplies independent evidence for a principle.

What I want explained:

1. Whether the specification dilemma is genuine or false. If genuine, is it a fact about the psychology of readers, a fact about the semantics of descriptions, or a fact about what a thought experiment is for?
2. The proposed escape route: that a good pump is not meant to produce a verdict that stands as evidence, but to relocate a disagreement — to convert a dispute about what to say about a case into a dispute about which features of the case are relevant, or about which principle to reach for. Does relocation escape the dilemma, or merely move it? I half-suspect the latter, since the choice of which features to leave open is itself an authorial choice that can be made well or badly, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right.
3. The asymmetry I keep half-seeing: mirror mode manufactures agreement (readers concur because each reads in their own view), while stipulation mode manufactures agreement of a different kind (the verdict follows, so no one can dissent). Are these genuinely opposite failure modes, or is the contrast less clean than that? What would the contrast predict about which mode is more dangerous in which kind of dispute — metaphysical cases, where the description does the heavy lifting, versus moral cases, where the reader's commitments are already in place?
4. The diagnostic problem. The usual remedy for a pump that has gone wrong is a robustness test: vary the description along dimensions that the intended conclusion treats as irrelevant, and see whether the verdict survives. Show me why this test cannot distinguish a pump that is robust because it tracks a real feature of the case from a pump that is robust because it has stipulated the answer, and say whether any test does better. If none does, say what follows for the practice of arguing by example.
