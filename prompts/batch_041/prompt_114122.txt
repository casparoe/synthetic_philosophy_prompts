I have just come from a long evening's argument with a close friend who has lately become a thoroughgoing Szaszian about mental illness, and I want to pin down what she is really committed to. Her settled verdicts run as follows. General paresis, the syphilitic brain damage that produces grandiosity and delusions, is in her view a genuine disease, and treating it medically is right; so is the personality change that follows a stroke with visible brain damage, and so are hallucinations caused by a brain tumour. By contrast, a widow's year of intense grief is not a disease but a problem in living; a man consumed by the conviction that his wife is unfaithful, with nothing demonstrably wrong with his brain, is not diseased; a teenager whom a school psychologist calls oppositional is not diseased, and she says that label is doing the work of justifying treatment the teenager never agreed to; and chronic low mood with no bodily abnormality is likewise a problem in living rather than an illness.

More than one rule fits everything she has said, and I could not make her choose between them. Suppose I now put a fresh case to her: an adult who, some weeks after a strep infection, suddenly develops disabling tics and compulsive rituals; investigators propose an autoimmune mechanism, but repeated structural scans of his brain are normal, and his family is pressing for him to be treated against his wishes. Predict her verdict, both on whether she will call this a disease and on whether she will think the treatment legitimate. Tell me which rules fit her earlier verdicts, what each of them predicts here, which rule you think she is really running and why, and what further case would best tell the candidate rules apart. Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, lists, bold text, or tables.
