I am a philosopher serving on a joint working group, half humanists and half computer scientists, that is drafting our faculty's policy on research uses of large models. At last week's meeting I tried to raise a concern I have defended at length elsewhere — that systems trained hard against a proxy objective tend to acquire convergent instrumental dispositions (protecting the channels by which they obtain whatever they are scored on, resisting correction, accumulating resources and influence) quite apart from whatever their final objective is — and a senior colleague stopped me with a formula he has now used three times, that intelligence and goals are orthogonal: a system's capability, he says, tells you nothing whatever about what it wants, so my worry is science fiction. Several people nodded, and I could not say on the spot why the slogan, whatever it means, seemed to me not to do the work he asked of it. Before the next meeting I want the readings laid out in cold blood.

Please give me a menu of the distinct precise claims that "intelligence and goals are orthogonal" could be making, keeping apart at least the following questions: whether "orthogonal" is intended modally (no agent that is highly intelligent is thereby excluded from any final goal), statistically (measured cognitive ability is uncorrelated with the content of an agent's motives), or definitionally (intelligence is a capacity for selecting means, and as such says nothing about ends); whether the claim concerns the space of possible minds or the distribution of minds we should expect to be built or to arise; and whether its scope is final goals alone or extends to instrumental ones. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false, and give the best reason for your verdict rather than gesturing at one; where a reading is defensible, say what it would take to falsify it. I would also like the metaphorical residue made explicit — "orthogonal" is imported from linear algebra, and I want to know what is lost when a relation of independence between two measurable quantities is transferred to the relation between a capacity and a set of ends.

Then tell me, for each reading in turn, whether it would actually license my colleague's dismissal of the instrumental-convergence worry, and say plainly which reading he is most likely to have in mind — my own suspicion is that most readings of the slogan concern final goals and are therefore simply irrelevant to a claim about instrumental ones, but I want the case made rather than assumed, and I want to know what the slogan would have to mean for his move to work. Lay the answer out as a numbered menu with a verdict on each item, since I intend to circulate it, and close with a final line of the form \answer{...} whose braces contain the label of the one reading that best supports his dismissal, or the word none if no reading does.
