I'm in a small philosophy-of-science reading group that has spent the last few weeks circling the so-called F-twist -- the idea, usually credited to Friedman, that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its quality, and on some readings that a theory is better the more its assumptions depart from reality. I keep finding that my reactions to particular models refuse to line up with any single slogan, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, my reactions add up to. Here are the verdicts I'm fairly confident about. Treating a planet as a point mass when computing its orbit: fine. Treating a gas as a collection of non-interacting point particles with no volume: fine, even though I know the molecules have size and attract one another. Ignoring air resistance when working out how long a cannonball takes to fall: fine. Ignoring air resistance when explaining why a feather and a hammer fall at different rates: not fine, because the resistance is precisely the thing I'm trying to explain. Assuming a coin is fair while testing whether it is fair: not fine, and not merely because it's unhelpful -- it seems to make the question unaskable. Assuming that traders have perfect information in a model whose entire point is to explain the price effects of information frictions: not fine. Then there are the cases that make me uneasy. I'm perfectly happy to treat a population as effectively infinite, so that genetic drift plays no role, when what I'm modeling is the direction of selection; yet I balk at the representative-agent move in macroeconomics, where a single idealized household stands in for millions, and I can't articulate what separates the two beyond my priors about the fields. And I have a nagging suspicion that the point-mass planet and the representative agent are both "ignore the heterogeneity" moves, so I ought to treat them alike -- but I don't.

The tentative principle I keep reaching for is this: an unrealistic assumption is acceptable just in case the model's conclusions are robust to relaxing it, i.e., the idealization is a harmless approximation that could be perturbed away without changing the qualitative result. But that seems to get the ideal gas wrong, since perturbing it yields intermolecular forces and a qualitatively different equation of state, and yet I still think the idealization is legitimate; and it seems far too permissive about the fair-coin case, since a biased coin is just a small perturbation of a fair one. I'd like three things from you. First, say whether a single principle captures my verdicts or whether I need a small set of them, and state each candidate precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause. Second, tell me which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon -- I would rather lose a verdict than keep an ugly disjunction patched together to save every case. Third, and most usefully, apply whatever you settle on to three cases I haven't mentioned: assuming no mutation in a model of short-term allele frequencies; assuming that all firms in an industry are identical price-takers when the phenomenon of interest is the persistent dispersion of profits across firms; and assuming that space and time are absolute in a calculation of projectile motion. I don't need citations or a reading list -- I want the principle, the reasoning behind each clause, and an honest accounting of the cases it cannot handle.
