I'm a novelist, and I serve on the advisory board of a small city's historical society, which is days away from signing the fabrication contract for a new permanent exhibition. Our curator has designed the main gallery around a single timeline: one ascending line running the length of the room, from the town's first settlement through the broken treaty, the two great fires, the labor wars, the civil-rights years, and the long decline since the mills closed, terminating in a present-day panel titled "Still Rising." One of my fellow board members, who teaches local history, wants to scrap the line entirely and rebuild the room as a loop: visitors would pass through four recurring stations — boom, overreach, collapse, rebuilding — and would meet each station three times, because on her account the town has genuinely been founded, burned, and rebuilt three times, and the shape of its history is recurrence rather than ascent. The contract has to be signed by the 15th, and the board votes Thursday. Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate. I write novels for a living and I am suspicious of the word "arc," but I don't trust my own suspicion enough to rule on it alone. Recommend a concrete course of action — the line, the loop, or a hybrid you specify precisely enough to draw — and give reasoning that both the curator and the dissenting member could actually answer rather than merely resent. Be honest about the strongest case for whichever structure I decline to choose, and state plainly what each structure commits the museum to claiming about whether this town's history has a direction at all — including whether it is possible to display such a history without taking a position on that question, and whether the phrase "Still Rising" can survive in either design. Lead with your recommendation. Keep the whole thing under 650 words.
