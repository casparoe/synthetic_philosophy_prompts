Here's the setup we were given, roughly, in case it matters for judging the argument: a small society, a set of possible outcomes, everyone has a ranking over those outcomes, and the job is to turn the individual rankings into a group ranking. Three conditions, each of which sounds far too weak to object to. First, if everyone strictly prefers one outcome to another, so does the group. Second, each person is 'decisive' over at least one pair of outcomes that's their own business — one pair is enough, nobody has to be handed a big bundle of rights — so if I prefer A to B over my own pair, the group ranks A above B regardless of what anyone else thinks. Third, any pattern of individual rankings at all is admissible as an input. Sen's result is that you can't have all three, because the group ranking comes out circular. The illustration was two people and a copy of a scandalous novel, call them Prude and Lewd, with three possible outcomes: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, or nobody reads it. Prude ranks: nobody reads > Prude reads > Lewd reads. Lewd ranks: Prude reads > Lewd reads > nobody reads. Give Prude the say over whether he reads it and Lewd the say over whether he reads it, and you get nobody reads > Prude reads and Lewd reads > nobody reads; then Pareto, since both prefer Prude reading to Lewd reading, hands you Prude reads > Lewd reads, and the whole thing loops.
