I teach an upper-level seminar on rationality and decision theory and I am drafting the final exam. I want one essay question on superrationality that cannot be answered by reciting the standard prisoner's-dilemma story, so I have invented the game below and want the question built entirely around it.

The game: two players, one shot, simultaneous moves, each chooses C or D. Payoffs are symmetric — both C, each gets 4; both D, each gets 2; one plays D and the other C, the D-player gets 6 and the C-player gets 0. The exam question will then add a modification: before playing, each player is told only that the other is, with probability p, their algorithmic twin (an agent who will make whatever choice they make), and otherwise a committed defector who always plays D.

The question should require four things of the student: (1) state what ordinary dominance and Nash reasoning prescribe here, and why; (2) state what the superrational argument prescribes, and name the assumption that licenses the step from D-dominance to playing C; (3) derive, for the modified game, the value of p at which C becomes the recommendation, showing the expected payoff on each side; and (4) assess the objection that once (3) is done, superrationality reduces to ordinary expected-utility maximization over a correlation between the players, and so is an empirical bet about one's opponent rather than a principle of rationality — a strong answer should say what would have to be true of the two players for p to be 1, and whether that is ever the case.

Give me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer must contain, the specific errors that should cost marks, and a one-line note on how a merely competent answer typically goes wrong at each point. The question is one of several on the paper, so it should be answerable in about forty minutes.
