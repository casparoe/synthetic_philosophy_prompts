I'm a novelist, not a poet, and I've talked myself into a corner. A friend who runs a tiny literary magazine asked me to do an English version of a short 16th-century French poem for an anniversary issue, and I said yes before I read the thing properly.

Here's what I'm up against. Twelve lines, eight syllables a line, a rhyme scheme that never lets up, and the first letters of the lines spell the name of the woman its addressed to. On the surface it's a tease -- the poet is needling her about being ill -- and underneath it's a serious poem about the fact that she might die of it, and there's one pun holding those two readings together, and the pun sits exactly where the rhyme is tightest. I can have the pun or I can have the rhyme. Not both.

This has turned into a running argument with a couple of translator friends and now I want to write it up as an essay, because I'm tired of agreeing with everyone. What I actually want settled: when you translate a poem that's built out of constraints -- rhyme, meter, acrostic, a fixed shape -- is carrying the constraint across part of carrying the meaning across, or is it a second-order game you're allowed to drop the moment it starts costing you sense? I keep getting both answers and both feel like a dodge. I write sentences for a living and I still can't tell whether a good rhyme is worth a lie.

So take a position and defend it. Don't hand me a reading list, I want a verdict. Don't tell me it depends -- tell me what it depends on and then pick. Along the way I want you to sort out at least these: (a) whether it matters that the acrostic here is doing a job, i.e. the constraint isnt mere decoration, it's carrying a second message that only exists if the constraint survives; (b) whether it matters that a constraint is audible to the reader as opposed to hidden and only findable by hunting; (c) whether a version that keeps the rhyme while quietly swapping out the images is lying about the original in a way a free-verse version isn't, or whether that's just snobbery; and (d) the strongest version of the case that I'm wrong -- the one where the constraint is precisely what made the original good, because the rhyme forced the poet into a word he'd never of reached for otherwise, and so a translation that drops the constraint is a translation of a poem that never existed.

One practical constraint of my own: my version has to be readable out loud without sounding like a dog walking on its hind legs. If your verdict requires me to write something that sounds like that, say so, and defend it anyway.
