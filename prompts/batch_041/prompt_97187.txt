I'm a backend engineer and I've never taken a philosophy class, but my team keeps running into the same wall and I've started keeping a mental list of cases about it. Everyone calls it Goodhart's law, the idea that once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. Fine, but my gut says these cases don't all go the same way, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut or if I'm just being inconsistent. Here's my list and my verdicts.

1. We used story points, then started reporting velocity to management. Estimates ballooned, throughput in real terms didn't move, and honestly it got a little worse because people now spend time arguing about points. Verdict: gamed, and bad — the thing we actually cared about (shipping) went down.

2. A previous job counted lines of code. Obviously gamed, obviously bad.

3. Support team got a "time to first response" target. Agents started firing off canned "we're looking into it" replies within seconds. The number looks amazing. Verdict: gamed, bad, because customers now wait longer for a real answer and the metric tells us nothing.

4. The old Soviet nail factory thing: quota by weight, you get one giant nail; quota by count, you get a million tiny useless nails. Verdict: gamed, bad.

5. The cobra bounty — pay per dead cobra, people breed cobras. Verdict: gamed, bad. (I think this one has a name but I cant remember it.)

6. Sales team got measured on "demos booked." They started booking demos with leads who were never going to buy, so the metric is definately being gamed. But they also just started reaching out to more people, and actual revenue went up. Verdict: gamed, but... I can't bring myself to call it bad. The goal moved in the right direction.

7. Recommender optimized for clicks. Clicks went up, and watch time went up too. Verdict: I don't even want to call this gaming.

8. HR put a target on "survey participation rate." Managers pressured everyone to fill out the survey, so now it's 100% and totally worthless as a signal of how people feel. Verdict: gamed, bad — and this one feels different from the others, like the metric didn't just fail, it actively destroyed the thing it was supposed to measure.

9. A hospital gets a target for how long patients wait in the ER, so they start keeping patients in ambulances until a bed is free, because the clock only starts at the door. Nobody's faking anything; they're following the rule exactly. Verdict: gamed, bad, but it feels like a different flavor than #1, where people were consciously inflating estimates.

10. This one might be the odd one out: a company I worked for pays salespeople straight commission. Nobody calls it a proxy for anything — the money is the goal. So you can't really "game" it in the Goodhart sense, even though a salesperson could lie to a customer. Verdict: not the same phenomenon.

Here's the tentative rule I came up with: gaming hurts when the metric is only a proxy for the goal AND the cheapest way to move the metric doesn't move the goal. If the cheapest way to move the metric also moves the goal, it's harmless. But that makes #3 and #6 the same shape — in both, the cheapest move (canned reply / book junk demos) moves the metric — and I gave them opposite verdicts. So either I'm wrong about one of them, or the rule is missing something. #8 and #9 also don't fit; #8 had a metric that was basically the goal itself and it still went bad, and #9 has nobody even trying to cheat and it still went bad.

What I want: a principle, or a small set of them, that actually gets these, stated precisely enough that I can tell which clause is doing the work for which case. Tell me what each clause predicts for a few cases I didn't list — e.g. a metric used one time to pick a vendor versus the same metric used forever as a target; a metric that's an aggregate for a whole org versus a ranking of individuals; a metric you stop using the moment you notice it's being gamed; and a metric where the "cheapest way" to move it is something you'd actually want anyway. And be honest about which of my ten verdicts I have to give up, because I suspect at least one is just me being sentimental about a number going up.
