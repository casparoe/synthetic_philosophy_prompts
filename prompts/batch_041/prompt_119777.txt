I'm in an MA philosphy program and my seminar this term is all about blame — specifically the standing stuff, the thought that a hypocrite can tell you you've done wrong and be *right* about it, and yet somehow they can't really blame you for it, or at least their blame doesn't land the way a non-hypocrite's would. I've read the usual positions and honestly they all seem to assume the same thing: that standing is a status the blamer either has or lacks, sitting there in the world independently of what anyone actually does with the blame once it's issued. I want to write my final paper arguing for the opposite. My line is that there's no fact of the matter about whether A has standing to blame B — standing isn't a permission the blamer holds, it's something that gets constituted by uptake. Specifically I want to distinguish what I've been calling thin uptake (B registers that they're being criticised, hears it as a complaint) from thick uptake (B treats the criticism as actually giving them a reason to answer for what they did — takes it as an address with authority behind it), and argue that standing tracks only the thick kind. On this view hypocrisy doesn't *defeat* anything, because there was never a permission there to be defeated; what hypocrisy does is make thick uptake rationally unavailable to the addressee, and that's a fact about the addressee's reasons, not about the blamer's credentials. My flatmate is in law school and when I tried this out on him he said it sounded like I was just redescribing the original puzzle, so I want the argument to be sharp enough that that charge doesn't stick.

Can you write it for me? I want it rigorous and I want it to actually earn the conclusion instead of gesturing at it. A few things it needs to do: explain why the intuition that the hypocrite "can't" blame is so persistent if it isn't tracking a defeated permission; say what the view predicts about the reformed wrongdoer who used to do the very thing they're now blaming you for (my gut says they can have *more* standing, not less, and I'd like the view to deliver that rather than have it bolted on as a special case); say whether the asymmetry between blame and praise falls out of the same machinery or has to be treated seperately; and then take the obvious objection head-on — that this makes standing hostage to the guilty party's willingness to listen, so anyone can unblame themselves just by refusing to engage, which smells like a reductio. If the view has to bite that bullet I want to know exactly how big the bite is and whether there's a way to keep the core insight while blunting it. Don't worry about citing anyone or giving me a reading list, I just want the argument itself, as tight as you can make it.
