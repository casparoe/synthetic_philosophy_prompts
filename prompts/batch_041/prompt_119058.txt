My university's dining office just rolled out a menu scoring rubric and I got asked to sit on the commitee that reviews it, and the longer I stare at it the less I can tell what it actually believes. Every dish gets scored 0-100 on carbon, water, and land. Then there's a separate animal impact line: they count how many farmed animals go into a serving and multiply by a suffering weight the office made up themselves, beef 1.0, pork 0.9, chicken 0.2, farmed fish 0.1, insects 0. There's also a humane bonus of up to 10 points for pasture-raised and cage-free sourcing. So the beef burger gets wrecked on carbon but barely moves the animal impact needle, the chicken sandwich is the other way around, a lentil bowl scores a perfect 100 on animal impact even though harvesting lentils mows down alot of mice and rabbits and ground-nesting birds, and the cricket-flour protein bar is the office's pride and joy because crickets come out to zero. They're using these numbers to decide which suppliers get contracts. What bugs me is that we count the deaths we pay for and not the deaths we cause by harvesting, and those seem like the same kind of fact.

So what is this thing committed to? I don't want a general intro to animal ethics, I want to know what the rubric itself is presupposing about moral status, about what a harm is, and about whose suffering counts as our responsibility. Tell me which parts it actually has to believe and which parts just happen to sit comfortably with several views at once, which rival theories it quietly rules out, and where it's genuinely underdetermined. And if you swapped the view it's running for a different one, what would visibly change in the scoring.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion, and make the fifth one about where your reading is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind about it. American English please.
