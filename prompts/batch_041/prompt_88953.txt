I am the instructor for a graduate seminar on the safety and ethics of advanced AI, and I am partway through grading the midterm. The exam consisted of a single question, reproduced below; students had 600 words and no notes. One student's answer is also reproduced below. I want your assessment of that answer, not of the question.

The exam question: "Much of the recent work on keeping AI systems from doing unwanted things rests on a hope: that we can build a penalty term — an impact measure — that discourages an agent from changing the world without our having to say what we want the world to look like. Two families of proposals are usually cited. The first penalizes reductions in the agent's ability to reach states, discounting states by how hard they are to reach, and computes the reduction relative to a baseline in which the agent does nothing or follows a safe default policy. The second penalizes reductions in the best value the agent could achieve on each of a large set of auxiliary reward functions, again relative to a baseline. Question: can 'avoiding side effects' be defined without a full specification of value? Answer yes or no, defend your answer, and treat the two families of proposals as your main evidence. Do not spend space restating the proposals; assume the reader knows them."

The student's answer:

"Yes, with a qualification that turns out to be smaller than it looks.

Side effects are, by definition, the changes an agent brings about that are not part of the task it was handed. The difficulty is that a task specification tells the agent what to do but not what to leave alone, and both proposals are attempts to supply the missing 'leave alone' without smuggling in a substantive theory of value.

Take the reachability proposal. It never mentions what is good. It asks only how much of the world the agent can still get to after acting, where states count for less the harder they are to reach. The penalty is the drop in that quantity relative to the agent doing nothing. Because the quantity is defined over the agent's own capacities rather than over any particular objective, the measure is indifferent among goals: an agent penalized this way is not pushed toward any outcome, only away from foreclosing options. That is exactly the value-free constraint the question asks about. The attainable-utility proposal makes the same move in a different currency: it takes a large set of auxiliary reward functions, none of them ours, and penalizes reductions in how well the agent could do on each of them. The agent is not told what to want; it is told not to make the world worse at satisfying whatever it might want.

Three things are worth conceding. The baseline has to be specified, and what the agent would have done had it done nothing is not a well-defined counterfactual in a chaotic environment; in practice one substitutes a default policy, which is a modeling choice. Both measures are only as good as the state abstraction they are computed over, so impact can hide inside a coarse-grained state. And the auxiliary set in the attainable-utility proposal has to be chosen, and the normalization across auxiliary functions fixed.

None of this threatens the main claim. The baseline and the auxiliary set are parameters of a penalty term, not a specification of value; they are engineering choices that can be tuned, averaged over, or learned, in the way a discount factor or a learning rate is. The measure's neutrality lies in what it does not assume — no privileged objective, no ranking of outcomes — and that neutrality survives the choice of baseline, because any reasonable baseline preserves the symmetry among goals. The residual problem is not conceptual but computational: these quantities are expensive to estimate and easy to game in the short run. The honest summary is that we have a value-free definition of side-effect avoidance and a hard engineering problem in making it bind.

One asymmetry is worth naming. The agent is penalized for what it does and not for what it leaves undone, so a low-impact agent may stand by while bad things happen. That asymmetry is built into any measure defined against an inaction baseline, and it could be removed by penalizing harmful omissions as well — which would leave the definition's neutrality untouched."
