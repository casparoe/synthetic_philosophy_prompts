Ok, context: I'm a retired soil microbiologist (forty years, mostly nematodes and carbon cycling) and since I stopped going to the lab I've been reading philosophy, which is fun and also mildly alarming. I now chair the selection committee for our institute's postdoc fellowships — seven of us, five to nine applicants, twice a year — and the funder wants a full ranked list rather than just a winner, because the money gets split in proportion to where each candidate lands. So the whole ordering matters, not just first place.

Last cycle was a mess. We hit a Condorcet-style cycle (A beats B head to head, B beats C, C beats A — I think that's the name for it), two members had only read four of the seven files and left the rest blank, and afterwards one member said she'd ranked honestly in the first round and then realized she should have put her second choice first if she wanted it to survive. Meanwhile the director reckons we should just let the chair decide and stop pretending, which I dont love, partly because I'd be the chair.

I gather Arrow's impossibility theorem says no ranking rule can satisfy all of a short list of fair-seeming conditions at once, and that there's a companion result that any non-dictatorial rule with three or more options can be gamed. That's about the level I'm at. What I want from you is a procedure the committee can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because it's a values question and not a technical one. Constraints: it has to survive blank and partial ballots, be explainable to seven tired people in a ninety-minute meeting, produce a full ordering for the funder, and nobody on the committee has any formal training in this stuff. Then run it on three cases — the cycle, a two-member bloc ranking to help a friend, and a late applicant entering who flips the winner — saying for each which of Arrow's conditions we'd be quietly giving up and whether that's a technical choice or a values one. And if the honest answer is that we should elect a chair and let them own it, say so.
