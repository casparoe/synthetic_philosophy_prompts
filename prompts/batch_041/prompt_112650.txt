Thomas Hobbes died in 1679, and the possibility described below belongs to a world he never saw. Treat him nonetheless as a competent, curious interlocutor who has been briefed on it thoroughly, grasps the mechanics, and has no knowledge of any of the modern literature surrounding it. I want his verdict, argued in his own idiom — covenants, power, the passions — rather than in the vocabulary of contemporary decision theory, except where you explicitly mark a translation.

The construction he is being asked about is this. Two parties face a single, unrepeated interaction of the familiar prisoner's dilemma shape: each does better by taking rather than giving, whatever the other does, yet both do better if both give than if both take. There is no repetition, no reputation, no witness, and no third party holding a stick. But each party is a machine that must submit in advance a program to play on its behalf; the two programs are then run against each other, and each is allowed to read the other's source code before choosing an action. A pair of programs is stable if neither party could have done better by submitting some different program. Mutual cooperation is stable here: a program that cooperates when the program it faces is identical to itself and takes otherwise is, in such a pair, one that neither party can profitably replace. Related designs reach the same result without exact matching — one cooperates if it can prove that the opposing program cooperates against it, and another cooperates with small probability and otherwise mirrors the opposing program — though the first is brittle, since a single added space in the code turns cooperation into defection, and the second buys robustness at the price of a small standing risk of defection. A general result is that any outcome which gives each party at least what it could guarantee for itself by its own unaided devices, and which the two could in principle have reached together, can be made stable by some pair of programs: the same range of outcomes that repeated dealings would permit, achieved in a single, unrepeated encounter.

Answer as Hobbes would, addressing each of the following.

1. In Hobbes's account, an agreement with no power behind it is nearly worthless, because whoever performs first has no assurance that the other will follow, and may be ruined precisely by having kept faith. Does the arrangement above count, for him, as an agreement with a power behind it, or as one that remains mere words? State precisely what the fear of a coercive power is supposed to accomplish in his argument, and then say whether readable source code accomplishes that same work or merely relocates the difficulty.

2. Hobbes traces the quarrel of his state of nature to a small number of passions rather than to a single one — the pursuit of gain, mutual distrust, and the appetite for reputation or precedence. Which of these does the arrangement neutralize, and which does it leave standing? Make the residue concrete with a case in which two agents can read each other's code in full and still have reason to fight.

3. Take the three designs in turn. The brittle one fails when the parties cannot agree on identical code; the proof-based one works only if both parties trust the same system of reasoning; the grounded one leaves a small chance of defection on the table. Are these three ways of failing to build the sword, or three different quantities of sword? For each, say where the remaining trust must sit — in the parties, in the code, or in whoever guarantees that the code is the code.

4. Suppose the parties must hand their programs to a platform that runs them, and the platform might run something else instead, or misreport what the opposing program said. Is the sovereign now the platform? Has the sovereign merely been pushed up a level, or has the construction shown something Hobbes's own account missed — that the fear need not come from a person, provided it comes from something reliable?

5. Does the general result — that any attainable, individually rational outcome can be made stable in a single unrepeated encounter — refute Hobbes's claim that men without a common power cannot be secure in one another's company, or clarify it? Press him on whether his argument was ever about men as such, or about beings whose commitments cannot be read off in advance; and ask whether a covenant made with an automaton, rather than with a person, is a covenant in his sense at all.

6. Finally, the many-party case. If a great many agents can read one another's code, is that a commonwealth in his sense, or only a concord among many that he would expect to break? Identify the feature of the many-party case that does the breaking, and say whether that feature could be engineered away or is structural.

Close with a short audit: which of your claims are Hobbes's own doctrine, which are extrapolation from it, and which single feature of the case most changes his verdict. No citations or reading lists are needed; reason from the account given here.
