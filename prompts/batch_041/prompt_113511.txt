I'm putting together a quiz for a small reading group I run — we've been working through decision theory, and we've gotten to the point where everyone can say the words causal, evidential, and updateless but nobody can agree on what they actually imply for cases that aren't in the textbook. The problem is that when I ask people directly which theory they hold they just pick the label they like, so I want a quiz that sorts them by what they say about concrete cases instead. Can you write me eight to ten questions, each a short case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions? The positions I need as leaves are: causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, the updateless view where you choose a policy before knowing which situation you're in, and a separate updateless-ish position that says you should choose a policy but can still revise it when you learn which situation you're in — I'm not sure that view is stable, but I want it as a leaf so the quiz can catch people who talk like updateless theorists but flinch at precommitment. Also include a deflationary option for someone who thinks all these cases are underdescribed and no stable verdict follows. The questions should not ask whether you are a causalist; each one should be a case where the positions genuinely come apart. I especially want the quiz to tease apart three things people often run together: (a) the predictor's accuracy gives you evidence about what you will do, (b) your action and the prediction are logically correlated because they are both outputs of the same decision procedure, and (c) only causal influence matters. The positions should come apart on which of those they accept.

I don't want the quiz to just recycle Newcomb and the smoking lesion — we've all seen those. Use at most one standard case, and make the rest new. Here are some I've been sketching, feel free to use them and add your own: (1) a predictor who is only 90% accurate and whose prediction is made by running a simulation of you that is then destroyed, so the simulation can't later be consulted; (2) a counterfactual mugging where the coin is flipped after you decide, and if it comes up tails the AI not only doesn't pay you but also erases all records of your decision, so your choice can't serve as evidence for anyone; (3) a pill you can take that will make you a causalist, where the question is whether you should take it before or after you learn which case you're in; (4) a transparent Newcomb where the predictor's prediction is shown to you before you choose, but you know the display was generated by a process that already ran your decision procedure. For each case, phrase the answer options so someone can answer without knowing which theory they're picking, and make sure the options actually correspond to different theories. Then give me a scoring key, flag any pairs of positions your quiz can't seperate, and tell me which case is doing the most work separating the updateless view from evidential decision theory and which one is doing the most work separating it from causal decision theory. Finally, since I keep flip-flopping on my own answers, please add a short note on where the scoring key is most likely to be wrong and what argument or consideration would change your mind about which position a given answer pattern indicates.
