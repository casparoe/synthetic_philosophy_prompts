Hi — I'm in a phil of mind / cog sci seminar that's spent the last few weeks on GEB, and I have to hand in a short response essay on Hofstadter's strange-loop stuff. I wanted to run my draft argument past you before I commit, because I can't tell if I'm being fair to him.

The gist of what I want to argue is that the Gödelian analogy in GEB is doing less work than it looks, because it slides between two very different things whenever it says the system "refers to itself." When Gödel builds the unprovable sentence, the self-reference is really just a coding trick — the system's formulas get mapped onto numbers, and then some sentence happens to say, under that coding, that a certain number isn't the number of a proof. The system never refers to itself in any sense it could act on; the self-reference is a fact for us, the ones reading the meta-theory, not something the system does. I've been calling this "off-line" self-reference, as opposed to "on-line" self-reference, where the system actually runs a process that includes a model of itself and uses that model to steer what it does next. My claim is that consciousness needs the on-line kind, and Gödel only hands you the off-line kind, so the strange loop is a loop through a code and an external reader, not a loop a mind could live in. Hofstadter seems to just wave at the coding, say "see, self-reference!", and then the self is supposed to fall out for free.

The part I'm least sure about: am I cheating by defining "on-line" however I need it to? Because someone could say that once the system has a provability predicate it *does* have a representation of itself (of its own provability), so it is modelling itself after all. I think I want to reply that a provability predicate represents a *property* of the system, not the system as an agent or a whole, and that this is exactly why it can't generate anything like a point of view. But I don't know if that distinction survives contact with the details.

Could you give me comments? Specifically: (1) is the off-line/on-line distinction a real distinction, or is it just "syntax vs. semantics" relabelled, which would make it boring; (2) is my reading of what Gödel's construction actually does even right, or am I overstating how absent the "self" is; (3) where would Hofstadter actually push back — does he already have a response to this that I'm missing, so that I'm attacking a strawman; and (4) any suggestions for making the essay less hand-wavy without turning it into a technical logic paper, since its supposed to be short.
