So I need advice on a situation I didn't expect to end up in. I volunteer two days a week at a very small local archive — basically me, a retired librarian, and a photocopier. A few years back the county closed a residential institution that had been running since the 1880s and we ended up with its records, about forty boxes, mostly admission ledgers. They're a goldmine for anyone doing family history: name, age, where the person was picked up, a column headed 'friends' for whoever would vouch for them on release (blank in probably half the entries), a column headed 'character' that gets filled in with the same three or four words over and over, and a 'cause' column. The whole town's poor families are in there, including alot of people whose descendants still live here.

We got a grant to digitize one collection and put it online, deadline's in the spring, and the ledgers are the obvious pick — it's what the funders want and what researchers keep asking for. But at our last meeting a woman whose grandparents are in volume two said she doesn't want her family's entries published. Her objection wasn't privacy exactly. She said those books were the thing that was done to them — that being written down in that column, with those words, was the injury, and scanning it just does it again with better resolution. She also said half the entries about her family are wrong anyway (wrong ages, a surname spelled three ways, a 'cause' that was a lie) and that posting them as 'the record' would make the lie permanent. Then a man from the same family stood up and said the opposite: that the institution kept the book and kept it shut, and that hiding it now is the same power doing the same thing, just in a nicer voice. Both of them lost people in those ledgers.

I keep flip flopping. Our training says you don't edit and you don't withhold, you describe things and let people argue about them later. But 'let people argue later' assumes the people in the book get to be in the argument. And the deeper thing that keeps nagging at me is that only the people the institution formally took in are on those pages at all — everyone else who was poor in this town in 1900 left no trace whatsoever, and putting these books online is going to look like we've got the whole picture. Practically, I have to tell the funders something in about six weeks.

What would you actually do? Lead with your answer, then the reasoning, but write it as one continuous piece — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables. Just prose, the way you'd write it in an email to me.
