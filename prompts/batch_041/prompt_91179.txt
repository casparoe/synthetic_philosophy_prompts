I am a novelist, and I have been quarreling with myself for years over a question that philosophers have been quarreling over for longer. Twice now I have sat at my desk and wept over the death of a woman who never existed, a woman I invented and then killed in a sentence I had already rewritten four times. I knew precisely what I was doing. The grief was not the less for that. My readers write to me about the same thing, and they report two opposite aftermaths: some say the feeling is gone within the hour, and some say it changed what they did with their lives.

I want the two camps in this dispute to stop talking past each other and to draft the document they could both sign. Hold them to the theses below, and do not let them drift into a survey of the field.

The as-if camp: our responses to fiction are not genuine emotions. They are real as experiences, the throat closing and the eyes burning, but they are states internal to an activity of imagining, on the model of a game of make-believe. They take no object in the world, and they carry none of the commitments or readiness to act that genuine emotion carries. The customary name for them is quasi-emotions.

The real-tears camp: the requirement that an emotion be aimed at something that exists is simply a false theory of emotion. Our responses to fiction are genuine emotions, aimed at something that does not exist, or at a thought, or at the work itself; nothing in the nature of emotion demands an actual object.

Six things have to be handled, because they are what actually happens.

1. My own tears. The author is the one person who cannot be uncertain about the outcome, since she chose it, and she is also the one person with a professional stake in the reader's tears. The as-if camp says she is simply the first player in her own game of make-believe. The real-tears camp says there was never anything to explain.

2. The tenth rereading. I have watched readers weep at an ending they have known for twenty years, with no suspense left to trade on.

3. The memoir reveal. A reader weeps over a novel, then learns it is a memoir of something that happened, and reports that the feeling changes. I want to know whether it changes in kind or only in intensity, and which camp's account predicts the change better.

4. The residue. Tears gone by dinner, in one reader; tears that become a decision, in another. If fictional emotion is quarantined from action, the second reader is the anomaly. If it is not quarantined, the first reader is.

5. The single vivid victim against the vast number. Particular, well-drawn suffering moves us far more than suffering on an enormous scale, which is the scale at which our compassion is supposed to matter most. The experimental support for this asymmetry is patchier than the parables about it suggest, and I want both camps to say what they predict it would show if it were measured properly. Fiction works at exactly the scale where real compassion fails.

6. The actor. One performer reports feeling nothing at all and reliably makes a theater weep. Another weeps nightly on stage and moves no one. If the audience's tears are genuine emotions, the performer's inner state ought to be nearly irrelevant; I want to see whether both camps can live with that.

Produce the joint statement in the following sections, in this order.

What We Both Accept. Numbered claims that a careful member of either camp would sign without reservation, including what both make of the original three-premise formulation of the paradox, and what both agree would not dissolve it. 350 to 450 words.

Where We Still Differ. The disagreement stated twice, once in wording the as-if camp would accept as its own and once in wording the real-tears camp would accept as its own. No strawmen, and no formulation that only the opposing side would recognize as fair. 150 to 200 words per statement.

The Cruxes. Three to five sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each: the crux named plainly; the case, test, or argument both camps agree bears on it; and each camp's prediction, written down in advance, in a form that could turn out to be wrong. 500 to 700 words.

What Would Move Us. For each camp, the result or argument that would count against its own position, and what exactly it would concede. 150 to 250 words.

Words or Substance. Which parts of the dispute are terminological, and which are real. If the fight over the word "real" is doing work that the argument should be doing, say so and say where. 150 to 200 words.

What This Means for My Book. Address my practical question directly: are the tears I manufacture doing anything, and would knowing which camp is right change how I write the next one. 150 to 200 words.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no appeals to who said what. I have no use for a literature review; I want the argument.
