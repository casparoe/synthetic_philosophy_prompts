I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book about two economists who were once friends. One of them built internal prediction markets for companies and agencies and believes that a market is an instrument of discovery — a machine for finding out what no one in particular knew. The other came out of the rational-expectations tradition and holds that a price can only ever encode what the participants already brought to it, so that the look of discovery is either an illusion or a description of something else. Their quarrel is the engine of the plot, and the last chapter turns on a document they sign together. I would like you to write that document for me: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, the sort of text two honest adversaries could put their names to without either becoming a strawman.

The dispute I need reduced to exact form is whether a market produces knowledge or merely displays it. Both characters know the no-trade results; both know that speculative trade happens daily on an enormous scale; each regards the other's position as nearly unfalsifiable. The document is meant for readers of a novel rather than for referees, so it must stand on its own arguments: no citations, no appeals to what the literature supposedly shows, and no formulations that one side would accept only because the other has been made to sound foolish.

Please hold apart three claims that this argument habitually runs together: that a price encodes dispersed information; that trade generates information no participant had; and that a market's existence can make the priced proposition true. I also want the document to face the dilemma I suspect lies under the whole quarrel: the no-trade results seem to require the traders to be alike in some relevant respect — a shared prior, an already-efficient starting point, common knowledge of how information is acquired — while the discovery picture seems to require traders who genuinely differ. If the price is an average of incompatible models, there may be nothing for it to be an average of. Say what the result actually needs, and whether the discovery view survives the answer.

The document should contain the claims both can sign, each doing some work rather than conceding nothing; the residual disagreements, each stated twice, once in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and for each crux the case, argument, or result both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Two cases must appear, and both sides must say what they predict about each: a firm's internal market in which every trader sees exactly the same data, and a contract whose payoff is defined by its own closing price. If you judge that part of the quarrel is verbal — that the two of them mean different things by information, or by aggregation — say which part, and why it nevertheless looked substantive.

Give me the statement in prose, with the agreed claims numbered and the disagreements following in order of how much they matter, and then a JSON object summarizing it, with these fields: "agreed_claims", an array of objects with "claim" and "why_it_is_not_vacuous"; "disagreements", an array of objects with "disagreement" (a short name), "encoding_side_wording", "discovery_side_wording", "genuine_or_verbal" ("genuine", "verbal", or "mixed"), "crux", "shared_evidence", "encoding_side_prediction", and "discovery_side_prediction"; "verbal_disputes", an array of objects with "term", "what_the_encoding_side_means", and "what_the_discovery_side_means"; and "left_open", an array of strings naming what the document does not settle and why the two of them could not settle it. Use "encoding side" and "discovery side" consistently as the two labels.
