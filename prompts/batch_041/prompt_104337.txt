I am a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I am designing a session for a graduate seminar on coordination failure — specifically on what people now call multipolar traps: the situations in which each party, acting perfectly rationally on its own, helps bring about an outcome that every party would have rejected had they been able to choose jointly, and in which no party can afford to move first. The demon's name in these discussions comes from a poem and a popular essay that most of my students have already half-absorbed, so I want to keep the concept but force the analysis onto a text they have to genuinely think through. I would like you to act as a philosopher of social coordination who is also a careful reader of film, and to give me a sustained reading of Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove, or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb, read through the lens of Moloch, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom. I have been arguing about this for weeks with a friend in the international-relations department, who insists the film is really about deterrence theory and the security dilemma and that the trap language is a modern gloss we are reading back onto it, so I want a reading that can survive his objection.

Before you commit to a thesis, please brainstorm on the page: lay out the film's main mechanisms — the arms race, the Doomsday Machine, the bomber wing's autonomy, the war room — and sort them into those that are genuine structural traps and those that are merely accidents or individual pathologies, since the whole reading turns on that sorting. Then answer the questions I care about most. Does the film actually dramatize a multipolar trap, or does it cheat by making the catastrophe the product of one man's private psychosis — General Ripper's fluoridation delusion — rather than of the structure itself? What exactly is the Doomsday Machine doing in the argument: it looks like a precommitment device built to escape the trap by making retaliation automatic and therefore credible, and yet the film insists that a deterrent kept secret deters nothing, which is a puzzle about the credibility of intentions close to Kavka's toxin puzzle. And where does the film resist the Moloch reading and force an honest critic to say so — the farce, the accident, the sheer professionalism of the bomber crew, the fact that the end arrives through mechanical malfunction and a remembered recall code rather than through anyone's deliberate choice? Point me to the specific scenes that carry the weight: the war room and its big board, Ripper's monologue about his bodily fluids, General Turgidson's fretting about a mineshaft gap even as the world ends, the final ride down. I do not want a plot summary, a list of quotations, or a survey of what critics have said; I want an argument that is faithful in both directions — that does not flatten the film into an illustration of a concept, and does not bend the concept to fit the film — and that ends by telling me what the film shows about the trap that a philosopher's argument alone cannot. Please write in polished American English, in roughly three to five paragraphs, and think carefully before you begin.
