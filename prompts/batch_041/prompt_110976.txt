For the past month my friend and I have been arguing about a co-op we both belong to: a neighborhood group that runs a tool library and maintains the trails around the reservoir. To become a member you have to put in fifty hours of unpaid work, and there is no way to buy your way out — a proposal to let people pay a fee instead was voted down last year. My friend thinks the requirement is indefensible. His argument is that the hours prove nothing: they show only that you have fifty hours to spare, not that you care about the neighborhood, and the people the co-op is meant to serve are precisely the ones who cannot spare them. He calls it a pointless arms race, and says the group could ask for a modest fee, or a refundable deposit, or two references from existing members, and get the same information more cheaply. The membership secretary's records, for what it is worth, show that the people who joined under the old pay-in route mostly drifted away within a year, while those who did the hours tend to stay; my friend says that only shows the rule filters people, and that a personality questionnaire would filter them just as well.

What I cannot articulate, and have failed to articulate to him all month, is why something would be lost. When I say the work is the point, he asks what the co-op gets from my blisters that it could not buy with my money. I would like to know who is right, and whether there is a version of the requirement that keeps whatever is real in it without pricing out the people it exists for.
