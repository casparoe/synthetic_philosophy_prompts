I've been rereading the Meno for a reading group and want to audit the analogy Socrates uses to solve his own problem. The problem is roughly this: a true belief that the road to Larissa runs north gets you to Larissa just as reliably as knowing it does, so what is knowledge worth that true belief isn't? His answer is that true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are fastened down, and that knowledge is true opinion fastened by working out the reason. The group is split. One person says the statue comparison is doing all the argumentative work: it turns knowledge into true belief plus a tether, and the tether is a piece of machinery the metaphor supplies and the text never independently cashes out, so the claim that knowledge is more valuable than true belief is being carried by the picture rather than supported by it. Another says that's too quick, since the tether just is the working-out of the reason, which the recollection story is supposed to underwrite.

Please give me a careful audit. What corresponds to what — the statue, the tether, Daedalus, the running-away, the workshop, the onlooker who notices the statue has moved? Where does the mapping break down? Which of those breaks actually bear on the work the analogy is being asked to do, namely licensing the conclusion that knowledge is more valuable than true belief, and which are merely decorative? Then tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If a better analogy would do the job, propose one.
