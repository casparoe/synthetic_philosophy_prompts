I keep running into the following analogy in discussions of eliminative materialism, where it is used to license a strong conclusion. Folk psychology — our everyday practice of explaining behaviour by attributing beliefs and desires — is said to be a theory, and beliefs and desires are said to be its theoretical posits. Phlogiston was a posit of a theory that was eventually abandoned, and we do not say that phlogiston was reduced to something else: there simply is no such thing. So, the argument runs, once a mature neuroscience arrives, belief and desire will go the way of phlogiston, and the honest conclusion will be that they never existed. The analogy is then put to work a second time, defensively: when someone objects that we predict and manipulate one another by belief-talk with conspicuous success, the reply is that phlogiston theorists also predicted combustion successfully for a century.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on eliminativism. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of phlogiston, of the phlogiston theory, of the combustion phenomena, and of the oxygen chemistry that displaced it — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in the cases where the history of science did not take the eliminativist path: sometimes a phenomenon outlives the theory that explained it, with the old term retained and given a new referent, and sometimes the theoretical term simply drops out, and I want to know which pattern this analogy predicts for belief and whether it can tell the two apart. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion from those that do not, say whether the conclusion survives them, and, if the analogy is beyond repair, sketch what would do its work better.
