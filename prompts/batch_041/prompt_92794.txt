I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the theory of punishment, and I want to work through one case in detail before I put it to the students. The case is the federal practice of acquitted-conduct sentencing. The salient facts, which you should treat as given rather than look up: a defendant is tried on two counts; the jury convicts on the first and acquits on the second; at sentencing, the judge, applying the relevant-conduct provisions of the federal sentencing guidelines, finds by a preponderance of the evidence that the defendant did in fact commit the conduct underlying the acquitted count, and increases the sentence on the count of conviction accordingly. The Supreme Court upheld this practice in United States v. Watts, decided in 1997. The Sentencing Commission has since revisited the matter, and the practice has drawn sustained criticism from judges and commentators on the ground that it punishes a person, or lengthens his punishment, for conduct of which a jury found him not guilty.

Analyze the practice twice, once from inside retributivism and once from inside consequentialism. I do not want a survey of the standard positions. I want you to say, for each theory, what its central commitment implies about counting acquitted conduct at sentencing, which features of the practice the theory explains naturally and which it strains to explain, and whether the theory's verdict changes when the details change: the strength of the evidence at the sentencing hearing, the size of the enhancement relative to the base sentence, whether the acquitted conduct is a lesser included offense of the count of conviction, and whether the defendant pleaded guilty rather than standing trial.

Then reverse the direction and ask what the practice shows about the two theories. A retributivist might reason that what a person deserves depends on what he actually did, not on what a jury was able to establish beyond a reasonable doubt, which suggests that retributivism should be untroubled by the practice, or even welcome it. Yet many retributivists object to it, typically on grounds of proportionality or of the moral significance of the criminal verdict. Is that objection internal to retributivism, or is it a procedural constraint imported from outside the theory, and if the latter, does retributivism as a theory of punishment have the resources to generate it on its own? A consequentialist, by contrast, should find the practice easy to justify, since more accurate information about what the defendant did and what he is likely to do should improve the deterrence and incapacitation calculations. If the practice is nonetheless intuitively objectionable, does that count against consequentialism, or does it show only that the consequentialist has been undercounting a cost the theory can perfectly well accommodate, such as the damage to the perceived legitimacy of the jury, the incentive it creates for prosecutors to overcharge, or the erosion of the finality of an acquittal?

I am especially interested in whether the case forces a distinction within retributivism between desert understood as actual culpability and desert understood as culpability attributable through a legitimate procedure, and whether that distinction is stable under pressure. If it is not stable, say what has to give. Where a theory is genuinely silent at the point the case presses on it, say so rather than manufacturing an answer. Conclude by saying which theory, if either, comes out of the case better than it went in, and describe what the case would have to look like for that verdict to reverse.
