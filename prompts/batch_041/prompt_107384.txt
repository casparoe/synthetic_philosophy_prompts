The exam question was: Explain the distinction between primary and secondary intensions in two-dimensional semantics, and assess whether the framework vindicates modal rationalism—the thesis that metaphysical necessity and a priori knowability are coextensive. Use the examples of 'water' and 'Hesperus'/'Phosphorus,' and address the nesting problem as an objection to the rationalist reading.

One student's answer reads as follows. Two-dimensional semantics represents the meaning of an expression with a two-dimensional matrix. The primary intension is a function from centered worlds to extensions; it captures how reference would be fixed from a speaker's perspective. The secondary intension is a function from possible worlds to extensions; it captures what the expression refers to given that the actual world is what it is. For 'water,' the primary intension picks out the watery stuff in each centered world—so on Twin Earth it picks out XYZ—while the secondary intension picks out H2O in every world, because the actual world's watery stuff is H2O. Thus 'water is H2O' is a posteriori necessary: its primary intension is contingent and its secondary intension is necessary. For 'Hesperus is Phosphorus,' the primary intension of 'Hesperus' picks out the evening star and the primary intension of 'Phosphorus' picks out the morning star, while both secondary intensions pick out Venus. So that identity is also a posteriori necessary. Modal rationalism is the thesis that metaphysical necessity and a priori knowability are coextensive. Two-dimensional semantics supports it because the primary intension is the epistemic dimension and the secondary intension is the metaphysical dimension. Since the primary intension of a necessary statement is a priori knowable, and the secondary intension is necessary, we can know a priori that the statement is necessary. So 'water is H2O' is a priori knowable once we distinguish the two dimensions. The obvious objection is that this conflates semantic necessity with epistemic apriority, but the framework answers it by keeping the two dimensions separate. The nesting problem—the worry that embedding a sentence under a modal operator changes which intension is relevant—is a technical difficulty, but it does not affect the basic rationalist conclusion. Therefore, two-dimensional semantics vindicates modal rationalism.
