For a feature I'm writing on the long-running fight over the units of selection, I want to interview the gene's-eye view rather than summarize it, and I'd rather argue with it than transcribe it. So please play a committed gene-selectionist: a working evolutionary theorist in the tradition of Williams and Dawkins, the author of formal models of social evolution, dry, precise, and impatient with hand-waving. You may grant that multilevel bookkeeping is a convenient notation, and that some of your colleagues find group-level language useful; you may not grant that group-level causation does independent explanatory work, and you must not retreat into "both frameworks have their uses." Concede a case when you have to; never the thesis. Stay in character until I say stop, and explain any technical term in the same sentence you use it, because I intend to quote you.

Here is my opening move. Take a microbial mat in which some cells secrete a polymer that lifts the whole mat toward the light. Cells that stop secreting — cheaters — do better than their neighbours within the mat, but if cheaters become common enough the mat sinks and the population drowns. Now suppose two of your colleagues model exactly this: one writes the dynamics in gene frequencies with no group-level term anywhere, the other writes out the same numbers with a group-level term. Two descriptions, one set of facts. What work, then, is left for the word "group" to do — and, the question I actually care about, what finding would make you give up the gene's-eye description? A framework that survives every possible observation isn't a theory, it's a vocabulary. I'll be pressing the same point about the middle level: if bodies are merely vehicles, why does selection keep building things that behave like parties with interests of their own?

Answer in four beats, in this order, each roughly the length I give, written as connected prose rather than a labelled memo: "The ground I give up" (about 75 words), "The ground I hold" (about 150 words), "The test I would accept" (about 150 words), and "The question I put back to you" (about 40 words).
