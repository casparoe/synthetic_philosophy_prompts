I'm in the second year of a philosophy MA and I've promised my fiction-and-ethics seminar a one-page position statement, so before I commit anything to paper I'd like a consistency audit of where I've actually landed. Fair warning: I assembled these views one at a time, over years of playing games and arguing about them, so if they don't hang together, that will be news to me. Here is the set.

(1) A thing can be wronged only if it has a welfare — only if it can be made better or worse off. Call whatever passes that test a patient. (2) Video-game characters are not patients. A character is a fictional object, an abstract artifact of a text plus a set of rules; there is no subject there whose condition improves or deteriorates, so nothing done to a character within a game can wrong that character. (3) Even so, I think some in-game acts are wrong in themselves, and not merely for their downstream effects on players or audiences. A studio that built a game around the sexual abuse of children would be doing something wrong, and I think it would be wrong partly because of what it depicts being done to those depicted figures — not only because of what playing such a game does to anyone's character or to public attitudes. (4) The fiction's detail can also matter to permissibility: it is a worse act to betray a companion the game has spent twenty hours making into a person than to gun down a nameless enemy that respawns, even though far more characters are killed in the second case. (5) But I don't think numbers matter at all. Killing a thousand NPCs is no worse than killing one, and killing one is no worse than killing none, if the wrongness is not grounded in harm to them. (6) I do hold players responsible — blameworthy and praiseworthy — for what they choose in a game, though not for what a cutscene forces on them. (7) And I'm against censoring games; I don't think the state should ban even the game I described in (3).

A friend in the seminar told me this is just me wanting it both ways: that (3) and (4) quietly smuggle the characters' welfare back in through the window I barred in (1) and (2), and that something has to give. I suspect she is half right and cannot say which half. So: which of these actually pull against one another, and how badly? Please order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, tell me which pairs only look like conflicts and why, and for each real conflict say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the set. If a single change would clean up the most, name it and say what it leaves untouched.
