I'm a novelist — two books out, both fairly domestic, and I'm trying to write my way out of that. I have a contract for a collection where every story circles the same itch: people whose job is to put a number on something nobody can know yet. Lately I've been reading around this thing called logical uncertainty (my brother-in-law is a philosopher and keeps forwarding me papers, maybe half of which I actually follow), and I want a story that gets the idea right without ever turning into a lecture. I'd write it myself but I keep sliding into essay mode, so I want to see what someone else does with my setup before I commit.

So here's the world, and I'd like you to write in it. A small private institute — name it whatever you want — where a team of maybe six people prices bets on unresolved mathematical questions: whether some enormous specific number is prime, whether a conjecture somebody scribbled down decades ago will finally fall, whether two famous open problems are secretly the same problem wearing different clothes. No roulette wheel, no coin flip, no weather. Just the fact of the matter, sitting out there, unproven. My protagonist is the youngest person on the desk, and she's begun to see three things that are quietly wrecking her. First: ordinary probability doesn't obviously apply, because for a coin you can list the ways the world might have gone, but for a theorem there's only one arithmetic — either the proof exists or it doesnt — so what exactly is she taking a fraction of? Second: a sharp counterparty can bleed her dry without knowing a single thing she doesn't know, just by chaining her own beliefs together — if she prices A at 30 cents and "if A then B" at 80, then B has to be worth at least a dime, and when she forgets that, somebody buys and sells around her and walks off with free money, and she can't even claim she was wrong, because nothing has been settled. Third, the one that keeps me up: some of the instruments on the board let a trader bet on where her prices will be a month from now, which means she can get cleaned out for a belief she hasn't formed yet. And I want one contract in the mix that I can't stop thinking about — a bet that pays a dollar if and only if its own price at the close of trading is under forty cents. I'm fairly sure no price for that is stable (under forty it's worth a buck, at or above forty it's worth nothing), and I want the story to sit in the vertigo of that, not solve it. Meanwhile the older guy on the desk, her mentor, insists there's no such thing as the probability of an unproven statement and that anyone quoting one is lying — while still making her quote one every morning by nine.

Write it as a short story, around 2,000 words, close third person on her, one or two scenes, maybe just a single day on the desk. Please don't let anybody deliver an explainer or a mini-lecture; whatever the reader comes to understand has to arrive through an argument, a trade, a screw-up, or the way she talks at night to her father (retired actuary, dying, keeps asking her to give him odds on things). Keep it contemporary — no AI characters, no sci-fi props, no real mathematicians walking through the door, no dates, no citations. I don't want a history lesson, I want the texture of a mind that's paid to be calibrated about things that can't be known. And dont land the plane — I don't want the paradox resolved at the end, I want her still in it.
