My supervisor has spent her career defending a realist reading of the theory of Forms, and over years of tutorials and examinations she has committed herself, case by case, to verdicts about which predicates name a genuine Form. By "a Form of F" she means a single non-perceptual entity that all and only F things are F by participating in, and that a definition of F would capture. Her verdicts are as follows. There are Forms of justice, beauty, equality, likeness, health, the triangle, the bed, and the human being. There are no Forms of hair, mud, dirt, or noise; she maintains that this second list is principled and not a mere concession to what strikes her as dignified.

I am writing a chapter on whether her criterion for Form-hood is a single principle or two competing ones, and I want to test it against a case she has never discussed: the comic — jokes, funny people, the ridiculous generally. Would she say there is a Form of the comic? I would like you to (i) reconstruct the candidate criteria that fit her recorded verdicts, noting that more than one seems to do so, and state what each predicts about the comic; (ii) give your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for it; (iii) identify the further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving criteria from one another; and (iv) say which of those criteria, if it is the one she is really running, would make her exclusion of hair, mud, dirt, and noise follow from a principle, and whether any of them is instead a preference for the dignified dressed up as a criterion. Assume she has been consistent — I want the best reconstruction of her, not a diagnosis of confusion. Bear in mind that equality and likeness are relational predicates, so a criterion that rules out Forms for relations is not available to her. Do not tell me what Plato himself held; I want the rule that fits her.
