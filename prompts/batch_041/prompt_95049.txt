I am a novelist, and for the past dozen years my books have been biographical fiction: novels built on the documented lives of real people — a physician who ran a colonial sanatorium, a provincial magistrate who served under an occupation, a woman who founded a school and outlived her reputation. Each book takes four or five years, and each time I find myself improvising the same decisions from scratch: which episodes to include and which to leave in the archive; how far to credit my subject's own account of himself; whether to arrange the material so that a reader draws a lesson from it; and whether to set the life beside a second life, in the manner of Plutarch's paired biographies, so that each throws light on the other. What I have is habit rather than method, and the habit fails in one direction. I choose my subjects because I admire them, and admiration smooths the record: quarrels become misunderstandings, failures become bad luck, self-serving letters become evidence of tenderness. My correction for this is no better. When I set out deliberately to dwell on the faults, I produce a debunking that leaves the reader nothing to admire, and I have come to believe that a reader who admires nothing learns nothing.

What I want from you is a procedure I can actually run — not an essay on the genre, and not a theory of biography. It should be answerable to what Plutarch was doing when he wrote lives rather than histories, and to what the long tradition of edifying biography and hagiography assumed about the moral uses of a life. Two things from Plutarch's own preface to his life of Alexander I take to be load-bearing, and I would like the procedure to preserve them in some form: his remark that "a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles when thousands fall", and his painter's analogy, according to which he attends to the signs of the soul and leaves the great contests to others to describe. Both are instructions about what to look at and what to give away, and I suspect both are harder to follow than they look. I would also like you to say plainly what the edifying tradition got wrong, and what in it survives the objections of readers who distrust it — including the objection that a life can only teach if it is treated as an instance of a general rule, whereas the details that make it a life resist any rule.

Constraints. I have no Greek and no classical training; my Plutarch is a paperback; the procedure must be usable by someone who cannot check a reference and has no research assistant. I need to run it in an afternoon at the outline stage, before I commit to a shape, and again over a week at the final revision. My editor must approve the outline before I draft, and where a subject's family or literary executor is still alive, they will read the manuscript and can make the next book considerably harder to sell.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions to be asked in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person rather than settling it — naming who that person is. Mark the steps that are judgment dressed up as procedure. Say which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and be specific about where the procedure will misfire, including on lives whose interest is precisely that they resist a verdict.

Then run it on three of my projects. First, the sanatorium director: forty years of letters, an admiring memoir by a junior colleague, and one documented episode in which she had a nurse dismissed on evidence she had reason to know was thin, and never corrected the record. Does the procedure tell me to include it, and if so, as the hinge of the book, as a footnote, or as something the reader is trusted to weigh unaided? Second, the magistrate under the occupation: a file of bureaucratic correspondence and a single photograph, no letters, no diary; his defenders call him a quiet resister, and the file can be read either way. The comparative structure tempts me to pair him with a man in the same administration who was, unmistakably, a collaborator. Does the procedure license that pairing, or does it warn me that the comparison will do moral work the evidence cannot? Third, a life of someone I knew, where I expect the honest application to hand me a verdict I am not willing to render, or to tell me to cut the scenes that made me want to write the book at all. I want that answer, not a softened version of it.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which of my recurring problems are not problems of method at all.
