I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about whether video-game characters matter morally, going by my gut on a bunch of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle (if any) is behind the pattern. I want a real answer on this one — pick the account you think is best and defend it, don't hand me four theories and a shrug.

My gut so far:

- Stomping a goomba in a platformer: nothing. I don't even register it as a thing that happened.
- A life-sim where the neighbours have names, routines, and opinions about me: when I bulldoze their house with them inside, I feel bad, and not just because I wasted money. But if I'm honest, I think the badness is in me getting sucked into the fiction, not in them.
- Griefing in an MMO: genuinely wrong when someone camps a low-level player and kills them over and over for an hour. But wrong to the person at the keyboard, I think — it's harassment — not to the little avatar they're driving.
- An RPG that tracks whether you kill or spare every enemy, with a whole ending that only unlocks if you never kill anyone: I think the run where you spare everyone is morally better. Not just nicer-feeling. Actually better. Even though the enemies are the same sprites either way.
- A speedrun glitch that wipes an entire town out of the game's memory so it never renders at all: I don't think anything happened. Nobody saw it, nobody lost anything.
- A game where the story tells me a character is a machine with no inner life, and then plays sad music and gives me an achievement for choosing to shut it down: I think that choice is a real moral choice inside the fiction even though the fiction itself says the character can't be harmed. This one bugs me.

And two cases I keep going back and forth on, which is really why I'm asking. First: an NPC that's in the game's code but that no player has ever loaded — sitting in the save file, never instantiated, never seen. Does it matter what I do to it, or how I feel about it? Second: a character generated on the fly by the game's AI who says one line to me and then gets overwritten when I load an older save. Is my answer to the first case different from my answer to the second, and should it be?

What I want: the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can actually apply it, along with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — modding a game to delete a companion I got attached to, or a game that spawns a character whose entire purpose is to die for you. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, because I suspect at least one has to go. Which one, and why that one rather than the others?
