I am a retired industrial chemist, and since retiring I have been trying to make up for forty years of reading nothing but the literature the job required. For the past two years I have been meeting most weeks with a small discussion group run by a former colleague, a physiologist who left the bench for philosophy some twenty years ago and now works on questions about belief and evidence. Her practice is to put a short case to us and then rule on it, and I have kept a record of the rulings. The question in every case is the same: was the scientist entitled to the conviction she formed, not whether the conviction turned out to be true?

1. A paleoclimatologist had to commit a field season, and a graduate student's year, to one of two rival reconstructions of an ancient temperature record. The evidence leaned toward one of them but settled nothing, and no further data could possibly arrive before the commitment had to be made. She came to believe the one reconstruction and built the season around it. Ruling: entitled.

2. A postdoc, writing a fellowship application, asserted that a mechanism was established when he privately judged the evidence thin. He could have written it as a conjecture at no cost to the application; the stronger claim simply read better. Ruling: not entitled.

3. A neuroscientist read a preprint reporting a striking effect, noticed that the authors had posted their data, could have rerun the analysis herself in an afternoon, did not, and came to believe the effect was real. Ruling: not entitled.

4. An infectious-disease physician faced a patient who was deteriorating. Her diagnosis was plausible but unconfirmed, and the confirmatory test would take four days she did not have. She came to believe the diagnosis and treated. Ruling: entitled.

5. A materials scientist's student reported an anomaly. A repeat measurement could have been arranged the following week at negligible cost. She announced at a conference that the material had the property in question. Ruling: not entitled.

6. A field ecologist staked a five-year survey on the hypothesis that a relict population survived, on the strength of one ambiguous sighting. The survey was the only way to find out whether it had, and had she waited the funding would have gone elsewhere. Ruling: entitled.

She has never contradicted herself, and I have tried to write her rule out in a single sentence; I keep producing two or three candidates that fit her record equally well, and I cannot tell which of them is doing the work.

Next week I am to bring the case, and it is this. A physician-scientist I know sits on her hospital's clinical-ethics committee. The committee must vote on a Thursday evening on whether to permit a single patient who has exhausted his approved options to receive an experimental treatment. She has come to believe that the treatment will help him; what she has behind the belief is a mechanism she finds compelling and two or three inconclusive reports. The committee's own dossier contains an unpublished case series that bears directly on the question. She has an hour before the meeting in which she could read it. She does not read it, and she votes to permit the treatment.

Please predict her ruling. Lead with it in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. I want: the two or three rules that fit her earlier rulings, and what each of them predicts here; which rule you think she is actually running, and on what grounds; the case I should bring the week after, which would best separate whichever rules survive her ruling on mine; and, if her record genuinely leaves the matter open, what she would be committing herself to, in each direction, by ruling as she does.
