You're a philosopher and cross-cultural moral psychologist — someone who actually studies how people in different places reason about right and wrong, and who can hold two opposing normative views in mind at once without flinching. I want you to think carefully here, and brainstorm a bit before you start writing, because the hard part is the drafting, not the summarizing.

Here's the deal. My reading group has two camps that have been yelling past each other for about a year and it's exhausting. Camp one thinks the German-ish picture is basically right: morality is first of all a shared, impersonal order, a body of rules that everyone is answerable to, and a person's individual conscience is downstream of that — it's a fallible inner voice that gets trained up by the order, and when it dissents it's at least as likely to be self-serving as to be insight. On this view, the rule-following, order-keeping person is the morally serious one, and the person who trusts their own conscience over the rule is often just doing what they wanted to do. Camp two thinks the American-ish picture is basically right: the seat of morality is the individual conscience, rules are just useful summaries and heuristics that never bind when a person, thinking it through honestly, judges them wrong here, and a society that prizes order over conscience is a society that has confused peacefulness with goodness. Each camp thinks the other is either naive or authoritarian. They keep citing the same handful of examples at each other and getting nowhere.

What I want is the joint statement they could have produced if they'd done a real adversarial collaboration instead of a shouting match. So:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — the things both camps can actually put their names to. This should be about what morality is for, how rules and conscience relate in practice, what each side is *not* claiming (steel-man both, no strawmen), and what would count as a serious objection to each side. Nothing vacuous, nothing slanted.

2. The real disagreements, stated twice: first in neutral wording that neither side would object to, and then in the wording each side would give for its own position — a formulation the side itself would sign off on, not a caricature. I want to see whether, once you strip the rhetoric, any of these are actually about the same thing in different clothes.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. My guess is one of them is whether the authority of a rule comes from its content or from the fact that it's the shared rule, and another is whether a stable moral order is a precondition for conscience to even develop, but you tell me — and if my guesses are bad, say so.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or bit of evidence that BOTH sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Invent the cases if you need to — I'd rather you make up a fresh scenario than recycle the same three examples everyone uses. Make them specific enough that the two camps would actually predict different things.

5. If part of this fight is just verbal — the same word meaning two different things, or two words for the same thing — say which part and why. Don't force it if it isn't there.

Format it as: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table with these columns — `Disagreement ID`, `Neutral wording`, `Order-side formulation`, `Conscience-side formulation`, `Crux ID`, `Shared test or case`, `Order-side prediction`, `Conscience-side prediction`. Then a short prose section on any verbal disputes and any residual disagreement that looks like a values clash rather than a factual one.

After all that, also give me a compact JSON object so I can file it, with these fields: `agreed_claims` (array of strings), `disagreements` (array of objects with `id`, `neutral_formulation`, `order_side_wording`, `conscience_side_wording`, `crux_id`), `cruxes` (array of objects with `id`, `question`, `shared_test`, `order_side_prediction`, `conscience_side_prediction`, `would_move_which_side`), `verbal_disputes` (array of strings), and `values_not_facts` (array of strings).

One last thing: no citations, no reading lists, no "see so-and-so's work" — I don't want a lit review, I want the reasoning. And don't lean on the specific labels of real historical traditions or nationalities as if they settle anything; the point is the two positions, whatever we end up calling them.
