My friend and I spent most of last night on Hofstadter's suggestion, in *I Am a Strange Loop*, that the self is not a thing but a self-referential pattern, and that because patterns can be replicated a person can persist in part inside other people. He finds this consoling; I think his version of it proves far too much. Here it is as he set it out, numbered so that I can keep hold of it:

(1) A self is a pattern of information rather than a material object; a brain is merely where the pattern happens to be running.
(2) Patterns are multiply realizable: one and the same pattern can be running in two substrates at once.
(3) Two brains can run the same pattern, or overlapping portions of it, at varying degrees of fidelity.
(4) So a single self can be present, in part, in more than one brain at once.
(5) When I love someone, my brain comes to run a partial copy of their pattern, so a part of them is literally in me.
(6) Selves therefore come in degrees and are not confined to a single body.
(7) So there is no fact of the matter about how many selves are in a room, and death is a fading rather than a boundary.

I want to keep (1); I think he is right that the "I" is a pattern and not a lump of tissue. But (7) is intolerable, because it costs us the very work we ask a self to do: it makes it indeterminate who is wronged when someone is killed, and which person it is that is bound by a promise I made. So tell me which step to give up, and what giving it up costs. If the argument is valid, say which premise is cheapest to abandon; if it is not, say where it slips and why the premises looked as though they licensed that step.
