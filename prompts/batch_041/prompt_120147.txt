Ok this is a slightly weird request but bear with me. I'm in a consciousness reading group — mixed bag, two grad students, one neuroscientist who is very patient with us, and a guy who brings up panpsychism every single week — and we've spent the whole term circling the theory that says a system is conscious exactly to the extent that it constitutes a maximally irreducible cause–effect structure: conscious for itself and not for an observer, where the structure isn't a cause of the experience but just is the experience. The problem is we keep argueing about whether the theory is right, and I dont think we've ever written down what a theory of consciousness would have to deliver in the first place. So I want the spec before we read anything else. Give me the desiderata: the full list of what any adequate theory of consciousness has to get right — the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the empirical results it has to be consistent with, the questions it has to actually answer rather than defer — and for each one, the consideration or case that puts it on the list. By "cases" I mean roughly: the cerebellum, which has a huge share of the brain's neurons and seems to contribute nothing to experience; a deep feedforward network that passes every behavioural test you could ever run for pain or for seeing red; a grid of logic gates that behaves like nothing in particular; a system that behaves exactly like you but is implemented as a lookup table; the split-brain patients; the fact that blindsight and implicit processing and a whole dorsal stream can do real work with no experience attached; the graded fading under anaesthesia and the return in dreaming. And the distinctions I'd guess matter: between being conscious and how conscious; between the amount of experience and its structure or quality; between a theory and a mere correlate or a measure; between substrate and the function it computes; between what a system does and what it is. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's prejudice wearing a lab coat — be blunt about that, because I suspect half of what we'd write down in the abstract is smuggled in from the theory we already like.

The part I most want is the end. I have a hunch that some subset of these desiderata can't all be satisfied at once. My candidate is this one: you can't simultaneously demand (a) that the theory be about what experience is like for the system itself, (b) that it be sensitive to the system's causal structure rather than to what the system does, and (c) that it be testable — because every test we can run only ever sees behaviour, so (c) drags you back toward the very thing (b) was meant to rule out. If that's right, say so and say exactly which one has to go and what you'd sacrifice to keep the others; if I've got the wrong pair, name the pair that actually collides. Then flag which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out — the one that, if you dropped it, would let the most theories back in — and after that, briefly, how the integrated-information theory and its live rivals (global workspace, higher-order, recurrent processing, whatever else you think is actually still standing) do against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; I can do the scoreboard myself at the meeting. This is for a section I have to write on whether we're in any position to say anything about machine welfare, so I care about the practical version of the question too: what would a theory have to deliver before it could tell us, about a system we can only poke from the outside, whether there's anyone home. And no citations please, obviously — I'm not going to go read anything, I want the argument.
