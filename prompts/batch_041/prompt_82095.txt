I'm leading the session on the historical Jesus at our little "how do we know anything about the past" reading group next month, and I have to circulate a written position note beforehand, so I'd rather you think it through with me than hand me a servey. The thing has been running since the 1700s and near as I can tell nobody has moved an inch in fifty years, which is why I'm asking about the disagreement itself and not for a verdict on Jesus.

Here's the fight as I see it. Team A: the criteria of authenticity — dissimilarity (and double dissimilarity), embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, plus the ones about fitting a first-century Galilean Jewish setting. Used carefully, they supposedly leave you a modest but real core of sayings and deeds. Team B: the criteria are circular and self-defeating — dissimilarity authenticates only what fits neither Judaism nor the early church, i.e. a figure who belongs nowhere and can't be situated in the history he's supposed to belong to; embarrassment needs the premise that we know what the early church wouldn't have invented, and we know that only from the same texts we're judging; the criteria assume the sources are seperate when they may just be literary dependents; and they can only ever isolate sayings, never the framework, the narrative shape, the deeds. Team C, the memory people: stop treating the gospels as quarries of units to be authenticated and treat them as social memory — reconstructive, present-serving, not therefore useless — and then the criteria are simply the wrong instrument, because the question isn't "did he say it" but "what sort of memory of him is this, and what does its shape tell us".

My hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than flattered: I think 70% of this is a fight about the word "authentic". Said-by-him, characteristic-of-him, faithfully-remembered-as-him, and plausible-in-his-context are four different targets, and the camps have each quietly picked one, so they're marking different exams and calling each other's papers failures. If that's right, the remaining 30% is real — something about whether these sources can bear weight at all — and no definition is going to dissolve it.

Two things I want you to take seriously as possible game-changers rather than as asides. (1) Base rates: even a criterion with a decent likelihood ratio can't lift a low prior very far, so whether the toolkit is over- or under-powered depends on how likely a given saying is to be authentic before any criterion gets applied, and nobody wants to say that number out loud. (2) I've never seen a classicist run a criterion of embarrassment on Socrates, or on whether Alexander really gave the speeches the ancient historians put in his mouth; so either our sources for Jesus are unusually bad, or "authenticity" is a theological category that came in the back door and never left.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result, or the honest admission that nothing will move it? Don't give me a list of who said what; I don't need names or a reading list, I need the shape of the thing.
