For the past two terms our graduate seminar in the history of science has been circling the so-called Needham question — why the sustained, cumulative investigation of nature that consolidated in early modern Europe did not, on the standard telling, consolidate in China, given how much relevant groundwork China had laid — and I have grown convinced that most of what we do with the question is a genre exercise rather than an inquiry. Our department has a small tradition of a satirical year-end broadsheet in which the graduate students roast the field's habits, and I have been asked to write the centrepiece. Please draft an Onion-style news report — headline plus roughly 600 words — announcing the founding of a (fictional) research centre devoted to what its director calls the study of comparative non-invention, whose remit is to fund only projects phrased as contrastive questions of the form 'why did X not do Y, whereas Z did?'. The piece should be deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader.

The humour has to come from getting the underlying conceptual trouble right, so that someone who works on this literature laughs rather than winces. A few threads you can pull: a why-question of this shape silently presupposes a contrast class — asking why Britain and not China has already fixed the space of admissible answers, and swapping the foil ('why not the Ottomans? why not Japan? why not anyone?') changes the question rather than merely re-describing it; the counterfactual at the centre of these projects is usually unfalsifiable, so the research programme can absorb any evidence whatsoever; 'China' and 'Europe' get treated as single deliberating agents persisting across centuries, when the honest unit of analysis is some much narrower practice or institution; and the outcome is smuggled in as the premise, so the question only looks well-posed in hindsight. Let the centre's portfolio expose the reductio — any pair can be plugged into the template, so its funded projects should be comically arbitrary while each is defended in fluent methodological prose. Include at least one quotation from a sceptical philosopher of science, who should make a fair point rather than a strawman, and one from the centre's director, who should answer it confidently and miss the point entirely.

Close with a sidebar of example grant titles in the centre's house style (three or four will do, e.g. 'Why did the Song not patent the steam engine, while the Romans did not either?'), which should land the arbitrariness of the foil without ever saying so. The report should be legible to a reader who knows nothing about the Great Divergence — no names, dates, or citations are needed — but it should feel to a specialist like it was written by someone who has read the California School and the contrastive-explanation literature closely enough to be annoyed by both.
