I'm a first-year PhD student in a natural history museum lab, and my supervisor is the person who in practice decides what counts as a species in our group. She calls herself a pluralist, which so far as I can tell means she definately decides case by case, so I'm trying to reverse-engineer her actual rule before I submit my first monograph chapter (she's the reviewer). Her recent track record:

1. Two allopatric populations of a flightless montane beetle — a valley between them, neither one flies. Fixed allele differences at 4 of 12 nuclear loci; reciprocal monophyly in the mtDNA tree and in 3 of 5 nuclear gene trees. No lab crosses (would be a nightmare to set up). She described them as two species.

2. Two parapatric salamander populations with a 20-km hybrid zone. Inside the zone about 5% of individuals come out admixed (q between 0.2 and 0.8 in her clustering runs); outside it each population is fixed for different alleles at 3 loci, and the mtDNA is reciprocally monophyletic. Two species.

3. Two broadly sympatric grass populations that interbreed freely — same flowering time, same pollinators, no intrinsic isolation in crossing trials — with no fixed differences at 15 loci and no reciprocal monophyly, same soil, same habitat. Fst between them is 0.30, but she lumped them into one species.

4. Two sympatric beetle populations that are morphologically and genetically near-identical: no fixed differences at 20 loci, Fst 0.15, no reciprocal monophyly, same host plant, same microhabitat. But lab crosses give F1s that are all sterile. Two species.

5. An all-female, obligately parthenogenetic stick-insect lineage — no males at all, so nothing for it to interbreed with, diagnosable at 6 loci, reciprocally monophyletic. She gave it its own name.

6. Two allopatric snail populations, all markers hypervariable microsatellites: Fst 0.45, no fixed differences anywhere, no reciprocal monophyly (they share ancestral haplotypes), no interbreeding data, no ecological differences. One species.

Now my case. Two populations of a beetle from a different genus, overlapping across most of their range, same host plant, same microhabitat. No fixed differences at 20 loci, Fst 0.04, no reciprocal monophyly — they share mtDNA haplotypes and plenty of nuclear alleles and the gene trees are a tangle. Lab crosses give F1s that are viable but completely sterile; in the field I've genotyped about 2000 individuals and found 3 F1s, no F2s, no backcrosses. So the only thing pointing to these being seperate lineages is the hybrid sterility.

What I want: what will she say, and why. Which of her old cases is the closest analog? What rule is she actually running, and what other rules fit all six of her verdicts just as well? Which of those rules does my case separate, and which further case (describe it) would separate the ones that survive? Also flag any rule her record rules out, and say which case does the ruling out.

Take the role of a systematist slash philosopher of biology who has thought carefully about species delimitation. Start by brainstorming the candidate rules — I know the usual suspects (reproductive isolation, diagnosability, monophyly, genotypic clustering, some pluralist "any strong evidence" story), but I want the ones that actually fit her behavior, not a textbook recitation. Score each candidate against all six verdicts before you commit. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so instead of faking a winner. No citations needed, just the reasoning.
