Im in a history-of-cybernetics seminar and my paper is supposed to be an exercise in counterfactual intellectual biography: take somebody who died before a bunch of stuff existed, and work out from their actual commitments what they would have thought about it. I drew von Neumann, which sounds easy and is actually hard, because he died in 1957 and everything I can come up with on my own about him and modern AI is stuff I could've guessed without reading a page (he'd be fascinated, he'd want the numbers, he'd ask about energy per operation). So I need you to go past that. What I want is his likely positions on a specific set of questions that only became live after he was gone. First, whether you can get thought out of parts that are individually unreliable and stochastic -- I know he cared about reliable computation from flaky components, and I think the brain lectures he was writing when he died, the ones that came out as The Computer and the Brain, turn on that comparison. Second, whether the brain is really a digital computer or whether he'd have insisted it's some analog-statistical hybrid, and whether that distinction does any real work for the question of whether a machine can think at all, or whether it's just a fact about engineering. Third, whether he'd have thought that simply scaling up -- more parameters, more data, more compute -- gets you a mind, or whether he had a specific reason to think the architecture or the learning story is missing something. Fourth, what he'd make of the modern control problem, building something that outruns us and trying to keep it pointed in the right direction, given how untroubled he was by machines beating us at particular tasks.

For each one, give me the prediction, say which of his views or habits of mind it's riding on, and be honest about where you're extrapolating past what he actually argued. If for some of these the sources just dont determine an answer, say that and say what the closest thing to an answer is, rather than manufacturing a quotable position -- a paper full of confident von Neumann opinions about transformers is worse than one that marks where the evidence runs out. And the part I actually care about most: flag anywhere a modern debate looks like it lines up with a distinction he drew but doesn't, i.e. where people are reading him through fights he never had. My study group is split between "he's basically a functionalist" and "he's basically a Searle-style skeptic," and I suspect both are anachronistic in different ways -- tell me if I'm wrong about that. No citations needed, I'm not asking you to guess at page numbers, and skip the game theory and the bomb stuff, this is only about brains and machines.
