Erwin Schrödinger's What Is Life? (1944) argued that the hereditary substance must be an aperiodic solid whose structure encodes a code-script, and that organisms hold decay at bay by importing what he called negative entropy; the picture was built before the double helix, before prions and giant viruses, before chemically synthesized minimal genomes, and long before anyone seriously proposed that a machine might be alive. Your task is to reconstruct, as rigorously as the evidence permits, how Schrödinger would have answered a set of questions about the definition and the boundaries of life that arose only after his death — and to do so as disciplined extrapolation rather than free invention.

Work outward from what he actually committed himself to, in What Is Life?, in Mind and Matter, and in his published reflections on physics, biology, and philosophy, and then extend those commitments to cases he never saw. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not attribute particular claims to particular pages. Where a prediction turns on a detail of his position that you cannot reconstruct with confidence, say so explicitly and say what would settle the matter.

Rule on at least the following borderline cases, one by one:

- viruses, including the giant viruses that carry large genomes and genes for translation-related machinery;
- viroids and other naked, self-replicating RNAs;
- prions, which propagate a conformation rather than a nucleic-acid sequence;
- transposable elements and other self-replicating stretches of a genome;
- a synthetic cell whose genome was chemically synthesized down to a minimal gene set and which grows and divides;
- self-replicating computer code and computer viruses;
- a large language model that produces text indistinguishable from a human being's.

For each case, state whether he would have classified it as alive, as not alive, or as a case in which the question is ill-posed, and identify the criterion doing the work. Where several candidate criteria are available — metabolic self-maintenance, Darwinian evolvability, structural encoding of a code-script — say which one he would have privileged and why.

Then address the deeper questions. First, would he have treated life as a natural kind with a determinate essence, or as a cluster or family-resemblance concept whose boundaries are fixed by convention and convenience? Second, since his own criterion leaned on a structural code-script, how would he have received the discovery that the code is a physical molecule — as confirmation of his position or as a destabilization of it? Third, would prions and large-genome viruses have compelled him to revise the criterion, or could his framework absorb them without amendment? Fourth, what in his wider metaphysics — including his long-standing interest in the relation between consciousness and the physical world — bears on whether a machine could be alive, and does that material cut for or against his biological criteria?

Conclude by separating the predictions you would stake a great deal on from those you would stake little on, and name the single piece of evidence or argument that would most change your reconstruction. Structure the response as follows: a compact statement of the criteria you take Schrödinger to have held; a case-by-case table of rulings; a discussion of the four deeper questions; and a closing section on confidence and revision.
