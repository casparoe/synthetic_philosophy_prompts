I am assembling a two-year working group — four philosophers and two doctoral students, with modest funding and no expectation of archival or empirical work — and what I need from you is a defensible research agenda rather than a survey of the terrain. The subject is what I will call commitment races. I have in mind the following structure: two or more agents, each seeking advantage by making some course of action more costly or more difficult to reverse for itself, arrive at an outcome worse for each of them than the outcome of no one moving first; and, crucially, each agent's move is defensible on its own terms, so that the race is not merely a cascade of individual errors. The stock illustration is the competition among the great powers in July 1914 to mobilize first, but I take the structure to be far more general — entrenchment clauses in constitutions, preemptive patents, irreversible infrastructure, escalation ladders, even the personal strategy of burning one's bridges.

Which open questions in this area most deserve the group's two years, and in what order? I want the ordering defended on stated criteria: how far upstream a question sits, in the sense that other questions cannot be settled without it; how tractable it is with conceptual tools alone; how much further work it would render unnecessary; and how badly practice would be misled by getting it wrong. Tell me also which of the questions that look central are not, because their resolution would change nothing downstream, and what developments over the two years would reorder the list.

Here, in no particular order, are the candidates I have been turning over. Keep, reorder, merge, or discard them as you judge best.

1. Whether commitment races are one phenomenon or several, and whether the class is unified by irreversibility, by mutual anticipation, or by the absence of any enforcer who could make standing down credible.
2. Why a race occurs at all. If the commitments are common knowledge and the participants are forward-looking, the race should be foreseen and never entered. The phenomenon therefore seems to require opacity — commitments that cannot be observed, or commitments whose credibility is itself what is being contested. Is a commitment race, at bottom, a race in the production of credible belief?
3. Whether a race in which the commitment is a physical fait accompli — a fence, a factory, a deployed weapon — is the same phenomenon as a race in which the commitment is a public announcement with nothing enforcing it.
4. Responsibility under many hands: whether participants can be blameworthy when each contribution is individually rational, individually minor, and individually dispensable, and whether anyone has a duty to be the first to stand down.
5. Whether races can be good. A race to be first to commit to a moratorium, or to entrench a valuable institution, looks structurally identical to the cases we deplore. If it is, can such a race be engineered, and what does the possibility do to the diagnosis of the bad cases?
6. Whether the harm in a race is caused by the earlier commitments or merely made possible by them — whether lock-in is better described as the elimination of options than as the production of outcomes — and whether the phenomenon survives the death of every participant, as it seems to in path dependence.

Finally, I have a conjecture I would like tested rather than accepted. I suspect the load-bearing distinction is not between commitments and non-commitments but between commitments that are irreversible and commitments that are merely costly to reverse: a fully irreversible and observable commitment ought to end a contest by concession rather than prolong it, which suggests that races live in the intermediate zone of partial and privately known reversal costs. If that is right, the usual framing of commitment is aimed at the wrong question. If it is wrong, I would like to know precisely where the argument fails.

And tell me what is missing — one question I have not raised that belongs near the top, together with your best guess as to why I have been blind to it.
