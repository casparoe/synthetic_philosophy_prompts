I am a novelist at work on a book about a community that has no use for the concept of lying — not because its members are saintly, but because nothing in their form of life calls for the move. I keep returning to the fourth voyage of Gulliver's Travels, where the Houyhnhnms have no word for a lie and must talk their way around it with a periphrasis meaning, roughly, to say a thing that is not. I would like a reading of Book IV through Wittgenstein's later philosophy: the language-game as inseparable from a form of life, and family resemblance as the alternative to definition by essence. Does Swift dramatize what Wittgenstein argues — that a lie is not a failure of reference but a move in a practice, unavailable where the practice is absent? And does Gulliver's position among the Yahoos — his horror at being classified with them, his body betraying a kinship his reason denies — amount to a family-resemblance problem before the fact, or does Swift want a sharper answer about what a human being is than Wittgenstein would permit? The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not turn Swift into a philosopher with a thesis, nor Wittgenstein into a moralist about horses, and it should say plainly where Book IV resists the lens — above all at the moment the Houyhnhnm assembly reasons its way toward a proposal about the Yahoos, where their rationality looks least like a form of life and most like a calculus. Close with what the novel shows that the Investigations cannot argue.
