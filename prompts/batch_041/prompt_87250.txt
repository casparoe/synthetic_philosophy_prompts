I'm on the board of our town's little local history museum — not an academic, I'm a retired postman who got roped in, so don't bury me in jargon.

Here's the mess. There's a field at the edge of town that everyone's called Bloody Meadow for as long as anyone remembers, and the story is that a battle was fought there involving King Arthur's men (or a local lord who's supposed to be the "real" Arthur, depending on who's telling it). A Victorian vicar wrote the story down, and that's the earliest version anyone's got. Last year a metal-detector bloke pulled a buckle and two coins out of the field; the finds officer dated the coins to roughly the 5th–6th century, one of them struck maybe eighty years after the battle's supposed to have happened. Then the county archaeologist dug a trench and found a ditch, some cattle bones, and nothing else. Now the tourist board wants a sign saying "Site of the Battle of ___" to pull in coach tours, a family in town says it's their history passed down for generations, and a donor has offered to pay for a new gallery if we tell "the real story."

We meet Thursday and I have to bring something. What I want from you: a recommendation on how we word the field sign and the gallery labels — do we say "legend", "tradition", "possible site", and does the buckle change anything — plus a rule we can actually apply the next time someone walks in with a story like this, and what we tell the donor if they insist on the stronger wording. One board member keeps saying you can't prove a negative and that Troy and the Exodus are the exact same argument; another says a museum has a duty not to launder a story into a fact. I think they're both partly right and I can't work out how to split it.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no closing paragraph — I'll read them straight out at the meeting.
