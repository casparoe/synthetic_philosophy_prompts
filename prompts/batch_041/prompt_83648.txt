So I got into a long, stupid Discord argument the other night — the usual crowd, a couple of people who've read way too much LessWrong, plus one guy who actually ships production ML models for a living — and it somehow landed on the sorites paradox, and I walked away with this nagging feeling that I couldn't tell whether the entire philosophical industry around vagueness is doing anything my day job doesn't already do with better notation. Some background on me: I'm a working ML engineer, no formal philosophy past one undergrad ethics course, and my mental furniture is feature spaces, decision boundaries, a threshold on a logit, label noise, soft labels and calibration curves, the aleatoric-vs-epistemic uncertainty distinction, and that miserable moment in a clustering problem where you have to pick k and there's no fact of the matter about what k is. I know that vocabulary cold. I do not know the philosophers' vocabulary cold, and every time someone explains 'supervaluationism' to me I nod and then suspect they've just described a credal set with extra steps.

Here's what I want. Take the four big families of response to the sorites — the one that says there really is a sharp cutoff for 'heap'/'tall'/'bald' but we can never in principle know where it falls (epistemicism), the one that says a vague predicate has many admissible precise sharpenings and a sentence is true iff it comes out true on all of them (supervaluationism), the one that says these predicates come in degrees and the truth value of 'x is tall' is some number in [0,1] rather than a yes/no (degree theory / fuzzy approaches), and the one that says the cutoff quietly shifts with conversational context, which is why no single step of the sorites ever feels like the wrong one (contextualism) — and restate each of them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Policies, boundaries, thresholds, calibration, soft vs hard targets, ensembling, hyperparameters, whatever fits. I want the honest translation, not a flattering one: don't quietly import the ML framing's assumptions (like 'there's a ground-truth label that just happens to be noisy') and then declare victory, because half the point of the exercise is to see whether my framing is assuming away the puzzle.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. For each of the four: what maps cleanly, what only maps if I let you fudge something, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. And at the end I want your read on the meta-question — when something fails to translate, is that because my framework is genuinely missing a concept, or because the philosophical claim was less substantive than it sounded and the translation just exposed that? My priors, which I'd like you to either puncture or confirm: that supervaluationism is basically a credal set or an ensemble of admissible models, that degree theory is basically soft labels with a fuzzy ground truth, and that contextualism is basically 'the threshold is a hyperparameter that depends on the deployment context and there's no privileged context' — i.e. that the whole debate is a fight about how to talk about a threshold nobody can locate. Tell me where that's right, where it's too cute by half, and whether the epistemicist in particular is saying something my framework literally cannot represent.
