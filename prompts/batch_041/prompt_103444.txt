So my history class is doing a unit on how historians decide what counts as real history, and my teacher spent a whole day on the demarcation problem — Popper and falsifiability, Lakatos, and that Laudan guy who says the whole thing is a fake problem — and then over break she had us read The Da Vinci Code, which I'd somehow never read before. Now I can't stop thinking about it. The very first page, before the story even starts, is a little list of claims presented as flat fact, including that the Priory of Sion is a real secret society going back to 1099, and then the novel goes on to build this whole world of Merovingian bloodlines, Jesus and Mary Magdalene having a kid, hidden documents, the works. And then at the end you're supposed to go "well it's a novel, it's fiction, so it's fine." But it's not fine, because my friend now definately believes the Priory was real and I can never articulate why the book isn't allowed to do that. It sits on the same shelf as the Atlantis and ancient aliens stuff but it's a bestseller that people actually take seriously.

What I want is a reading of The Da Vinci Code through the demarcation problem — not just "is this pseudohistory," but what the novel itself is doing with the question. Some things I've noticed that I want you to take seriously: Langdon's whole method is pattern-matching across paintings and symbols, which is basically the same move the pseudohistorians make, so the hero and the cranks are running the same software; Teabing is clearly the villain, but the book mostly accepts his history and only punishes his methods and his violence, which feels like a weird place to draw the line; and the cryptex, where you can't just smash it open, you need the right key or the message destroys itself — I don't know if that's a real point about unfalsifiable theories or just a fun puzzle. Does the novel dramatize the demarcation problem, or does it smuggle in an answer like "it's fiction so anything goes" that a philosopher would say doesn't actually work? And where does the book resist the reading — where is it honest about something the Popper-versus-Laudan debate misses, like why a conspiracy theory feels more explanatory and satisfying than the boring scholarly version? Point me to specific scenes I can use in my essay, and tell me honestly where this reading breaks down and I have to admit the novel isn't really about this at all. Please don't give me citations or a reading list, my teacher will kill me if I hand in another bibliography I didn't actually read.
