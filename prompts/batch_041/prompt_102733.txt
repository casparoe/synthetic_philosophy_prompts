I am trying to work through W. V. Quine's case for the indeterminacy of translation, and I have reached a point where I can restate the argument but cannot see what holds it up. I am working from notes, so what follows is my paraphrase rather than his wording, and I would like you to correct it where it is wrong.

The argument, as I have it: the radical translator records the native utterance gavagai in the presence of rabbits, and having settled by trial and error which native noises serve as assent and dissent, renders the sentence as Rabbit or Lo, a rabbit. Nothing in the native's behavior compels that rendering. Every occasion on which the native is disposed to assent to gavagai is equally an occasion on which he would assent to Undetached rabbit part, Rabbit stage, There is rabbithood here, and indefinitely many other renderings, since each of these is keyed to the same pattern of stimulation. What would separate them is the native's treatment of identity — whether this gavagai is the same as that one — but the linguist cannot put that question until he has already framed analytical hypotheses about the native's apparatus of identity, pronouns, plurals, and copulas, and those hypotheses answer only to the same behavioral evidence. So a manual of translation may be empirically flawless and still distribute reference in ways no further observation could adjudicate. Quine's moral, on my reading, is not that translation is impossible or that the native's meaning is concealed, but that there is no fact of the matter over and above the adequacy of the manual.

What I cannot get past is that last step, and a case Quine does not discuss makes it worse for me. Suppose the natives themselves argue, at length and in apparent sincerity, about whether gavagai is really about rabbits or really about a relation among hunters — one faction treating the word as naming a thing, the other as naming an event in which the speaker participates, each offering reasons and correcting the other's usage, and the language containing a particle the translator will be tempted to render as really or literally. Is this argument evidence for the linguist, or only more behavior to be accommodated? On one reading it is precisely the missing data: the natives' own corrections show which identity questions they treat as well-formed, which is what Quine said the linguist could not get at. On the other reading it changes nothing, because the linguist must still decide whether the two factions disagree about a shared subject matter or are talking past one another under different manuals — and that decision looks underdetermined in just the way the original one was.

Three things, then. First, is my paraphrase faithful, and if so which step is doing the real work — the premise that all evidence is stimulatory, the claim that the identity apparatus is itself only hypothesis, or something I have not named? Second, in the dispute case, does the natives' own meta-linguistic argument reduce the indeterminacy, or does the indeterminacy simply re-apply at the level of that argument, leaving us to ask whether the two factions mean the same thing by really? Third, and this is what I most want: if it does re-apply, is there any principled stopping point short of a regress, and where would Quine locate it?
