I am a novelist at work on a book in which one of the two principal characters is a scholar of Buddhism, and for the last several months I have been reading a popular introduction to Madhyamaka in the evenings, chiefly so that I do not put nonsense into her mouth. The author of that book offers the following account of Nāgārjuna, which I reproduce as faithfully as I can:

Nāgārjuna's central teaching is emptiness: nothing has a nature of its own, because whatever we can name arises in dependence on something else, and what depends on another cannot be found in itself. His instrument is the tetralemma, the four-cornered argument he devised, in which a proposition is run through as true, as false, as both, and as neither, and all four corners are refused. The point of the exercise is that reality lies beyond the reach of logic altogether and can only be approached in silence. It follows that causation, change, and the self are illusions, and that the world of everyday experience is a conventional fiction — a raft to be abandoned once the far shore is reached. Only one thing survives the demolition: emptiness is not itself a view, and to hold it as one would be the final and most incurable mistake. Nāgārjuna, on this reading, is not a philosopher in our sense at all but a mystic whose arguments exist to be dismantled along with everything else.

I have no philosophical training and no way of checking the author against the sources; I have a translation of the Root Verses on the shelf, but I open it and find only refutations of positions I cannot reconstruct. What I would like from you is an audit I can act on. Go through the summary as a specialist in Madhyamaka would, and tell me which sentences such a specialist would accept without demur, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong; where the author is repeating a common simplification rather than committing an error; and where a defender of Nāgārjuna would lodge an objection rather than a correction. I am especially interested in the last sentence, since the whole portrait of the character depends on whether her tradition is finally a philosophy or a mysticism — say plainly whether that verdict follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is the author's own addition dressed as a conclusion. And since I am writing fiction rather than an essay, end by telling me which of these claims my character could hold as her own without betraying herself as an impostor, and which ones I should keep out of her mouth entirely. For what it is worth, the mystical reading rather appeals to me — it is the sort of thing I would like to be true — which is precisely why I do not trust my own ear for it.
