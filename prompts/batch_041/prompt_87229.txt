Here's the question I set:

Consider this argument:

(A) If eating meat is wrong, then paying someone else to eat meat on your behalf is wrong.
(B) Eating meat is wrong.
(C) So paying someone else to eat meat on your behalf is wrong.

Explain why an expressivist about moral language seems unable to account both for (i) the validity of this argument and (ii) the fact that "wrong" means the same thing in (A) as it does in (B). Then say whether you think the expressivist can answer the problem, and why.

And here's the answer I'm marking:

Expressivism is the view that moral judgements don't describe the world; they express attitudes. Saying that eating meat is wrong isn't like saying that eating meat is bad for you — the second can be true or false, while the first just gives voice to the speaker's disapproval. The Frege-Geach problem is the standard objection to this view, and it turns on Frege's distinction between the content of a sentence and the force with which it's uttered.

The problem shows up most clearly in the argument above. In (B) the sentence "eating meat is wrong" is asserted, so on the expressivist account it expresses the speaker's disapproval of eating meat. In (A) it isn't asserted at all: the speaker needn't disapprove of eating meat, they might just be pointing out that someone else's attitudes don't hang together. So (A) doesn't express any attitude, and the word "wrong" there isn't doing its usual work — it's just part of a hypothetical. But then "wrong" means something different in (A) and in (B), and the argument equivocates. Since the argument is plainly valid, the expressivist looks stuck between keeping their theory of meaning and keeping the validity of the argument.

I don't think the problem is fatal. One way out is to deny that the same word has to carry the same meaning in every context. Plenty of words shift about — "tall" means something different of a jockey and of a cathedral — so the expressivist can say that the occurrence of "wrong" in the antecedent is a thinner, inherited occurrence, and there's no equivocation after all.

The other and more orthodox route is Blackburn's quasi-realism. The thought is that the expressivist is entitled to everything realist-sounding that we say — that moral sentences are true, that moral knowledge is possible, that there are moral facts in some suitably deflated sense — because these ways of talking are the natural, earned extension of the practical business of expressing attitudes. Part of the machinery is the "logic of attitudes": the attitude expressed by (A) is a higher-order attitude, roughly one of disapproving of anyone who disapproves of eating meat while not disapproving of paying someone else to do it. So someone who accepts (A) and (B) but rejects (C) isn't contradicting themselves at the level of propositions; they're holding a combination of attitudes that can't be held together. That's all the validity we should want.

What I'd defend hardest is the appeal to deflationism about truth. Once you see that "true" is just a device for agreeing and generalising, there's no reason to think moral sentences can't be true — and once they can be true, the Frege-Geach problem dissolves. So expressivism survives, though Blackburn owes us a great deal more detail about how the logic of attitudes is supposed to work.
