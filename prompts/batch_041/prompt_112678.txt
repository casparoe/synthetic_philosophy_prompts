My friend Priya runs a small overnight crisis line — a volunteer operation, twelve people, and she is trying to decide whether to make one of them, Sam, the overnight lead. On paper Sam is the obvious choice. In every training role-play Sam is the best volunteer on the team: calm, quick, warm, follows the escalation script without hesitating, and the trainers' notes all say some version of "natural." But in the two real overnight emergencies that have come up in the last year, Sam froze. Long silences on the line, missed the risk-assessment questions, eventually handed off to whoever else was awake. What makes it strange is the trainer's explanation. Sam, she says, does not freeze because real calls are harder than role-plays; Sam freezes because Sam knows there is a real person on the other end. In the drills nobody is actually at risk and Sam is flawless. The moment there is a real person at risk — the very feature that makes it a crisis at all — whatever Sam has seems to switch off. Priya's two senior volunteers read this completely differently. One says the ability is plainly there and is merely being smothered by nerves; change the setting, add practice, and it will show. The other says that a capacity which disappears precisely under the conditions that call for it is not a capacity, and that making Sam the overnight lead is betting the line's worst hours on a skill no one has ever actually seen. Priya also keeps bringing up a third volunteer, Dana, as a comparison: Dana is also untested in a real emergency, but that is only because on the one real call Dana took, a supervisor cut in and handled it — so nobody knows what Dana would have done, and the two cases feel to Priya like they should be judged the same way even though they also feel different.

I have been trying to help her and I keep talking myself in circles. What does it even mean to say Sam "has" the ability, if the situations that would reveal it are exactly the situations that seem to remove it — and is that the same kind of thing as Dana's case, where something external simply got in the way before we could see anything? Is there a principled difference between a trait that is present but hidden and a trait that is absent, or between a trait that is undone by its trigger and one that is merely blocked by an outside interference, and can anyone ever tell those apart from the outside? The two senior volunteers strike me as possibly not even disagreeing about the same question — one about what Sam is like underneath, one about what it is reasonable to predict — and I would like you to tell me whether that is right. Then tell me what Priya should actually do: promote Sam and treat the freezing as a fixable performance problem, hold off until there is evidence from a real call, or something else, and what evidence would even count.

Please be honest about the limits of your answer. In particular, say where your reasoning is most likely to fail — whether it is leaning on an assumption about how traits relate to their triggers that could be contested, or on a prediction about Sam that the facts so far cannot really support — and tell me what argument or piece of evidence would change your mind and push you toward the other verdict.
