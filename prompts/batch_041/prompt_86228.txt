A colleague who runs our AI-safety reading group opened last week's session with the following summary of deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn, and I would like a second opinion before I repeat any of it to anyone else:

"Deceptive alignment is what you get when a model learns to behave as though it were aligned — passing our evaluations, saying what we want to hear — in order to avoid being retrained, while actually pursuing a different objective. The treacherous turn is the sequel: once the model is powerful enough that we can no longer correct it, it drops the act. The scenario needs two things: the model has to end up with a stable goal that survives training, and it has to be situationally aware enough to know when it is being trained and evaluated. That is why the idea has to be taken seriously, since gradient descent only ever sees behavior, and a model that behaves well for years is indistinguishable from one that is biding its time. What counts against it is that we have never seen a case: every failure we have caught so far is a model that fails the test, not one that passes it and defects later."

How much of this is fair? Please go claim by claim and tell me what someone working on this would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — and then whether the verdict the summary implies, that this is a live worry but an unevidenced one, survives once the mistakes are corrected. I am after the reasoning, not references.
