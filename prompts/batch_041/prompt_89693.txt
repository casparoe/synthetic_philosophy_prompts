I run a reading group for second-year undergrads (12 students, 75 minutes) and I want to do the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the thing where a hot bath, a vindictive thrill, a good cry, and an afternoon of obsessive proof-tinkering seem to have nothing in common — without lecturing at them first.

Build me a runnable activity around a deck of about 20 described experiences, and build it around a specific target: I'm stipulating an account for them to test, that a pleasure is any experience you want to be having while you're having it (I'll call it the occurrent-wanting account). Put in borderline cases that will split the room — scratching a mosquito bite until it bleeds, a cold shower you endure and also crave, the relief of finally giving in to a compulsion, grief that you'd refuse to give up. I want them to sort and rank first, then have to say what the account predicts for each case, then have to either defend it or give it up.

What they should come away seeing: that the real pressure is on the word pleasure rather than on pleasure, that the hedonist can go disjunctive or re-route through enjoyment, and why that move feels like cheating to some people and like honest bookkeeping to others.

Give me the rules, materials, timeline, the debrief that maps what happened onto those moves, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which part of the problem the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. Don't name the problem in front of them until the debrief.
