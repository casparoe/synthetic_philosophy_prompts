Question 3 (25 marks). State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and identify the assumptions on which it rests. Does the theorem establish that classical utilitarianism — the doctrine that the correct social welfare function is the sum or average of individual utilities — is the uniquely rational basis for social evaluation? In your answer, distinguish the aggregation theorem from Harsanyi's impartial-observer argument, and explain how each bears on his disagreement with Rawls.

The answer reads as follows. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem concerns a society of n individuals, each with a preference ordering over prospects, together with a social preference ordering over the same prospects. It says that if each individual's ordering satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, if the social ordering satisfies those axioms as well, and if the weak Pareto principle holds — so that a prospect everyone strictly prefers is strictly preferred by society — then there are utility functions representing the individual orderings and non-negative weights such that the social ordering is represented by the weighted sum of those utility functions. Read on its own, this is a representation result rather than an ethical doctrine: it tells us that within a welfarist framework with cardinal, interpersonally comparable utilities the social welfare function has to be additive, but it does not fix the weights, and so it does not yet give us utilitarianism.

The interest of the theorem is that it shows how little normative input utilitarianism needs. The Pareto principle is accepted by almost everyone, and the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms simply spell out what it is to be rational in the face of risk, so the only additive social welfare function left standing is the unweighted sum: any unequal weighting would give one person's interests more weight than another's for no reason that the axioms could supply, and would therefore fail the requirement that the social ordering be determined by the individual orderings alone. Utilitarianism thus follows from the aggregation theorem together with the Pareto principle, and the impartial-observer argument, in which the observer is assumed to have an equal chance of being anyone, is best read as an illustration of that result rather than as an independent premise doing real work.

The comparison with Rawls is instructive. Harsanyi took over the device of the original position from Rawls, but substituted an equal-probability assumption for the parties' ignorance of probabilities, so that each party chooses by maximising expected utility behind the veil of ignorance. Against Rawls he argued that maximin is not a rational rule of choice, since it effectively treats the worst outcome as certain and so violates the sure-thing principle. The difference principle is therefore either a departure from rational choice or something that has to be defended on grounds external to decision theory. Since Rawls never claimed that the parties were maximising expected utility, the disagreement is really about whether their reasoning behind the veil of ignorance should be modelled as choice under risk at all.
