I'm the data governance lead at a company that swallowed a competitor last year, and my main job is running the shared data dictionary -- deciding when a term one team uses and a term another team uses are actually the same term and can be merged into a single definition, and when they only look alike and have to be kept seperate. We get a dozen or so of these alignment tickets a month and they aren't trivial: they end up in dashboards, in the numbers we hand the regulator, and in a few cases in people's bonuses. A bad merge is expensive, and a pointless separation is expensive in a different way.

I need a procedure a bright junior analyst with no philosophy training can run in an afternoon, that I can sign off on, with legal signing off separately on anything feeding a regulatory report. So: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is values rather than facts.

The frame I want it built on is the semantic externalism stuff -- Twin Earth, if you know it. The bit I actually care about is that what a term means isn't fixed just by the description the user carries round in their head; it's fixed partly by the environment the term gets used in, and partly by the community whose usage the term is answerable to. So two teams can write the same sentence-long definition and still be picking out different things, and two teams can write completely different definitions and be picking out the same thing. I want that made operational.

Roughly I'm imagining three tests run in order, but redo this if you think the philosophy says otherwise: (1) a swap test -- hold the definition fixed, change the environment the term is used in, ask whether the set of things it picks out would change; (2) a deference test -- ask whose usage the team's term is actually answerable to, the local environment or some external expert body (the regulator, a standards body, the clinicians), and where it's the latter, treat the expert body's usage as fixing the meaning even if the team has it wrong; (3) a duplicate test -- different descriptions, same things picked out, so merging is safe.

Then run it on three cases: (a) 'recyclable' as used by our two packaging teams, where the wording of the definition is identical but the collection and processing setups in the two markets aren't; (b) 'active user', where the two teams' descriptions differ but both track the same underlying events; (c) the one my boss will hate -- a term we very much want to merge because one number would be simpler for the board, but where the procedure says no.

Be honest about the limits. Tell me which steps are just judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and whether the externalism framing actually earns its keep here or whether plain old "do they pick out the same things?" would do the same job with less ceremony. Tell me what the procedure can't do -- I suspect it's useless for the cases where the only thing that matters is what's going on inside the analyst's head, but say so if that's wrong. And say how confident you are in the philosophical claims you're leaning on; I'd rather you flag the bits you're shaky on than sound authoritative, and single out the one step you dont think will survive contact with our actual tickets.
