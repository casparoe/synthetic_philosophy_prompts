I am a novelist, and the book I am at work on has Émilie du Châtelet at its centre. I have been reading her essay on happiness in the French — the piece modern editions print as the Discours sur le bonheur and older ones as the Réflexions sur le bonheur — and I have come to a passage I cannot get past. I can follow her grammar well enough to translate it, but I cannot see how its parts are supposed to hang together, and since the whole novel turns on how I render her inner life, I would rather understand the passage than write around it. Here it is, as it stands in the text I am using:

« Il faut pour être heureux, s’être défait des préjugés ; être vertueux ; se bien porter ; avoir des goûts et des passions ; être susceptible d’illusion ; car nous devons la plupart de nos plaisirs à l’illusion, et malheureux est celui qui la perd. Loin donc de chercher à la faire disparoître par le flambeau de la raison, tâchez d’épaissir le vernis qu’elle met sur la plupart des objets ; il leur est encore plus nécessaire que ne le sont à nos corps les soins et la parure. »

And then, a few lines later, this:

« Il faut commencer par se bien dire à soi-même, et par se bien convaincre que nous n’avons rien à faire en ce monde qu’à nous y procurer des sensations et des sentimens agréables. Les moralistes qui disent aux humains : Réprimez vos passions et maîtrisez vos desirs, si vous voulez être heureux, ne connoissent pas le chemin du bonheur. »

What defeats me is the list itself. She begins by requiring that a person have got rid of her préjugés — received opinions, the inherited furniture of a mind — and puts virtue in the same breath as health and appetite. Yet within two lines she makes happiness depend on being susceptible to illusion, says we owe most of our pleasures to it, calls the person who loses it wretched, and then gives what sounds like an instruction: do not let reason dissolve the illusion, thicken it instead. Then the next paragraph tells me the only business of a life is to procure agreeable sensations and sentiments, and that the moralists who preach mastery of desire have missed the road altogether.

I want a reading of this, and I want it to take a position rather than lay out the options and leave them there. Three things in particular.

First: what is illusion here, such that it is not simply one more préjugé? Is she telling her reader to believe what is false — in which case I do not see how shedding préjugés and cultivating illusions are both requirements of the same happiness — or is she after something closer to the light a thing is seen in, a gloss on what one already knows to be the case? The varnish suggests the second; the word illusion and the phrase susceptible d’illusion pull toward the first. Tell me which the text actually supports, and what work the distinction between préjugé and illusion is doing, whether or not she states it.

Second, a point of grammar, since it changes the sense for me: in « tâchez d’épaissir le vernis qu’elle met sur la plupart des objets », I take elle to be l’illusion, but the nearest feminine noun is la raison. Can elle be reason here? Would that reading invert the sentence? I would like to know how a careful reader of her century would have heard it, and whether the ambiguity is mine or the sentence’s.

Third, and this is what I most need for the novel: épaissir is an instruction to do something, and yet being susceptible to illusion sounds like a temperament one either has or lacks. If it is something one can work at, what does the work consist in? Give me two or three concrete instances drawn from the world she actually inhabited — a woman in her forties with a lover, a reputation to protect, a body that is beginning to age, an evening to get through — rather than instances from modern talk of positive thinking or reframing. I need to be able to put one of them in a scene.

I do not need a bibliography or a reading list, and I would rather not have the general portrait of her as an Enlightenment rationalist; I want this passage read. If the position is finally inconsistent, say so plainly, and tell me which inconsistency a woman of her intelligence and appetite could have lived with and which she could not.
