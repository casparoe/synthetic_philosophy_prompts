I serve on the selection committee for my university's mid-career research prize. The charter is deliberately thin: the prize goes to the person to whom the field owes the most, and the committee may take into account how far the field has already repaid the debt. What we have instead of a theory is a decade of decisions and the one-line citations we wrote for them.

We awarded it to a chemist whose papers are barely cited, because she spent eleven years building an instrument facility that most of her field now depends on; we wrote that the field's current results would not exist without her. We denied it to a historian with the highest citation count in the college, writing that his celebrated book had accelerated a consensus that was already forming, and that he had assembled it in a single productive year. We awarded it to an economist who spent nine years on a problem the field had abandoned and whose solution is cited fewer than fifty times; we wrote that she kept a hard question alive when no one else would. We denied it to a physicist whose one paper remade his subfield, writing that the same result appeared in two other groups' papers within the year and that he had found it quickly. We awarded it to a public-health researcher whose intervention was unoriginal, cheap, and slow to adopt, and who spent fifteen years fighting her own agency to get it used; we wrote that she had paid for the result with her career.

This year's nomination is a statistician who, from a comfortable tenured position, noticed a connection while preparing a lecture, worked it out over a summer, and published a four-page paper that redirected her field; the method now bears her name and she is its most cited member. The only thing it ever cost her was a senior colleague's public dismissal of the paper as trivial, which did her no professional harm.

Predict what we will do. Say which single rule our past decisions are best explained by, name at least one other rule that fits them just as well, give each rule's verdict on this nomination, and identify the further nomination that would separate them. I care more about whether our precedents express one rationale or only a family of them than about this year's outcome.
