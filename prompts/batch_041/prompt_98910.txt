Ok so I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in philosophy (decision theory / formal epistemology) and I run our department's decision theory reading group — about 15 people, mix of phil grad students and a couple of econ people who wander in, 90 minutes, one seminar room with a whiteboard. I want to do a session on expected value fanaticism, but not me standing up and explaining Pascal's mugging at them, because they've all heard that and they'll nod along and it won't land. I want them to build the problem themselves, with their own choices, and get stuck in it before anyone names it.

Here's my rough sketch, please fix it up and flesh it out into something I can actually run. Phase 1: everyone privately writes on an index card the smallest probability p at which they'd accept a lottery against a sure loss — something like "I'd give up 1 chip for a p chance at 1000 chips" — and then the smallest p they'd accept no matter how large the prize gets. Phase 2: I collect them, read a few out, and then start putting lotteries on the board that beat each written threshold, one at a time. For any p > 0 anyone wrote, I can scale the prize until p × N > 1 and the bet is EV-positive, so no finite threshold survives. Phase 3: they each get 20 chips and take turns playing a stranger who makes a demand — "hand over 5 chips or, with probability 1 in a trillion, something astronomically bad happens to you" — and the EV-maximizer has to pay every single time, and I want them to feel the mugging grind them down over a few rounds. Phase 4: swap the astronomically large prize for an infinite one, let them watch every threshold collapse at once, then have them pair off and each try to construct a lottery the other can't refuse — and notice they can do it to any infinite lottery, and so can the other guy, and it goes nowhere.

Then a debrief that maps each phase back onto the actual structure of the fanaticism objection: that there's no principled probability floor, the gap between "EV-positive" and "worth taking," ex ante vs ex post, bounded utility, probability neglect, and where the thing really bites in practice (population ethics, longtermism). They shouldn't hear the words fanaticism or mugging until the debrief, and I don't want any lecture up front.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — I'm guessing (a) people stop taking it seriously once the numbers get absurd, (b) someone says any probability that small is just zero to them and derails the room, (c) my arithmetic on the whiteboard is definately unreadable. And be honest about what the activity can't get them to see: I assume it can't reproduce the actual argument that the fanatic is right, or the coherence problems with infinite value, and that I have to supply those myself. Give me rules, materials, a timeline, roles, the debrief, and the failure modes. No real money, chips are fine.
