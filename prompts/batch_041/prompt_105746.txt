I lurk on LessWrong and read the Sequences years ago, so I can follow this kind of argument in shape but not in detail, and last week two people at our local meetup got into it about accuracy-first epistemology and I've been chewing on it ever since. I'm outsourcing the referee job.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Owen opened by saying the accuracy-first program basically settles what's wrong with incoherent credences. The way he told it: the only thing that makes a credence good or bad is how close it is to the truth, and once you fix any reasonable scoring rule for that closeness, it's a theorem that any set of credences that breaks the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated -- there's some coherent alternative that scores better no matter how the world turns out, at least in the usual finite cases. So probabilism gets justified without smuggling in extra assumptions about what you ought to believe.

(2) Nina said she likes the theorem but thinks its being oversold. Her line: dominance only ever tells you "don't be incoherent." It never tells you what to actually believe -- it's silent on contents. So as a foundation it's weirdly empty; it explains why you shouldn't be a certain kind of irrational, but not why you should believe anything in particular.

(3) Owen said that's fine and not a bug -- nobody claimed accuracy-first generates contents; the contents come from your evidence, and the framework's job is to explain the norms, not the inputs. And the norm it does explain is non-trivial: coherence is required no matter what your priors or goals are, so long as you care about accuracy at all.

(4) Nina jumped on that last clause: "so long as you care about accuracy" is doing all the work. Why should accuracy be the only thing you care about, epistemically? She gave an example -- someone who cares more about having an explanation, or a settled picture of the world, or just understanding a thing, than about maximizing expected accuracy, isn't obviously irrational. Owen said if you don't care about accuracy you're not really in the business of believing at all, and that understanding and explanation are downstream of getting things right anyway.

(5) Then Nina raised another one. She said accuracy-first treats true and false symmetrically -- a scoring rule just measures distance from the truth, so missing a truth and believing a falsehood get penalized in the same currency. But that's not how we actually reason, she says: we're often way more worried about not believing false things than about not missing true ones, and that asymmetry looks like a real epistemic value the framework can't even represent, let alone justify.

(6) Owen's reply: the asymmetry isn't part of accuracy, it's a further fact about how you weight different kinds of error, and you can just build it in by picking a scoring rule that penalizes falsehood more heavily -- so the framework has room for it. Nina said that proves her point: if the asymmetry only shows up once you choose a scoring rule to encode it, then it isn't derived from accuracy-first, it's an extra assumption you fed in, and now you owe us a story about why that assumption is right. He said every normative theory has primitives, so that's not a special problem.

They both turned to me and I had nothing. Can you go through moves (1)-(6) and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying what each of them is getting right and where each overreaches? And then the part I actually care about: is there a version of accuracy-first that survives Nina's symmetry complaint without just stipulating the asymmetry up front, or does the framework have to give up on being the whole story and become one epistemic value among several?
