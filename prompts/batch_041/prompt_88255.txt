For the year-end humor issue, I need a deadpan mock document rather than an essay: an official guidance note, issued by an invented public body — call it the Bureau of Borderline Cases — that explains to citizens how the state determines when a heap of grain has become a heap, and how to contest the determination. Write it straight, with no narrator, no winking, and no frame: just the document, about 600 words. The comedy has to come from the machinery of vagueness itself, not from generic bureaucratic absurdity, so that someone who has actually thought about the sorites paradox laughs rather than winces, while a reader who hasn't still follows what is happening. The note should keep colliding with the tolerance principle — one grain can't make the difference — and with the forced march from a plainly insufficient pile to a plainly sufficient one, and its staff should be shown trying, and failing, to locate a sharp threshold. Along the way it should quietly work through the standard escape routes: the threshold exists but is known only to the Registrar and is in any case exempt from disclosure; "heap" holds on every admissible way of drawing the line; the line shifts with context; and there is a separate form for citizens who wish to dispute whether their case is borderline at all, since that classification can itself be borderline. Name no positions and no philosophers — the piece stays entirely in-universe — but each device should be recognizable to someone who knows the literature, and nothing it asserts about the paradox should be something a philosopher would call simply false.

Format the response as the document itself, in this order: a title-and-issuing-office line; "Purpose" (about 80 words); "Procedure: The Forced March" (about 150 words); "Borderline Cases and Appeals" (about 150 words); "Frequently Asked Questions" (about 150 words); and a two-line "Contact" block. Close with one short section of about 100 words headed "Note to the desk," telling me which philosophical position each running gag is aimed at, so the copy desk doesn't cut the wrong joke.
