I've been roped into giving the after-dinner speech at the centenary dinner of our little history-of-science society, in three weeks, and I'm dreading it. The room is half colleagues who've spent thirty years on this material and will hear every anachronism in the first sentence, and half spouses, sponsors and a couple of emeritus members who came for the food and the wine. So it has to be warm, mildly funny, and it has to land as affection for the society. It also has to be read aloud by me, who is not a performer.

The trouble is that a centenary dinner is basically a machine for Whig history. The genre wants me to stand up and say: they started it in 1924 with a hunch, and look, here we are — the hunch turned out to be us. I don't want to do that, and I also don't want to give a speech that's secretly a scolding of my own audience.

Here's what I keep turning over. I think two different things get called Whig history and only one of them is a habit you can just stop. The first is selection: you notice the people and papers that lead to what we now think, so Aristarchus gets recruited as the Copernicus of antiquity, the dead ends get quietly dropped, and everyone's arguments get translated into our vocabulary whether or not they'd recognise it. That one you can push back on just by being more thorough. The second is direction, and it's harder: any narrative told to an audience sitting at the end of it will make the end feel like the destination, even if the present is never mentioned once. The order of telling does the work. And a speech is a shape with a last sentence, and the audience is standing on the last sentence.

I have a crude test I've been applying, and I'd like the speech to actually survive it: strike out every sentence that would have to be rewritten if things had gone the other way, and see what's left. I suspect what's left is thin, and I suspect the anti-Whig version of the story has it's own Whig shape — the saga of how we finally learned not to be Whiggish. So I don't want the distinction mentioned and then abandoned; I want the speech built so that it demonstrates the problem rather than describing it.

Concrete things you can use, all of them real: the founders' flagship project was a survey of the influence of climate on national scientific style and they spent eleven years on it before it fizzled out; we still have the minute book, and it's mostly fussing about whether the Edinburgh branch was entitled to send two delegates; the 1970s split over the journal is still mildly sore; my predecessor's portrait is hung slightly too high. About eight minutes, no citations — it's a dinner, and I couldn't check them anyway.

Then, separately, for me and not for the room: a short note saying which parts of the Whig-history picture I'm leaning on are actually settled among the people who work on it and which are genuinely contested, and where the speech takes liberties with the ideas. Don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the consensus.
