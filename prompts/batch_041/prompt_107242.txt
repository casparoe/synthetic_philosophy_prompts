I'm a science-and-ideas reporter and I've been working on a peice about the Thucydides trap — you know, the "rising power vs. ruling power, war is inevitable" thing everyone applies to the US and China. I wanted to go back to the actual sentence instead of the meme version, and now I'm stuck. Here's the bit, from Book 1 of the History (Crawley's translation):

"To the question why they broke the treaty, I answer by placing first an account of their grounds of complaint and points of difference, that no one may ever have to ask the immediate cause which plunged the Hellenes into a war of such magnitude. The real cause I consider to be the one which was formally most kept out of sight. The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war inevitable. Still it is well to give the grounds alleged by either side, which led to the dissolution of the treaty and the breaking out of the war."

Two things I can't get straight. First, how can the "real cause" be the one that was "kept out of sight"? If nobody said it, how does he know it's the cause — what is he actually claiming there? Second, my editor reads "made war inevitable" as a law of history, but the next sentence basically says "anyway, here are the stated grievances," which feels like a different kind of explanation entirely. Is Thucydides saying the war had to happen, or telling us where to look when we explain why it happened? And does that distinction survive the way the trap gets used?

Plain terms please — I don't need the scholarship, I need to not embarrass myself in print. End with one line starting "Verdict:" that sums up your reading in a sentence.
