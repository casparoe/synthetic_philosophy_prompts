I spent thirty-five years as a marine invertebrate taxonomist before I retired, and now I'm trying to teach myself philosophy of science, which is a bit like learning to swim by reading about buoyancy. I've been reading alot of philosophy of science lately, and I've gotten interested in the natural kinds debate—do the sciences carve nature at its joints?—and I've talked myself into a position I want you to attack. Roughly: the sciences do carve nature at its joints, but the joints are plural and causal rather than essential. A natural kind is a stable, projectible cluster of properties that hang together because of some homeostatic mechanism (or a tangle of mechanisms), not because all members share a hidden essence. I've also been kicking around a sharper test, which I'll call the interventionist scoreboard: a classification is a natural kind to the extent that interventions on members of the kind produce more systematic, transferable effects than interventions on nearby arbitrary groupings. I like this because it makes kindhood a matter of what the world does when you poke it, not just what our concepts happen to find useful. But I can't seperate the metaphysics from the pragmatics, and I worry I've just dressed up conventionalism in causal clothing.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this view—not a survey of the literature, and please no citations or reading lists, since I can't get online and I'm not interested in who said what. Start with the defeaters that should actually move me: observations, arguments, formal results, or cases. Think about things like microbial lateral gene transfer and the mess of bacterial species, the way mineralogy sometimes treats a category as real only relative to a pressure-temperature regime, or the possibility that some successful classifications (say, in ecology or psychiatry) are projectible for reasons that have nothing to do with a shared causal mechanism. For each defeater, tell me whether it's fatal, serious, awkward, or cosmetic; whether a committed defender would concede it or wave it away; and what the best reply is, plus what residual damage remains after the reply. I especially want to know whether the interventionist scoreboard is doing any independent work or whether it collapses into 'whatever the successful science groups together'—and if it's the latter, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what we should expect from any naturalistic account of kinds. Don't just give me the standard objection that species aren't kinds; I've heard that one. I want the less obvious pressure points, including any case the view cannot describe without cheating. I'd also like to know which assumptions of the view would have to fail for it to be false, what the world would have to look like for each failure, and what the view predicts that a pure conventionalism or a strict essentialism would not.
