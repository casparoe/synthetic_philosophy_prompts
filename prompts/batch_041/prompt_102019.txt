A friend of mine who's a pretty good science writer sent me a draft of an explainer he's doing on biological function, and he asked me whether the philosophy part holds up. I know enough to be suspicous but not enough to tell him where it goes wrong, so I'm outsourcing this one. Here's basicly what his section says:

There are two rival theories of what it means to say a trait has a function. The etiological theory says the function of a trait is the effect it was selected for: the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping blood is what got hearts kept around. The causal-role theory says a function is just whatever a part contributes to how the larger system it sits in works, so on that view anything the heart does that helps the body along counts as one of its functions. He says the etiological theory is the one that matches how working biologists actually talk, since they only call something a function when it's an adaptation -- which is why they'll tell you a vestigial trait has no function at all, and why "the function of the appendix is unknown" is a thing you hear people say. Then he says the causal-role view is a philosopher's invention, since it would let you say the function of the heart is to make a thumping noise, and nobody believes that, so that account is basically dead. He wraps up by saying the one thing both camps agree on is that function talk is normative -- that saying the heart's function is to pump blood commits you to "the heart ought to pump blood" -- and that this normativity is the only reason the debate is worth having.

How much of this survives?
