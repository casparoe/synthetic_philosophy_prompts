Ok so I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size ML company, zero philosophy background, and my team has this recurring argument that comes back every few months and nobody ever moves on it. It's about the so-called alignment tax and the competitiveness constraint.

Here's the shape of it. One camp says: as long as there are several labs and no way to actually enforce a global slowdown, anyone who spends time and compute making their model safer instead of just more capable is handing the lead to someone who won't. So paying the tax is the losing move, and it's not really a moral choice a single lab can make — the constraint is competitive, not ethical. The other camp says that's mostly a story: the stuff that makes models safer — better evals, interpretability, red-teaming, actually understanding what the model is doing — is either cheap or it helps you build a better model, so the tax is small or even negative, and the race framing is a convenient excuse for shipping fast. Then there's a third group who say it's a coordination problem: everyone would prefer to go slow if they could trust everyone else to, so the real question is institutions and verification, not per-lab incentives.

Nobody's budged in like a year. So my actual question is what kind of disagreement this is. My working hypothesis (and I could be wrong) is that it's partly verbal — the alignment tax and competitiveness are each doing two or three different jobs and people are talking past each other — and partly a hidden empirical question about how steep the tradeoff really is, which nobody can settle because we dont have the counterfactual. But I also suspect a chunk of it is just different pictures of what other labs are: adversarial racers, vs. other people facing the same bind, vs. optimizers who'd take any edge.

Two things I keep bringing up that nobody engages with. First, the tax probably isn't a fixed number, it's a curve, and it might not even be monotonic — early on, safety work eats capability, but past some point understanding the model better is just good engineering, so maybe the whole fight is really about where on that curve we are, and people are generalizing from different points on it. Second, competitiveness seems to cover two different constraints — a race to build the thing first, and a market where if you don't ship, someone else fills the demand — and those don't obviously pull in the same direction.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute, an empirical dispute we can't run, a values dispute in disguise (how much risk is acceptable, and who gets to decide), or are the camps answering different questions under one name? Half the time I think one side is telling me what will happen and the other is telling me what should happen, and we're just confusing a prediction with an argument. What would actually move this — an experiment, a formal model, a distinction, or nothing at all? And if it's stuck because of something structural (no counterfactual, an unmeasurable tax), just say so, don't hand me a new framework that pretends to fix it.
