I have been arguing with two friends in a reading group about whether Schopenhauer deserves his reputation as the founding ancestor of the suffering-focused wing of ethics — the family of views on which the reduction of suffering is either the whole of the moral task or at least its overwhelming priority. My friends treat the continuity as obvious: he holds that suffering is what is positive and real in experience while satisfaction is only the temporary absence of a want, that the world's ledger is massively in the red, and that compassion for the suffering of every sentient being is the only genuinely moral motive; on that basis they call him a negative utilitarian who simply lacked the vocabulary. I am not convinced. The modern view strikes me as a policy view: it aggregates, it is impartial, it looks forward, and it is meant to tell us what to do. Most of what is distinctive in Schopenhauer is instead a claim about what the will is and what it would take to be released from it, and I suspect that does not translate into a program at all. What I want from you is a prediction, as disciplined as you can make it, of the positions he would actually have taken on questions that only arose after his death, and a verdict on where the continuity story holds and where it breaks.

Take it that I know the outline of his system and do not need it rehearsed. I want the inference from his commitments to four modern questions. First, the doctrine itself: if you present negative utilitarianism to him as a proposal about what we ought to do, does he accept it, and does he accept the asymmetry between pain and pleasure that does the work in the modern antinatalist argument — that the presence of pain is bad and its absence good even when no one exists to benefit from the absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only where there is someone deprived of it? Second, the familiar objection that a purely suffering-minimizing view would approve of painlessly extinguishing all sentient life: does he take the implication and accept it, deny that it follows, or reject the question as malformed? Third, the current dispute over whether we should intervene in nature to reduce wild animal suffering — predator control, contraception, engineering — would he support it, and does his compassion even treat the suffering of animals in the wild as the sort of thing a program could address? Fourth, antinatalism and its extension to all sentient life: would he endorse a general prescription against bringing new beings into existence, or is his admiration for the ascetic's celibacy a private route to the denial of the will to live rather than a public ethic? Put a price on it: if a philanthropist gave him a very large budget and asked where to put it, which of these, if any, would he fund?

For each question, keep separate what he actually committed himself to and what a consistent Schopenhauerian would be driven to say, and tell me plainly where the framework yields no answer rather than constructing one on his behalf. Do not give me citations, page references, or a reading list — I have the texts and I am not testing your bibliography; argue from the positions and from what makes the system work. And do not smooth over the seams: if his metaphysics of the will as one undivided reality cuts against the aggregative individualism that the modern view assumes, say so and say what follows from it. Please structure the reply as follows: Reconstruction (about 200 words, the commitments you will be reasoning from); On the normative doctrine (about 300 words); On the asymmetry and the extinction objection (about 300 words); On intervening in nature (about 300 words); On antinatalism (about 250 words); Where the continuity story breaks (about 250 words); Bottom line (about 100 words).
