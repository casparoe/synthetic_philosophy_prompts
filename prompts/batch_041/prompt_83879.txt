I have spent the better part of two decades reading the rationalist blogosphere and its comment sections, and for several years I have sat on the safety review board of an applied AI lab, where I am the member expected to supply whatever conceptual scaffolding the engineers and lawyers lack. I need to draft the board's written opinion on a question that has split us cleanly, and I would like you to write it.

Background for the memo. We practise what we call mechanistic anomaly detection: rather than judging a model only by its outputs on a battery of tests, we also inspect the internal computation that produces those outputs, and we treat it as a warning sign when the computation doing the work looks structurally unlike the kind of process we would have predicted would produce that behaviour. The motive is familiar — a model that passes our evaluations may be passing for reasons that will not survive deployment, and if the mechanism is wrong, correct outputs tell us less than we would like.

The case. Our model V2 received a full safety case last year, resting substantially on mechanistic evidence: we located the internal structures that appeared to implement the capability we cared about, probed them, and told a story about why V2 did what it did. We have now trained V3 — same architecture, but a changed data mixture, a revised tokenizer, and a fresh random initialisation. Its benchmark behaviour on the covered tasks is indistinguishable from V2's. Engineering wants to carry the V2 safety case over wholesale, on the grounds that the relevant mechanism is preserved. Half the board agrees; the other half says that a fresh initialisation and a new tokenizer make V3's internals a different object, that nothing we learned about V2's circuits transfers, and that the case must be rebuilt from scratch — which, at our release cadence, means either indefinite delay or shipping with no mechanistic evidence at all.

The disagreement is not really about V3. It is about what we have been asserting when we say that two systems implement the same mechanism, and I think we have been trading on an ambiguity between at least two readings. On one reading, a mechanism is fixed by its causal profile: what matters is which variables it takes in and puts out, and how interventions on the intermediate steps change the output, so that two systems share a mechanism when they realise the same pattern of counterfactual dependence, whatever the substrate. On the other, a mechanism is fixed by its specific internal structure — components, organisation, parameters — and two systems share one only if they share that structure, so the question becomes how much structural difference is compatible with sameness. There are further wrinkles I would rather you handle than smooth over: mechanisms seem to be identifiable at more than one level of description, so V3 may share V2's mechanism at a coarse grain and not at a fine one, and it is not obvious which grain a safety case requires; and some of our probes may be sensitive to artefacts of the training run rather than to the mechanism we meant to study.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should reach a clear bottom line on the case in front of us — whether the V2 case transfers to V3 in whole, in part, or not at all, and if only in part, which parts — and it should state criteria the board can apply, now and later, to decide when two systems implement the same mechanism. The criteria must be ones an engineer can actually apply to a system on her desk, not a definition that only works in the abstract; if the honest answer is that sameness of mechanism is a family of notions and the board must choose one for a given purpose, say so, and say how to choose. Give the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting and say what it gets right, since half the board holds it and I need them to feel that the memo understood them. Finally, tell us what evidence we would have had to gather — about V2, or about V3 — to make this call on better grounds than the ones we have, so that we can build it into the process rather than relitigate the question at the next release. Assume the reader has no appetite for citations and will not look anything up; argue from the case.
