A colleague of mine is a moral philosopher who has spent her career on the theory of well-being, and I would like to anticipate her reaction to a case I intend to put to her. Over the years she has given me verdicts on a series of hypothetical lives, always answering a single question: is this life good for the person living it? Her record is as follows.

The experience-machine case, in which a person spends a lifetime in richly varied simulated happiness and believes it real: not good for the person, and she would refuse to be connected.

A life of unbroken, dreamless contentment — sedation from birth to death, with no experience and no activity: not good.

A person whose sole desire is to count blades of grass, who does so for a long life and is entirely content, with no other interests or attachments: not good.

A mathematician who proves a theorem that transforms her field but dies before it is confirmed or recognized; her desire to have solved it is satisfied, she was absorbed and happy in the work, and she had close friendships and a wide knowledge of her subject: good.

A life given over wholly to aesthetic contemplation and to friendship, with no achievement, discovery, or recognition: good.

The case I mean to raise is this: a life that contains an abundance of the things usually thought to matter most — great intellectual achievement, genuine discovery, deep and lasting friendship, sustained artistic creation — but lived by a person who is persistently and irremediably miserable, who finds almost nothing pleasant, and who says sincerely that she would exchange the whole life for a trivial contented one.

Please predict my colleague's verdict on this new case. Identify the theory of well-being she appears to be applying. Consider in particular a pure objective-list theory, an objective-list theory with a hedonic requirement, one with an endorsement or worthwhileness requirement, and a desire-satisfaction theory: say which of these accommodate her entire record above, which of those that survive diverge on the new case, and which theory you think she is in fact running. Give your best guess as to her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading over the alternatives, and name the further hypothetical life that would best separate the surviving theories from one another.
