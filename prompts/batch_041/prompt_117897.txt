I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and the annual predictions issue has fallen to me. What I need from you is a deadpan parody of a methodology handbook from a sovereign-risk rating agency — a firm that sells continuity ratings on states to governments, insurers, and institutional investors, and that has imported reference-class forecasting wholesale into the question of how long a polity has left.

Form: a numbered methodology document of roughly 800 words, with a title, a two- or three-sentence preamble in the agency's house voice, eight to twelve numbered clauses, and a short appendix containing a dissenting note from a staff methodologist who has lost an internal argument. Everything quoted should be invented and in-universe: no real firms, no real officials or scholars, no real events asserted as fact.

The humour has to be load-bearing on the epistemology rather than on the silliness of bureaucrats, and every absurdity should be an exaggeration of something the outside view genuinely does when it is pointed at a single, non-repeatable case. Build the piece around the following, and let the clauses interact rather than ticking them off one by one:

- The reference class. The agency has to define the class of polities whose fates stand in for the rated state's, and the definition must be visibly gerrymandered: broad enough to yield a sample, narrow enough to yield a rate. Have the document defend a choice the reader can see was made after the outcome was known, and include a clause permitting the class to be revised when a rating needs revising.
- The sample. The historical record of empires is a record of empires that ended; polities that persisted are under-represented in it, because persistence leaves fewer dramatic archives. The unconditional rate is therefore close to a tautology, the interesting quantity is a conditional one, and the document should be shifty about which of the two it is quoting.
- Hazards versus dates. The method can honestly yield a distribution over durations, or a constant hazard, but the sales desk needs a date. Let the document slide, in full view, from a probability that a state of this type falls within a century into a forecast that this state falls in a particular year.
- The single case. Reference-class forecasting was built for portfolios of similar projects and for planners who get to run many of them; a state is one draw, cannot diversify across its own history, and does not get re-rolled. The document should concede this in a clause and then proceed as though it hadn't.
- The override. The doctrine holds that case-specific causal knowledge may override the base rate when the case is a genuine outlier — the broken leg that makes the cinema base rate irrelevant. Give the agency an override clause, and let it be the mechanism by which an ordinary political judgement gets laundered into a number.
- Non-stationarity. The sample spans centuries in which what kills polities changed — succession, plague, credit, conscription, nuclear weapons, whatever comes next — so the draws are not exchangeable and the base rate is a rate for a world that no longer exists. Let a footnote concede this in a way that does not slow the product down.
- Reflexivity. The rating is published, the rating moves borrowing costs, and the borrowing cost moves the hazard. A downgrade is thus an intervention in the reference class rather than a measurement of it, and the document should treat that as a feature to be disclosed in small type.
- The dependent variable. Collapse is assigned retroactively, by the archivists of the survivors, and the threshold is contested in every case in the table. Have the document admit that its event definition rests on historiographical consensus while continuing to publish a figure to two decimal places.

A reader should finish the piece able to state, in one sentence, why a base rate drawn from the past is not a forecast about a particular present, and also why the outside view remains the honest starting point. The satire should be fair to the method rather than a sneer at it: someone who has taught the planning fallacy should find the document uncomfortably plausible, and someone who has never encountered reference-class forecasting should still get the joke and come away with the distinction between a class and a case. Keep the prose deadpan and the register bureaucratic, with no winking at the reader and no narrator stepping out to explain the satire.

Please give me a headline and a standfirst in the magazine's voice, and close with the dissenting appendix at around 120 words.
