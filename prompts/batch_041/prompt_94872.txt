Question: State Aumann's agreement theorem and explain what it implies about rational disagreement. Then consider two agents, A and B, who share a common prior over a finite state space but have different private information. Suppose it is common knowledge between them that A assigns probability 0.7 to event E and B assigns probability 0.4 to E. According to Aumann's theorem, what follows? Explain why, and identify one assumption that is doing essential work.

Student answer: Aumann's theorem says that two rational agents cannot agree to disagree. If they have a common prior and their posterior probabilities are common knowledge, then their posteriors must be equal. In the example, A assigns 0.7 and B assigns 0.4, so the theorem implies that at least one of them is not rational, or that they do not really share a common prior. The essential assumption is that their information partitions are common knowledge. The theorem does not apply to disagreements about values, only to beliefs about facts. It also shows that if they publicly announce their probabilities, they will converge to the same probability, since the announcement makes the probabilities common knowledge. The theorem therefore rules out persistent disagreement among fully rational Bayesians, except when the disagreement is about something non-probabilistic.
