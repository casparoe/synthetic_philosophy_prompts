I'm a second-year PhD student in a formal epistemology group that sits between philosophy and computer science, and I have to commit to a dissertation area by the end of the spring term. For the past few months I have been reading about program equilibrium — the setup in which each player submits a program to a referee and we look for mutual best responses defined over the programs themselves rather than over the actions they output — and it keeps striking me as the most interesting thing on my list. My difficulty is that I cannot tell which of its open questions are worth a dissertation and which are either already settled in spirit or will turn out to require machinery that nobody in my position could build in three years. So I would like a prioritized agenda from you: the open questions that matter most, in order, with the reasoning made explicit. To be clear about what is being prioritized: one person, roughly three years, a strong background in logic and computability but no serious machine learning or engineering experience, and a decided preference for conceptual work over simulation or experiment.

Here are the candidate questions I have been chewing on, though I would rather you tell me if I am missing the real ones. First, how much of the framework depends on there being a shared referee or a common semantics for the programs — what survives if the players instead simulate one another, or if only part of the other player's code is visible? Second, whether the cooperation phenomenon is really a theorem about the proof system: if I replace provability-style reasoning with something that imposes resource bounds on proof search, does it persist, and if it does, is the live question then about computational complexity rather than about equilibrium at all? Third, whether the framework extends past two players to coalitions, and whether it gives any grip on bargaining rather than merely on cooperation. Fourth, whether the right reading of the whole enterprise is descriptive or normative — a theory of what agents ought to do, or a theory of what selection pressure produces. Fifth, whether there is a mechanism-design question in the background, namely what a designer can accomplish simply by choosing the language, or the referee, to which programs are submitted. A friend in a mechanism design group insists over coffee that the entire literature is the repeated-game folk theorem with extra steps; I do not believe him, but I cannot articulate why not, and that worries me.

What I want is a defended ordering, not a survey and certainly not a reading list. For each question: why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is with the toolkit I actually have, how neglected it is relative to how much hinges on it, and what other work would have to wait on its resolution. Where you think a question is overrated because settling it would not change much downstream — say, because it collapses into something already familiar from evidential decision theory or from superrationality — tell me so directly rather than listing it politely alongside the rest. Finally, state what specific result, counterexample, or argument over the next year or two should move each item up or down my list, and which one I should rule out entirely.
