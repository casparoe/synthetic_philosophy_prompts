I'm in a grad seminar on pleasure and the good life and we keep going around in circles. Someone proposes that pleasures are all just sensations with a certain felt quality, someone else points at the pleasure of finally solving a proof or of hearing good news, someone proposes that pleasures are all states we want to be in, and then someone mentions the person who keeps playing a sad song that makes them feel awful, or the fact that you can be having a pleasant time and still wish it would stop, or that you can want a cigarette without enjoying it. I'm tired of adjudicating one theory at a time. What I want first is the specification: what does any theory of what pleasures have in common actually have to get right? Give me the desiderata a theory of hedonic unity has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one, and be clear about which are non-negotiable and which are just commitments of one camp (e.g., whether pleasure must be a feeling, whether it must be introspectively accessible, whether it must be a single natural kind, whether it has to come in degrees, whether it has to be separable from desire, etc.). Don't give me a bibliography or a scorecard of the usual suspects; I'll read those later. I want the list.

Include the weird cases I keep tripping over, because those are where the real work is: the pleasure of a sneeze or a scratch, the pleasure of relief when pain stops, the pleasure of a very sour candy that's almost painful, the pleasure of a sad song, the pleasure of schadenfreude if it is one, the pleasure of anticipation, the low-grade pleasure of a boring but reliable routine, the pleasure of a dream you only remember as pleasant, the pleasure of a joke you don't understand but laugh at because everyone else is, and the case of someone who says they enjoyed something but would never choose it again. For each, say what a theory has to do with it: explain it, count it as a counterexample, or explain it away. Also include constraints from the outside: it shouldn't make pleasure a matter of verbal convention, it should leave room for being wrong about one's own pleasure, it should say something about animals and maybe infants, and it shouldn't collapse pleasure into goodness or desire.

And flag the joints. I most want to know which pairs of desiderata pull against each other and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. If the upshot is that no single theory can meet the whole list, say so and tell me which requirement is doing the most work to rule out candidates. A table is fine if that's clearer. But keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them, not on scoring theories.
