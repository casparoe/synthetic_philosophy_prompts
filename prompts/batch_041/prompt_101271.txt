In the strategic-studies literature, brinkmanship is commonly modeled on the game of chicken: two drivers accelerate toward each other down a single lane, the first to swerve loses, and the rational move, on this picture, is to commit in advance to not swerving — to burn one's bridges or throw the steering wheel out the window, so that retreat is no longer possible. A colleague is leaning hard on this analogy in a paper he is drafting. His argument runs: because nuclear deterrence just is a game of chicken, the only stable posture is an irrevocable commitment to escalate; any public sign of restraint is a form of swerving; and arms-control agreements and crisis-management hotlines are not merely naive but positively destabilizing, since they soften the very commitment on which deterrence depends. He adds that the analogy is symmetric — whichever side can credibly make retreat impossible wins — so doctrines that remove the option to hold back, such as delegated launch authority or weapons that cannot be recalled, are the point rather than a defect.

I would like an audit of the analogy, not a survey of deterrence theory. Please do the following.

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the drivers, the cars, the lane, the oncoming collision, the swerve, the victory, the spectators, and the act of disabling one's own steering. A table is fine.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for my colleague's conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In doing so, address at least: whether the payoffs really line up (who, if anyone, wins a nuclear exchange, and what each side's best and worst outcomes actually are); whether the one-shot, two-player, complete-information structure of chicken survives repeated play, more than two players, allies and third parties, and the internal politics and perceptual limits of real states; and whether the commitment device in chicken — an irreversible physical act that removes the option to swerve — has any genuine analogue in a setting where what does the deterrent work is the adversary's belief rather than the physical impossibility of retreat.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports from the game that the target may lack — for instance, that the situation is fundamentally a contest of wills with a determinate loser, or that the costs of collision are symmetric and terminal for both parties. Note as well how the deterministic chicken model has no place for deliberately leaving a small chance of accidental escalation, which seems to matter in deterrence and which the model cannot represent.

4. Finally, give me your verdict: does the chicken analogy actually support the conclusion that irrevocable escalation is the only stable posture and that restraint is destabilizing, or was that conclusion riding on features of the game that do not transfer? If the analogy is being overworked, propose a better one and say what it would change about the policy conclusion.
