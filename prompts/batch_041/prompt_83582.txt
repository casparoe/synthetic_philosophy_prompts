At a seminar on supertasks, a colleague defended the coherence of Thomson's lamp by likening it to an infinite alternating series. In the standard setup, a lamp is on at the start and is switched at the halfway point, then again at three-quarters, then at seven-eighths, and so on, so that infinitely many switches occur before the one-minute mark; the question is whether the lamp is on or off once the minute has elapsed. The series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ... has partial sums that never settle, my colleague conceded, but the running average of those partial sums converges to 1/2. The series therefore has a perfectly good value, he argued, and by the same token the lamp has a perfectly good final state: the state at the one-minute mark is just the settled outcome of the switching history, and the demand for a last flip is a piece of naivety. His broader conclusion was that the lamp reveals no real difficulty in the idea of completing infinitely many tasks.

Please audit the analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what corresponds to the terms, the partial sums, the running average, the limit, and the lamp's final state — and then say where the mapping breaks. Consider what the lamp's state space actually allows (on or off, with nothing in between), what the averaging operation presupposes about the structure of the thing it is applied to, whether the described history fixes any state at the one-minute mark, and whether the switches can legitimately be treated as instantaneous. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter to my colleague's conclusion from those that are merely incidental, and say whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy is available for either side of the dispute, propose it and explain what it captures that the series does not. Please answer in American English.
