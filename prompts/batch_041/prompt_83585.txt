ok so I'm an undergrad and I somehow ended up as the student rep on our campus pest management advisory group (I'm in the environmental ethics seminar this term, my advisor chairs the thing, that's how I got volunteered). Thursday we have to vote on the grounds office's request for a whole summer of insecticide, and I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting, so I need you to write it. The plan is three parts: truck fogging with a pyrethroid along the quad and the edge of the campus wetland every three weeks from June through September, a systemic drench around the elms and lindens for the leafminer problem, and larvicide briquettes dropped in the campus pond. Grounds says its about mosquito complaints and West Nile worry and the summer orientation events, and that we definately have to do something before the town's fogging schedule kicks in or we look like the negligent ones. What's actually on the table, as far as I can tell: the pollinator garden the bio department planted two years ago sits right in the fogging route, the limnology class's aquatic invertebrate counts in the pond are the only long-term data anyone has for that wetland, the community garden at the edge of campus is downwind, the grounds crew applying the stuff is out there in full Tyvek in July, and the dorms next to the quad are where the students with asthma and scent sensitivities live. Our written policy says we use the least toxic method that's effective, but it's never been applied to insects, so nobody knows whether effective means fewer bites or fewer complaints. The vendor contract auto-renews for another season if we don't vote it down by Thursday, and the town is going to keep fogging the park across the street from our north gate either way, so one of the guys on the committee says our not spraying is either free-riding on their control or a public service for resistance management depending how you look at it, and honestly I can't tell which.

The part I actually need help thinking through: my roommate in the seminar keeps saying spraying doesn't really harm the insects in any way that matters, because the ones we kill would've died in a few weeks anyway and the habitat gets recolonized by next spring, so the total number of insect lives (or insect-days, or whatever the right unit is) might come out about the same or even higher with spraying than without — I think it's called the replaceability argument, something like that, and he says it's the same move people make about animals raised for food, and I can't tell whether it's a serious point or a trick. The other thing is that the benefits here are all statistical, a small drop in a small risk spread across everybody, while the harms are countable, a visible pile of dead bees and caterpillars under the lindens that the grounds crew sweeps up, and I don't know whether to treat that as an argument for or against, because it cuts both ways depending on whether you think we're over-weighting the visible dead things or under-weighting the invisible risk. And nobody on the committee has said one word about whether the insects themselves count for anything, which seems like the thing we should have an opinion about before we vote on killing however many million of them.

Format: a short written opinion, 1.5 to 2 pages, addressed to the chair, that I can read out and hand around. Give me an actual recommendation — approve as proposed, reject, or approve with specific conditions, dates, and triggers — with a time frame and with who decides what, since doing nothing counts as renewal. Lay out the reasoning and be honest about the strongest thing on the side I'm probably voting against, because two of the five of us are going the other way and I need to answer them in the room instead of just outvoting them. Please engage the replaceability thing my roommate makes and the free-riding-versus-resistance question head on, since those are the two objections that could actually change my mind. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, we're not writing a paper, I just need the argument to hold up when I'm sitting in that meeting.
