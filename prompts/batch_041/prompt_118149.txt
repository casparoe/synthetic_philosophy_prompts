I recently had a falling out with a close friend. She called me out, fairly sharply, for something I had done, and my first reaction was not that she had her facts wrong but that she had no business saying it to me — she had done the same thing herself about a year earlier, and I remember thinking at the time that this disqualified her from saying anything. That reaction has nagged at me since, because I genuinely cannot tell whether it was a legitimate objection or just an unusually articulate way of dodging a criticism I deserved. I have been reading around the literature on standing to blame ever since, and I am now putting together a session for a small discussion group — graduate students in philosophy, none of them specialists in this area — and I would like an instrument rather than a list of names. Please build me a decision tree that takes someone from a handful of concrete judgments about cases to a named position on when a would-be blamer's own record, attitudes, or relationships defeat their standing to blame. Every node must be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to: questions about what is going on in a particular case, or about what an act of blaming is fundamentally doing, never questions of the form "do you think hypocrisy defeats standing?" The branches I care most about are these: whether standing is a threshold condition such that a blamer who lacks it is not blaming at all, versus a scalar consideration that merely enters into how much weight the blame should carry; whether the defect in a hypocritical blamer is a wrong done to the person blamed, a wrong done to the moral community, or merely a conversational impropriety; whether the disqualifying feature is the same wrong, any wrong at all, or specifically a failure to be committed to the norm being invoked; whether standing attaches to the act of blaming or to the underlying reactive attitude of resentment; and whether third parties can blame on a victim's behalf, including what happens to their standing once the victim has forgiven the wrongdoer. Please also give the tree a branch for self-blame, since I want to know whether hypocrisy toward oneself is even coherent.

Use these cases at the nodes, and feel free to add others of the same kind: the friend who has done exactly what she is blaming me for; the person who has done something entirely different but equally bad; the reformed wrongdoer who blames someone for what she used to do; the co-conspirator who encouraged the behavior and now condemns it; the busybody stranger with no relationship to me or to anyone affected; the person who benefits from the practice she is telling me not to engage in; the person who has forgiven the person who wronged her but will not extend the same to me; and the person who insists she is not blaming me, only describing what I did. The positions I want to see appear as leaves are at least these: hypocrisy fully defeats standing; hypocrisy never defeats standing and is at most a reason to take the criticism less seriously; standing consists in being committed to the norm and a participant in the moral community, so an outsider has none regardless of her record; standing is relational and depends on the specific accountability relationship between the two parties; standing-talk is a confused proxy for a weighing question and should be deflated rather than theorized; and a two-track view that separates moral standing from epistemic standing, so that a hypocrite may know perfectly well what I did wrong while having no standing to say it. Where two of these turn out to be the same position stated twice, say so rather than padding the tree; where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag the pair explicitly rather than papering over it; and where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and diagnose it briefly — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered? Keep it to roughly eight to twelve nodes and six to eight leaves, give each leaf a one-line statement of its most awkward commitment, and please write the whole thing in American English.
