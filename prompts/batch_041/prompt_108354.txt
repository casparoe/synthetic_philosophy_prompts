I sit on the risk committee of an asset manager, and I would like an analysis of the philosophical commitments buried in one of our standard procedures. The procedure runs as follows. No systematic strategy may receive capital unless it first clears a backtest on our historical data; it then trades for a year at minimum size; only then is it scaled up. Every strategy we test is entered in a trial ledger together with the number of variants we tried before settling on it, and the ledger is reviewed annually. Our client materials, meanwhile, must carry the standard warning that past performance does not indicate future results, and our compliance department treats that warning as substantive rather than ceremonial. The committee has also noticed something we cannot explain: the strategies with the most spectacular backtests are rarely the ones that survive their year at minimum size.

What does this apparatus presuppose? More specifically: what must be true about the process that generates returns for a backtest to count as evidence about a period we have not observed -- the old problem of induction, I take it, in a new setting -- and is the needed assumption metaphysical (some uniformity of nature), local and empirical (stationarity over a stated horizon), or merely pragmatic (a policy that pays for itself)? Which parts of the procedure strictly require such an assumption, and which would be licensed by several rival views at once? Does the trial ledger commit us to a claim about how our trials are related to one another that we cannot check from inside the practice? How should I understand the disclaimer: is it a genuine denial of the premise on which the rest of the procedure rests, or can both be held together? And since the no-free-lunch results tell us that, averaged over all possible problems, every learning rule does equally well, any edge we claim must come from a bias matched to a restricted class of problems: is the practice therefore presupposing a substantive claim about which problems markets present, and could such a claim ever be established from inside the practice, or must it be imported from outside?

I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature. I want the implicit theory reconstructed, an explicit statement of what the procedure is genuinely committed to as opposed to what merely fits it, a verdict on where the practice is underdetermined, and what would have to change in the procedure if the central assumption turned out to be false.
