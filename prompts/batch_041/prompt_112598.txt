I run a small political-philosophy reading group and next month I want to do a case study instead of a paper, and I've landed on the American dynasty-trust thing — the states that killed off the rule against perpetuities — read through equality of opportunity. Background, so we're on the same page (I'll put this on the handout, so just take it as given): the old common-law rule said a trust had to vest within a life in being plus twenty-one years, so about a century at most, and its whole point was that the dead don't get to tie up property forever. South Dakota abolished the rule outright in 1983, then Delaware, Alaska, Nevada and a bunch of others loosened or scrapped theirs through the 90s, and they advertised the change to wealthy families. The trust buisness moved there, and now a family can park a fortune in a trust that lasts forever, pays out to descendants generation after generation, and sits largely out of reach of those descendants' creditors and ex-spouses. Nobody in the line ever has to earn any of it, and no estate tax is paid as it passes down.

What I want from you is an analysis of this case through Rawlsian fair equality of opportunity. Not a general essay on inheritance taxes — the case. Be precise about the mapping. Who exactly is being denied what? The lazy version just says the heirs get an unfair advantage, but FEO is a claim about access to offices and positions, and a perpetual trust mostly buys consumption and security, which FEO may not even be about — so does FEO have anything to say about this at all, or does the real objection have to come from somewhere else (a fair-value-of-political-liberty argument, a reciprocity argument, whatever you think is strongest)? Tell me where FEO is silent rather than making it speak.

Then run it the other way, because a friend in the group keeps insisting the objection that matters isn't fairness at all, it's the dead-hand thing — that a trust like this lets a man two hundred years dead keep governing the lives of his great-great-grandchildren, and that's a problem about freedom or domination, not equality. Take that seriously. Is the dead-hand objection genuinely distinct from the equality objection, or does it just collapse into it? Does it even bite in this case, given that the money is a gift and not a constraint? And which of the two theories actually handles the case better?

Wrap up with what the case shows about FEO itself: confirmation, anomaly, or something the theory never anticipated.
