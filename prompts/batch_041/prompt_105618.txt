At a research retreat last month, a colleague cut off a dispute about whether our group should commission one more experiment before committing to one of two interventions. His line, which I have now heard in several forms, was this: information is always good, and you can never be worse off for knowing more. It was not decoration. He was using it to defeat a specific objection — that the study might return bad news, and that the group might be worse off for having commissioned it — and also to suggest that the study was costless in some sense deeper than money. I want to know exactly what that sentence could be claiming before I decide whether I agree with him, because I suspect it is smuggling several different theses under one agreeable phrase.

Please separate the distinct precise claims the slogan might express, ordered from the trivial to the strong. For each reading, state what it asserts, what it presupposes, whether it is true, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports the use my colleague put it to. The readings seem to turn on several axes, and I would like each made explicit: whether "worse off" is measured ex ante, in expected terms, or ex post, in realized terms; whether the value of the information is merely non-negative or strictly positive; whether the claim is about the decision-maker's own information or about information held by others; whether it requires the agent to be a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer who remains free to ignore what she learns; what happens once information is costly, or once acquiring it effectively commits you to acting on it; whether the claim concerns instrumental value only or also some epistemic good; and whether it survives when the agent's preferences are themselves reshaped by what she learns. I would also like you to flag which readings are close to tautologies given how "worse off" and "expected value" are defined, and which are substantive and contingent on assumptions that may fail.

I gather there is a formal result in decision theory to the effect that a Bayesian's expected utility cannot fall when a cost-free experiment is made available. I would like to see how narrow that result really is, and exactly how much of the slogan it does and does not underwrite; if the theorem licenses only a thin ex ante reading, say so plainly.

Please give me the result as a menu of readings with a verdict attached to each, rather than a defense of any single reading. I also want your best guess at which reading my colleague most plausibly had in mind, and whether that reading — as opposed to a weaker or a stronger one — is the reading that would actually license his conclusion that no one may object to the extra study.
