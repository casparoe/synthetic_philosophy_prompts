I was recently appointed the lay member of a citizens' panel advising my city on a coastal defense scheme with a design life of roughly two centuries, and the consultants' cost-benefit analysis turns almost entirely on a single number: the social discount rate. Sitting through those meetings has made me see how much of the real disagreement is buried inside the standard way of decomposing that rate into a pure time-preference term and a term that folds together growth and inequality aversion, and I want the final examination in the MA seminar I teach on the ethics of long-term policy to expose exactly that burial rather than let students recite the usual slogans about future generations.

Please write one essay question — answerable in about 75 minutes and worth 25 marks — together with a model answer of roughly 650 words and a marking scheme for it. The question must not be answerable by summarizing the familiar claim that pure time preference is ethically indefensible; it should force candidates to keep three distinctions straight at once: first, discounting well-being versus discounting consumption; second, pure time preference versus the other components of the standard decomposed discount rate; and third, an axiomatic case for a positive rate — of the kind that derives impatience from how any consistent ranking must behave over an unbounded future — versus a merely prudential or market-based case. Build in one feature that makes the arithmetic bite: require candidates to say what happens to the recommended rate, and to the direction of the policy recommendation, in a scenario where future per-capita consumption is expected to fall rather than grow. A strong answer must notice that this can push the consumption rate below zero and must explain why that does not contradict a positive pure time preference; it must also resist declaring that all discounting is irrational.

For the marking scheme, distribute the 25 marks across the specific moves a top answer has to make, and for each move add one line naming how candidates characteristically get it wrong — for instance, running the two components of the rate together, or treating the axiomatic argument as a restatement of impatience rather than as a constraint on any consistent ranking. Separate an A from a B explicitly, saying which moves a B answer may still contain while losing marks for the omissions. Do not build in any requirement for citations or reading lists; I want the question to stand on the reasoning alone.

Begin with the essay question itself, with no preamble and no restatement of this request; then give the model answer; then the marking scheme.
