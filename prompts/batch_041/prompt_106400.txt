I run a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of the infinite, and I keep hitting the same pedagogical wall with Thomson's lamp: everyone arrives already fluent in the three or four standard reactions, so the session degenerates into reciting labels instead of exposing what anyone actually thinks. What I want from you is a diagnostic instrument that sorts readers by the commitments they hold rather than by the names they've memorized. Please take on the role of an expert in the metaphysics of the infinite and the philosophy of mathematics, think carefully about the logical space before you reach for any terminology, and build me a decision tree. Begin every reader at the same entry commitment — say, "a supertask can at least be described without immediate contradiction, since each step is well-defined and the schedule of steps converges" — and let each subsequent node be a question about one assumption that the family of puzzles turns on: whether a completed infinite sequence must have a final member; whether the outcome of a process is fixed by the limit of the states it passes through; whether the answer is settled by the *number* of steps (and its parity) rather than by any state the system occupies; whether the idealization is physically admissible at all (discrete time, a minimum duration, bounded energy, finite precision); and whether the right response to the parity problem is to call the question ill-posed, to deny that there is a fact of the matter, or to revise classical logic. Every node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer about a concrete case without already knowing where it leads, and I'd like the tree to be forced to run on at least four of them: Thomson's lamp, the Ross–Littlewood balls, a converging variant in which the lamp's brightness (rather than its on/off state) tends to a limit, and one further case of your choosing in which the limit exists and yet the resulting answer still feels coerced.

I want at least the following leaves represented: the view that supertasks are impossible because the description is inconsistent; the view that the description is consistent but simply fails to fix a final state; the limit view on which the outcome is whatever the sequence converges to; the view that the question is ill-posed because an infinite number of switches is neither even nor odd; the view that grants logical coherence but denies physical realizability; and one non-classical or dialetheist leaf on which the lamp ends up both on and off. Where a leaf is occupied only by a position you have had to reconstruct rather than one anybody has actually defended, say so plainly, and flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate. Please also mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies and give a one-line diagnosis of each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Before drawing anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming the assumptions in play and telling me which ones you deliberately left off the tree and why — I'd rather see the logical space first and the labels second. Then lay out the tree itself as an indented outline, each node a question and each branch labelled with the answer that takes you down it, and close with a compact table mapping every leaf to its single most awkward commitment. Keep the whole thing to roughly eight to twelve leaves so that it fits on two sides of a handout; if you have to cut something, cut cases rather than branch questions.
