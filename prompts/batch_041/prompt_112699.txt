I have drafted the following essay for a graduate workshop on agency and time. I would be grateful for a detailed referee-style report. Please assess: (1) whether the central distinction is clear and whether it does genuine explanatory work; (2) whether the argument equivocates between descriptive discounting and normative authority; (3) whether the empirical characterisation of hyperbolic discounting is accurate enough for a philosophical audience, and where it overreaches; (4) whether the parliamentary metaphor earns its keep; (5) whether the conclusion that akrasia should be dissolved for hyperbolic discounters is too quick; and (6) line-level suggestions for tightening. I am not asking for references, reading lists, or citations; please comment on the argument as it stands.

The Ledger and the Curve: Akrasia Without a Unified Will

The classical problem of weakness of will is usually posed as a conflict within a single agent at a single time. An agent judges that, all things considered, she ought to do A, and yet, knowingly, she does B. The puzzle is how a judgement can fail to move the will when it is the agent's own judgement. I want to suggest that this framing is too narrow for the phenomena that interest us, and that hyperbolic discounting gives us a better model not of weakness of will but of a distinct failure: diachronic incoherence. My central distinction is between Point-Akrasia and Curve-Akrasia.

Point-Akrasia is the classical case. At the moment of action, the agent's all-things-considered judgement favours A, but she does B because of the immediate pull of desire. Her judgement is stable; her will is not. Curve-Akrasia is different. Here the agent's preferences are time-inconsistent because her discount rate is not constant. At t1 she prefers the larger-later reward; at t2, when the smaller-sooner reward is near, she prefers the smaller-sooner reward. At t2 she does not judge that she ought to have the larger-later reward and then fail to pursue it. She judges that the smaller-sooner reward is better. The conflict is not between judgement and desire but between two temporal standpoints. If we call the second case weakness of will, we are treating the earlier standpoint as authoritative simply because it is earlier. That is a normative assumption, not a description of agency.

Hyperbolic discounting is often described as a declining discount rate: the near future is discounted steeply, the far future less so. This produces preference reversals when the near future becomes the present. But the description is sometimes overread. It does not show that hyperbolic discounters are incapable of stable long-term preferences, nor that they are always impulsive. It shows that their preferences over dated outcomes are not time-invariant. An agent can be perfectly consistent at every time slice and still be diachronically incoherent. The incoherence is not located in any one slice; it is located in the sequence.

This suggests a parliamentary model of the agent. The agent is not a single sovereign will but a legislature of time-slices, each with its own discount curve. Akrasia, on this model, is not a failure of the will to follow reason; it is a failure of constitutional procedure. The earlier self may pass a resolution, but the later self may repeal it. The question is whether the earlier self has any authority over the later self. If it does, it must be because the later self has reason to recognise that authority, not because the earlier self is closer to the agent's true preferences. There is no true self outside the sequence, only a ledger of commitments and a curve of discount rates.

I propose a test, the Ratification Test. An action is akratic only if the agent, at the time of action, would upon cool reflection with her long-run discount curve endorse the earlier judgement and yet act otherwise. But the test threatens to collapse. Cool reflection with a long-run discount curve is just another standpoint, and it may not be the agent's standpoint at the time of action. If we privilege it, we have smuggled in the very unified agent we were trying to avoid. If we do not privilege it, we seem to lose the distinction between akrasia and mere change of mind.

My provisional conclusion is deflationary. For agents whose preferences are radically time-inconsistent, the concept of akrasia should be replaced by the concept of diachronic incoherence. Akrasia remains useful only where there is a relatively stable constitutional self that can be betrayed. Where there is no such self, there is no one to do the betraying. The language of weakness of will is then a projection onto a sequence of standpoints, not a description of a single agent's failure.

I am uncertain about this conclusion. It may prove too much: if it dissolves akrasia for hyperbolic discounters, it may dissolve it for all of us, since all agents discount. The parliamentary model may also be doing no more than redescribing the problem. And the Ratification Test may be either circular or unnecessary. I would welcome comments on whether the distinction between Point-Akrasia and Curve-Akrasia is stable, whether the empirical gloss is fair, and whether the deflationary conclusion is defensible.
