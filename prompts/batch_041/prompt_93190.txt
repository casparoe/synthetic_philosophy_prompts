I serve on the ethics and grants advisory council of a mid-sized human-rights foundation, and I have to circulate the written opinion that will frame Thursday's vote.

The request before us is this. In a country where a military commander annulled an election eighteen months ago and has since dissolved the courts and the legislature, detained several thousand people, and presided over a famine in the north, a coalition of civic associations and surviving members of the deposed government has asked us to fund and publicly endorse an operation to remove him. The operation would in all likelihood kill him and four or five senior officers; the planners themselves concede a serious risk of a violent succession struggle, of reprisals against the communities that have sheltered the opposition, and of a hardline faction emerging from the officer corps stronger than the man it replaces. The coalition's political wing is a government-in-exile that no one elected and that is dominated by one regional faction. Our money is not decisive, but our endorsement is: two European donors have said they will follow our lead.

Our charter requires that any endorsement of removing a ruler satisfy four conditions, which it takes over from the medieval discussion of tyrannicide, and from Aquinas in particular. First, the ruler's defect must be one of title rather than merely of exercise: he must hold power without right, as a usurper does, rather than hold it rightly and use it badly. Second, no lawful authority must remain that can depose or try him. Third, the killing must be the act of the community's public authority rather than of private initiative, since private killing invites faction, private vengeance, and the settling of old scores under cover of public justice. Fourth, the removal must be expected to leave the community no worse off than the tyranny it ends, which the charter glosses as requiring that the successor not be worse, that reprisals and civil conflict be contained, and that the act not discredit the cause it serves. The charter adds a proviso that has not come up before: a ruler who seized power without right may in time acquire title through the community's acquiescence, and the council is to say whether eighteen months of uncontested control, with no rival authority able to displace him, has begun to do so.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend one concrete course of action — endorse, decline, or endorse on stated conditions — with a time frame and with the conditions spelled out precisely enough for the board to vote on them item by item. Work through each of the four conditions against the facts above, saying plainly which are met, which are doubtful, and what further evidence would settle the doubtful ones. Two of my colleagues will press hard, and the opinion has to answer them rather than talk past them. One holds that because the commander is a usurper and not a lawful ruler governing badly, the third condition is relaxed, and any private party with the means may act. The other holds that the fourth condition can never be satisfied in advance, since we cannot know the successor, and that a test no case can pass is a prohibition in disguise. Give each of them the strongest version of their case before you say whether you accept it, and be candid about what the foundation would be risking if the majority follows you.
