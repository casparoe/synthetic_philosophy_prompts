I'm a science-and-ideas journalist of fifteen years' standing, and next month I'm presenting a paper at a historiography workshop. My thesis is that the ancient historian's central epistemic instrument and the modern reporter's are the same one — the independence of sources — and that the newsroom's two-independent-sources rule is a folk degeneration of a criterion Herodotus and Thucydides were already feeling toward. Both traditions, I argue, make the same mistake: treating multiplicity as reliability when what matters is the shape of the transmission tree, since a dozen witnesses who all trace back to a single informant, a single leaked document, or a single press release are one source wearing twelve hats. I go further and claim that the criterion of embarrassment and the reporter's instinct about who has a stake are the same move, and that Thucydides' promise to report only what he saw or heard from eyewitnesses is a rhetorical self-presentation rather than a method.

The room is mostly ancient historians — Herodotus and Thucydides specialists — plus two media-studies scholars. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this audience rather than the ones I'd get from journalists, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a classicist would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
