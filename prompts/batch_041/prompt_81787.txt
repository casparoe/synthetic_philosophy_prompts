I've been in a seminar on social choice this term, and the thing I keep turning over is the discursive dilemma — the pattern in which a group is majority-consistent on each of several linked propositions and yet ends up endorsing a set of judgments that no individual member holds. It strikes me as a drama before it is a theorem, and I'd like you to write a short story that stages the pattern without ever naming it or explaining it in the narration. No character should diagnose the problem; no narrator should step back and tell the reader what went wrong. The reader should be able to reconstruct the votes from what the characters actually say and do, and should feel the trap close.

Here is the setting I have in mind. A five-member planning commission in a small coastal town meets at night to decide whether to let a developer rebuild a seawall. The commission's rules require a separate vote on each of three propositions, in this order: (1) the applicant holds valid title to the foreshore; (2) the proposed works comply with the town's coastal management plan; (3) the permit shall issue. The rules also provide that (3) is entailed by (1) and (2) as a matter of law, so no commissioner may vote yes on (3) unless she also votes yes on both (1) and (2), and none may vote no on (3) unless she votes no on at least one of them. Give me the roll call in the scene itself. I want the tally on (1) to carry, the tally on (2) to carry, the tally on (3) to fail, and each individual commissioner's three votes to be consistent with the entailment rule, so that the reader can check the arithmetic against the dialogue. The second half of the story should turn on a wrinkle I'd like you to work into the commission's standing orders: the chair, who once clerked for a court that had been badly embarrassed by precisely this pattern, invokes a provision nobody has ever used — call it the reservation of the losing premise — under which the commission enters the majority's conclusion on (3) but must set out in full, as part of its recorded reasons, every premise that a majority of the commission accepted, even where those premises support the opposite conclusion. Work out what that provision actually accomplishes. Does it restore the commission's rationality, or does it merely relabel the inconsistency in a form that will embarrass someone later? And does the developer — who is in the room, and who understands the commission's rules better than most of the commissioners — walk out with a permit or without one?

Keep the story close to the room: paper cups, tide charts, a blown projector bulb, the one commissioner who insists on reading the management plan aloud. Let the philosophical content come through the votes, the reasons the commissioners give for them, and the chair's maneuvering over the provision, rather than through anyone's lecture. If a character ends up arguing for aggregating by premises or by conclusions, let her argue it as someone with something at stake in the outcome — a re-election, a flooded street, a grudge against the developer's lawyer. I'd like the ending to leave the reader genuinely uncertain whether the commission has made a decision or merely produced a document.
