I'm a backend dev with zero philosophy training and I've stumbled into what feels like a consistency bug in how I model my own beliefs. Heres the setup that keeps bugging me. I'll happily treat a big pile of facts as true — say a thousand claims from a textbook I trust — even though I'd bet good money at least one of them is wrong. Each one on its own, I'd say true. All of them together, I wouldn't. Same thing with a lottery: for a million-ticket draw I'm sure ticket 1 loses, sure ticket 2 loses, sure ticket 3 loses, and so on, yet I'm not sure none of them win, even though that's the same statement. People tell me the first case is the preface thing and the second is the lottery thing and that there are roughly eight standard escapes. Heres the list I've collected, in my own words, and I've probably mangled some of them:

1. You don't actually know or believe the lottery proposition at all: knowing something takes more than it just being very likely (it can't be the kind of thing that could easily have gone the other way), so ticket 1 loses never makes it in. Cost: this also kicks out most of the textbook.
2. Keep all the individual beliefs but drop the rule that believing A and believing B forces you to believe A-and-B. The million ticket beliefs are fine, they just never fuse into one belief.
3. Rational belief is allowed to be inconsistent. Each belief only has to be probable enough on its own; the whole set doesn't have to hang together, and you just make sure your reasoning never notices the clash, or you use a logic that doesn't explode when it does.
4. The bar for sure enough isn't fixed: it slides with what's at stake and with which question you're answering, so each ticket clears the bar for the ticket-sized question and none of them clears it for the all-of-them question.
5. Separate acceptance from belief. You adopt a policy of treating p as true for practical purposes, and policies can be locally fine without being globally consistent, so there's no paradox, just two different attitudes doing two different jobs.
6. Drop binary belief entirely, credences only. The paradox is an artifact of pretending there's a line you cross. Awkward part is you still have to act and assert sometimes.
7. A belief is something you'd still hold after learning the relevant facts, after the conditionalising you'd do anyway. Lottery tickets flunk that test and ordinary textbook claims pass it, so the line between belief and mere high probability is drawn by stability instead of by a number.
8. Same as 3 but you actually build it: a non-monotonic engine where conclusions are defeasible and the belief set isn't deductively closed, so it's fine to hold p and q and then retract p-and-q later.
9. My own invention, roast it if it's dumb: keep a global error budget. You're allowed a pile of less-than-certain beliefs as long as the total chance you're wrong somewhere stays under a fixed cap; adding a new low-confidence belief forces you to evict something or bump your confidence in the rest.

What I want: put these in tiers by how well they handle the textbook case and the lottery case at the same time, not just one of them. One sentence each on what it buys and what it costs. Then rate each 0-10 on would I actually ship this in an agent, assuming the agent has to store facts as true/false and act on them, with maybe degrees under the hood but no degrees at runtime. Also tell me which of these are the same move wearing different hats, which ones are quietly just refusing to answer the question, and which pairs I can't have both of. No names or citations needed, I'm definately not going to go read papers about it.
