I've got a workshop slot in two weeks and I've decided to finally put my half-baked Allais thing in front of people, so what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the argument. The thesis, roughly: I think the Allais pattern — everyone takes the sure million over the 89/10/1 gamble, then turns around and takes the 10% shot at five million over the 11% shot at one — has been misdiagnosed for decades. The standard line is that people are violating the independence axiom. I say independence is fine, and that what people are actually flouting is a much weaker and much less defensible principle, something like: if two options differ only by a common consequence, then the presence of that common consequence shouldn't change how you rank the parts that differ. Call it common-consequence invariance. My hunch is that independence can be gotten without it, and that the two are being run together. The reason I believe this is that I've built a variant of the Allais pair where the sure thing is gone: the top branch is a near-certainty instead of a certainty, and the common consequence is a small chance of a catastrophic loss instead of a small chance of nothing. In that pair the intuitive choices, for me and for the dozen or so colleagues I've ambushed with it, go the way the standard theory predicts — which makes me think the whole paradox is really about certainty rather than about substitution. I also want to float the idea that the certainty in the sure thing is doing evidential work, it tells you something about the world that a 99.5% chance doesn't, and that once the agent is allowed to condition on that, the two choices in the classic pair aren't even the pair the axiom is about.

Now the audience. The room is mostly decision theorists who think expected utility is basically right and that Allais just shows people are sloppy, plus two behavioral economists who treat Allais as a settled refutation of the whole framework, plus one philosopher of economics who's a hardline rationality realist and is going to despise the evidential move. I need the objections I'll actually get from this specific room, not the generic ones out of a decision theory textbook — ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does to me, and phrased the way each of those three camps would actually phrase it. For each one: what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply, and a one-line note on where that reply is soft or quietly question-begging. Flag anything that would be fatal if it lands rather than merely annoying, tell me which objections I should meet with a concession instead of a fight, and include the one question I'm dreading — the one that would make me look like I haven't read the literature I'm so breezily dismissing — so I can have an answer ready.
