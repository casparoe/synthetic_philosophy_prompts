Here's the question and the answer.

**Question (25 marks).** Malthus's predictions about population and subsistence are often said to have been "right but early". Distinguish at least three different things that claim could mean — for instance, that the theory is a conditional whose antecedent has not been satisfied, that the prediction concerns a horizon longer than the evidence covers, or that the theory correctly describes a regime that has since ended. For each, say what kind of evidence would count for or against it, and assess whether the "right but early" defence of Malthus adds testable content or is an ad hoc rescue.

**Student's answer (approx. 550 words).**

Malthus's theory is best stated as a conditional. If the food supply can only grow arithmetically while population grows geometrically, and if the technology of production and the stock of land are fixed, then population will press against the means of subsistence, and any improvement in living standards will be absorbed by more people rather than higher income per head. So the theory is not the simple prediction that the world will starve by a particular date; it is a claim about the equilibrium level of income in a world of diminishing returns.

Judged as a prediction, the record looks like a failure. Real wages in England rose steadily through the nineteenth century, fertility fell rather than rose with income, and the famines Malthus expected in the industrialising core never materialised. The theory says living standards will be pinned near subsistence, and we observe two centuries of rising living standards. The most natural reading is therefore that Malthus was refuted.

The defender's reply is the "right but early" move. The theory was not refuted, only postponed, because the escape from the trap came from something the theory held fixed: technological change and the opening of new land. On this reading the last two centuries are an anomaly in the antecedent, not a counterexample to the conditional. I think this rescue is legitimate rather than ad hoc, because a conditional cannot be refuted by cases in which its antecedent is not satisfied, and because nobody has shown that the mechanism has stopped operating. The crude versions of the theory are dead, but the underlying logic still constrains what can happen in a world of finite resources.

Two things complicate the picture. First, as Popper argued with his idea of degenerating research programmes, a theory that is continually patched up so that no possible observation counts against it stops being science. If every failure is explained by "the conditions weren't right" and every success by "the mechanism is at work", there is no testable content left. Second, Boserup's account of agricultural intensification actually strengthens Malthus, since it shows that population pressure is the engine of agricultural change: the food supply does respond to the number of mouths, which is why the trap keeps receding.

The best verdict, I think, is that the theory has not been falsified, and a theory that has survived two centuries of attempted refutation deserves to be treated as confirmed rather than merely unfalsified. We cannot rule out that resource limits will bite in the long run, and until they do, Malthus's warning stands.
