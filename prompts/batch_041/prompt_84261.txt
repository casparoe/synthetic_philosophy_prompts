I work on programming language semantics and compiler verification: operational semantics, type soundness proofs, bisimulation, contextual equivalence, and the occasional argument about whether a denotational model is fully abstract with respect to the operational one. I know that world well, and I keep trying to use it to make sense of a philosophical debate that I suspect it does not fit. I want you to do the translation properly and then tell me what leaked.

The debate is about identifying an ontology from the outside. We have access to something limited — a system's behavior and dispositions, or, in the scientific cousin of the problem, only the observable consequences of a theory — and we want to say what entities, kinds, and structure the system or theory is committed to. One side holds that rival ontologies can agree on everything accessible while disagreeing about what there is (enduring objects versus temporal parts, rabbits versus rabbit-stages, one basic substance versus two, a world of things versus a world of stuff), and that in at least some of these cases nothing further fixes which one is correct. The other side holds that the apparent underdetermination is always either trivial — a difference in notation rather than in ontology — or removable by some further constraint: simplicity, charity of interpretation, causal contact with the entities in question, whatever the proposal on offer.

Translate this into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: terms and their denotations, operational versus denotational semantics, observational and contextual equivalence, bisimulation, alpha-equivalence, non-isomorphic models of one and the same theory, initiality and uniqueness up to isomorphism, full abstraction, intended versus merely admissible models, and what I mean when I say that two programs are the same program.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which parts of the debate survive cleanly, which survive only as distortions, and which cannot be said at all in my vocabulary? Test two suspicions of mine rather than granting them. First, I suspect the translation makes the problem look trivial: in my world, models being unique only up to isomorphism is normal and unalarming, and alpha-equivalence is a fact about notation rather than a threat to realism, so if the philosophers' indeterminacy thesis comes out looking like something I would shrug at, is that because their problem is shallower than it sounds, or because my vocabulary has no way to register what worries them? Second, I suspect I am smuggling something in: in my world the denotation of a term is stipulated by a definition I write, not discovered from behavior, and I may be quietly assuming the existence of a specification that the philosopher does not have. If that assumption is doing the work, say so, and tell me what remains of the translation without it.

Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. Do not present a live philosophical dispute as settled, and do not present the semantic analogy as the consensus if it is a move that some people make and others reject.
