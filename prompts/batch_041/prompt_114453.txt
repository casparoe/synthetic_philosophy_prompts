So I've been poking at the discursive dilemma / doctrinal paradox for a while now — you know the setup, you let a group majority-vote on each premise and on the conclusion seperately, and you can end up with a majority for p, a majority for q, and a majority against "p and q" — and I keep feeling like the interesting questions arn't anywhere near where the literature's attention is.

If I had three or four people for a year, each maybe a day a week, and the goal was to make collective judgment procedures actually work better in real groups rather than to prove more theorems, where should they go? I want a ranked agenda with the reasoning, not a survey: for each question, why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people who are smart but not social-choice PhDs, and how neglected it is relative to how much it'd matter.

Things I'd want you to weigh, and feel free to tell me some of these are dead ends: the escape hatches that look like single-peakedness in disguise — does any of that actually turn into design advice for a real committee?; who gets to set the agenda, and how much of the impossibility literature is secretly about agenda power; premise-based vs conclusion-based procedures and who each one advantages; whether aggregating probabilistic forecasts and aggregating yes/no judgments are the same problem or two different ones; and the one I think is weirdly underexplored — multi-agent LLM setups where an ensemble or a debate is supposed to "decide" something. That's a group making judgments under an aggregation rule, the errors are correlated in exactly the way the theorems assume away, and as far as I can tell nobody's applying any of this.
