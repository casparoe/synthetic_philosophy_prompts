I am preparing the methodological appendix to a narrative history of a political collapse, and I want to know which of the following remedies for hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy is actually worth the cost of adopting. The two distortions should be kept apart: hindsight bias proper, meaning the historian's post hoc credences about what was likely, which silently reweight the causes; and the narrative fallacy, meaning the distortion introduced by narrative form itself, which rewards inevitability, foreshadowing, and a single unbroken line of causation. Rate each of the five remedies below from 0 to 10 for its net epistemic value — how much distortion it removes, minus whatever explanatory or evidentiary content it destroys — for an account built from a fixed body of evidence.

1. Outcome-blind drafting: the causal skeleton is assembled from the sources with the terminal outcome withheld from the drafter, who then reconciles the skeleton with the outcome once it is revealed.

2. Symmetric counterfactual obligation: for every cause the account treats as decisive, the historian must write an equally detailed and equally long narrative of a near-miss outcome that the same evidence would support.

3. Adversarial reversal: a reviewer rewrites the account so that the opposite outcome looks inevitable, and the historian reports how much of the text had to change, as a fragility score attached to the account.

4. Comparison-class discipline: a proposed cause is admitted only if it also distinguishes the actual case from a comparison class of similar cases that ended otherwise.

5. Model-first, story-second: the narrative is replaced by an explicit causal model — variables, mechanisms, stated dependencies — with prose permitted only as a clearly marked presentational layer above it.

Return exactly five numbered items, in the order given, and nothing else: no introduction, no conclusion, no restatement of the list. Each item is two sentences. The first sentence gives the rating, identifies which of the two distortions the remedy actually addresses, and says why it lands where it does. The second sentence names the single consideration — an argument, a claim about how historians actually work, or a structural feature of the remedy itself — that would move that rating the most, in either direction. No references are needed; I will supply those myself.
