I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs for years, and Aumann's agreement theorem is one of those results that gets cited constantly and, I suspect, sloppily. Here is how a recent essay summarizes it:

"Aumann's theorem is the mathematical proof that rational people cannot disagree. If two agents are both Bayesians and each is aware of the other's opinion about some proposition, their opinions must coincide: common knowledge of a disagreement is impossible. (Technically, 'common knowledge' just means that each of them knows what the other thinks.) Note that the theorem doesn't require the two agents to start from the same prior -- only that both be rational and that the disagreement be out in the open; even agents who begin from very different starting points are driven to a single shared posterior. It follows that when two informed, honest people persist in disagreeing, at least one of them is being epistemically irrational, and that the correct response to discovering a peer who disagrees with you is to split the difference and move toward the midpoint of your two credences."

I'd like a sentence-by-sentence audit. Which parts would someone who works on this theorem accept as stated, which would they wince at, and which are straightforwardly wrong? I'm especially interested in whether the closing lessons -- that persistent disagreement among informed people indicts someone's rationality, and that the rational move is to average -- follow from anything true in the summary, or are additional claims smuggled in. I'd also like to know how much work the theorem's setup is doing here: whether the prior is taken as given and shared, whether that sharedness itself has to be common knowledge, and what happens if the two agents disagree precisely about the prior. Please reason it out rather than pointing me to the literature.
