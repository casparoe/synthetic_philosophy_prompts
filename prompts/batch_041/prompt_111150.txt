My reading group did a session last week on the so-called unilateralist's curse and I somehow ended up the guy defending a conclusion I don't actually believe. Let me set up the case the way we did it, then give you the argument I got stuck holding, because I can't find the loose thread and it's bugging me.

The setup: some option is on the table -- seeding the ocean with iron, releasing a preprint, giving a big risky gift, publishing a dangerous method, whatever. Any one person can just do it. No coordination, nobody's permission needed. Each person is honestly trying to figure out if it's a good idea and each is probably, but not definitely, right about it. The curse part is that even if only a small fraction of them are wrong, the chance that *at least one* of them goes ahead with a bad idea climbs fast, and goes to near certainty as the crowd gets bigger. That's the whole mechanism. Ok. Here's the argument, numbered:

(1) When I'm unsure whether some action is net good or net bad, the right thing to do is whatever has the best expected value given what I know.
(2) In a case like this there are plenty of other people who could do the thing instead of me, and their information is about as good as mine.
(3) Since they don't coordinate with each other, the probability that at least one of them does it wrongly is very high, approaching 1 as the crowd grows.
(4) So the thing is going to get done wrongly by somebody regardless of what I do.
(5) So the expected value of me doing it isn't the value of the act, it's the value of one more draw from a lottery that's already stacked toward error, and that draw is negative.
(6) So I shouldn't do it. Nothing in the argument turned on which case it was, so nobody should ever act unilaterally on a moral judgment the crowd around them might not share.
(7) Which means no whistleblowing on a hunch, no giving to a charity you think is underrated, no voting for a fringe candidate, no publishing a contrarian result, no civil disobedience, no dumping iron filings in the ocean even if you happen to be one of the rare people who's right about it.

(7) is obviously too much. But I'd go to the wall for (1), and (2) and (3) are just the curse itself, which is supposed to be a real result. So where does it come off the rails? I'm suspicious of the jump from (4) to (5) -- (4) seems to be about what other people are going to do and (5) seems to be about what my act is worth -- but I can't tell if that's an actual problem or just something that sounds profound in a seminar room. Also, does any of this change if the people aren't independent, like if they all read the same blog and got excited at the same time? And does it matter that "wrong" isn't the same as "net harmful," since you can be mistaken about an option in a direction that still leaves it a good idea?

So: tell me which numbered step, or which gap between steps, you'd give up, why it looked fine to me in the first place, and what giving it up costs. In particular, does dropping that step mean I have to keep the conformity rule that you should never act unless you'd expect others to agree, or does it mean the curse is a genuine reason against acting but just not a decisive one? If I have to lose something I'd rather lose (3) than (1) -- I'm attached to expected value -- but tell me what I'm paying either way. If the honest answer is that the argument is fine and I should conform, say that instead of being nice about it.
