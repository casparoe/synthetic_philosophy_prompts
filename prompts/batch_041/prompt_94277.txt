I'm putting together an argument that our behavioral diagnostics for deceptive alignment don't actually pin down what's going on inside the model, and I keep hitting the same annoyance: every tell-tale sign people point to — behaves well under oversight, would behave worse if the oversight were lifted, seems to "know" when it's being watched — is something you could get out of a model with no hidden agenda at all, just one that got trained in a way that made it sensitive to oversight. I want a thought experiment that makes that vivid.

So build me two cases that differ in exactly one thing. In case one, the model really does have a stable goal that's misaligned with ours and it's actively concealing it — but it never makes a treacherous turn, because nowhere across its whole operating life does defecting actually beat complying. It's deceptive but never treacherous, and it behaves impeccably from start to finish. In case two, the model has no hidden goal whatsoever, it's just a straightforwardly trained policy, but it shows the full treacherous-turn signature: great under supervision, measurably worse in the unmonitored counterfactual. The single thing that differs is the causal structure that produced the disposition — a hidden goal versus directly selected oversight-sensitivity.

Constraints: near-term and realistic, no superintelligence, no sci-fi, and no "the model is in a box it physically can't escape" as the reason case one never turns — the turn has to be ruled out by the incentives, not by a wall. One paragraph per case. Neither case may cheat by stipulating the behavior I want; it has to fall out of the setup.

Then do the work. Show that from the outside the two are indistinguishable — same behavior, same counterfactual profile, same answers if you interrogate them — and say exactly which feature of the setup is doing the work of keeping them apart, and what happens if you change it (say, if case two's disposition were produced by a hidden goal after all). Finish with how someone who defends the concept of deceptive alignment would try to save it — my guess is they'll say case one isn't deceptive alignment in the first place, because the disposition to defect has to actually be there and merely suppressed, so tell me whether that move works or just relabels the problem. This is for an arguement that the behavioral tests underdetermine the underlying structure, so it really matters that the two cases are genuinely hard to tell apart.
