I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on institutional design, and one of the modules covers futarchy — the proposal, associated with Robin Hanson, that a polity should fix its values by ordinary vote and then settle its policy questions by prediction markets, adopting a proposal precisely when the market says the world will go better if it is adopted than if it is not. The summary version is easy to teach and easy to mock; what I want is a piece of sustained satire that students who already know the proposal will find genuinely funny, and that a reader who has never encountered it can still follow without a glossary. So please write me a customer-help FAQ page — numbered items, roughly 650 words, deadpan from first line to last, with no winking at the reader — from the fictional Office of Conditional Legislation of the charter city of Bellhaven, which has governed itself this way for nine years. Seed the piece with the house mechanics I'll give you, so that it reads like a real institution rather than a thought experiment: every proposal is paired with two conditional markets on a designated welfare metric, the Composite Flourishing Index, whose fourteen components are revised annually by a Standing Committee on the Index; the state pays a liquidity stipend to market makers; a Bureau of Adjudication resolves ambiguous questions of fact; and a recent episode, the Harbor Bridge Reversal, is widely believed to have involved a single large trader who moved a market and thereby brought about the very outcome he had bet on.

The humor has to come out of the actual structure of the idea rather than out of generic disdain for markets. Let the FAQ's calm, procedural answers quietly surface the real difficulties — that the choice of metric is itself a value choice smuggled into the belief side of the ledger; that a market on what will happen if a proposal passes is partly a market on whether it will pass; that thin markets, subsidized market makers, and a funded whale behave nothing alike; that resolving ambiguity is itself a political act; and that betting well is not obviously the same competence as governing well — without ever naming any of these as an objection or stepping out of character. Include at least one item that answers a complaint by citing the constitution's rule that no one may hold a position on the Index, and another that tells citizens what to do if they suspect manipulation; end on an item that is entirely calm, entirely procedural, and quietly chilling. Define every term of art in the course of using it, so a newcomer never has to look anything up.
