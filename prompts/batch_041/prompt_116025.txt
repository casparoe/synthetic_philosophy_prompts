I am preparing to lead a graduate reading group on the 'Self-Consciousness' chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology of Mind, and I need help with a passage that I find genuinely puzzling rather than merely obscure. Near the end of the section on recognition, the text says: 'They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another.' A few lines later it continues: 'This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes.' And then: 'Self-consciousness is primarily simple existence for self, self-identity by exclusion of every other from itself. It takes its essential nature and absolute object to be Ego; and in this immediacy, in this bare fact of its self-existence, it is individual. That which for it is other stands as unessential object, as object with the impress and character of negation. But the other is also a self-consciousness; an individual makes its appearance in antithesis to an individual.'

What I want is a careful reading of the step from the first sentence to the second and third: why does the mutuality of recognition, once achieved in conception, immediately present itself as disparity, and what exactly distinguishes the one who is 'merely recognized' from the one who 'only recognizes'? Is the asymmetry a requirement of the concept of recognition as Hegel has developed it up to this point, or is it a claim about how recognition first shows up for consciousness before it is properly achieved — and if the latter, what work is the word 'appears' doing? I should also like to understand how this asymmetry makes the later outcome of the chapter necessary rather than merely possible: that the master's recognition proves one-sided, while it is the bondsman who, through fear and labour, arrives at something like self-consciousness. I am not asking for a summary of that outcome, but for the argumentative link between it and the passage above. Please do not substitute the familiar later narratives about masters and slaves for what these paragraphs themselves say, and be explicit about where the text is ambiguous and the interpretation is yours.
