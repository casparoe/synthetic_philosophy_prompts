I'm in a graduate seminar on Kant's philosophy of religion, and a friend of mine from the modal logic group has been pressing an argument that I can't answer. He cheerfully grants Kant's objection that being is not a real predicate, that tacking existence onto a concept adds nothing to its content, and then claims the objection simply misses Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument, the one Gödel sketched in his late notebooks. That argument, he says, never treats bare existence as a perfection. What it treats as positive is necessary existence, the property a thing has when every essence of it is necessarily exemplified, and necessary existence is a real predicate if anything is: it genuinely adds something to the concept of the thing. His reconstruction of the proof runs roughly like this. Positivity is a property of properties, closed under entailment, with exactly one of a property and its negation counted as positive; a being is God-like just in case it has every positive property; God-likeness is itself positive, and necessary existence is positive; from these it follows that a God-like being is possible, that God-likeness is the essence of anything God-like, and therefore, by the S5 principle that what is possibly necessary is necessary, that a God-like being exists of necessity. He also waves away the modal collapse worry, the complaint that these axioms seem to make every truth a necessary truth, as an artifact of one particular reading of the positivity axioms.

What I'd like from you is this: put yourself in Kant's position, not as a scholar of the Critique but as Kant himself, reading this proof, and give me his verdict on it. Would he say the proof has merely relocated the old fallacy rather than escaped it, that the concept of necessary existence is doing exactly the illicit work he accused the older argument of doing with existence? Or would he allow that a modalized perfection is a genuinely different case and look for the error somewhere else? If he looks elsewhere, where does he think the proof goes wrong, and does that verdict sit comfortably with the rest of the critical philosophy, with the limits he places on the categories, his account of real as opposed to logical possibility, and his treatment of the ens realissimum as a mere ideal of reason rather than a possible object? I want his verdict delivered as a verdict, with the reasoning laid out step by step, not a survey of what various interpreters have said.

Please also be explicit about where this reconstruction is most likely to fail. Which of your claims about Kant's probable response are the shakiest, and what piece of evidence about Kant's texts, or what feature of the mechanics of Gödel's proof, would lead you to a different verdict?
