I am the teaching assistant for a 300-level course in philosophy of mind and action, and I am grading the third of five short-essay questions on this term's midterm. The question is worth 25 points and the parts are weighted 8, 9, and 8. I need a numerical mark out of 25 with the corresponding letter, and, more importantly, comments keyed to the parts that justify every deduction and note anything the answer gets right that a hurried reader might miss or wrongly penalize. I have not settled on a mark yet and would rather form my own view after seeing the reasoning laid out. The class had read the Hofstadter essay and the relevant chapter of Dennett, so the answer is not expected to supply references, and it should not be marked down for lacking them.

The question, as it appeared on the paper:

"Q3. Hofstadter introduced the term 'sphexish' for the behaviour of the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus. Returning with a paralysed cricket, the wasp leaves it at the threshold of the burrow, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes out, and drags the cricket in. If the cricket is moved a few inches while she is inside, she brings it back to the threshold and inspects the burrow again — and will do this however many times the cricket is moved. It never occurs to her to pull the cricket straight in. Dennett used the case to give a name to a fear: that if our behaviour is caused as the wasp's is, then our deliberation is a spectator effect, and we are wasps with longer routines. Hofstadter also remarks that even a painter with an unmistakable personal style is sphexish — confined within boundaries of mental space that she can neither see nor step outside.

(a) What exactly makes the wasp's behaviour sphexish? Say which features of the case carry the philosophical weight and which are incidental. [8]
(b) Dennett's reply to the fear of sphexishness: what is the reply supposed to show, and what does it not show? [9]
(c) Does the difference between the wasp and a human agent survive the worry that we might be sphexish at a higher level — sphexish, that is, about the very routines by which we detect routines? [8]"

The student's answer:

"The wasp's behaviour is sphexish because it is released rather than guided. Nothing in the wasp represents the burrow as already inspected, or the cricket as already at the threshold; the inspection is triggered by the cue of the cricket's being at the entrance, and it is triggered again whenever the cue recurs. What carries the weight, then, is the loop and the absence of any internal state that tracks the goal; what is incidental is the cricket, the paralysis, and the fact that the animal is small — any cue-release routine would make the same point.

Dennett's use of the case is to name a fear rather than to offer an argument. If determinism is true, our actions are caused by prior states, and so are the wasp's; the difference between her routine and a human deliberation would then be one of length and of how many sub-routines are nested inside it, and the sense we have of weighing reasons would be an effect of the process rather than a cause of what we do. His reply is that the wasp is a misleading example because it is simple. A deterministic system can be arbitrarily complex, and complex deterministic systems can generate novelty that no one could have predicted from the rules — cellular automata are his example, where a one-line rule produces endlessly varied patterns. So determinism does not imply sphexishness; freedom just is the complexity of the control structure, and the wasp is unfree because she has almost none.

That reply is not enough if Hofstadter's remark about the painter is taken seriously. A style is a set of dispositions that also releases behaviour, and if the painter cannot see the boundaries within which she paints, then we have no case of a routine that escapes its own terms. At best we are sphexish about our sphexishness, and the difference between Sphex and us is one of bookkeeping.

The case therefore shows that what makes the wasp unfree cannot be the mere presence of a routine, but it also shows that freedom cannot be a matter of the routine's internal richness. If the cricket's position is what releases the inspection, the wasp is not free; and if some sub-routine of ours is what releases our deliberation, we are not free either, however many levels we nest. Breaking a routine means acting independently of the cue that releases it, which requires that the release not be fully determined — some indeterminism at the level of the mechanism. The wasp has none. This is why the case is at bottom about experience: the wasp's loop is not hers because there is nothing it is like to be in it, and if a machine had a routine of that kind but with something it was like to run it, the routine would be its own."
