I am a catastrophe-risk analyst at a mid-sized reinsurer, and I am the person who signs off on the "event rate" — the annual probability of a loss-causing event — that goes into our pricing and into the risk report our board reads every month. We work from a vendor's catastrophe model, and for fifteen years our house rule, left behind by a chief underwriter who had read a little philosophy at university, has been painted on the wall of the modelling room: defer to the model's event rates except where the countervailing evidence is inadmissible. Nobody can tell me what that means in practice. Some of my colleagues take the model output as the answer and stop thinking; others quietly shade it toward whatever the last few storm seasons felt like, and neither group can say why what they did was defensible. I have been asked to write the team a decision procedure for when we may depart from a chance estimate, and I want it to rest on what the philosophy of probability actually says about deference to objective chance — the claim, as I understand it, that if you know the objective chance of an event is x, then your own credence in it should be x, at least when your evidence is of the right kind. I believe this is called the Principal Principle, and I suspect that "of the right kind" is doing all of the work.

Please write that procedure. It has to be something an analyst with no philosophy and an hour per account can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says that the estimate governs, where it says that we may not defer, and a short justification of each step, including the parts of the theory that make this harder than it looks. In particular it must cope with the fact that the vendor's number is an estimate of a chance rather than a chance itself; with the possibility that some of my evidence bears on the event rather than on the conditions that make it likely; with the fact that the reference class the model used is not always the class our exposure falls in; with the climatologists' insistence that the baseline chance is itself drifting over the horizon we price; and with the awkward case where a model assigns an event a probability near zero and the event happens anyway. Then run it on three live problems: our own twenty years of claims data suggesting that the model's landfall frequency for one coastal county is roughly forty per cent too low; a seasonal forecast that would have me raising next quarter's rates even though the model has not moved; and a low-frequency, high-severity product where the model gives a one-in-ten-thousand annual rate and we have booked two such losses in five years.

I am asking partly in order to settle an argument, so I would rather have the uncomfortable answer than a diplomatic one: tell me plainly where the procedure returns something I will not like, and where it cannot decide and has to hand the question back to a person. I also need one paragraph on the step where a settled credence becomes a price, since a small error in a rare-event rate costs us more than a large one in a common rate, and I do not want the procedure to pretend that the two questions are the same. I have to circulate this to the modelling team and to the chief underwriter, who signs off on it, before Thursday's meeting, so keep it to a document they will actually read, and mark clearly which steps are procedure and which are judgment wearing procedure's clothes.
