I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on rationality, commitment, and time, and I'm drafting my final paper around an argument I constructed myself. The course treats both individual self-binding (the sirens case: an agent arranges in advance so that a later choice, even if made, will not take effect) and the constitutional analogue, in which a polity entrenches a rule so that a later majority cannot easily undo it. I built the following argument to test how far the usual justification for self-binding can be pushed, and I now find it suspicious precisely because it seems to prove far too much.

(1) A choice is authoritative for an agent to the extent that it issues from her considered judgment rather than from a passing distortion — panic, craving, infatuation, fashionable enthusiasm.
(2) When an agent can foresee that a later choice of hers will issue from distortion rather than considered judgment, she is entitled to make that later choice non-authoritative in advance by binding herself, so that the later choice, even if made, does not govern.
(3) An act of binding is itself a choice, and any choice can be foreseen from an earlier standpoint as issuing from distortion rather than considered judgment: the agent who designs the contract may be in the grip of exactly the enthusiasm that a calmer earlier standpoint would disown.
(4) So by (2), that earlier standpoint is entitled to bind the designer — that is, to render her very act of binding non-authoritative, so that the contract never takes effect.
(5) The same reasoning applies at every stage: for any choice whatever, there is an earlier standpoint from which it can be diagnosed as distorted and stripped of authority.
(6) Therefore no choice is authoritative unless some choice is first; but a first choice would be made by an agent with no considered judgment to draw on — an infant, a founding moment, a state of nature — and so would itself be unauthoritative.
(7) Hence no choice is authoritative, and therefore no act of self-binding binds: the practice is either empty or a matter of raw power.

I cannot accept (7), and I would rather give up almost anything else than (2), since (2) is the only premise that explains why the practice is anything more than a trick with incentives. My concrete cases are a student union that first adopts a rule requiring a two-thirds majority to amend any bylaw and then uses that rule to entrench a second rule; and, at the individual level, an agent who binds herself never again to enter into a self-binding arrangement.

Which step do you reject? For whichever step you reject, explain why it looked fine on a first pass, since I was convinced by every step when I wrote it. Then price the alternatives: what exactly must someone give up in order to keep (2) and deny (4)? Does anything change if authority is treated as a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing? Does your fix carry over to the constitutional case, or does the analogy break there for a reason independent of the regress — for instance, because the parties bound are not plausibly the same agent, so that a binding of one person by another is being smuggled in under the language of a self binding itself? And once the offending premise is gone, which of the following defences of precommitment can still be stated, and at what cost: (a) one that privileges a calm or considered standpoint as genuinely more authoritative, rather than merely later or earlier; (b) a purely procedural one, on which binding just is whatever the rules provide, with no claim that the binding standpoint is wiser; (c) a deflationary one, on which self-binding is nothing but a device for changing one's own future incentives, with no transfer of authority at all? I care less about a verdict than about which of these I can hold without inconsistency, and I would like to keep the claim that a later self can be wrong about its own good.
