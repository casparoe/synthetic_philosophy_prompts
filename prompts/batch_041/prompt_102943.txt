I am assembling an anthology of short fiction built around puzzles in decision theory, and I would like you to write one of the stories. The puzzle has a specific shape, and I want the story to stay faithful to it rather than drifting into a generic tale about greed or trust.

Here is the mechanism. A town of exactly one thousand adults, all of them aware of one another and all of them competent reasoners, receives a single, one-time offer from a reclusive benefactor. The offer is public and its terms are common knowledge: every resident may privately and simultaneously submit a claim on the benefactor's fortune, which amounts to nine hundred thousand. The fortune is divided equally among those who claim it; if nobody claims it, the fortune is released to no one and is simply gone. Separately, and independent of the fortune, each resident who refrains from claiming receives a guaranteed payment of one thousand. Decisions are made in private, submitted at the same moment, and cannot be coordinated, contracted, or enforced; there is no second round and no way to observe what others have done before choosing. So if everyone claims, each claimant ends up with nine hundred. If everyone refrains, each ends up with one thousand. If a single resident claims while the rest refrain, that resident ends up with nine hundred thousand.

Two habits of reasoning pull against each other here, and the story should let both live. One treats a person's choice as a sample of the choice that anyone reasoning the same way would make: since everyone is alike and knows it, ask what happens when the reasoning is universalized, and notice that universal refraining beats universal claiming. The other treats a person's choice as independent of what the others do: given what the others will in fact do, ask what is best for the chooser, and notice that a lone claimant takes everything. The mechanism also has a sting worth keeping in view: the collectively best outcome, everyone refraining, is one that any single resident, expecting the others to refrain, would abandon in a heartbeat. I want the story to sit inside that instability, not to dissolve it by authorial fiat, and not to import outside machinery — no enforcers, no reputations, no repeated play — that would make the puzzle disappear.

You do not need any prior reading to write this; everything required is stated above. Keep the arithmetic exact, keep the mechanism airtight, and let the characters reason in their own voices and registers rather than lecturing. The fiction should carry the concept, not announce it.

Please give me the response in two sections, in this order:

The Story — roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. A complete short story that dramatizes the offer and the reasoning it provokes, with the mechanism reproduced exactly and the two habits of reasoning embodied rather than explained.

Commentary — roughly 400 to 600 words. Cover the choices you made in the fiction: which characters carry which reasoning, how you staged the instability, and where you chose to leave the question open. Then give me your own verdict on the underlying puzzle — whether there is a determinate answer to what a resident should do, what turns on how the residents regard one another's reasoning, and which single feature of the mechanism does the most work in forcing the tension.
