I work in the office at a primary school, no kids of my own there, so when a row breaks out I'm just standing by the photocopier listening. There's one that's been going for weeks and I've genuinely stopped being able to tell who's got the better of it. I keep turning it over at night, which is a bit sad, I know.

Whats happened is three families clubbed together and pay a retired teacher to run a maths club in one of their living rooms on Tuesdays. Its private, nothing to do with school. Now some other parents want the school to send a letter telling families not to arrange this kind of thing, because it splits the year group, and everyone knows a letter from the school means people would quietly stop. The two loudest are Sam and Rae, and here's roughly how it went.

Sam: The club doesn't take anything off anyone. The kids who aren't in it are exactly as well off as they'd be if it didn't exist. So stopping it can't help a single person, it can only take something away from the kids who go. That's making the world worse for no reason. It's spite with paperwork.

Rae: It doesn't do nothing. My girl sits with two of the ones who go and she's started saying she's thick. That's a real thing happening to her every week, and it stops if the club stops, so she gains. You're only counting marks. And I'm not after everyone being the same for its own sake, I care about the kids stuck at the back, the ones who never get picked.

Sam: But if it's the kids at the back you care about, this does nothing for them. They can't do long division either way, they just won't have to watch anyone else learn it. So the only thing your letter actually buys is less of a gap. Say that out loud and see if you still like it. And if feeling small because someone else has more counts as a harm, then we have to level everything, forever — no child allowed to be better at anything, no family with a nicer car than the neighbours.

Rae: It isn't that she's jealous of maths. It's that she's worked out the whole thing is a race and some people get a head start. And even if it was plain jealousy — so what? It's pain. You don't get to wave it away because you don't like where it comes from.

Sam: Then try this. Two schools. One is like ours: some kids flying, some not, and the ones who aren't are no worse off than they'd be in the other school. The other school is the same, except every child is only as good as the weakest kid in the first school, and nobody anywhere is better off than before. You have to say the second school is better. Nobody believes that.

Rae: I don't have to say it's better. I'd say its better in one way and worse in another, and here the worse beats the better. That's a different thing from saying the mediocre school wins.

Then they both turned and looked at me, like I was the referee. I'm definately not on either of their sides. Can you go through it turn by turn and tell me who's right at each step, and where each of them is missing something — not just who's closer overall?
