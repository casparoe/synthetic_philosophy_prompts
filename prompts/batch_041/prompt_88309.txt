I am chief of staff to a cabinet minister, and two or three times a month a proposal reaches my desk that would require her to do something we would ordinarily call wrong — a covert action, an immunity deal with a militia commander, a promise broken, a report released only in part — and I have two days to put a one-page recommendation in front of her, knowing it may surface in a public inquiry years later. I want a decision procedure I can actually run, grounded in the philosophical debate about dirty hands and moral remainder rather than in gut feel, and terminating in one of four places: refuse; authorize as merely costly, with no remainder; authorize as a genuine dirty-hands case, with an explicit statement of what residue is left behind and what would or would not discharge it; or send it back to her as a question of values that no procedure of mine can settle. The procedure has to distinguish a wrong done to an identifiable person from one that consists only in breaching a norm or a role, has to force me to say whether I am claiming the act was justified or only that whoever does it should be excused (the two leave different residue), has to say something about whose hands get dirty when a decision is taken collectively rather than by one person and whether it can be delegated clean, and has to include a precedent step — what this authorizes the next officeholder to do, and where that gets recorded. Tell me which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the procedure will systematically misfire. Then run it on three proposals: misleading parliament about the timing of a withdrawal in order to protect an evacuation; granting a militia leader immunity from prosecution in exchange for a ceasefire that will hold; and closing a hospital against the advice of its clinicians in order to keep two others open. I would rather you tell me plainly which verdicts I will not like than smooth them over; no citations needed, I will find the sources myself.
