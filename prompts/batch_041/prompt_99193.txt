I am a novelist at work on a book about a pharmaceutical company on trial, and a scene in which the prosecution must establish that the company knew its drug was dangerous has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. The government's theory, as my legal consultant explained it, is the doctrine of collective knowledge: the corporation's knowledge is treated as the sum of what its employees knew, so the company can be said to have known a fact that no single employee ever knew — one chemist knew about an impurity, a second knew the impurity was toxic, a third knew the batch had shipped, and the company, assembled from them, is held to have known the whole. It is then convicted and fined, while its officers, as individuals, are imprisoned for what the company did; and when the corporate form turns out to be a sham, a court will pierce the veil and insist there was never a real agent there at all. I keep thinking of an ant colony: no ant knows where the food is, yet the colony finds it, and we casually say the colony decided to move; the colony Aunt Hillary in Hofstadter's Ant Fugue is never far from my mind. I am not looking for a survey of the free-will literature or a reading list, and I have no way to look anything up — I want you to reason from the practice itself.
