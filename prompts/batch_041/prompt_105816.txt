I am preparing a seminar on Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education and am trying to get clear on a passage that I find deceptively difficult. In the essay on the aims of education, Whitehead distinguishes between ideas that are merely received into the mind and ideas that are actively used, tested, and connected to other knowledge; the former he calls inert. He says that education with inert ideas is not only useless but harmful, and he also insists that culture is activity of thought and responsiveness to beauty and humane feeling, so that a merely well-informed person is a bore and useless. The passage I am paraphrasing runs roughly as follows: education should produce both culture and expert knowledge in some special direction, and the expert knowledge should not be inert but should illuminate and be illuminated by general culture. He then introduces the idea of a rhythmic cycle in education, with stages of romance, precision, and generalization, which he thinks is the remedy for inertness.

My difficulty is this: Whitehead seems to demand that knowledge be useful and applied, yet he also criticizes mere utility and insists on culture as an end in itself. How are these two demands meant to be reconciled? Does the rhythmic cycle resolve the tension, or does it simply restate it? I would like an explanation of what Whitehead means by inert ideas, why he thinks they are harmful rather than merely useless, and how the stages of romance, precision, and generalization are supposed to prevent them. Please also say whether the view is internally consistent, and how the historical context of the address, delivered during the First World War to an audience concerned with educational reform, shapes the argument. I am not looking for citations; I want the conceptual structure laid out clearly.
