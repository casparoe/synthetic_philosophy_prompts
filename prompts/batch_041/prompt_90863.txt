I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British weekly, and I am midway through a piece on the therapy/enhancement distinction — specifically on whether the line can be drawn in a way that a public funding body could actually administer. Over lunch last week, a philosopher friend floated a criterion I had not come across before, which he called the trajectory test: an intervention counts as therapy when it restores a person to the trajectory of functioning they were already on (arresting a decline counts as restoring), and as enhancement when it bends that trajectory upwards, or establishes one where none existed beforehand. I would like the column to test his proposal against the more familiar lines of division, but I keep losing the thread of which criterion is doing the real work.

So I would like you to rank five candidate criteria by how well each would serve a health service that must decide, case by case, which interventions count as clinical treatment (and are therefore eligible for public funding) and which count as enhancement (and are not), judging each on two counts. The five are: the statistical line, on which an intervention is therapeutic to the extent that it restores functioning to the species-typical range; the medical line, on which it is therapeutic if it treats a condition that medicine classifies as a disease or disorder; the opportunity line, on which it is therapeutic if it is needed to preserve a fair share of opportunity relative to others; the welfare line, on which the distinction tracks whether the intervention lifts a person's well-being from below some threshold to above it; and my friend's trajectory test. The two counts are, first, whether the criterion delivers the verdicts I would want on the awkward cases — prophylaxis (a vaccine, or a statin for someone at low risk) counting as treatment, stimulants for a diagnosed attention disorder counting as treatment while the same drugs taken for exam performance do not, growth hormone for a child of unremarkable height not counting, and a hypothetical mood-lifting wearable that nudges a healthy adult's affect upwards without any underlying complaint not counting — and, second, whether an administrator could apply it consistently without either drowning in borderline cases or leaving the line open to capture by whoever shouts loudest. Give me an ordered ranking from best to worst, with a short justification for each placement, a note on where the trajectory test lands and whether any of the five should simply be struck out, and please write your answer in British English.
