Okay so here's something I keep going back and forth on, and I'd like your actual number. Setup: the Church–Turing thesis is usually stated as the claim that the effectively calculable functions are exactly the Turing-machine-computable ones. Fine. But I've become convinced that the two halves of that 'exactly' sit in completly different epistemic boats, and that once you split them the thesis kind of evaporates as a substantive claim about the world.

The half that matters is: no function that some ideal finite procedure could grind out falls outside what a Turing machine can do. That's the bit people actually mean. My collegue says this is a genuine, mind-independent fact about what computation can be, and that we have overwhelming-but-fallible evidence for it — all the independent formalisms converged, nobody has ever found a counterexample, and so on. I say it's closer to a stipulation: that 'effectively calculable' is a slightly vague term we've now precisified, and that there's no fact of the matter we could have gotten wrong. Not that it's uninteresting — just that calling it true-by-discovery misdescribes what happened.

So: what's your credence that my reading is right, i.e. that the substantive half of the CTT is conventional/precisificatory rather than a discovery about a mind-independent fact? Decompose it. I want at least: P(there's a single determinate pre-theoretic notion 'effective calculability' with a fixed extension); P(if so, that the convergence of the independent formalisms is good evidence about that extension, rather than an artifact of them all being built to capture the same intuitive target — I suspect this is doing way more work than anyone admits); and P(that the discovery/stipulation dichotomy is even the right frame, versus the notion being fixed by mathematical practice and neither discovered nor stipulated). Add whatever else you think belongs, with a number and one line each, then the combination. Then tell me which term the bottom line is most sensitive to, and what would move it most.

Does that falsify the thesis, or does it just mean their word for 'computable' isn't ours?
