Hey — I'm drafting a piece for the magazine on races to the bottom and I could use a brain to bounce it off before I file on Thursday. It's the Moloch-adjacent stuff: alot of agents, each one acting in a way that makes sense on its own, all of them sliding into an outcome none of them wanted. Tax competition, antibiotics in livestock, engagement-optimized feeds, arms races, that whole family. My angle is that everybody writing about this is describing one particular kind of trap and then generalizing as if all of them work the same way, and I think there's a cleaner cut. Rough version of the argument:

Take any bad equilibrium that all the players would rather not be in and call it a trap. Standard story: many players, each one better off defecting no matter what the others do, so nobody can stop unilaterally. Standard fix is an agreement everyone would sign from behind a veil — a treaty, a cartel, a standard, a regulator. Fine as far as it goes.

My claim is that there's a seperate species that gets filed under the same heading and behaves nothing like it. In the second species the trap is a path, not a point. Each step down is profitable on its own AND it makes the next step down cheaper. One farm's routine antibiotic use changes what the next farm's vet considers normal. One jurisdiction shaving its rate resets the reference point for its neighbor. One feed optimizing for watch time sets the bar the next one has to clear. I've been calling these ratchet traps, because the defining feature isn't that the players are many, it's that defection doesn't reverse. You can't un-shave a tax rate.

Two things fall out of that, if it's right. First, in a ratchet trap a binding agreement at t=0 isn't self-enforcing even if literally everyone signs it, because the payoff to defecting grows while the ink dries. You're negotiating against a moving target. Second, the fix isn't a treaty at all — it's either removing the option (a hard wall you can't defect through, something that makes the low road illegal rather than merely unfashionable) or flipping the direction of the ratchet so each step up in compliance makes the next step up cheaper, which I suspect is most of what's going on when EU rules end up becoming the de facto global standard. So the interesting variable isn't multipolar vs. not, it's reversible vs. not.

There's a sub-point I'm less sure of, which is that traps with a floor are a different animal from traps without one. Race to the bottom implies a bottom exists. Tax competition has a floor at zero and probably self-limits; the pasture example has a floor, since the grass either regrows or it doesn't; but competition for attention or for military advantage doesn't obviously have a floor at all, and those seem like the genuinely dangerous ones. Is that a real distinction or am I double-counting the ratchet idea?

What I want from you:

1. Is ratchet trap a real subtype, or is it just a repeated game with drifting payoffs, which any game theorist would tell me is old news wearing a new hat? If it's old news, tell me the standard name so I can stop pretending I invented it.
2. Strongest counterexample you've got: a case that looks like a ratchet trap but got reversed by a plain old agreement among the players, no hard wall and no ratchet-flipping. If those exist in numbers, my second claim is dead.
3. Is my fix-dichotomy (remove the option vs. flip the ratchet) actually exhaustive? What's the third option I'm missing?
4. Where am I just wrong — factually, or in the game theory. I'd rather hear it from you than from the comments section.
5. And the one I actually care about: is it interesting, or is this a distinction without a difference? The kind of thing that sounds sharp in a pitch meeting and then evaporates when you try to write it down. If it's evaporating, say so plainly and tell me which claim to cut.

I should say I'm not looking for reading recommendations or citations — I've got plenty of those and no time to chase them. Just want the argument pressure-tested.
