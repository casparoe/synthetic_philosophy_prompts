I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I also take a regular shift on the standards desk at a mid-sized publication. A recurring problem lands there: someone we cover — a researcher, a science communicator, a public intellectual — criticizes something (hype, undisclosed conflicts of interest, misinformation, ethical corner-cutting), and then a reporter or a tipster points out that the critic has done the same thing, or something uncomfortably close to it. Sometimes the hypocrisy is the story we are pitched; sometimes it is a paragraph a writer wants to slip in to take the critic down a peg; sometimes it is the defense a subject raises against the critic, insisting the critic has no right to complain. We face three or four of these a month, always against a same-day or next-morning deadline, and I have been resolving them by gut. I would like a procedure instead.

Please turn the philosophical work on standing to blame — the question of who is entitled to hold whom to account, and what hypocrisy does and does not take away from a criticism — into a decision procedure that a non-philosopher on the desk can run in about twenty minutes. Keep what the underlying ideas actually say while making it usable by someone who has never read any of it, and be explicit about which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped.

The procedure should ask its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the point at which it stops with a no, and mark the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of editorial values rather than fact. It should keep separate at least these threads: whether the charge is accurate; whether the critic meets whatever conditions the literature attaches to having standing — their own record on the very norm they are invoking, their complicity in the practice, whether the matter is any of their business, and whether they enforce the norm selectively against opponents while excusing allies; and whether, standing aside, the underlying issue has independent news value. It should have something to say about the reformed wrongdoer who has acknowledged and changed course, about the standing objection raised in bad faith purely to change the subject, and about the case where the norm itself is contested and no one is sure the critic has violated it. And it must not let the messenger question become a way of quietly dropping a true charge, or a way of running a true charge while pretending the messenger is irrelevant.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work at each step: a prominent science communicator who campaigns against overstated claims and has made an overstated claim about their own field in a promotional setting; a public-health figure who urged strict caution and was photographed ignoring their own guidance; and a researcher who years ago did exactly the thing they now campaign against, has since disclosed it and changed practice, and is now leading a push for the norm they once broke — a case where I suspect the procedure will tell me the person has standing, which is not the answer the colleague who wants to write the piece is hoping for. Use one more case only in passing if it helps: an industry-funded outfit criticizing a researcher's conflicts of interest, where the messenger is compromised but the underlying point may still be checkable and publishable on its own.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — in particular which of these decisions remain value judgments that no procedure will settle, and where the procedure is likely to misfire. State how confident you are in the procedure's fidelity to the underlying philosophy, and single out the one step where you are least sure the simplification preserves the point rather than losing it.
