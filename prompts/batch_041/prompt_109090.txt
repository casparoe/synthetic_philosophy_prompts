ok so I just got home from a two-hour arguement with my friend Nadia about utilitarianism and I came out of it way less sure of myself than I went in, so I'm writing my commitments down to see wether they actually hang together. roughly:

1. I think Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is the best argument there is for utilitarianism. The way I understand it: stick everyone behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of turning out to be any member of the society, then notice that a rational person behind that veil maximizes expected utility. That gets you "the right social rule maximizes the sum (or the average — same ranking) of everyone's utilities." I find that pretty much decisive.

2. I accept the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms — completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence (the sure-thing principle) — as what it is to have rational preferences at all. And not just for individuals: I think those same axioms are the right constraint on the social ranking.

3. But I'm a prioritarian. A unit of well-being going to someone worse off counts for more than the same unit going to someone better off, and I mean that as a fact about well-being itself, not just the diminishing marginal utility of money. Even with everyone's incomes equalized you'd still get unequal welfare, and I'd still say the boost to the worse off matters more.

4. I think utility is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable — that claims like "this gain for A is bigger than that gain for B" are genuinely true or false.

5. I'm ex post. What has value is how people's lives actually go, not the ex ante chances they faced. If two people flip a fair coin and one ends up miserable while the other ends up blissful, I think that world is worse than one where both get the average of the two, even though ex ante the prospects were identical.

6. I'm not an egalitarian. I don't think inequality is bad in itself — a world where everyone is equally miserable isn't, just for that, better than one with the same total welfare unevenly spread. If I care about the worse off, that's because of (3), not because of equality.

So: can all six of these live together? I don't want a scattershot list where you flag every possible tension — I want to know which pairs genuinely can't both hold, ranked by how bad the clash is. For each one: which commitment is doing the damage, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me somewhere else. And if some of these only look like they clash and actually don't, say so and tell me why. Be specific, don't hedge.
