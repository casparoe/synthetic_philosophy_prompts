At the end of next month our department will hold a retirement dinner for the colleague with whom I have co-taught the intermediate logic sequence for fifteen years, and I have been asked to give the toast. There will be about seventy people in the room, and they divide cleanly. On one side are the logicians and theoretical computer scientists, most of them her former students and co-authors: people who have spent their working lives on provability, fixed points, and the arithmetization of syntax, who know precisely what the incompleteness theorems do and do not say, and who will hear a false step inside a sentence. On the other side are her family, her book club, and three colleagues from the music school, who know that she works on something to do with self-reference and will be lost the moment I say the word "consistent."

I want to give a six-minute toast (roughly eight hundred and fifty words at a dinner pace) whose theme is the Gödelian analogy: the passage from a formula that refers to itself to a mind that is aware of itself, the analogy that gives *Gödel, Escher, Bach* its shape and that she has been arguing about since graduate school. She has published, more than once, the case that the analogy is a category mistake; she has also told me, at a party, that it is the most beautiful mistake she knows. I want the toast to live in that gap without pretending to close it. And I want the analogy to be the toast's structure rather than merely its subject. The question she asked at the end of every seminar she ever attended was: "But what does the system know about itself?" I want the toast to end by asking that question again, and I want it to land differently the second time because of what the middle of the toast has done. The return has to be earned; it must not be announced, and it must not be a trick.

Three things to get right, since the room will be listening for them. First, the Gödel sentence is not literally a sentence about itself. It is a statement about numbers — that no number with a certain arithmetical property exists — and the self-reference is manufactured by the coding of syntax as arithmetic and by a fixed-point construction. If the toast lets this slide into "the sentence says of itself that it is unprovable," the front half of the room will stop listening, and rightly so. The loop I want is stranger and better than that: nothing in the sentence is looking at itself. Second, the toast must not use the incompleteness theorems to argue that the mind outruns any formal system. That inference is the one she spent a decade refuting, and it is the one most of her colleagues regard as a howler; the analogy may be offered as a suggestion about structure, something to stand next to, and no further. Third, the toast must be affectionate. The room is there to honor her, not to hear my views on mechanism.

Two resources are available. In Bach's *Musical Offering* there is a canon that modulates upward with each repetition, so that it arrives back where it began but a tone higher: a loop that returns and does not return. The crab canon, which reads the same forwards and backwards, is good for a line or two, and the book's own name for what I am after is the strange loop. Keep the technical content to one sentence at a time, and make every such sentence both true and intelligible to the book club. Do not quote from the book; I want this in my own words. Do not end with the standard move, in which the speaker says that the retiree, like Gödel's sentence, is true but unprovable — that line is available to anyone, and I would rather have something better.

Write the toast, and then a page of notes for me: where the loop turns and what makes it turn, which sentence is load-bearing, what I should cut if the room is still finishing dessert, and the one thing most likely to go wrong on the night.
