Ok, I need a second opinion on something before I embarrass myself in a seminar. I read this blog post a while back (some philosophy-adjacent substack, can't remember the author) summarizing Miranda Fricker's idea of epistemic injustice, and I've been half-repeating bits of it ever since. Here's the gist as I remember it.

Fricker says there are two kinds of epistemic injustice. Testimonial injustice is when someone's word is given less credibility because of prejudice against their identity — like the cops not taking Duwayne Brooks seriously after his friend was murdered. Hermeneutical injustice is when you can't make sense of your own experience because the culture hasn't given you the concepts for it, the classic case being women in the 70s who had no term for what was happening to them at work until "sexual harassment" got coined. Fine so far, I think.

But then the post pushes it further, and this is where I get nervous:

- It says the two are really one phenomenon. Both are the marginalized person being denied the resources to be understood; it's just that in one case the resource is credibility and in the other it's vocabulary. The post's line is "one injustice, two currencies."
- It says hermeneutical injustice is basically a cognitive deficit in the victim — she literally can't know what's happening to her until someone hands her the word — and that the injustice is over once the word exists and has circulated a bit.
- It closes by saying that because both kinds are structural, baked into how a society doles out credibility and meaning, no individual is ever really to blame for committing them, which is why the fix has to be political rather than personal — you change institutions, not hearts.

I'll be honest, the "one injustice, two currencies" bit sounds right to me, and I buy the ending more then I probably should. The "she can't know until she gets the word" part is what nags at me.

So: which parts would someone who actually works on this accept, which parts are the popularizer smoothing off the edges, and does that closing conclusion about blame actually follow from anything true in the summary? Don't just tell me it's "roughly fine" — I want to know where to stop repeating it.
