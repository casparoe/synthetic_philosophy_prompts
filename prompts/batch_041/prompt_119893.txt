I convene a monthly reading group of educated non-specialists — no formal training in logic, but comfortable with abstraction — and last month we finished Gödel, Escher, Bach. The group came away with the book's central arc intact and, I suspect, unearned: self-reference inside a formal system, then strange loops, then, in the closing chapters, the self-aware mind as the obvious continuation. For the next session I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard, and I want to teach the Gödelian analogy honestly — not to deflate it, but to make the group see precisely what would have to hold for the passage from a self-referential formula to a self-aware mind to go through.

Don't explain it to me; plan the explanation. Which ideas have to arrive in what order for the analogy to feel built rather than asserted, and which single idea, introduced too early, will let them nod along while understanding nothing? What one example should carry the whole session — a fully arithmetized Gödel sentence, a toy formal system of the sort the book itself constructs, or the natural-language stand-in 'this sentence cannot be proved' — and, whichever you pick, why the others would mislead at this stage? I have a further constraint I care about: I want the group to see that the sentence does not literally announce its own unprovability, but says something about arithmetic which, once the coding is in place, proves to be equivalent to that announcement, so that the machinery of the coding does the work rather than self-reference arriving as magic.

Expect these confusions, and tell me where in the sequence to head off each and where the point is simply too early to make: that the theorem shows every sufficiently rich system to be 'incomplete' in some loose, portentous sense; that self-reference within the formalism is already self-awareness; and that the human mathematician's advantage is merely that we can 'step outside' the system. Finish with a single closing question that would separate a group that has grasped the shape of the argument from one that has only learned to recite it, and tell me what to leave out at this level — and to say aloud that I am leaving it out, rather than gesturing past it.
