My flatmate and I spent most of last night arguing about the teletransporter, and I came out of it more convinced then I went in that Parfit has basically got it right. What matters in survival isn't being literally the same person; it's psychological continuity — memory, character, intention, connected in the right causal way — and identity is neither necessary nor sufficient for it, and probably isn't a deep further fact at all. So the teletransporter is its just an odd way of getting to Mars, and when the machine makes two of you at once there's no fact of the matter about which one is you, and that's fine rather than tragic.

I want you to try to break it. What should count against this view? Not "but my intuition says I'd be dead" — I want the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a Parfitian would themselves concede and which they'd wave off as disguised longing for a further fact.

Three things I couldn't answer last night, and would especially like you to take seriously:

- The delayed copy. I get scanned tonight, the file sits in a drawer, I live another thirty years, then someone runs the file. The copy wakes up with tonight's memories and none of the last thirty years, and I'm still here. So two of us stand to the scanned me in much the same relation. Does the view have to say the copy is as much a continuation of me as I am? If so, is that harmless, or does it show the relation can't be what matters?

- Causal route. Suppose my memories and character end up in someone else by a freak route — hypnosis, or a machine that reads my brain and writes it into theirs. Nearly everyone says that isn't survival. If the view has to count it as survival, is the real criterion something narrower — the normal cause, the brain, the living organism?

- Branching and intransitivity. Parfit is happy to say what matters isn't an all-or-nothing equivalence relation. Is that a genuine cost or just the point? And does the view get hedged to the point where nothing could count against it — a matter of degree, no fact of the matter, and so on?

What does the view predict or rule out that its rivals don't? And if it turns out to be well insulated, tell me honestly whether that's a bug or whether the insulation is the whole content of the view.

British English please, and don't hedge — I'd rather be told which defeaters are fatal than get a tidy list of everything ever said on the subject.
