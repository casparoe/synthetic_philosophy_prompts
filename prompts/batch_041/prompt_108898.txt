So my friend and I went at it for like two hours last night about safe Pareto improvements, and I came out of it more convinced than when I went in, which is annoying, so I want you to try to take the thing apart.

Here's roughly what I'm now committed to. When you're dealing with another agent whose behavior you genuinely can't put probabilities on -- you've delegated some decision to a process, or you're picking which game two parties will be playing, and there just isn't a principled credence to assign to how they'll play it -- the only target you can aim at without smuggling in assumptions is a safe Pareto improvement: a modification of the game, or of the strategy profile, or of your own available moves, such that everyone comes out better off with certainty, no matter how the ambiguity resolves. Not better off in expectation, not better off given your best guess about them. Better off under every way the uncertainty could shake out. The cases I keep chewing on: you disarm, i.e. you throw away some action you'd otherwise be tempted to play, and that leaves everyone safer; or you set up a token game -- pure cheap talk, its outcome means nothing in itself -- where both sides commit in advance to play a specific profile of the real game as a function of what happens in the meaningless one, so the meaningless game becomes a real one; or you make your commitment conditional on whatever the other player's default behavior turns out to be, once that default has been credibly revealed. And none of the machinery gets you anywhere unless you accept some qualitative assumptions about how games get played -- that strictly dominated strategies can be iteratively stripped out, that isomorphic games get played isomorphically, that sort of thing. That's the frame. I think it's the right target, and I think people working on cooperation between AI systems should be aiming at these instead of at good outcomes in expectation.

My friend's objection, the one still bugging me, is that the safety is a mirage. All the real work gets done by those qualitative assumptions, she says, and those are behavioral assumptions about the other player in a lab coat -- so once you notice that, the view is either vacuous (with no assumptions about how outcomes correspond across games, you can't prove any non-trivial improvement exists) or it's ordinary equilibrium reasoning with a fancier word for robust. She also thinks an agent that insists on certainty before it improves anything just gets out-competed by agents that don't, and that a lot of what gets called safety is really just committing to throw away your own options, which can function as leverage and isnt obviously benign.

What I want: what would actually count against this? Not generic complaints about game theory, the specific things. Which results would be fatal and which merely awkward? If it turns out non-trivial safe improvements exist only under assumptions as strong as just assuming some particular equilibrium gets selected, does the view collapse into its rival, or is it still doing work? Is its exposure different when the ambiguity is over the other player's preferences versus over which equilibrium they'll land on versus over whether they're even trying to play the game? What would a committed defender concede as counting against them -- the strongest thing you could grant without giving up the frame? And be honest about whether it's been insulated: insisting on certainty sounds like it risks a lot, but if the safety is always defined relative to a set of possible resolutions that the modeller gets to choose, you can make almost anything safe by choosing a small enough set, and I want you to say whether that's what's happening and whether it's fatal or just a discipline problem.

Then tell me what would change your mind, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong. I'd rather have the one consideration that would flip you than a hedged survey. And dont worry about citing literature, I don't care about that -- just don't invent results, and flag when something is your reconstruction rather than something you're actually confident about.
