I spent thirty-five years as a microbiologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology. I would like to work through a case that has never stopped nagging me. Treat the discovery of the giant viruses, Mimivirus first and then the other large DNA viruses that followed, as a historical episode, and analyze it through the lens of the philosophical debate over what it is for something to be alive. Keep the episode bounded: the discovery of Mimivirus and the ensuing debate over whether giant viruses are alive, rather than the whole history of virology. I am not interested in a survey of that debate for its own sake, and I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the episode and the accounts to be put to work on each other.

The episode, as I understand it, is this: a virus was found that was large enough to be mistaken for a bacterium, with a genome exceeding that of some cellular organisms and containing genes that had been thought to belong only to cellular life. It still could not replicate on its own; it required an amoeba host. The question then became whether such an entity belongs on the living side of the line, and whether the line itself was ever as clear as textbooks suggested. That is the case I want examined.

For the lens, use at least three accounts of life and keep them distinct. First, a definitional account that treats life as a natural kind with necessary and sufficient conditions. Second, a homeostatic property cluster account, on which life is a cluster of properties such as metabolism, self-reproduction, evolution by natural selection, and boundary maintenance that tend to co-occur because of underlying mechanisms, without any one property being strictly necessary. Third, an organizational or autopoietic account, on which the crucial feature is a self-producing network that maintains its own boundary. You may add a fourth if it does real work, but do not let the list become a catalogue.

Then I want the mapping done with some rigor. For each account, say what it must say about a giant virus, an ordinary virus, a viroid, a plasmid, a prion, and an ordinary bacterium. Which of the features of these entities does the account capture, which does it have to explain away, and where is it simply silent rather than forced to deliver a verdict? Be explicit about the difference between an entity that fails a definition and an entity that the definition was never designed to classify. I am especially interested in whether the giant viruses are a genuine anomaly for the definitional account or only a hard case that a more careful definition can absorb.

Finally, reverse the direction. What does the giant-virus episode show about the accounts themselves? Does it confirm one, embarrass another, or reveal that the whole dispute was framed around a case, the small obligate intracellular virus, that was never representative? If the episode suggests that life is a term we use for different purposes rather than a single natural kind, say so, but also say what is lost if we give up on a single account. If you conclude that the question is merely verbal, do not stop there; say what practical or theoretical consequences follow from the choice of one usage over another. Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus. Where you are confident, say so; where you are reconstructing or guessing, mark that too.
