My flatmate Rosa is doing an MSc in cognitive science, and her mate Dev, who's doing a philosophy MA and thinks about consciousness all day, came round on Friday and the three of us ended up arguing till nearly 2am about whether global workspace theory actually explains consciousness or only explains access — you know, which contents get broadcast and become available for report and control. I was mostly making tea and refereeing, though I did wade in twice and got shot down by both of them, so I'm not after you taking my side. I just genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and it's been bugging me all weekend.

Rosa's line was that the whole point of the theory is that when information gets amplified and broadcast across the system — ignition, global availability, all of it — that broadcast just is what makes the content conscious. So if someone ever builds a system with a real workspace, recurrent amplification and all, and it behaves like us, there's nothing left over to argue about; the access/phenomenal split is a distinction without a difference. Dev said everything in that story is about which contents become available for report, control and reasoning — broadcast explains availability, it doesn't explain why any of it should feel like anything. Rosa said that's the cheapest objection going: you can say 'and yet it doesn't explain experience' about literally any mechanism, neurons or quantum goings-on in microtubules, so it's an intuition, not an objection. Dev said the difference is that her theory only has access-words in it: it defines consciousness as availability and then announces that it's explained consciousness, which is a stipulation dressed up as a result. Rosa said at least her story takes risks — if contents could be broadcast with no effect on report or control at all, or reported with no broadcast behind them, she'd be in trouble. Dev said every scrap of her evidence is behavioural or report-based, so it can never touch the phenomenal claim; the phenomenal bit is a free rider she can neither confirm nor lose, and she's using the testability of the access story to sell the experience story. Rosa said his alternative is a posit that by his own admission makes no observable difference anywhere — consciousness that can't show up even in principle, doing no work — and asked why we should think there's anything there to explain. Dev said the thing to be explained is precisely why any of this processing is accompanied by experience; that's the datum, not a posit. Dev also said her view makes consciousness a matter of degree — more broadcast, more consciousness — which he reckons is a big extra commitment she never argued for.

Then it got properly in the weeds, because Dev came up with two cases. Case one: we build a system with a genuine workspace, ignition and broadcast and all, but we cut the output side so that nothing it broadcasts can reach any module that produces speech or movement or any outward sign. Rosa said it's conscious — it's got everything the theory asks for and the report pathway is just a readout. Dev said the theory can't actually tell us that, because every criterion we have for calling something conscious is downstream of report, so in that case neither we nor its designers could know. Case two, which he said was the more interesting one: a system with no workspace at all, just a lookup table, that nevertheless produces exactly the reports a conscious system would. Rosa said obviously that one isn't conscious, because the reports are ungrounded — there's no workspace behind them. Dev said that's just her re-describing her favourite mechanism; a lookup table is a mechanism too, and on her own evidence-based methodology she's got no business calling it unconscious — she can only do it by appealing to something beyond the evidence, which is exactly what she accuses him of. Rosa said no: the whole point of the theory is that the mechanism, not the behaviour, is what matters, so the lookup table fails for lacking the mechanism while the cut-output system passes for having it — same criterion, applied consistently.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step — rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why — then say what each of them is missing and who's more right overall? I don't want 'it depends what you mean by consciousness' and everyone's a winner; I definately want to know which specific steps actually hold up. Also tell me whether Dev's two cases really cut against Rosa or whether she's got an answer she didn't give, because that's the bit I keep going round in circles on.
