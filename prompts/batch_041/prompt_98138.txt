After forty years at the bench I have retired into philosophy, and I now find myself defending a view about the demarcation of science that I would like you to try to break. The view is this: what marks a theory, or a research tradition, as scientific is neither its truth nor the bare fact that it forbids something, but the maintenance of what I will call its refutation surface -- the set of possible observation-reports that its adherents would treat as a defeat rather than as an anomaly to be absorbed. A theory is scientific to the degree that this surface is large and well-defined, and a tradition remains scientific only while the maneuvers by which it survives apparent refutations enlarge that surface rather than merely shield the core. I concede that this makes scientific status a matter of degree, and a matter of the conduct of a community rather than a binary property of a sentence; I am not yet sure whether that concession is fatal or merely a cost worth paying.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or historical developments would count against it? I am less interested in the standard recitations -- that the criterion is not itself falsifiable, that auxiliary hypotheses can always absorb a refutation -- than in whether such objections can be sharpened into something that actually bears on the formulation above, and in which further objections I have failed to anticipate.

Three worries I have already noticed, and would like you to adjudicate rather than merely restate. First, the refutation surface appears to grow monotonically under conjunction: since a conjunction is harder to satisfy, and therefore easier to refute, than either conjunct, I can inflate a theory's surface by conjoining claims wholly unrelated to it -- so that an arbitrary pairing would rank as more scientific than its parts, which looks like a reductio. Second, the surface looks time-relative: a claim that nothing in my lifetime could have disconfirmed may become disconfirmable once an instrument is built, so is the property a feature of the theory or of the state of our instrumentation -- and if the latter, has my criterion quietly collapsed into a piece of sociology? Third, my view seems to slide from a normative criterion into a description of whatever scientists happen to do: any community that refuses to absorb anomalies would count as scientific by my lights, which threatens to let the demarcation track the very boundary it was meant to explain.

Please sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, separating those that should genuinely move a defender from those that are merely awkward. Tell me what someone holding my view ought to concede as counting against it, as opposed to what critics will say and I can afford to dismiss. And tell me honestly whether the position, as stated, risks anything at all, or whether its qualifications have insulated it -- and if it is insulated, whether that is a defect in a criterion of this kind or an acceptable feature of a normative proposal. I would be most persuaded by being shown either a claim that everyone regards as scientific yet which on my account maintains no refutation surface, or a claim that everyone regards as pseudo-scientific whose surface is, by my own definition, large and growing.
