I'm a philosophy of physics PhD student and I'm putting together a class session on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument (the relativity of simultaneity → no objective present → all events equally real move), and I keep thinking Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five dramatizes it better than anything in the actual literature. The Tralfamadorian thing — all moments just sit there, none of them privileged, Billy hopping between them without ever choosing where he lands — reads to me like eternalism viewed from the inside. But I can't tell if the book is really doing the Rietdijk–Putnam move or whether its just fatalism wearing four-dimensional clothes, which is a different thesis and doesn't need relativity at all. And then the bit where Billy watches the war film backwards, bombs reassembling over Dresden, seems to be about the arrow of time, not the block — you can have a block universe with a perfectly good thermodynamic arrow, so if the novel is using that scene to argue that time's direction is an illusion, it's conflating two things. Could you give me a reading that takes the novel and the argument equally seriously: where the book genuinely captures eternalism, where it slides into fatalism or confuses the block with the arrow, whether the physics is doing any real work in it or is just atmosphere, and where the book resists the reading entirely? I'd also like to know what it adds that the argument alone can't — the argument hands you an ontology, but the novel is obviously after something about what it feels like to live inside one, and I want to say that without telling my students the book proves eternalism.
