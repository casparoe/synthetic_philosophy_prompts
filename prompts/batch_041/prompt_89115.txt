Ok, I need help with something. I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly for a fairly general audience — and I keep getting dragged into the same stupid fight. Every couple of years somebody publishes an index or a ranking or a listicle and the headline is 'Sweden is more liberal than Norway' or 'Norway is more liberal than Sweden' (or Canada vs the US, or Austria vs Germany, fill in the blank), everyone argues about it online for a week, nothing gets settled, and I still have to file something. So what I want from you is a procedure I can actually run before deadline, not an essay about the concept of liberalism.

Concretely: give me a decision procedure for claims of the form 'country A is more liberal than country B.' The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which I can write the sentence flat with no hedge, the point at which I have to hedge, and the point at which I should stop pretending there's a fact of the matter and make the disagreement itself the story. Then run it on two cases: Sweden vs Norway, and Canada vs the US.

A few things I've bumped into that I think the procedure has to handle explicitly rather than hand-wave, because they're where these arguments actually go wrong:

- 'Liberal' means at least three or four different things — the political ideology, the sheer absence of state restriction, permissive social policy, and in American usage roughly 'left of centre' — and the ranking can flip depending on which one you mean. So the first move is probably fixing the sense. But I don't want the procedure to just stop there, because readers don't care about our terminology fight and I still have to write the piece.
- Two countries can be ranked on every individual indicator and still not be ranked overall, if the indicators point in opposite directions. I want a test that tells me when I've got dominance — A is more liberal on everything that matters — versus when I'm being pushed into a trade-off, and I want it honest about the trade-off being a value weighting that isn't in the data.
- Any single number is a weighting in disguise. This is basically the index-number problem: the ranking can flip when you change the weights or the base year, and nothing in the world has changed.
- Laws on the books versus how they're enforced. A de jure measure and a de facto measure can point opposite ways, and I suspect a lot of these arguments are really about that without anyone saying so.
- Thresholds versus comparisons. 'Free' might be a threshold predicate while 'freer' is comparative, which would mean two countries can both count as free with neither being freer than the other. I think something like this is behind a lot of the confusion and I want it in there.
- Whose liberalism? A reform can make a country more liberal for one group and less liberal for another, so 'the country' may not have a position at all.
- I want the difference between 'we don't know which is more liberal' and 'there's no fact of the matter about which is more liberal' doing real work in the procedure, not just being gestured at.

Constraints, because this has to be usable by me and not by a research team: I've got maybe an afternoon, I dont have access to paywalled datasets, I can't get the people who built the index to answer an email, and I'm not going to run a regression. It has to be runnable by one person with a browser and a phone. Where the procedure can't decide, say so, and say what I should write instead. Also flag the places where the procedure will misfire, or where I'm most likely to fool myself into thinking I've settled it when I haven't.

On the two cases: don't sweat getting the country-level facts exactly right, I'll check those myself. Keep the empirical detail coarse and be explicit about what I'd have to go look up. What I need is the reasoning to be right. And I'd like at least one of the two cases to come out in a way I won't like — e.g. the procedure tells me my piece can't say the thing I wanted it to say, or that the honest version is much more boring than the pitch.

Very last line: give me a single line in the form \answer{...} with one label for what my Sweden/Norway piece should carry — settled, hedge, or no-fact-of-the-matter.
