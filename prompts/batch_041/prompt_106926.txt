You are a philosopher of science and metaphysician with deep expertise in the philosophy of mind and in the general metaphysics of science, and you have agreed to act as the neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration. Work carefully: before drafting anything, run a short private brainstorm in which you map the logical space of the dispute and decide which disagreements are genuine and which are verbal; then produce only the finished document. Do not cite sources, name authorities beyond the framework already in play, or gesture at a reading list — argue from the cases themselves, since the point is to make progress on the merits rather than to report a literature.

I moderate a long-running reading group of rationalists and scientifically minded philosophers, and for the better part of a year two of our members have argued past each other about Daniel Dennett's framework — the three stances (physical, design, intentional) and the claim that a pattern can be real without being intrinsic. They agree on the framework and disagree on what follows from it, which is exactly why the argument never terminates.

The first, call her the pattern realist, holds that the posits of the design and intentional stances are real patterns in Dennett's sense: objective, projectible, causally relevant features of the world, even though they are not intrinsic to the bare physical description and are visible only from a stance. The second, call him the stance fundamentalist, holds that the physical stance is privileged — that what there is is what the fundamental physical description says there is, and that design- and intentional-stance talk, however indispensable in practice, is a heuristic overlay rather than a description of further real patterns.

I want the document they should have written themselves: a single text both of them could put their names to. Please include the following.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — what both accept about what a pattern is, about the roles of compression and prediction, about what each stance is for, and about what is and is not at issue between them. Nothing vacuous; every agreed claim should have enough content that a third party could reasonably deny it.

2. A statement of the residual disagreement in which each side's position is phrased in wording that side would accept as its own.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each crux, the case or argument both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance.

4. A disagreements table summarizing the above.

Build the whole document around two pieces of new material, and make both sides engage with them rather than talk around them.

The Two-Compressions Puzzle. Take a finite deterministic system — specify something simple and fully explicit, e.g. a small cellular automaton with a stated update rule run for a fixed number of steps, so that its entire micro-history is a finite bit string. Suppose two description schemes both compress that string to exactly the same length. Scheme A carves the history into persistent localized objects that move and collide; scheme B instead represents long-range correlations that cut across those objects. The two schemes make identical predictions for the next stretch of the history, and neither can be derived from the other without loss. On a Dennettian account of real patterns, which of these patterns — if either — is real? Does the tie show that real-pattern talk is interest-relative, or is there a fact of the matter that the puzzle simply fails to expose? Have each side commit to a checkable prediction about what should happen to the two competing patterns when the system is perturbed.

The Budget Problem. A third scheme compresses the same history much better than either A or B, but only a reasoner with an enormous computational budget could ever find it. Is that pattern more real than A's? Is pattern-reality relative to a reasoner's budget? If it is, does that hand the win to the fundamentalist (the pattern depends on us) or to the pattern realist (the pattern is out there, waiting to be found)?

Finally, if you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — that the two use the word real in different senses, or that one is making a claim about projectibility while the other is making a claim about fundamentality — say exactly which part and why, and say whether identifying that dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it. Keep the document tight and non-repetitive; roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words is the target, and the disagreements table may be terse.
