I am finishing a novel whose protagonist has spent thirty years as a registrar of marriages and, on the side, as a notary and a taker of oaths; she has spent a working life administering other people's binding words, and the book's crisis is her slow, unwilling suspicion that none of it did anything at all. In the chapter where she writes the only poem she will ever write, I need that poem.

The form should be a pantoum: five quatrains, twenty lines, with lines two and four of each quatrain becoming lines one and three of the next, and the closing quatrain bringing back the third and first lines of the opening quatrain as its second and fourth, so that the poem's last line is also its first. She would choose this form because it is a form in which nothing is said that has not been said before, every stanza merely re-ordering words already in play. I want the form to be doing the argument, not ornamenting it.

The lines that recur should be the words of a genuine binding utterance: a promise of constancy made by one person to another, in the plainest available language, invented by you rather than borrowed from any real rite, liturgy, oath, or legal formula, so that nothing in the poem depends on getting an existing text right.

Across the stanzas the formula must never change; only what surrounds it. Let the circumstances come apart in the four ways I have come to think of as the four ways a binding utterance can fail. The words may be spoken by someone with no standing to speak them. They may be spoken out of order, or with a step omitted, so the procedure is never completed. They may be spoken by someone who does not mean them. They may be spoken with full intention and then simply not honored in what follows. The first two failures leave nothing behind: the act did not occur, and there is nothing to accuse anyone of. The last two leave the act standing and stain the speaker instead, so that the words bound and the person failed them. I want a reader to feel that difference without ever being told it. Let one recurrence also be heard, silently, as a different act altogether than the others, though the words are identical, and let one stanza make plain that the act is not finished when the speaking stops but is still being ratified or undone long afterward.

As for execution: no end-rhyme, since the recurrences are the music; lines of roughly equal length, eight to twelve syllables; plain contemporary diction, no archaism and no inverted syntax to prop up a meter. Keep out the technical vocabulary of the philosophy of language — nothing about performatives, felicity, or illocution — and attach no preface or explanatory note. The poem should satisfy someone who has thought hard about how words can make things so, and remain legible to someone who never has.

Because the last line is the first line, the poem closes as a loop. I want that closure to be genuinely double: a procedure correctly and completely executed, and a trap. Do not resolve which it is.
