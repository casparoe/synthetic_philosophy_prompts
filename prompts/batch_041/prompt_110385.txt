I'm a features writer who covers art, museums, and the ideas that get attached to them, and for the past year I've been reporting on a small regional museum where my closest friend from college is the director. The museum's centerpiece — a carved wooden figure a little under a metre tall, its face polished shiny by a century of handling — is the town's emblem, reproduced on the municipal seal; newborns are carried past it in a spring ceremony; the institution's whole claim to seriousness rests on it. Two years ago a visiting conservator noticed tool marks that didn't fit the catalogue date, and the board has been sitting on the results while it argues. The figure is not medieval. The medieval original burned in a parish fire in 1893, and a local carver rebuilt it from the charred remains and a single surviving photograph. Three things complicate the story further. First, some of the original is still in there: a hand, a shoulder, part of the base. Second, the carver departed from the photograph, so what we have is less a copy than an interpretation. Third, the photograph shows the medieval figure was frankly cruder, and everyone who has been told prefers what we have. There's also a timing detail the board keeps tripping over: the cult around the figure — the processions, the seal, the spring ceremony — only dates from the 1880s, so the object that people have actually venerated for a century and a quarter is the 1893 replacement, while the medieval original was the focus of all that devotion for barely a decade before it burned.

My friend has to decide within the month what the wall label says and whether to call a press conference, and the board has split into two camps that mostly shout past each other: one says the object has been exposed as a fake and has to be demoted in how the collection presents itself, the other says nothing that mattered has changed and that disclosure will only wound the town. I'd like to hand her something better than my own instinct. Could you sort out what is actually at stake — the distinct kinds of value the figure might carry (how it looks; its place in art history; the fact of having been made by a particular hand at a particular moment; the work it does in the life of the town; whatever else belongs on the list) — and then say which of those the discovery genuinely touches and which it leaves alone? A colleague in the art history department keeps insisting that a museum always owns an object and only sometimes a work, and that the two come with different values; another says the whole quarrel is confused because the value in question is relational — things matter because of the role they play in a practice — and that this is not a lesser or consolation-prize kind of value. I don't know whether either distinction survives contact with this case, and I'd rather you tell me than guess.

Specifically, I want to know whether there is a principled case that the figure's value is unchanged, or even greater, now that we know its history; what would have to be true about the value of originals for the first camp to be right that it must be demoted; and whether it matters that the carver invented rather than faithfully copied — that is, whether the case is really about age, about authorship, about continuity of material, or about something else entirely. If the answer turns on distinctions that don't survive this particular object — between a copy and a forgery, between the material and the form, between the maker's achievement and the thing's beauty — say where the case lands and why. Finish with a recommendation she can act on, including the substance of what the label ought to say, and mark clearly the points where you're genuinely uncertain rather than retreating into saying the question can't be answered.
