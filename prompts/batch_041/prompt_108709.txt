I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of science seminar, and the thing I cannot stop turning over is how my university actually sorts the scientific from the unscientific in practice, as opposed to in the textbook story. Our state's science curriculum standards say a topic earns a place in the science classroom only if it issues in testable predictions that could in principle be shown false, and that is the stated reason a set of religiously motivated biological claims are kept out of biology class; the same reasoning is invoked in the editorials whenever a school board fights over it. But in my own department, people cheerfully work on models that, so far as I can tell, no observation could refute -- string-theoretic constructions, certain many-worlds readings, some optimality models in biology -- and when I press them they say those are fruitful, or unifying, or progressive, or that they are just not yet developed enough to be tested, and that this is entirely different from pseudoscience. Meanwhile a friend in the psychology department says her entire methods grade turned on pre-registering a directional, falsifiable hypothesis before touching data, and that anything she finds by exploration is dismissed as merely descriptive and unworthy of publication. When I try to state the rule we are all following, I get three different answers in one afternoon: it is falsifiability, except when it is novel prediction, except when it is fruitfulness over the long run. I would like you to treat this whole cluster -- the curriculum rule, the reviewer's demand for a refutable claim, the tolerance for the unfalsifiable-but-fruitful, and the demotion of the merely exploratory -- as a single existing practice and tell me what it presupposes.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of science this practice commits us to: what it assumes about the relation between a theory and experience, about what makes a claim answerable to evidence, and about whether the difference between science and pseudoscience is a sharp line or a matter of degree. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with -- I suspect the curriculum rule and the lab bench are running on incompatible pictures and that at least one of them is doing rhetorical rather than philosophical work -- and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of science would license the very same behavior. Consider, as candidates for the presupposed view, a strict falsificationism on which a claim is scientific only if it forbids some possible observation, a view on which what matters is the long-run fruitfulness or progressiveness of a research program rather than any single test, and a view on which scientific status is really about method, community, or inference to the best explanation rather than about logic. Say which of these the practice can actually get by with, which it quietly rules out, and whether any part of it is internally inconsistent. Finally, tell me what would visibly change -- in the curriculum standard, in the referee report, in the methods course -- if we swapped the presupposed view for one of the rivals, and whether anything we care about would be lost.
