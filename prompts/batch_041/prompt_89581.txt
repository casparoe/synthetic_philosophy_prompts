So I need to write something for Thursday and I've been going back and fourth on it all day, so here goes.

Background: I sit on the ethics committee of our professional association, a few thousand members, humanities-ish. Last month one of our fellows — tenured, pretty well known in his area — put up a post that went viral making fun of a grad student's conference paper by name, with a screenshot of her abstract and a couple of lines about how nobody should let her near a dissertation. It was cruel in a fairly specific way and she's been getting garbage for weeks. The president wants us to strip his fellow status and pull him off the spring keynote. Our bylaws let us withdraw honors from members whose conduct brings the association into disrepute, and in forty years nobody has ever definately said what that means. The student herself hasn't asked us for anything; her advisor says she mostly wants it to stop.

My friend Priya and I argued about this for two hours last night and she got under my skin. Her view: this isn't punishment and we shouldn't pretend it is. Punishment needs an authority with standing to impose it, a rule you could have known you were breaking, and a hard treatment imposed because of the breach. We're just declining to keep associating with him, and declining is a liberty — nobody has a right to be a fellow or to give a keynote. She says the moment we call it punishment we owe him notice, a chance to answer, proportionality, all of it, and that's silly for what amounts to not inviting someone to our party. My view: if you take away a benefit because of what someone did, intending it to sting, and it stings, then it's punishment whatever we call it, and the only reason to insist otherwise is that the word drags obligations along behind it. Priya's reply, which I'll admit is decent: then the label is doing all the work in my argument, and that ought to make me nervous.

Two things I can't get past. First, what actually punished her was ten thousand strangers, not us — we didn't do that, and if we publicly censure him we're handing the mob a permission slip with our letterhead on it. Second, when I listen to people on the committee I notice they want different things and won't say which: some want him to change, some want the association to say out loud that this is unacceptable, some want us to stop being complicit by continuing to honor him, and some want the student's standing put back — which is a different job entirely and might be one she doesn't want us doing.

So: write the opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Give me a concrete recommendation with a time frame — strip the fellowship, cancel the keynote, a statement, nothing, something I haven't thought of — plus the reasoning, and make it reasoning the other side could actually push back on rather than just restating that cruelty is bad. I especially want you to settle whether what we'd be doing counts as punishment, and whether that changes what we owe him procedurally before we do it. And be fair to the case for doing nothing: we're a professional body, not a court, and we can't control the thing that did the damage. No reading list obviously, just the argument and a bottom line I can defend at the table.
