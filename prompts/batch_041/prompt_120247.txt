I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy. What follows is not a request for a summary of what philosophers have written about custom and obligation — I want the arguments in it audited on their merits. It reminded me of watching two engineers argue over whether an undocumented convention in a shared codebase binds a new contributor. I've changed the names, and the town is small and obscure enough that there's nothing useful written about it, so please work only from what I describe.

Background. On a work trip last spring I spent a week in a coastal town where the fishing cooperative sells the day's catch at a single morning market. When the catch is gone the stalls close, and people who arrived early can still be turned away if enough of the catch went to the people ahead of them. The queue there has a feature I'd never seen: a place in line is treated as belonging to a household rather than to a body. One person — often an older relative who wants only a single fish — will stand in line for an hour while the rest of the household is elsewhere, and when their turn comes the whole group materializes and is served together. Locals regard this as ordinary courtesy; a place is understood to be a claim that can be handed to relatives, not a property of the person standing in it. There is no rule against it and nobody grumbles.

The incident. On the fourth morning, Omar — a colleague — was in line with about a dozen people ahead of him when an elderly woman near the front gestured for six members of her family to join her. Omar objected, loudly enough that the stallholder intervened, and refused to let them in ahead of him. The family was served after him; as it happened he got the second-to-last fish and they got the last one, so nobody went without, though it could easily have gone the other way. The woman called him rude, and a man behind him said something in the local language that a bystander translated, roughly, as: a guest eats last but does not eat first. Lena, another colleague, thought Omar was in the wrong, and they argued about it for the rest of the trip. I've reproduced the argument as faithfully as I can.

1. Omar: A queue is just a device for turning arrival order into service order. If places are transferable, the device stops doing that, because anyone can park a relative at the front and stroll in later. The woman wasn't buying anything for herself; she was a placeholder, and a placeholder isn't a customer.

2. Lena: In this town the place belongs to the household, not to the body in it. Everyone knows this and plans around it, including the stallholder. Omar didn't lose a position he was entitled to, because he was never entitled to be next — he was entitled to be served in the order the town's rules produce, and those are the rules of the line he chose to stand in.

3. Omar: Watching a rule operate for a week isn't agreeing to it. If a shop posts a sign saying customers must clap for the owner, I'm not bound just because it's posted and everyone claps. And the reason the cost is invisible to Lena is that she isn't paying it: those six waits didn't disappear, they moved onto me and everyone behind me.

4. Lena: They didn't move onto you; they rotated. Everyone behind you is playing by the same rule and will send someone ahead themselves, so over a season the burden evens out. That you personally can't cash in this week doesn't make the rule unfair. It makes you a visitor.

5. Omar: Then call it what it is: a rule whose payouts require a network I don't have. Formally anyone can hold a place; in practice you need someone willing to stand for you for an hour, and I know nobody here. It's a transfer from people who are passing through to people who live here, and it gets steeper with household size — a family of ten takes one place and costs nine waits. And this isn't a museum queue. The catch is finite; being behind six people is the difference between dinner and no dinner.

6. Lena: By that standard every local advantage is an injustice — knowing which stall is freshest, speaking the language. Visitors are always worse off at someone else's game; that can't be the test, or the only fair outcome is that nobody has customs, which just means yours win by default. And you had an exit: there are three stalls, and the walk is five minutes.

7. Omar: I checked. The other two had closed by then; that's why I was in that line at all. And "you could have said yes" cuts both ways — she could have accepted that I'd been standing there. If a rule only holds as long as everyone quietly consents, it isn't doing the work you say it's doing.

8. Lena: I never said it was self-evident. I said you're a guest, the rules aren't yours, and the burden of proof runs the other way. If a place could only be held by a man for a man, or if one household were starving while others feasted, I'd be on your side. This is an old woman buying fish for her grandchildren.

Addendum. The night before, the same two had a shorter version of this argument. The restaurant bill came with service already included, and the local habit is to leave nothing extra; a tip, I was told, reads either as an accusation that the host underpaid or as showing off. Omar left twenty percent anyway. Lena said he'd insulted the owner in the owner's own house; Omar said a tip is a gift, and gifts don't insult.

What I want. Please adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — I'd like both parties to get credit where they've earned it, because I thought each of them won a round or two. Then tell me what neither of them has said that matters most. Then give an overall verdict on whether Omar was obligated to let the family in. Finally, tell me whether the tipping addendum should come out the same way as the queue or differently, and whether your answer to the queue would change if the holding rule were restricted to men. I'm not asking for reading; I'm asking for the audit.
