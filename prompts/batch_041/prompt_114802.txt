I'm a philosophy graduate student who has been sitting in on a computational biology lab meeting, and I keep hearing the sentence "DNA is a code" deployed as if it settled arguments instead of starting them. In one meeting a student used it to argue that a noncoding regulatory sequence carries information about when a gene should turn on; ten minutes later someone used the same sentence, via the redundancy of the standard genetic code, to argue that most single-letter mutations are "silent" and therefore carry no meaning at all; and at the end of the meeting someone pointed to the patterns of chemical marks on the proteins that package DNA and called them a second code written on top of the first. I left unable to tell whether these people agreed with each other, disagreed, or were simply attaching one phrase to three unrelated things.

Please separate out the distinct precise claims that "DNA is a code" could be making, ordered from the most trivial to the most ambitious. For each reading, I want to know exactly what it commits you to: what would have to be true of DNA itself, and of whatever does the decoding, for the claim to amount to more than a metaphor, and whether it requires a sender, a receiver, a shared convention, arbitrariness between symbol and referent, compositionality, or something like aboutness or semantic content. Where a reading demands one of these and DNA or the cell does not obviously supply it, say so. Then tell me which readings are platitudes dressed up as discoveries, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or incoherent; and for each of the three uses in that meeting, say which reading, if any, would actually license the conclusion that was drawn from it.

I would rather have verdicts than a survey, so please commit: where you think a reading collapses into a truism or into a claim no one could deny, say that plainly, and where you think the slogan is doing real work, say which reading you would defend and give your reasons. If you think the most defensible reading is one none of the three speakers had in mind, say that too.

Write in American English.
