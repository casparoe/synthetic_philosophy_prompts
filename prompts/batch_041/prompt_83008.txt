I'm giving a talk to our departments social choice reading group next week and I need a Q&A prep sheet. Here's the thesis I'll be defending: Sen's liberal paradox isn't really a clash between liberty and Pareto, it's an individuation problem. It only bites because we fix the alternative set at one grain (who ends up doing what) and then read a personal sphere for each person off that coarse description; let the description of outcomes get fine enough to include what the acts mean to the people involved, and minimal liberalism stops colliding with weak Pareto — but only because the finer description makes Pareto either vacuous or question-begging. So the paradox isn't dissolved, it's relocated: an individuation dilemma rather than a rights-vs-welfare tradeoff, and I want to defend that as the actual lesson of the thing.

The room is mostly economists from the theory/mechanism-design group, who think the paradox is a toy and will tell me it evaporates once you model rights as moves in a game instead of constraints on outcomes; plus a legal philosopher who thinks decisiveness is a hopeless stand-in for a right; plus one Kantian who thinks any preference-based framing has already conceded too much. Give me the objections I'll actually get from these people, in there vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. And the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
