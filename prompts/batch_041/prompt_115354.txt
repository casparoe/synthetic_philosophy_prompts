I am a novelist, some two-thirds of the way through a book whose spine is a trial, and I have run into a philosophical objection that I would like to settle before I begin revising. A friend who teaches epistemology read the middle chapters and told me that what I am dramatizing — a proceeding in which the same evidence is weighed under one standard and then, in a later and differently constituted proceeding, weighed again under a looser one, so that the same person is acquitted of a killing in one courtroom and held answerable for the same death in the next — is not a curiosity of the law but a picture of the ordinary condition of knowledge. His formulation, which he offered as an argument and not as a flourish, was that what counts as knowledge is like what counts as proof: there is no answer to the question whether a character knew something that floats free of the standard then in force, and the standard is fixed by what is at stake. From this he draws a conclusion he would like me to endorse in the novel's last movement — that there is no fact of the matter about whether my characters knew, that the standard is ours to choose, and that my narrator should stop pretending to adjudicate.

I would like that analogy audited rather than applauded. Please give me, first, an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the part of the standard of proof, of the fact-finder, of the fact proved, of the stakes that raise or lower the standard, of the authority that fixes it, and of the verdict's finality. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be disciplined about which breaks matter for the use my friend is making of it and which are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in three questions. First, does the legal analogy in fact support the view that the verb shifts with the conversational context, or the view that the stakes enter the knowledge relation itself? Second, the standard of proof governs when an institution may act on the evidence, not what anyone knows; does that make the analogy evidence for a pragmatist thesis about action rather than for either of the epistemological views it is being used to promote? Third, and most important to me: does my friend's conclusion survive? My own untutored impression is that the law is precisely the case in which the standard varies and the fact does not — two verdicts, one event — and that the analogy therefore cuts against him rather than for him, but I do not trust my impression.

When I pressed him, he retreated to a linguistic version of the same point: that the verb behaves like words such as flat, tall, and empty, which are true or false only relative to a standard, and that this is enough to show the standard lives in the language rather than in the world. I gather this comparison has been attacked on the grounds that knowing is not gradable in the way those adjectives are and has no borderline cases, and that the same adjectives were once used to argue the opposite conclusion — that flat and empty apply to nothing, and that knowledge, being equally absolute, applies to nothing either. Say whether that fallback analogy carries more or less than the first, and, if neither is adequate, what a better analogy would be.

Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of. End with a single final line of the form \answer{LABEL; reason in no more than fifteen words}, where LABEL is one of SURVIVES, SURVIVES-ONLY-IN-WEAKENED-FORM, or DOES-NOT-SURVIVE, and where the reason states, in that compass, whether the trial analogy can carry my friend's conclusion.
