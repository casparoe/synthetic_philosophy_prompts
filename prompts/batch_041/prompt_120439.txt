My decision theory reading group has been chewing on Death in Damascus for about a month now and my friend Priya is convinced she can show that causal decision theory eats itself. Here's the case first, then her argument, then what I can't untangle.

The case: you're in Damascus. Death turns up and tells you he's coming for you tomorrow. You know two things about him — he never lies, and his information is perfect, so where he'll be tomorrow is fixed in advance by where he knows you'll be. Stay in Damascus and he's in Damascus; run to Aleppo and he's in Aleppo. You want to survive. Those are your only two options.

Her argument:

1. Whatever you do, Death is where you are: stay and he's in Damascus, run and he's in Aleppo.
2. So your survival probability is zero on either option, and the two options have identical expected utility.
3. When two options have equal expected utility, a rational agent is permitted to take either.
4. So there's a rational thing to do (either one) and the problem is solved. So far, this is just what CDT says.

Then the turn:

5. Now suppose you're in the middle of settling on staying. The fact that you, a rational agent, are about to stay is itself evidence about what Death knows, and since his information is perfect, it's conclusive evidence that he's in Damascus.
6. If he's in Damascus, then staying is the thing that kills you and running is the thing that saves you. So given that you're staying, you should run.
7. Symmetrically, given that you're running, you should stay.
8. So whatever you're about to do, you have conclusive reason to do the other thing instead. Deliberation has no fixed point.
9. A decision theory that can't return a stable answer on a fully specified problem — one that tells you to switch no matter where you're standing — isn't a theory of choice, it's a machine for paralysis.
10. CDT licenses 1-4. So CDT is refuted, and the fix is to stop treating your own choice as evidence and just commit to a policy up front.

She also says you can't wriggle out by randomizing: pick a 50/50 coin, and the moment you know the coin is going to come up stay, that's evidence Death is in Damascus, so you should switch — no mix is stable either.

My problem is I can't find the seam. If I really am about to stay, isn't that conclusive evidence that Death is in Damascus? That seems flatly true. But if its true, then 2 was already wrong, except 2 also seems obviously right, and I keep going around in circles. And here's the other thing that nags me: nothing in 5-8 mentions causality at all. It only uses the setup in 1. So if the argument is good, it seems to sink evidential decision theory too, and honestly anything that accepts 1 — including the people who'd say just flip a coin and stop agonizing. Which makes me suspect either the argument is broken or Priya has stumbled onto something alot bigger than a refutation of CDT, and I can't tell which it is.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd most like to keep 1 — I think the setup is exactly right and I don't want to weasel out by insisting Death can't really be a perfect predictor. I also want to keep the thought in 9 that a theory should be able to hand me an answer I can act on. I'll sacrifice 3 or 5 if I have to, but tell me which, and tell me what I'm buying by doing it. And if it turns out there's no flaw and something genuinely has to break, say so plainly.
