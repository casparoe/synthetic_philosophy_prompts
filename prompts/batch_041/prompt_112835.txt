I'm a freelance journalist, I mostly do long reads on science and ideas — philosophy, ethics, that sort of thing, for the weekend supplements — and at the moment I'm working on a piece about what actually goes on in a sentencer's head. The peg is that everyone argues about punishment in the abstract, desert versus deterrence versus rehabilitation, and nobody ever watches it being done. So I've spent a few weeks sitting in on a circuit judge's list here in England, and she's let me read back through her sentancing remarks from the last couple of years. Which has been great, except that I now have a question I can't answer and a deadline. Here's the pattern I've got. (1) A man who glassed a stranger in a pub queue with no provocation at all, the victim needed surgery to his face, he had two previous convictions for violence, denied it from start to finish and the pre-sentence report put him at high risk — six years. (2) A man stopped with a flick knife in his glovebox, no threat to anyone, nothing on his record, he'd bought it after being mugged twice on his own street, the report said the risk was minimal — a community order with unpaid work. (3) A builder who over three years took the savings of four elderly customers and spent the lot, denied everything, blamed the customers for being gullible, previous convictions for dishonesty, assessed as high risk — four years. (4) A man who'd put his partner through months of violence, broke her arm, lasting psychological damage, minimised the lot in interview but did the programme while on remand, assessed as medium risk — three years. When I asked her outright what her theory of punishment was she laughed, said it was all in the guidelines, and changed the subject, which wasn't much help to me.

The one I'm stuck on is Thursday morning. A 63-year-old woman, retired school business manager, no previous convictions of any kind, pleading guilty to taking about £280,000 over nine years out of the fund that pays for school trips for kids on free school meals. It was a gambling addiction she's since been treated for, and the pre-sentence report puts the risk of her doing anything like it again at low, with the prosecution not pushing back on that. The money's gone, two of the trips had to be cancelled, she's lost the job and her husband's left, and the local paper has already had her name and photo. I genuinely can't tell whether she's looking at a suspended sentence or four years, and the piece changes shape depending on which it is.

So: what rule or rules is she plausibly running on the basis of those four? Be straight with me about how many different ones would fit equally well. Then take each of them and tell me what it predicts for Thursday, and give me your best guess with the reasons — you've not got the guidelines in front of you and I'm not going to quote you, so go on the pattern alone. Tell me which of the four is the closest precedent and whether that precedent actually settles Thursday or only looks like it does. And if her record genuinely doesn't pin her down, tell me what sort of case I should be hoping lands in her list before I file — what offence, or what sort of offender, would tell the rules apart — because if I can catch one of those I can write the piece properly.
