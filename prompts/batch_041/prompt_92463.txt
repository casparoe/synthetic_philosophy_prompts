I'm a retired microbiologist who has been reading philosophy for a few years, and I'm trying to write down where I stand on the what-is-life question so a friend can tear it appart. Here's the pile:

1. 'Alive' is not a natural kind with necessary and sufficient conditions. It's a cluster concept, family resemblance, maybe a homeostatic property cluster if that's the right term. No single essence.
2. But there are still better and worse answers. When I say a prion isn't alive and a tardigrade is, I mean to be saying something true, not just expressing a preference.
3. Life requires metabolism in a broad sense: a self-maintaining chemical system that does work to stay far from equilibrium. This is why viruses aren't alive even though they have genomes and evolve. They're borrowed life, maybe, but not life.
4. Life requires evolution by natural selection. If a system can't reproduce with variation and be selected, it isn't alive. That's what separates a growing crystal from a bacterium, and a computer virus from a biological one.
5. Sterile organisms are alive. A mule is alive. A worker bee is alive. A person who is infertile is alive. So whatever the definition is, it can't require the individual to actually reproduce.
6. A single self-replicating RNA molecule in a test tube, if it really does replicate and mutate and get selected, is alive. Maybe minimally, but alive. A synthetic cell with a minimal genome is alive too.
7. A virus outside a host isn't alive, but a virus-infected cell is alive, and the virus inside it is doing something that looks alot like being alive. I don't think there's a sharp line between the virus and the host's life processes.
8. I don't think there's any sharp line between life and non-life in general. It's a matter of degree, with lots of borderline cases.
9. But I also think 'life' is a real category we didn't invent, not just a word we use for convenience. There's something in the world that makes the paradigm cases paradigm cases.
10. And I think the origin of life was a gradual process: there was no first organism. At some point there were things that were clearly alive, before that things that were clearly not, and in between things for which the question has no determinate answer.

Now, I know some of these are probably pulling against each other, but I definately can't tell which are real conflicts and which are just the usual philosophy-seminar noise. Can you audit this? I'd like the pairs that are genuinely in tension, ordered by how bad the tension is; the pairs that only look like tensions but dissolve once you say what the terms are doing; for each real tension, what I'd have to give up or modify, and what the cheapest version of that fix would cost me elsewhere; and which single revision would clean up the most without making me abandon the stuff I care most about. Also, please don't just tell me it's all family resemblance and leave it. I want to know whether the package has a consistent core or whether I'm just using 'life' in two or three different senses without noticing.
