A recurring move in computer science and adjacent writing runs like this: someone shows that a new substrate — a cellular automaton, a molecular computing scheme, a family of quantum circuits — can simulate a Turing machine; the result is announced as settling that substrate's computational power; and the moral drawn is that nothing new is computable, only that some things become faster. The same textbooks that make this move also insist that the Church–Turing thesis is not a theorem, that it admits of no proof, and that it concerns a pre-formal notion of effective computability. Meanwhile complexity theory defines feasible computation as polynomial time on a Turing machine, then treats the resulting class as a robust, model-independent feature of the world rather than an artefact of one idealised machine.

What account of the thesis's epistemic status does this cluster of practices presuppose — that it is a stipulative definition of 'computable', an empirical hypothesis about human or physical computation, or a theorem about some formal notion that happens to track a pre-formal one? Reconstruct that implicit account, say which of its rivals the practices quietly rule out, and separate what they strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with. Are the parts mutually consistent — in particular, can one both deny that the thesis is provable and invoke it to close off hypercomputation and claims about what physical systems could in principle compute? Finally, say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change — in what a universality result may be taken to establish, and in how feasibility is treated — if one adopted a straightforwardly stipulative reading of the thesis instead.
