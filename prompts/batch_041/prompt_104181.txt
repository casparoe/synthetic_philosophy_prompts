Right, so I need help diagnosing something thats been nagging me for years. I run a phil of math seminar and every single year we hit Benacerraf's dilemma and every single year the room splits into the platonists and the nominalists/structuralists/fictionalists and every single year we end up exactly where we started. It's not for lack of sophistication — everyone knows the access problem, everyone knows the "oh that's just a semantic point" comeback, everyone knows the "no, the semantics is fine, it's the epistemology that bites" counter-comback — and yet the thing won't budge. So my question isn't who's right. It's: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a genuine substantive disagreement about the facts — are there abstract objects, do we stand in any relation to them that could ground knowledge of them — or is it a verbal dispute about the word "exists" or about what we mean by "know"? Or are the two camps quietly answering different questions under one label, one about the truth-conditions of mathematical sentences and one about how mathematical belief could ever be justified, and just talking past each other? Here's my pet hypothesis, and I'd like you to either back it or kill it: I suspect the whole dilemma trades on running together two senses of "access" — access as being acquainted with the thing, having it before the mind, versus access as being entitled to it, being justified in using it in explanation. Once you split those, the horns stop looking like a dilemma at all. Is that the real fault line, or is something else doing the work? And whichever it turns out to be, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or whether we should just accept that no progress is coming and say why. Be concrete about what each side is actually committed to; don't just hand me a survey of the positions.
