You are a philosopher specializing in metaphysics, with a particular focus on mereology, material constitution, and the metaphysics of ordinary objects. I am a graduate student putting together a research proposal for a semester-long paper, and I want your help turning a half-formed idea into a rigorous, well-scoped plan. Work through the issues carefully before you write, and begin your response with a short brainstorming section in which you lay out two or three different ways the project could be framed, with a note on the costs and payoffs of each. Only after that should you produce the proposal itself.

Here is the background, so that you can see what I am after even if you would frame it differently.

There is a longstanding question about when some things compose a further thing: under what conditions do several objects together make up a single object? The familiar answers are (i) never, so that there are no composite objects at all and what we call tables and organisms are just pluralities of simples; (ii) always, so that any plurality whatsoever composes something; and (iii) only when the plurality is unified in some special way, for instance by a kind of life or by a causal/explanatory relation among the parts. Each of these has well-known costs, and I do not want to relitigate them.

My half-formed idea is this. The whole debate has been conducted as though "compose" names a single relation with a single extension, and I suspect the question only looks so hard because we keep demanding one answer for everything. My hunch is that composition is domain-relative: the conditions under which some things compose a further thing differ depending on whether we are asking a physical, a biological, or a social/legal question, so that each of the standard answers may come out right for some domains and wrong for others. The genuinely interesting question, on this view, is not which of the three is correct but how the domains relate to one another and whether one of them is basic.

But there is a puzzle I cannot get past, and it is the part I most want the project to confront. Suppose a plurality composes an object under one domain's conditions but not another's. It looks as though we then have two material objects occupying exactly the same region at the same time, differing only in the domain under which they are counted as objects. That is precisely the kind of coincidence the domain-relative move was supposed to help us avoid, so either the move collapses back into a single answer after all, or it forces a real revision in what we are willing to call an object — perhaps in the direction of treating "object" as itself domain-relative. I do not know which way this goes, and I would like the proposal to be honest that this is the crux.

Now write the proposal. It should contain, clearly separated:

1. A precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has a determinate answer and so that it is clear what evidence or argument would count for or against each possible answer.
2. An account of why the existing positions leave the question open — that is, what each of the standard answers presupposes, and why none of them by itself settles whether composition is domain-relative.
3. The method: what I would actually do, broken down month by month across a semester, including which sub-questions I would take in which order and how I would know when a sub-question is answered.
4. What a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing rather than merely being hard.
5. An honest assessment of scope, including whether the project as described is too large for a single semester and, if so, exactly what should be cut and what the reduced version would still establish.
