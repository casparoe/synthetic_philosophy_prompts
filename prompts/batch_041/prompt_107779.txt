I am three weeks into a metaphysics module on modality, and I have been trying to get a grip on the counterpart-theoretic account of de re modality as against the older picture on which a single individual is straightforwardly identical with an inhabitant of more than one possible world. We have also been told that the counterpart relation need not be one-one, transitive, or symmetric, and that which resemblance relation is in play can shift with the conversational context — which has only made it harder for me to see what the theory pins down. I built the following argument for a seminar and now find it quietly alarming, because I cannot locate the mistake and the conclusion is one I am unwilling to accept. (1) On the counterpart-theoretic account, a de re modal claim such as "I could have been a musician" is made true not by my being a musician in some other world, but by the existence, in some other world, of an individual who is a counterpart of me — one who resembles me closely enough — and who is a musician. (2) That counterpart is numerically distinct from me: resemblance is not identity, and the theory was introduced precisely so that I need not be literally present in more than one world. (3) So whatever is true of the counterpart is true of him and not of me; when my counterpart is a musician, I am not a musician but at most the individual who stands in a certain resemblance relation to a musician. (4) It follows that no modal predicate is ever true of me — it is never true of me that I could have been a musician, only that someone sufficiently like me is a musician elsewhere. (5) If that is right, then the "I" in "I could have been a musician" contributes no modal content of its own: the modal content belongs wholly to the other individual, and my contribution reduces to that of a mere reference point from which a resemblance relation is measured. (6) But this cannot be right. Some things are surely true of me about what I could have been — that I could have been a musician, and equally that I might never have been born — and I had understood the point of de re modality to be precisely to capture such facts about a particular individual. (7) So counterpart theory is either false, or it covertly replaces claims about what I could have been with claims about other people.

Each step looks to me as though it follows from the one before, and yet the argument must fail somewhere; I would rather not give up (1), which is the whole substance of the theory, or (6), which strikes me as the datum any account of de re modality has to respect. Please tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked so plausible, and then set out what the rival, transworld-identity picture would say about the same steps — and what each side has to pay for the treatment it gives them. I am less interested in being told that this resembles a well-known objection than in seeing exactly where the reasoning slips and what the alternatives cost.
