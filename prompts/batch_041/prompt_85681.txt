I run a 300-level seminar on knowledge and skill — 16 students, one 90-minute block — and I want a single in-class activity that makes them feel the trouble with knowing how, rather than hearing the usual Ryle-then-the-intellectualists summary. Two things I care about: the regress has to be felt rather than asserted, and by the end they should be genuinely unsure whether what they picked up during the activity counts as knowledge at all. Heres what I've been sketching. Make it runnable, or tell me where it breaks and give me something better.

The sketch: about 20 minutes where everyone gets a shuffled deck of ~50 cards. Each card has a simple line figure on the front — a little glyph with a stem and a crossbar, stem at a random angle, crossbar a random length, plus one or two other features that vary randomly and mean nothing. Each card is secretly type A or type B, and the rule is a ratio between the two features, so pick a threshold that's easy to feel but a pain to state exactly (A iff the stem is more than roughly 1.7 times the crossbar, or something like that). They sort card by card: guess, then flip to check, since the answer is printed on the back. Alone, no notes, no talking. By the end most are in the 85-95% range. Then they write down in one sentence the rule they were using. Then in pairs, one reads their sentence out loud and the other — who has never touched a deck — sorts 12 fresh cards using only that sentence, while the first person watches and says nothing. Score the pair. Swap. Usually the written rules are either wrong or too vague to apply, and the proxy sorts near chance.

Second phase, and this is the part I most want your help on: I want their skill pulled out from under them. The training deck carries an incidental cue — say every type A card is on slightly creamier stock, or the deck is stacked so A's and B's arrive in runs. The test deck keeps the real ratio rule intact but scrambles the incidental cue. Whoever was tracking the cue falls apart. I want that to raise the luck and defeater question for know-how: did they ever know how, if their reliability rested on something they never noticed and that was about to vanish?

Third bit, short, to stage the regress. Hand out an instruction card for a trivial sorting task where the instructions are nested: to do X, first determine which instruction applies; to determine which instruction applies, consult instruction (ii); (ii) tells you to run the same procedure one level down; and the chain bottoms out at the line that just says use your judgment. They write one line on where the chain stopped and what stopped it.

Then a debrief, which is what I'm least sure how to structure. It should map specific moments in the activity onto: Ryle's regress; the intellectualist reply that the knowledge was propositional all along and that what gets lost when you say it out loud is the mode of presentation, not the content; and the third possibility that what they acquired is perceptual learning, which may be neither knowing how nor knowing that. Keep Ryle's regress and the rule-following regress apart in the debrief — if they run together the moral comes out wrong and I'd rather not have to walk it back. They shouldn't hear any of this vocabulary until after they've been through the activity.

Give me the materials list, the exact timing, the rules for each phase, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, the debrief beats, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and a straight answer about what the activity can't show — I already suspect it can't touch the intellectualist, since she can redescribe everything they did as propositional knowledge they simply couldn't articulate, but tell me if that's right and whether the luck phase buys me anything she can't absorb. No reading, no citations, and it has to work with nothing but cards, a timer, and a whiteboard.
