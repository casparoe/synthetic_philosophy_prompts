Regulators and professional bodies increasingly insist that consequential automated decisions — credit limits, hiring screens, medical triage — keep a human in the loop and give the affected person a right to an explanation, even when the model in question is one no human can fully retrace. The same institutions rely without hesitation on other opaque instruments: no one demands that a loan officer reproduce the assay, the weather model, or the compiler, and no one treats reliance on those as a failure to understand. I want to know what this selective demand presupposes about understanding and about responsibility. Does it assume that a decision counts as understood only when someone could have reached it by a humanly followable route; that responsibility requires an agent who knew what they were doing and could have done otherwise; or merely that some human be positioned to certify or veto the output? And can the practice survive the fact that the humans it stations in the loop usually cannot do the task unaided, so that any understanding they have is second-hand at best? Tell me what the practice strictly requires, as opposed to what it merely fits well with, whether its parts presuppose consistent things, and what would have to change if we dropped the comprehension requirement for a rival rule — say, that the system be auditable and the human able to veto, but not to explain.
