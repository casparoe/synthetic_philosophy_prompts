I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've written a short essay that I'd like a critical read of. It's below. I'd rather have the argument attacked than praised.

Draft: Lock-in is about the shape of the landscape, not the sequence of events

I keep running into arguments shaped like this: some standard or convention or technology is the way it is only because of an accident early on; therefore it is arbitrary; therefore it is probably bad and we are stuck with it. I want to argue that this inference is broken, and that once you pull it apart, most of what is interesting about path dependence survives, but in a different form than people usually state it.

I'll use the keyboard layout as my running example, but I want to bracket whether that particular story is even true. My argument is about the structure of the reasoning, so if the keyboard case turns out to be a myth, I'd want the argument to be unaffected.

Claim 1: history matters is three separate claims. (a) Sensitivity: the outcome depends on early conditions, so a different early accident gives a different outcome. (b) Contingency: the outcome is not fixed by the underlying fundamentals, because the forces that eventually push toward a solution were not strong enough at the time to determine which solution won. (c) Inefficiency: the outcome is worse than an alternative that was available. Nearly every lock-in argument I have seen uses (a) or (b) as its evidence and (c) as its conclusion. But (a) and (b) don't get you (c). Something can be exquisitely sensitive to early conditions and still land on the best available option every single time, and something can be inefficient without being contingent at all, if the alternatives were simply worse.

Claim 2: what actually produces lock-in is increasing returns to adoption, and increasing returns gets you (a) and (b) but not (c). If each additional adopter makes the thing more valuable to the next adopter, through network effects, learning curves, complementary tools, or a pool of trained users, then the process has multiple stable outcomes and which one you land in depends on early noise. That is a claim about the shape of the payoff landscape: several basins, and a process that cannot climb out of whichever one it falls into. But a landscape with several basins does not tell you the basins are equal in height. Lock-in, properly stated, is a claim about the shape of the landscape; it is not a claim about the quality of the outcome. People slide between those two constantly, and that slide is where I think most of the confusion lives.

Claim 3: the question of whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway is usually asked in a form that cannot be answered. The tempting test is to perturb the initial conditions slightly and see whether the process converges to the same place regardless. But that only probes how deep the basin is. It tells you whether the lock-in was robust, not whether the locked-in outcome was good. A second tempting test is to ask what a well-informed designer would have picked. But that substitutes a different question, namely what is best from a position of full information, for the question of what was reachable from where the process actually was.

The harder version of the question, and the one I think is real, is this: was there a feasible trajectory from the actual early state to a better outcome, one that did not require anyone at the time to know more than they could have known? That is a much stricter test than people apply, and most lock-in stories quietly skip it by comparing the actual outcome to an alternative that exists only in the counterfactual where you change the early accident and hold everything downstream fixed. That is not a real alternative. The other standard would have had its own learning curve, its own complementary tools, its own trained users, its own broken corners. Comparing the actual standard minus the accident to the rival standard with everything else held equal is comparing a real thing to an imaginary one.

So the version of path dependence I would defend is narrower and, I think, more useful. History is locked in to the extent that exit is expensive: to the extent that the current arrangement has accumulated specific, non-transferable investments, in skills, tools, standards, contracts, that would have to be written off in order to switch. That is a claim you can operationalize, and it does not require you to be able to rank the two worlds against each other. It also explains why lock-in arguments tend to be right about how hard change is and wrong about how bad the status quo is.

Finally, an analogy I keep reaching for and do not trust: in statistical mechanics an ergodic system is one whose time average converges to its ensemble average, so the system forgets where it started. Path dependence looks like non-ergodicity, in that history does not wash out and the actual trajectory comes apart from the distribution over trajectories. Is that a real connection or am I pattern-matching on the word history?

What I want comments on:

1. Is the three-way split in Claim 1 the right decomposition of what people mean by path dependence, or does it leave out a fourth thing that the term is doing?

2. Is increasing returns to adoption really the only engine of lock-in, or are there mechanisms that produce lock-in without it, for example pure switching costs, coordination problems, or something about the way standards get codified into institutions? If there are such mechanisms, does my landscape framing survive them, or does it need to be rebuilt?

3. Is the imaginary-alternative objection in Claim 3 a genuine argument or a dodge? My worry is that it proves too much: if you can never compare an actual outcome to a non-imaginary alternative, then nothing can ever be called inefficient, and that seems clearly wrong. Where is the line between a fair counterfactual and an imaginary one?

4. Is the exit-cost reframing in the penultimate paragraph a real narrowing of the concept, or did I just define the interesting part away?

5. I have a vague sense that there is a term of art, hysteresis, used for cases where the current state depends on the history of the input rather than only on its current level. If that is the right neighborhood, tell me whether what I am calling lock-in is the same thing, a special case, or a different animal.

6. Should the ergodicity analogy stay or be cut?

7. Tell me which part of this is strongest and which is weakest. I care much more about the weakest part.
