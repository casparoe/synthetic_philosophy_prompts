I teach the philosophy-of-biology unit on the definition of life, and I am now grading the midterm. I would like you to grade one student's answer. I need a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, because I have to justify the grade if the student appeals.

The exam question was:

"Two broad families of criteria for life have been proposed. The first makes self-maintenance central: a living thing is a chemical system that sustains its own organized structure far from thermodynamic equilibrium. The second makes evolution central: a living thing is a chemical system with the capacity to undergo Darwinian evolution, that is, replication with heritable variation under selection. (i) State as precisely as you can what distinguishes these two families, and identify at least one thing that each treats as necessary and the other does not. (ii) Apply each family to (a) a virus and (b) the following case: an enclosed droplet of reacting molecules that holds its internal composition far from equilibrium by means of a self-restoring cycle of reactions, but that reproduces only when mechanical shear tears the droplet apart, the fragments then rebuilding chemically identical droplets, with no heritable variation from one generation to the next. (iii) Many cases — viruses, prions, sterile hybrids, laboratory protocells — fall near the boundary. Does the existence of such cases support the conclusion that 'life' is not a natural kind? Take a position and defend it."

The student's answer:

"The two families differ in what they take to be the mark of life. The self-maintenance family requires a system to keep itself going — to hold its internal state far from equilibrium by its own reactions. The evolution family requires only that a system be able to replicate with variation and be subject to selection. So the self-maintenance family treats ongoing metabolic self-organization as necessary, while the evolution family does not; the evolution family treats heritable variation as necessary, while the self-maintenance family does not.

Applied to a virus: under the self-maintenance criterion a virus is not alive, since it has no metabolism of its own and depends entirely on the host cell's machinery; under the evolution criterion it is alive, since it replicates and its replication is subject to selection. This is the standard reason viruses are called borderline.

Applied to the enclosed droplet: it fails the evolution criterion, because it does not vary between generations and so cannot evolve, and it therefore is not alive under either criterion. Its self-restoring cycle keeps it running, but since nothing is inherited and nothing is selected, calling it alive would stretch the word past usefulness.

A difficulty for the self-maintenance family is that nothing is truly self-sustaining: every organism takes in matter and energy from outside, so if self-sustaining meant needing nothing external, no organism would qualify, and the criterion would be empty.

On the last question: the proliferation of borderline cases — viruses, prions, sterile hybrids, protocells — shows that there is no sharp line between the living and the non-living, and I think it shows that 'life' is not a natural kind. A natural kind would have a boundary fixed by nature; since the boundary here keeps moving as we invent new cases and as our purposes change, it is really a matter of decision where we draw it. Different projects (medicine, astrobiology, ethics) can draw it differently, and there is no fact of the matter to settle which is right."
