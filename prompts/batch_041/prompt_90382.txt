Ok here's one I keep chewing on and can't settle. Kierkegaard's whole thing about the press and the public — the phantom public, leveling, reflection replacing action, everybody chattering and nobody answerable — was aimed at newspapers and the Copenhagen gossip sheets back in 1846. I want to know what he'd say about the internet, and I want it argued out of what he actually claimed about the press and the public, not just a vibe.

Five things, and for each I'd like a verdict plus reasons, and a note on which of his own moves (leveling, the public, chatter, reflection-without-action, the single individual) is doing the work:

1. Anonymity that isn't anonymous. He complained alot that the press let people speak without being anyone in particular. But now every anon has a persistent handle, a follower count, a history. Is that the same facelessness he was mad about, or has something changed, since the anonymity now comes with a quantified reputation welded to it?

2. Public metrics of approval — likes, upvotes, view counts. Does the scoreboard do the leveling for him, or does it just make visible a leveling that was already happening? I care about this becuase his leveling sounded like something nobody does on purpose; a like is something someone deliberately gives.

3. Parasocial stuff: millions of people who feel they know a person who has no idea they exist. Is that the public one step further, or a genuinely new thing — a fake single individual rather than a crowd? He was obsessed with the single individual; what happens when the object of mass attention is a person instead of a phantom?

4. The pile-on / so-called cancellation. A mass that punishes, with no one in particular doing the punishing and no one who can be held to it. Does his account of the crowd predict this, or is there something about the feedback loop he had no analogue for?

5. A small paid newsletter — one person, signing their name, writing to a few hundred subscribers who pay to read them. On the face of it that's exactly the single-individual-speaking-to-individuals thing he wanted. My worry is that it's still the same disease, the writer now performing sincerity for a market. If you think he'd bless this one, say why; if you think he'd call it the worst version yet, say what distinguishes it from what he wanted.

And the general question underneath: was his critique of the press really about the medium, or about a structural thing — mass-mediated reflection, second-hand opinion, everyone knowing everything and committing to nothing — that would just reappear in whatever technology comes along? If it's structural, say what the modern version looks like and whether he'd think anything is actually worse now rather than just faster. If it's the medium, say what about print specifically was doing the damage.

No bibliography obviously, reason from the argument itself. And don't just tell me he'd hate social media, I could guess that. I want where he'd be surprised, and where his own categories start to strain.
