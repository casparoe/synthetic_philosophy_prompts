"The recieved picture of joint action goes like this: for you and me to do something together, each of us has to intend to do our part, each has to intend that the other's intention plays a role in how we act, and all of this has to be out in the open between us. Searle's objection is that this builds the plural out of singular materials — a we-intention isn't a bundle of I-intentions plus beliefs about the other person. Gilbert pushes harder: a joint commitment makes the parties into a plural subject, which isn't just the two of them added up.

I want to say both sides are fighting over the wrong thing, because they both assume the parties have to agree about what they're doing, in the same sense of 'what'. Take two neighbours building a dry stone wall on the boundary. One of them thinks they're building something that'll outlast them both, a monument to not having a boundary dispute. The other thinks it's a temporary fence, intends to pull it down in spring, and is mostly trying to keep the peace. They coordinate beautifully: they agree on the line of the wall, on who lifts and who places, on when to stop for tea. Are they building the wall together? Obviously yes. So whatever shared intention is, it isn't agreement on the point of the activity.

Hence a distinction. Skeleton agreement: the parties converge on the shape of the activity — the sequence of doings, who does which, when it ends. Flesh agreement: the parties converge on what the activity means, why it's worth doing, what it's for. My claim is that joint action needs skeleton agreement and only skeleton agreement; flesh agreement is a bonus, and many of the interesting cases are ones where it's missing or actively disputed (co-parents, band members who can't stand each other, coalition politics, most marriages).

Second move, the one I actually care about. Everyone explains joint action by putting shared intention upstream: first the we-intention, then the doing. But the wall case suggests the arrow often runs the other way. A stable pattern of skeleton agreement generates mutual expectations — I expect you to show up with stones, you expect me not to wander off — and those expectations harden into something that looks exactly like the interlocking intentions people wanted to start with. Call it downstream jointness. Shared intention is often the sediment of joint action rather than its source, which would mean it does alot less explanatory work than anyone wants it to.

Objection I can't shake: if skeleton agreement is just behavioural fit plus expectations, I've redescribed coordination on an equilibrium and dressed it up in intentional language. And maybe flesh agreement is exactly what separates a joint action from two strangers doing the sidewalk shuffle at each other on a narrow pavement — they fit perfectly, and nobody would say they're dancing together. I want to say the difference is that skeleton agreement is mutual and known to be mutual, but I'm not sure that's enough, and I suspect I've either smuggled flesh agreement back in through the back door or just reinvented Bratman with the intentions filed off."
