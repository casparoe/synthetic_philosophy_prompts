People I trust keep telling me that the benefits system is really an insurance scheme, and that once you see it that way the question of who deserves what is settled. On their picture, everyone who works pays in through taxes and everyone who falls on hard times draws on what they have been paying into all along, so a person claiming unemployment or disability benefits is not living off anybody's charity but collecting on a policy they hold. My brother, who sells commercial insurance, calls this sentimental nonsense. Insurance, he says, is a contract whose price is set by the risk you bring, whose terms are fixed in advance, and whose provider cannot quietly rewrite the deal once the claims start to bite; nothing in the benefits system works that way, so the insurance talk is just borrowing the authority of a contract for what is really a political decision about whose money goes to whom.

I would like an audit rather than a slogan. First, set out the correspondence properly: if this is insurance, what plays the role of the insurer, the policyholder, the premium, the payout, the risk being pooled, and the event insured against? Second, tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for the argument my friends are making. I am especially suspicious of the features the analogy seems to borrow without earning them: the actuarial link between what you pay in and what you can claim, the voluntariness of the contract, the enforceability of its terms, and the insurer's freedom to refuse you or load your premium according to the risk you represent. My brother's version of that last point is that a private insurer would decline most of the people the benefits system pays, and would write the rest out of the policy by exclusion. I want to know whether that is right, and whether it is fatal to the analogy or merely shows the state doing what insurers choose not to do.

Third, there is a twist I keep coming back to and cannot settle on my own. My brother concedes that real policies are full of conditions: you must disclose what you know, you must not leave the house unlocked, you must not burn down your own shop, and if you break those terms the insurer need not pay. If conditions and exclusions are ordinary in insurance, then benefits being conditional — you must look for work, attend appointments, accept an offer, report changes in your circumstances — may not be a betrayal of the insurance picture but its natural extension. I want that taken seriously rather than waved away: which conditions in real insurance correspond to the conditions attached to benefits, which do not, and does the analogy end up supporting conditionality rather than undermining it? I also suspect the picture fits some parts of the system far better than others, and I would like you to say which parts, and why that matters.

Finally, if the insurance picture does not hold, tell me what a better analogy would be, and whether the better analogy is kinder or harsher to the people who claim. My underlying question is whether the division between the deserving and the undeserving poor is doing any work that survives these comparisons, or whether it is just a line that gets moved whenever the facts change.
