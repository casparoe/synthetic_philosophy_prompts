Ok so I'm in a seminar on American pragmatism and there's a guy in it -- smart, reads alot, but he has exactly one move. Whenever anyone presses him on whether some claim is actually true, he says truth is just what works and then acts like the question is settled. He did it three times last week and I need ammunition.

Deployment 1: someone asked whether a posited unobservable thing in a scientific theory really exists. He said who cares, if the theory predicts and the thing earns its keep then calling it real just means it does work, and asking for more is word games.

Deployment 2: he defended a belief I'm pretty sure is false but which makes the believer's life go better -- his example was a guy who believes his partner would never leave him, and that confidence makes him a better partner so the relationship thrives. He said the belief is "true for him."

Deployment 3: from there he slid to: since what counts as working varies person to person and community to community, truth is relative to what a community finds useful. He called this the pragmatist view and name-dropped James.

I'm sure those are three different claims and at least one of them is not what any actual pragmatist said. What I want is a menu, not a lecture. Lay out the distinct precise things that one-liner could be claiming, from the totally boring to the genuinely wild, and for each one tell me:

- whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a real position that's at least defensible, or just false;
- what "works" has to mean for it to be true -- works for whom, over what timescale, measured how, and whether the usefulness is being offered as a criterion of truth or as a definition of it;
- which of the three moves above it actually licenses, if any.

Two things I want seperated out in particular. First, the Peirce/James split: Peirce's maxim was a rule for getting clear on what a concept means by tracing its practical consequences, which is a claim about meaning and not about truth, and I gather Peirce thought James turned a method into a theory of truth and renamed his own view in 1905 to keep them apart. Peirce's own story about truth had something to do with what inquiry converges on in the long run, whereas James said truth happens to an idea and gets made true by events, and somewhere says the true is a species of the good. Which items on your menu did Peirce hold and which did James hold, and does the "long run" business rescue any of them from the short-run objections?

Second, I invented a test I call the cash-register test: a claim is true iff believing it pays off. Which reading on your menu is that, and is any real pragmatist stuck with it? Also, is there any reading at all on which "true for him" isn't just a category error, or is that phrase only ever doing rhetorical work?
