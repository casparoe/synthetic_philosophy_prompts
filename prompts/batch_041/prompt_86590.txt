I am a novelist, and I have spent four years writing the life of a man who appears in no reference work and deserves to: a physician who ran a two-room clinic in a mining valley for thirty years, kept a clinic ledger that survives, and left almost nothing else in his own hand — the only continuous record of his middle years is the diary of a nurse who worked beside him, did not always admire him, and is still alive. I have been asked to give the principal address at the annual dinner of the foundation that bears his name: six minutes, a room of perhaps sixty donors who know him as a story about a doctor who never turned anyone away, and, at the front tables, his widow and two sons and eight or nine people who worked with him and remember the years he was drinking and the last two years, when he should have stopped practising and did not. The director has asked me, courteously, to make people want to give. The widow has asked me not to embarrass her sons. The nurse will be in the room.

Please write that address. It must not quote or cite any real text, and it should not sound researched; the ledger and the diary are mine, and you may draw on them, inventing no more than a phrase or two at a time.

What I want it to think with — but never to announce as a thesis from the podium — is a contrast I have been living inside while writing the book. On one side, the old pairing of lives, one Greek set beside one Roman, the reader invited to weigh them against each other and against himself, faults included, because the faults are where the instruction is. On the other side, the lives of the saints, where the friction has been sanded off and the point is that you could not have done it. I have come to use two terms of my own for what is at stake, and you may use them: the first life, which a person actually lives, and the second life, which is the life he goes on living in other people's moral education after the first one has ended. The director wants a second life. The colleagues at the front tables know the first. I would like the address to take seriously, rather than settle cheaply, the possibility that the second life is not a corruption of the first but the whole point of having one — while still insisting that it answer to the first. It should not debunk him either; I have no interest in standing at that podium performing my own honesty. Whatever the address argues about learning from a life should be carried by what it chooses to say and to leave unsaid, not by a paragraph explaining exemplarity to donors.

After the address, please give me a short structured summary I can keep with the manuscript, as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "occasion": one sentence naming the occasion and the audience as the address itself frames them.
- "the_second_life": one sentence stating the version of the man the address asks the room to carry away.
- "kept_off_the_stage": an array of at most four objects, each with "fact" (something true that the address deliberately does not say) and "why_withheld" (the reason, whether protective, evidentiary, or rhetorical).
- "friction_kept": an array of objects, each with "unflattering_thing_said" (something against him that the address does say) and "what_it_is_doing_there".
- "the_colleagues_test": one sentence identifying the moment at which a former colleague who knew his faults would recognize that the address is not lying to the room.
- "the_ask": one sentence on what in the address is meant to make the room give money.
- "nearest_hagiography": the saint's-life move the address comes closest to making, and the sentence in which it turns away from it.
