I work as a policy analyst at a state environmental agency, and I administer two programs that have come to look to me like a philosophical contradiction wearing administrative clothing. In the radiation program, we assess risk as if there were no safe threshold: every increment of dose, however small, is assumed to add a proportional increment of cancer risk, and the governing rule is to keep exposures as low as is reasonably achievable rather than merely under a legal limit. In the chemical cleanup program, the same office applies a de minimis rule: when the estimated lifetime cancer risk to the most exposed resident falls below a threshold of roughly one in a million, the risk is treated as beneath notice and no remedy is required, even when the plume sits under a city and the arithmetic implies that a handful of people will get cancer who otherwise would not have. And in both programs, whenever we do act, the benefit-cost review caps what we are willing to spend per statistical life saved. So we sometimes treat a nearly imperceptible risk to each of a few thousand people as a decisive reason for a multi-million-dollar cleanup, and sometimes treat a nearly imperceptible risk to each of several million people as nothing at all.

This matters to me because a friend and I have been arguing about a thought experiment I gather is well known: one person tortured for fifty years, weighed against an incomprehensibly large number of people each receiving a single speck of dust in the eye, a number so large that it dwarfs the grains of sand on every beach on Earth. He is certain the torture is the worse outcome, and equally certain that a policy which disregards a one-in-a-million risk to millions of people is obviously sensible. I cannot see how he holds both views without saying something more precise than he has so far said. It has also occurred to me that the reasonableness qualifier built into the radiation rule, and the benefit-cost cutoff in the cleanup program, may already concede his point, in which case the real presupposition might not be about aggregation at all but about what counts as an acceptable excuse for inaction.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on risk regulation, population ethics, and the aggregation of small harms, and work through this carefully and slowly, beginning with a brief brainstorm in which you lay out the candidate readings before committing to any of them. Then reconstruct the implicit moral theory of the practice I described: what it presupposes about whether harms to different people are additive, about whether an individual risk can fall below the threshold of moral significance altogether, about whether expected-value reasoning is the right frame for regulatory harm, and about whether the practice is committed to any particular verdict in the dust specks case. Take care to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, and to identify where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same set of rules. Finally, tell me what each verdict in the thought experiment would demand of a regulator: what a policy built on the anti-aggregation verdict would look like, what one built on the aggregation verdict would look like, and whether either could actually be administered. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the reconstruction and the argument. Close by saying what would change your mind about which theory the practice presupposes, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
