I am a science and ideas journalist preparing a twenty-five-minute segment for a general-audience podcast on the proposal that we might make AI systems more honest by having two of them debate a question while a human judge decides who has won. I want the segment to turn on a claim that I think is underappreciated: the central vulnerability is not that a dishonest debater can assert falsehoods, but that it can force an honest debater to spend the judge's limited attention on unpacking an argument that is technically valid yet practically unverifiable, so that the verdict becomes a function of rhetorical economy rather than truth, and I want the segment to distinguish an obfuscated argument from a merely complicated one. Do not explain the idea to me; plan how I should explain it on air. What has to come first for the claim to land—what picture of debate, of the judge, and of the difference between falsity and uncheckability must be in place before the obfuscation strategy even makes sense—and in what order should those pieces go? What single carrying example should the segment be built around, and what must that example have and lack? I am inclined to use a dispute between two experts over whether a medical treatment works, with one expert relying on a meta-analysis whose inclusion criteria quietly determine the result, while the judge is a policy-maker with no statistical training; tell me whether that example can carry the weight, what it would need to be sharpened, and what a better example would be if not. Which misconceptions should I expect from listeners—that debate is inherently truth-seeking, that the judge is or could be a neutral expert, that obfuscation is just lying, that more debate time or more rounds would solve the problem, that this is a peculiarity of AI rather than a general feature of bounded judges, that the honest debater can always simplify—and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? What question should I put to listeners at the end to see whether they have understood the limit rather than merely learned a new worry? And what should I explicitly refuse to cover at this level—the formal game theory, the complexity-class machinery, the specific debate protocols, the question of whether the judge might itself be an AI—and how should I say so without seeming to dodge?
