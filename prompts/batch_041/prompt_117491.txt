In a seminar on ancient economic thought, the phrase 'money is a convention, not a product of nature' is regularly used as a conversation-stopper. Most recently it was deployed to dismiss a question about whether the exchange values we assign to goods track anything real: since money is conventional, the argument went, all value is conventional. I am not convinced the slogan can bear that weight. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the phrase could be making—from the trivial to the radical—and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or a claim Aristotle would have rejected. In doing so, distinguish at least claims about the particular currency token, the institution of money, money as a measure of value, and the nature of exchange value. Then say which reading is most likely at work in Aristotle's own discussions of money and exchange, and which readings would actually support the dismissal of my question. Please be explicit about what is settled among scholars who work on this and what is genuinely contested; do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
