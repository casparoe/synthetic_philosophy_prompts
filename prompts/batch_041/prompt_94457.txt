I retired four years ago after thirty-odd years of modelling fish stocks, and I've spent the time since reading ethics — badly, but enthusiastically. I've run into something I can't get out of, and I'd like you to find the bad step for me, because I'm sure theres one and I can't see it.

Here's the background, because it's doing the work. In my old job the one thing everybody knew was that you cannot project these systems far out. Change one recruitment parameter, add or drop a variable, tweak the shape of a distribution, and the projection doesn't just get vaguer — the ranking of two harvest policies flips. I said this in meetings for decades and nobody ever disagreed with me.

Then a friend who actually studied this stuff told me the ethics version has a name — cluelessness, something to do with the consequences of what you do running off into a future you can't see. He also said there's a distinction between not knowing what the consequences are and knowing them but being unable to add them up. I don't see how that gets you out; it looks like the same wall from both sides. Anyway, I built my own version and I don't like where it lands. Here it is:

(1) To say that doing A is morally better than doing B is to say the world goes better in expectation if A is done.
(2) To know which of two options has the higher expected value, you have to be able to rank them with the causal model you've actually got.
(3) For any causal model we could realistically hold of an action with long chains of downstream effects, there is some small change to the model — a parameter, an omitted variable, the shape of a distribution — that flips the ranking of the two options.
(4) If a ranking can be flipped by a small change in the model, then the ranking is a feature of the model and not of the two actions.
(5) Everything worth deliberating about has long enough downstream chains.
(6) So no action is better or worse than any other: the vaccination and the placebo, the famine donation and the yacht, the quota that holds and the quota that doesn't.

I think (6) is plainly false. I spent my working life on the assumption that some policies are better than others, and I'd say the same about the moral cases, definately. I'd like to keep (1) and (3) if I possibly can — (3) is just my own field, and (1) seems like what "better" has to mean if it means anything at all.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I want the honest price, not the reassuring one. And if the fix turns out to be something about the difference between not knowing and not being able to add up, tell me whether that actually repairs the argument or just gives the wreck a nicer name.
