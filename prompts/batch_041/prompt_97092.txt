I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, midway through a dissertation chapter on contractualism and the problem of aggregation, and I've been stuck for a week on an argument a colleague produced in our reading group. I'd like you to act as an expert in normative ethics with a specialist's command of the contractualist tradition and of the debate over whether contractualism can accommodate numbers. Please think carefully before committing to a verdict, and begin by laying out the moving parts in your own words. I don't want citations or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the argument itself diagnosed.

Contractualism, on Scanlon's official formulation, holds that "An act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement." My colleague's reconstruction of what follows runs like this:

(1) A principle is one that no one could reasonably reject just in case every person affected by it lacks a sufficiently strong objection to living under it; if even one person has such an objection, the principle is rejectable, and any act it permits is wrong.
(2) Whether a principle could reasonably be rejected is settled by the objections available to individuals taken one at a time. The fact that many people each have an objection of the same kind does not make any one of those objections stronger, and objections are never summed across persons.
(3) It follows that for any principle which imposes a loss on each of n people and confers a benefit on a single person, the principle is rejectable just in case some one individual's objection, considered on its own, is strong enough to reject it. The size of n never enters the test.
(4) Now suppose I can either save A, who will otherwise suffer a harm of magnitude 10, or save each of n other people, each of whom will otherwise suffer a harm of magnitude 9.9, and nothing else is at stake. No individual among the n has an objection to the principle "save A" that is as strong as A's objection to the principle "save the greater number."
(5) So A could reasonably reject "save the greater number," while no one among the n could reasonably reject "save A." By (1), saving the n is wrong and saving A is required — for every finite n, however large, and however small the gap between 10 and 9.9.
(6) That cannot be right. A morality that forbids rescuing a billion people from serious harm in order to spare one person a marginally greater harm, and that lets a single individual's objection outweigh the objections of any number of others of the same kind, is not a morality anyone could reasonably live under.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2). The whole point of contractualism, as I understand it, is that justification is owed to each person severally rather than to a collective, and (2) strikes me as simply what that means. But I can't accept (6) either. A friend of mine says the trouble is worse than the numbers case: on the same steps, no general rule at all could be justified, since any rule imposes some loss on someone, and by (3) the beneficiaries' numbers can never offset it. I would like to know whether that extension really follows, because if it does, something in the middle is doing work it has no right to do.

So: which step should I give up, and what would giving it up cost me? In particular, please do the following.

Second, state (2) as precisely as you can, and flag any ambiguity you find in it — I suspect it may be carrying more than one idea at once, but I can't say which.

Third, if you think the argument contains a mistake rather than a false premise, identify exactly where the slide occurs, explain why the offending step looked so plausible, and say what a defender of the argument should say in reply.

Fourth, if you think the argument is valid as it stands, tell me which premise is least costly to surrender, and be honest about the loss: what would I then have to say about the case in (4), what further commitments does the repair drag in, and is the resulting position still recognizably contractualist or has it quietly become a form of maximizing consequentialism?

Finally, if there is a way to keep both (1) and (2) while blocking the conclusion, state the repair precisely and price it; if there isn't, say why there isn't. I'd rather keep (2) than (6) if it comes to that, but I want to know exactly what I'm buying.
