I am a novelist some way into a manuscript, and I have hit a problem that workshops do not solve. One of my characters, a theoretical physicist in his late seventies, long retired and spending his last years with the Upanishads and Schrödinger's late essays, has adopted a single sentence of Schrödinger's as his answer to nearly everything: 'the plurality that we perceive is only an appearance; it is not real.' He deploys it when another character distinguishes his grief from hers, when someone insists that two people cannot want the same thing, when a doctor observes that a dying man is a separate person with separate interests. He says it as though it closes the question, and I have come to suspect that it closes nothing until one says which of several very different things it means.

What I want is a disambiguation I can keep beside the manuscript: the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, set out as a menu rather than argued one at a time, ordered from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say what it asserts, what it would commit a person to, and whether it is a platitude, a substantive thesis that could be defended, or something false or incoherent. Please separate the axes: what the sentence claims about the world, about experience, about language, and about what can be known. And please say what work the word 'appearance' is doing in each reading — appearing to whom, and unreal in what sense: misperceived, derivative, or simply not there. Where a reading matches a recognizable position in Indian philosophy, name it and say what it adds.

Then tell me which readings would actually license the uses my character puts the line to: the notion that the boundaries between my characters are a formal convention, so that one narrating voice may pass between their minds; the notion that death is a change of scene rather than an ending; the notion that grief for a stranger is grief for oneself. Which readings entitle him to these, and which leave him merely being poetical? I am equally interested in the reverse — which reading a physicist of his formation would most plausibly intend, and which readings are so weak that the sentence would not be worth a character's repeating.

Two further things. Say where two readings are unstable in combination; I suspect that the claim that consciousness is one cannot be held together with the claim that consciousness is private, and I would like to know whether that is right. And say which reading, if any, the sentence cannot support because it is not the sort of thing that can be true or false at all. No reading lists or citations are needed; I want the distinctions, not the scholarship. Close with a short paragraph on which reading the book should let him mean, given what each would commit the novel to.
