Adam Smith's impartial spectator is usually described as well-informed, disinterested, and sympathetic: we imagine a judge who knows our circumstances and those of everyone affected, feels with each of them, and is not biased toward our own case, and we test our conduct by whether that judge could approve of it. A colleague proposes a variant she calls the loyal spectator, which is equally well-informed and equally sympathetic to every party, but is required to give your interests priority in its verdict. She argues that because it is fully informed and feels for everyone, it will in practice discount your claims exactly as the impartial spectator would, so the two will always agree; impartiality, she concludes, is doing no independent work, and what really matters in Smith is information plus sympathy. Compare the impartial and the loyal spectator: is she right that the two coincide, and what, if anything, does impartiality contribute that full information together with sympathy could not supply on its own?
