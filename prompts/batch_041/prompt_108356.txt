Construct a pair of cases that isolate one variable: whether the good at stake is path-dependent — a good that exists only if the undertaking is carried through — or merely outcome-based. Two owners of small businesses must each decide whether to continue an undertaking that is losing money and that, on any ordinary forward-looking reckoning, is worse to continue than to abandon. Both have the same figures, the same options and the same beliefs about the facts, and both say that they have put too much in to stop now. In the first case, continuing is rationally defensible; in the second, it is the textbook fallacy; the two cases are to differ in nothing else. Constraints: realistic setting, no science fiction, one paragraph per case; nothing may turn on the past expenditure being evidence about the future, on its having created a saleable asset, or on reputations, contracts or third parties; and the goods at stake must be ones an ordinary person could genuinely have.

Then do the check: for each owner, what a strict marginalist says and why, and what an account that permits preferences over paths says and why; which single feature flips the verdict; and what becomes of each case when that feature is removed. Finish with the strongest objection from someone who thinks the case cheats — that attributing a path-dependent preference will license any continuation whatever, so the case proves nothing — and your best reply to it, and with a one-line verdict on whether the case shows the dictum that sunk costs should be ignored to be false or merely badly stated. The case is for a seminar paper arguing that the irrationality of honouring sunk costs is a claim about a restricted class of preferences rather than a theorem of practical rationality, so the verdicts must be derived from the cases and not stipulated. Do not attribute the outcome/path distinction to any author or supply references.
