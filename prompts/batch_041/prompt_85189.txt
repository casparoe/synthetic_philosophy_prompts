I run the data-visualisation desk at a county public health department — small team, me plus two designers, and we put out a pictorial "numbers page" every fortnight that goes into clinics, buses and school offices, in three languages. Our comms director signs off on everything and she is not a data person, so anything I hand her has to be defensible in one sentence. We've been fighting for a year about house style. Half the team wants our charts to look like the ones everyone else makes now: big numbers, a photo of a real person with a name and a face, a friendly mascot for the flu campaign, 3-D bars because they pop. I keep arguing we should be working in the spirit of Otto Neurath's picture language — ISOTYPE, the Vienna Method, whatever you want to call it — and I want to stop arguing from vibes and just have a procedure. Here's my reconstruction of the principles I'm actually trying to honour, from a history-of-science course I took years ago, so treat this as my summary and tell me where I've garbled it or where it's internally in tension: (1) a picture statement should be readable on its own, without a caption or a paragraph of text doing the explaining, so someone who reads none of our three languages can still get the claim off the page; (2) quantity is shown by repeating many identical small signs, each sign standing for the same amount, so the reader compares by counting rather than by eyeballing a length or an area; (3) the signs should be flat silhouettes with no perspective, no shading, no character — the same generic figure every time, because a sign is a unit and not a person, and any "characteristic" detail invites the reader to read individuality, mood or blame into a statistic; (4) nothing decorative that doesn't carry a quantity; (5) the whole page is meant to be read as one connected statement rather than a gallery of separate exhibits, and the order of the elements is part of the argument; and (6) it's for people who are not going to be talked at in technical language, so the pictorial statement is not a simplification of the real thing, it's a different and in some ways better instrument. I know there's something in Neurath about one sign sometimes standing for a round number of people rather than one, and about education being picture-first, but I can't reconstruct how those sit with (2) and (3), so flag that if it matters.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my desk can actually run, in order, on any chart before it goes to sign-off: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions stop the chart with a no, and which ones hand the decision to a person because it's a question of values or of our audience and not of facts. Keep the constraints real — I have maybe twenty minutes per chart, my designers have no philosophy and no time to read Neurath, and the comms director will overrule me for anything that looks cold or that she thinks won't land with a councillor. Then run the procedure on three charts we're actually building: (a) lead screening rates by ZIP code, where the honest answer is that two of the twelve ZIPs have samples so small that the rates swing wildly year to year; (b) overdose deaths by year for the county, 2014 to now, where the most recent year in our smallest town is three deaths — small enough that one sign per death makes the picture look like nothing is happening and one sign per hundred rounds it to zero; and (c) our own favourite draft, a big page on the county's mental-health line with photos of four real callers, first names, and a quote from each, which the team loves and which I suspect the procedure is going to tell me to throw out. Say plainly where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and where it can't decide and someone just has to own the call.
