I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of translation, and I use the exercise Hofstadter sets himself in Le Ton beau de Marot — dozens of English renderings of one short sixteenth-century French lyric addressed to a sick young girl, each negotiating the same very tight formal scheme — as a running illustration. What I want from you now is a decision tree that carries a translator or a reader from a few concrete commitments to a named position on what a translation under formal constraint must preserve.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of schools: first the questions that genuinely split positions, then the positions those questions locate. Every branch node must be phrased as a question my students could answer about a case without already knowing which leaf it leads to; no node may simply ask whether one is a formalist or a functionalist. At least four nodes must turn on the following distinctions, in whatever order the logic requires: (a) whether the formal constraint is part of what the poem says or merely a vehicle for saying it; (b) whether fidelity is owed to the properties of the source text, to the act the author performed, or to the experience of the target reader; (c) whether what must be carried across is the constraint's shape — the particular scheme, count, or pattern — or its function, i.e. what the constraint does (mnemonic, musical, ludic, devotional); and (d) whether translation is a success term, that is, whether there is a fact of the matter about whether a given rendering is a translation of the poem at all, or whether all renderings lie on a continuum of approximation. A fifth node should ask whether untranslatability is a property of the text and the language pair or of the individual translator's resources.

Use at least the following concrete cases as the material the nodes work on, and state explicitly where each one falls: a rendering that keeps the scheme exactly but loses a pun that the rhyme was doing; a rendering that drops the scheme but saves the pun; a poem whose form is a mnemonic device for children; a poem whose stated content is that it cannot be translated; a target language with almost no rhyming resources in the relevant register; and a version that prints footnotes and a literal prose gloss alongside the verse.

Give me eight to eleven leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position — use a standard label only where one is common coin, and never attribute a position to a person, work, or date — or the word unnamed where no one holds it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Identify every combination of answers that no position occupies, and diagnose briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any of the cases above, or by any you can supply, flag them as indistinguishable instead of pretending the tree divides them.

Write it so that a reader can actually walk it: at each node, say which answer routes to which child, and keep the prose tight enough to serve as a handout. Take a definite line throughout — commit to your cuts, commit to a placement for each of the cases above, and close by naming the leaf you yourself would defend and the strongest objection to it, rather than laying out the field and leaving the choice to me. No bibliography, citations, or references to specific works beyond the framing I have given.
