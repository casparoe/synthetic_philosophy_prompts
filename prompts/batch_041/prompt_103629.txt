A friend and I have now had the same argument three times, and I would like you to settle it. His view is that for any perceptual episode there is always a fact of the matter about what the person consciously experienced — a determinate answer to the question of what was seen or heard at that moment, even if no test could ever recover it. My view, which I take from Dennett's multiple drafts model, is that in some cases the question is simply ill-posed: there is no single place or moment at which the content is presented to an inner viewer, only a succession of partly concurrent revisions whose traces end up in the later report. We keep going in circles because we both reach for the same stock illustrations — the familiar colour-phi and masking effects, the familiar readiness-potential findings — and each of us can predict exactly what the other will say about them.

I want a fresh case that forces the issue. Construct a pair of scenarios differing in exactly one respect: in the first, the subject's later report is produced by a revision occurring after a content was genuinely experienced; in the second, the identical report is produced by a substitution occurring before anything of the original was experienced. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so, and observe these constraints.

First, no laboratory apparatus, no brain stimulation, no exotic physics: the manipulation must be something that could befall an ordinary adult in ordinary life.

Second, no colour phi, no metacontrast masking, no Libet-style readiness potentials, no split-brain cases, no blindsight. I want a case neither of us has seen before.

Third, the two scenarios must be indistinguishable in principle: every report the subject can give, every judgement they can make about their own experience, and every measurement anyone could take must come out the same.

Fourth, you may not rig the verdicts by stipulating in the scenario that one subject did and the other did not experience the content. The divergence has to fall out of what each theory says about a case described in neutral terms.

After the pair, do four things. State exactly what the Cartesian theater picture is committed to in your case — which fact about which moment it must treat as determinate, and why it cannot simply decline the question. State what the multiple drafts model says instead, and show that the two verdicts genuinely differ. Identify the single feature of your construction that is doing the work, and say what becomes of the case if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the theater — not a straw man — and say whether that reply survives your construction.

Finish with your own verdict, and commit to it: does the pair really show that the theater posits a fact the drafts model need not, or does the theater have a legitimate way to relocate the fact — to the report, to a neural threshold, to the subject's dispositions — that leaves your case intact as a description but harmless as an objection? Take a side and defend it. I would much rather have a definite answer I can argue with than a survey of the options.
