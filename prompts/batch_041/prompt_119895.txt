I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on time in literature and philosophy, and I want a reading of Slaughterhouse-Five through Borges's writings on time. I am not after the Nietzschean eternal-return pairing the novel usually attracts, nor the observation that both writers were preoccupied with time; I want the book sorted against three Borgesian positions that are commonly run together and ought not to be.

The first is the argument of A New Refutation of Time, where Borges extends Berkeley's and Hume's idealist negations to time itself: if nothing exists but present perceptions, then succession is unreal, the past is not a region lying somewhere behind us, and the self that was supposed to persist through time dissolves along with it. The second is the view dramatized in The Garden of Forking Paths, where Ts'ui Pên's novel realizes every possibility at once, so that the same man is killed in one branch of events and survives in another. The third is the stretched duration of The Secret Miracle, in which the condemned writer lives out a year of composition inside the instant before the volley while the world outside him stands still.

The Tralfamadorian doctrine in Vonnegut's novel — perception of all moments at once, every moment permanently in place, nothing ever alterable, equanimity in the face of death — is not obviously any of these. I want to know which one it is, and where the novel's own machinery commits it: Billy unstuck in time, the Tralfamadorian tales that are taken in all at once rather than in sequence, and above all the war film that runs backwards and un-happens the bombing. That last scene seems to me decisive, since it is a wish that the past be alterable, which one of these views permits, one forbids, and one renders meaningless.

Then the harder questions. Does the refrain that follows each death express the doctrine or betray it? Is the novel's quietism a consequence of its view of time, or an evasion the book knows to be one? And what does the novel show about living inside such a view that Borges's essays, which argue for the unreality of time while conceding that it cannot be lived, can only assert from outside? Please name the scenes I should put in front of the class, be honest where the novel resists the Borgesian frame, and end by saying what Vonnegut adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not.
