I'm not a logician or a philosopher, I just got weirdly obsessed with puzzle books during a slow stretch at work and now I can't stop chewing on one thing. There's this puzzle where you start with the string MI and you get four rules: if a string ends in I you can tack a U onto the end of it; if you've got an M followed by some block of letters you can repeat that whole block after it; any three I's in a row can be swapped for one U; and any two U's in a row can just be deleted. The question is whether you can ever turn MI into MU. I brute-forced it for two evenings and got nowhere, and then I eventually found out the answer is no, and the reason is that the number of I's in any string you can build is never a multiple of three, while MU has zero I's and zero is a multiple of three. Fine, whatever. But what's been bugging me is that that explanation doesn't feel like the same *kind* of thing as actually playing the game. It's not a move, it's a statement about every possible move, and it doesn't change a single one of the rules — it just steps back and looks at them all at once. Somebody once told me the word for that move is "jootsing," jumping out of the system, and now that I've noticed it I see it everywhere and I genuinely can't tell when it's a legitimate way of solving something and when it's just changing the subject.

So here are my gut reactions to a pile of cases and I want to know if there's one principle behind them, or if I'm just making it up as I go. (4) I think the whole business with the parallel postulate is a real jump — you can't get it from the other axioms, so you flip it and end up with entirely new geometries, and that turned out to be worth something. Which is annoying, because (1) and (4) look like the exact same move to me and I have opposite verdicts on them, and (2) doesn't change any rules at all yet I'd call it the biggest jump of the lot.

My half-formed principle is this: a jump is real when it's *forced* — when anyone who actually understood the old system would have to make that exact jump — and it's cheating when you could just as easily have jumped somewhere else.
