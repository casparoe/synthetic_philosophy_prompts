Okay, I'm not a philosopher, I just read a lot of stuff online, and this one argument has been bugging me for weeks. It's from some old Chinese text — I think the guy's name was Gongsun Long, part of that School of Names crowd — and the headline claim is that a white horse is not a horse. Which sounds like a joke until you actually see the reasoning, and then it stops being funny because I can't find the loose thread.

Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) The word horse picks out a shape or a kind. The word white picks out a color.
(2) Naming a color is not the same as naming a shape.
(3) So white horse is not simply the same as horse — it's horse plus a color.
(4) Down at the market, if I ask for a horse, they can bring me a brown one or a black one and that's fine. If I ask for a white horse, a brown or black one won't do.
(5) So what horse means and what white horse means are not the same thing.
(6) If two phrases don't mean the same thing, then the thing the one names isn't the thing the other names.
(7) Therefore a white horse is not a horse.

I feel like this is valid-ish and the conclusion is unhinged, and I also notice the same steps seem to prove way too much — they'd show a white dog isn't a dog, a fast runner isn't a runner, and honestly that no narrower term is ever a member of a broader one, which would wreck like every sentence I've ever said. So something has to give. My gut is I want to keep (1) through (4), they all seem obviously true, but I know that might be me being stubborn.

I want you to answer as a philosopher who knows both this old Chinese material and the philosophy of language side of it cold — someone who's seen this exact move before and can tell me what's really going on under the hood. Take a minute and actually think it through before you write anything, don't just reach for the first tidy answer.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion. Point 1 should name the specific step you'd reject and say what kind of mistake it is. Point 2 should explain why that step looked so convincing to me. Point 3 should tell me what it costs to give it up — what I have to say instead, and what that breaks. Point 4 should say whether there's a reading of the word is doing the damage here, and which reading. Point 5 should tell me whether anything survives of the original argument once the fix is in, or whether the whole thing collapses.
