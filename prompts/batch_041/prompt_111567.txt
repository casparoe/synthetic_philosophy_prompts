I run a monthly reading group at a robotics lab — about twelve people, mostly ML engineers plus a couple of philosophers, and basically nobody with any proof theory beyond a hazy memory of Gödel from undergrad. Next month it's my turn and I've picked the Löbian obstacle: the reason a proof-based agent can't just decide to trust itself. I have sixty minutes and a whiteboard. Don't explain the obstacle to me — I can read the papers. What I want is a plan for how I should explain it. You've taught this kind of material to people with engineering backgrounds, so think carefully about the audience before you start, and sketch a few candidate sequences and carrying examples for yourself before committing to one.

Assume they know the rough shape of the incompleteness theorems but have never seen a provability predicate, a modal logic, or a diagonalization. The thing I want them to actually feel by the end is why "if I can prove I'd take action A, then A is fine" is not a bit of healthy self-confidence but something that, taken as a general principle, either collapses into proving everything or buys you nothing. Löb's theorem is the pivot, so at some point the statement has to be up on the board in plain form: if a theory like PA proves that P is provable implies P, then PA already proves P — and I want them to see that this is a close relative of the second incompleteness theorem rather than a separate spooky fact they have to memorize, so tell me the order in which the pieces have to arrive for that to land instead of just be asserted (what has to come before what, and which step is the one where a wrong analogy will wreck everything downstream). I'm tempted to build the whole hour on one concrete agent — something that only crosses the bridge if it has a proof that crossing is safe, and that also wants to trust its own successor, which runs the same policy, so it needs to trust the successor's proofs about itself. But I can't tell whether that carries the idea or just piles on machinery; it might be doing the work for me in a way that hides the actual step. So: tell me what the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack, why the obvious candidates fail if they do, and be honest about which feature of the obstacle the example can't reproduce and that I'll have to state outright rather than stage. I'd also like the misconception map — the things they'll walk in believing (I'm guessing "this is just Gödel again" and "so the agent is just being irrationally paranoid"), the ones they'll form halfway through (probably "then self-trust is impossible, full stop"), and precisely where in the sequence I should head each one off, plus what I should refuse to go into at this level and how to signal the refusal so it doesn't look like I'm hiding the hard part. Skip the reading list, nobody reads it. Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that would actually tell me whether they've got the dependency structure or just nodded along, and two or three things that will predictably go wrong in the session and what I should do when they do.
