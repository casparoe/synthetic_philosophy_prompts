I'm a novelist — third book, the one thats supposed to be my serious one — and it's set in a small specialty coffee roastery just outside Trieste. Two of my characters, an old cupper named Vilma and a younger roaster named Dario, both come back to the same washed Ethiopian after Vilma's smell finally returns following a rough bout of covid, and both say the coffee tastes different than it used to. Vilma swears the coffee itself changed — new crop, different water, who knows. Dario swears the coffee is exactly the same and that Vilma is the one who changed. Same blind-test scores, same descriptors written down, no behavioral difference you could point to, and yet they seem to be disagreeing about something. I know this is basically the Chase and Sanborn setup from Dennett's Quining Qualia, so please don't burn space explaining the setup back to me.

Here's what I actually want: compare the two broad ways of saying what's going on in that scene, then tell me which one comes out better. The first says there's a genuine intrinsic felt property of the experience — the actual what-it's-like, private, immediate, not just a summary of what a person can discriminate and report — and that Vilma and Dario are disagreeing about whether that property changed, or about whether Vilma's memory of the old property is faithful; the property is real, the disagreement is real, it's just not checkable from the outside. The second says there's no extra property sitting on top of the coffee's chemistry plus each character's dispositions to notice, react, and talk, so what looks like a disagreement about a hidden fact is really two different stories told about the same bundle of capacities, and there's no fact of the matter about whose memory is right. Where do these two views actually come apart once you push on them, and which one handles my scene better?

Two complications to fold in. First, Vilma has her old tasting notebooks — years of written descriptors and scores from before she lost her smell — and Dario doesnt have anything like that, so the case isn't symmetric the way the original is. Second, my apprentice character Petra never lost her sense of smell, but she's on a medication that makes everything taste flatter to her, and yet her blind-tasting scores and her descriptor vocabulary are completely unchanged. Does that second case help one view and hurt the other? And if the second view wins, tell me honestly what I loose as a novelist — if there's no hidden fact, is there anything left for my characters to be wrong about, or do I just have two people with different vibes about coffee? If the first view wins, tell me what it can't explain without hand-waving.
