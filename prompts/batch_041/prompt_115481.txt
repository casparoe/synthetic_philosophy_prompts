I'm in a reading group that's slowly grinding through Augustine's stuff on lying -- the treatise on lying and then the later one he wrote against lying, whatever the exact titles are -- and we get stuck on the same thing every time: can the absolute ban survive any of the obvious counterexamples, or does it only look defensible because Augustine quietly stacks the deck?

I want an essay that actually takes a side on this. Here's what's bugging me. Augustine seems to think a lie is false signification with the intent to deceive, and that the wrongness is intrinsic -- it doesn't get cancelled by a good end -- and he seems to say the real damage is to the liar's own soul rather than to whoever got deceived. But he also seems fine with concealing things by silence, and with just not correcting someone's false belief, and there's that whole business with the midwives in Exodus and the patriarchs in Genesis where he does some kind of dance -- I think he claims those weren't lies because they were figures or mysteries pointing at something true, but I might be mixing him up with someone else. So I want you to press on the seams. In particular, take three cases I made up that I don't think the tradition really covers: (1) the truthful deceiver -- someone who tells the literal truth to a pursuer in a way designed to make the pursuer draw a false conclusion, so no false word is ever uttered; (2) the unbelived lie -- you tell a falsehood to someone who you know for a fact won't believe it (they can see the person hiding under the bed), so no one is actually deceived; (3) the vanishing lie -- you tell a comfortable falsehood to someone with severe dementia who will forget it within the hour and never suffer any false belief or disadvantage from it. If his account is really about the liar's act and not the consequences, then (2) and (3) should still be lies and still wrong; if it's really about harm, they aren't. Say what you think he's actually committed to, whether that position is defensable, and then give me your own verdict on whether lying is ever permissible. Don't hedge your way out of a verdict.

Structure it like this: a section called "The lay of the land" where you set out what you take Augustine's real commitments to be (a paragraph and a half or so); then "The case for the absolute ban" (two or three paragraphs); then "Three awkward cases", where you run my cases through the machinery (two or three paragraphs); then "My verdict" (one paragraph); and finish with "Where this is weakest", where you say which arguement or consideration would actually move you to a different verdict, and where your reading of Augustine is most likely to be wrong.
