I teach a grad seminar on combinatorial games, and we spend the last two weeks on the philosphical side of it -- existence proofs vs. constructions, what it even means to have "solved" a game, that kind of thing. I'm writing the final problem set and I want one essay question on strategy stealing, and I want it built so a student can't get away with just reciting "strategy stealing shows the first player wins at Hex and Chomp."

Here's what the question has to force them to do. First, pin down which structural features of a game the argument actually leans on -- in particular, make them be explicit that the fact that an extra move never hurts the player who takes it is doing real work and isn't just throat-clearing. Second, explain why the argument hands you a winner without handing you a move, and take a stand on whether that's a defect of the proof or just a fact about what got proven. Third, make them diagnose a game where the argument fails and say exactly which premise breaks -- zugzwang in chess is the cheap one, so I want at least one more, and feel free to invent a small variant game inside the question itself so they can't just recall it. Fourth, make them distiguish strategy stealing from the mirroring / Tweedledum-style arguments, which do give you an explicit strategy -- I want them to say why one of these arguments can produce a move and the other one can't.

So please write the question, a model answer of about 500 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that lists the must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line note for each point on how students typically blow it. Have the model answer open with a single-sentence bottom line. And keep the whole thing -- question, answer, and scheme -- under 900 words.

One constraint: don't lean on any particular paper, author, or citation. The students won't have web access and neither will the person marking, so anything I can't assume they've actually read is off limits. Naming games is fine; they'll know Hex and Chomp.
