I built the following argument for a seminar on pain and now distrust it, because its conclusion is one I find monstrous. The phenomena: pain asymbolia is a condition, associated with damage to the insula and its connections (and produced by other means as well), in which patients experience pain without unpleasantness — they report that something hurts, can localize it and describe its quality, but are not bothered by it and show little or no distress. The unpleasantness of pain, then, can come apart from its sensory content. Here is the argument.

(1) What makes a pain bad for the one who has it is its unpleasantness — the suffering — not its sensory content: intensity, location, quality.
(2) Pain asymbolia shows that the sensory content of a pain can occur without the unpleasantness.
(3) So the unpleasantness is not part of the pain sensation itself; it is something added to it.
(4) If it is added, it is a further state or reaction on the sufferer's part, caused by the sensation but not intrinsic to it.
(5) The badness of pain is the badness of that unpleasantness.
(6) Therefore the badness of a pain resides in the reaction, not in the sensation.
(7) A reaction can be absent or prevented while the sensation remains; so a creature given the sensation and no reactive unpleasantness has not been made worse off by it.
(8) Hence producing nociceptive sensation in a creature is not in itself bad, and we may be badly mistaken about the harms we inflict on beings whose inner lives we cannot read — infants, animals, the severely demented — since we infer their suffering from the sensations we presume them to have.

I would rather keep (1) than (8). Tell me which step fails, and be definite about it: name the step, explain why it looked unobjectionable, and tell me what giving it up costs — in particular, what becomes of the claim that pain is bad for its own sake.
