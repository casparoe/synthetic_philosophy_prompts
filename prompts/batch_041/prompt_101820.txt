A colleague recently argued against our department creating a prize for the year's most innovative research on the ground that it would merely set the winner up to be torn down; her phrase was that the tall poppy gets cut down. Since then I have heard the same proverb used to console someone who was passed over for promotion, to explain why a founder was quietly frozen out of her industry, and to justify a family's habit of mocking any member who announces a success. I cannot tell whether it is meant as a description of one culture's pathology, a recommendation for how to live, or a consolation, nor how any of those readings sits with the more familiar meritocratic faith that distinction deserves its reward. I would like the possibilities separated.

When I pressed her, she said that her part of the world has a name for the attitude — the Law of Jante — and summarised its rules: that you are not to think you are anything special, not to think you are smarter or more important than the rest of us, not to think you are good at anything, and not to imagine you can teach us anything. I had assumed this was an ancient folk code, but she said it in fact comes from a satirical novel published in 1933, which seems to me to bear directly on what the attitude is supposed to be claiming.

Please set out, as a menu rather than a single preferred interpretation, the distinct precise theses that 'the tall poppy gets cut down' could be asserting. For each, state whether it is a descriptive prediction about how people behave, a normative justification of that behaviour, a piece of prudential advice to the person who stands out, or a definitional claim about taste and status; say whether it is trivially true, substantively contestable, or simply false; and identify who is supposed to be doing the cutting, and on what warrant — envy, justice, self-protection, or the enforcement of a shared standard. Then say which of these readings would actually support my colleague's conclusion that the prize should not be created, which would leave her argument untouched, and which reading is in play when the proverb is used to comfort the person cut down rather than to warn the person standing up.
