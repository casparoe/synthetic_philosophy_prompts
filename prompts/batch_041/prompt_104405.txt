I've spent the past several months trying to decide whether to leave a career I've been building for over a decade and start again in something I can't really picture, and the process has pushed me toward a view I'd like you to stress-test. I keep coming across the phrase "transformative experience," but the standard framing strikes me as too coarse to help with the decision I actually have to make, so I've sharpened it into something more specific.

Here is the view. Ordinary decision theory is supposed to handle uncertainty about outcomes by assigning them values and maximizing expected value, and it is supposed to handle ignorance about what an outcome would be like by treating that as just more uncertainty. But there is a class of choices where the obstacle is neither of those. In these choices, the values that would make an outcome good or bad are values the agent would only acquire by making the choice, and the agent's present evaluative standpoint gives no grip on the options at all. Call a choice unreachable when every option's value is fixed only by a standpoint the agent does not yet occupy and cannot adopt even hypothetically. My claim is that for unreachable choices there is no fact of the matter about what one ought to do, and that the rational response is not to maximize anything but to decide in light of which standpoint one wants to end up occupying.

Tell me what would count against this. I don't want a survey of the literature; I want the specific arguments, intuitions, cases, and formal results that should actually move someone who holds it, sorted by how decisive they would be. In particular I want to know:

- whether the view collapses into ordinary decision under ignorance, in which case it is not a distinct position at all;
- whether the distinction between there being no fact of the matter and there merely being no justified answer available to me is doing real work, or whether I have simply conflated the two;
- whether unreachability, as I have defined it, is ever actually instantiated, or whether every candidate case turns out to contain some standpoint-invariant value (that a child one loves should flourish, that one should not be in chronic agony) after all;
- whether the clause about not being able to adopt the standpoint even hypothetically is a claim about the world or a claim about my ignorance, and whether it can be defended either way;
- whether my proposed remedy is itself evaluable: if wanting to occupy a standpoint is a preference I can assess, the choice was scrutable after all; if it isn't, the remedy looks vacuous, and there may be a regress of wanting to want;
- whether the view predicts anything that a plain expected-value treatment does not, or whether any decision whatsoever can be redescribed as unreachable and thereby made immune to counterexample.

Please also say which of these a sympathetic defender would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as missing the point, and be honest about whether the position has been insulated from refutation and whether that insulation is a genuine defect.
