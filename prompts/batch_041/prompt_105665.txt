I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, so I am steeped in the standard repertoire of acausal-decision-theory discussion: the Newcomb-style predictor, the precommitment trick, and the bargaining scenario in which two agents who can never causally interact nonetheless try to coordinate, on the grounds that each one's choice is a function of the other's decision algorithm rather than of anything either of them observes. A colleague and I spent an evening arguing about this over drinks and got precisely nowhere, which is what prompted me to write. I also make my living as a game theorist, and it is that professional habit which leaves me dissatisfied with one corner of the literature — the treatment of acausal threats, and the proposal that an agent might inoculate itself against extortion by adding to its utility function a surrogate goal that it regards as intrinsically worthless: a honeypot objective, chosen to be so rare or so dormant that it costs the agent nothing in ordinary circumstances (preventing the creation of a platinum sphere of some absurdly specific diameter, say), on the assumption that a threatener will redirect his threats towards this surrogate rather than towards what the agent actually cares about, so that carrying out a threat yields something the threatener dislikes rather than something the agent treasures. What I should like is a systematic translation of this entire apparatus — acausal threats, acausal extortion, surrogate goals, threatener-neutrality, and the associated claims about precommitment — into the vocabulary I actually use: extensive-form games, information sets, imperfect monitoring, subgame-perfect and sequential equilibrium, credible as against incredible threats, commitment devices, correlated equilibrium, common knowledge, and the mechanism designer's habit of holding preferences fixed and asking what can be implemented.

Then I want the residue report. Tell me which parts of the rationalists' position translate cleanly into that vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all. I am particularly curious about three things. First, whether 'acausal' is doing any work that imperfect information plus a suitable correlation device cannot already do — that is, whether the exotic flavour of the threat survives once I model the threatener's punishment as a merely probabilistic consequence of my choice. Second, whether the surrogate-goal proposal genuinely functions as a commitment device, given that the theory I know treats preferences as exogenous and would push any preference-modification into a meta-game in which the agent is at once designer and designed. Third, what is lost when the threatener's conduct is modelled as an equilibrium strategy rather than as the output of an algorithm logically correlated with my own. I am sceptical, though genuinely unsure, and I should like your honest view on whether the residue is a defect of my framework — something game theory is structurally unable to see — or a sign that the rationalists' claim was less clear than it looked. Please do not smuggle game-theoretic assumptions into the translation and present them as faithful; if something cannot be carried across, say so plainly. Write your answer in American English.
