I'm a small animal vet and I've got a recuring problem I can't get a clean grip on. Several times a day I have to tell an owner something like "he's not in pain" or "she's comfortable", and I sign off on decisions on that basis — pain scoring after surgery, whether to keep a chronically ill animal going, whether to call time on one. The evidence is roughly the same every time: the animal's quiet, doesn't flinch when I palpate, ate a bit, heart rate's ok. And I'm aware the evidence looks the same whether the animal is genuinely fine or a shade worse than fine. Epistemology has a name for that, I think — the anti-luminosity argument (Williamson's, if that matters) says no condition worth caring about is luminous: you can be in it without being in a position to know you're in it, because the gap between being in it and missing it by a hair is smaller than anything you can detect from the inside. There's also the KK principle, if you know P then you know you know P — I don't think I should be demanding that of myself, but I dont know what to put in its place.

So give me a procedure I can actually run, in order, on one animal, in about two minutes, with the owner standing in front of me. What to check first, what evidence counts, how to tell whether I'm in the safe zone or just near the edge of it, when the honest answer is "you can't know this from the inside" and I have to reach for a tool, a second opinion, or a different sentence. Mark the steps where I'm just picking a threshold and dressing it up as epistemic, and the points where the procedure has to stop and hand it to the owner or my boss because it's a values call, not a factual one. Also tell me what I'm allowed to say in each zone, because half my problem is the language — I need sentences that are true and still usable.

Then run it on three: a dog six hours after a routine spay who's quiet and won't eat; a cat with chronic kidney disease whose owner asks me point blank "is she suffering?" and can't afford more bloodwork; and a horse with a mild but persistent lameness where the owner is pushing me to say he's fine to ride. I expect the middle one to come out in a way I won't like — if the procedure won't let me say "she's not suffering", then say so, and say what I should say instead and why that's not just me being evasive.

Assume no philosophy beyond one undergrad course, so don't lean on jargon I'd have to look up, and no reading list — just the thing itself. Be straight about what the procedure can't do.
