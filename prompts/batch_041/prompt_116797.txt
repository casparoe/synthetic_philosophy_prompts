Okay, novelist here, not a philosopher, so apologies in advance if I get the vocabulary wrong.

I'm maybe a third of the way into a book and I've hit a wall that I'm pretty sure is a philosophical wall and not a plotting wall. Setup: a government office — in my draft it's the Bureau of What We Would Have Said — whose statutory job is to settle contested questions (reparations, animal welfare, what to do with a town that's dying) not by voting or polling but by working out what the public would have agreed to under proper conditions. The charter is deliberately vague about what "proper conditions" means, and that vagueness is the engine of the plot.

Two factions inside. One says you idealize the people: hand them the facts, more time, better reasoning, strip out the ignorance and the prejudice, then ask. The other says you idealize the procedure: keep the actual people with all their flaws, but design a process where only the good arguments can survive it — fix the rules, not the heads. The central scene is a case where the two methods return opposite verdicts and nobody can say which one has authority.

So what I want from you is a prioritization, not an explainer. Of all the open problems in the theory of ideal deliberation — what idealized agents would choose, and whether their choices bind the rest of us — which actually matter most, and in what order should I care about them? I want the ranking and I want the reasoning behind it: what's upstream of what, which problems are load-bearing (get them wrong and the whole procedure collapses), which ones just look deep, and which are genuinely open as opposed to me being confused. Someone told me this whole area is called indirect normativity, which I've stolen for a chapter title, so if that's a different thing than what I'm describing, say so.

Specific things I keep tripping over and can't tell if they're the real cruxes:

- the who-counts problem: any claim about what "we" would agree to seems to presuppose a boundry around the we, and that boundary is exactly the kind of thing you'd want the procedure to settle.
- the identity problem: the idealized me has different memories and maybe different values. Why is her decision mine? Real problem or word game?
- order of deliberation: it looks like you can reach different "converged" answers depending on what gets settled first, which makes the whole thing path-dependent.
- deferral: if I should do what my idealization would do, and my idealization would defer to a further idealization, does it bottom out anywhere?
- and the objection that idealization can't guide action because nobody can compute what the ideal agent would say — fatal, or just a practical nuisance?

Which of these have people actually made progress on, and which are just sitting there? And what would move your ranking — if one of them got settled tomorrow, which of the others would stop mattering? There's also an AI-safety version of this where you aim not at what we actually want but at what we'd want if we were smarter and better, and I can't tell if that's the same problem in a costume or a genuinely different one.

Please don't give me a reading list, I won't get through it, and don't tell me to go read the literature — I want the arguments and the shape of the thing. And one thing I do need, because this is a novel and not a seminar: which of these are actually dramatic? Which would change what my characters do on the page, rather than just give them something to say at a dinner party?
