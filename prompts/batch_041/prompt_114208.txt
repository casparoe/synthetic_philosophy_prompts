I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been writing a book about a doctor and a soldier and I've ended up with a view about killing and letting die. The view is: the distinction is real and morally deep, but it isn't the act/omission distinction. It's about shields. If a threat is already on its way and something is standing between the person and the threat, then removing that shield is killing; if nothing is standing there and you decline to put a shield up, that's letting die. So switching off a ventilator or a pacemaker is killing, while not starting a ventilator or not prescribing a cheap drug is letting die. A soldier who shoots a civilian is killing; a soldier who fails to warn a village is letting die. I know that's rough. What I want is the case against it. What observations, intuitions, cases, or formal results would actually count against the shield view, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? Which of those would a defender of the view have to concede, and which would they say are question-begging? And be honest about whether the view has been rigged so nothing can count against it -- if every case can be redescribed as shield-removal or shield-non-provision, is that a real problem or just what a moral distinction looks like? I'd find it most persuasive if you could give me two cases where the shield view has to call one killing and the other letting die, but the only difference is morally irrelevant, like who was standing closer or who had the better insurance. Please write in American English, and dont worry about being gentle.
