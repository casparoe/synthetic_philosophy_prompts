I chair the ethics committee of a scholarly association that publishes a journal and holds an annual conference. The board has asked the committee for a written opinion on a dispute it will vote on at its next meeting, and I need to circulate a draft beforehand. Please draft that opinion.

The dispute concerns a plenary invitation. Our program committee invited a mid-career member, Dr. A, to give next year's plenary lecture, and the invitation has been announced. A group of members has since circulated a letter demanding that we rescind it. The letter does not claim that Dr. A's scholarship is fraudulent, that the lecture would be poor, or that Dr. A has misbehaved at past meetings; it claims that Dr. A has, in public writing unrelated to their academic work, promoted a political cause that many members regard as harmful, and that giving Dr. A a plenary platform would make the association complicit in that harm. A counter-letter of comparable size argues that rescinding would punish Dr. A for off-duty speech, that we have no rule against that speech, and that we have no procedure for adjudicating it.

Two of our precedents are being cited on both sides. Three years ago we declined to rescind an invitation to a member who had been convicted of a serious crime, reasoning that the invitation was for scholarship and that the association is not a court. Two years ago we rescinded an invitation to a member found to have plagiarized, reasoning that scholarship and character cannot be separated. Some members say the precedents are inconsistent; others say the difference is that plagiarism bears on the scholarship while a political cause does not.

The committee has also been going in circles about what rescinding would even be. We keep sliding between three descriptions: (i) declining to extend a benefit we were never obliged to give; (ii) reneging on a benefit we had already promised; and (iii) publicly censuring Dr. A for their conduct. One member has proposed a test: the association may withhold a benefit such as a speaking slot for any reason whatever, since no one has a right to be platformed, but may censure a member only for conduct within the association's jurisdiction and only after a hearing; on this test, quietly rescinding the invitation is permissible while publishing a statement of reasons is not. Another member calls that sophistry, on the ground that a silent rescission communicates the same censure as an announced one.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- keep the invitation, rescind it, or something in between, such as keeping it under conditions -- and give the board reasons it can act on. It should reach a verdict on the proposed test and on the two precedents rather than surveying the options, and it should engage the petitioners' complicity argument seriously, since that is the strongest consideration on the side I am inclined to reject.

Close with a short paragraph stating how confident you are in the recommendation and identifying the single step in the reasoning you are least sure of.
