I teach a 90-minute session on social choice to about sixteen undergraduates, and I want them to feel the liberal paradox from the inside rather than hear it announced. Design a runnable in-class activity in which each participant is assigned a private domain and also holds preferences about other people's domains; they commit their orderings to paper before any decision rule is announced, then try to draft a procedure meeting three rules they will each find independently compelling (each person decides their own domain, unanimous preferences bind the group, and the group's ranking comes out consistent). They should hit the contradiction themselves in the smallest case you can make work, without being told what it is called until the debrief. Give me the materials, a timeline, roles or rules, a scoring scheme that needs no money, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. In the debrief, map each moment of the activity onto the conditions that generate the paradox, distinguish the demand that the group ranking be acyclic from the weaker demand that group choices merely be well defined, and spell out what each escape route costs — dropping unanimity, refusing to count meddlesome preferences, or tolerating cycles and living with path-dependent outcomes. Be explicit about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, and say which design choice, if it turned out to be wrong, would show that the activity fails to make the paradox's force felt.
