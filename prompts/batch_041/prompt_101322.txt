I am a philosophy PhD student assembling a short set of humorous pieces for a reading group on puzzles of practical reason, and I would like you to write one of them. Please write a joke about Kavka's toxin puzzle. I want the humour to be carried by the puzzle's actual logical architecture rather than by the mere oddity of philosophers dreaming up strange cases, so the punchline should turn on a feature that anyone who has worked through the case will recognise as load-bearing — for instance, that the reward is offered for the state of intending to drink rather than for the act of drinking itself; that the agent is stipulated to be able to know her own present intention yet unable to guarantee her future conduct; or that the familiar objection (form the intention, take the money, then simply change your mind) is precisely what the case is constructed to exclude. To keep the result from feeling like a lecture with a punchline bolted on, please transpose the scenario into an ordinary, low-stakes setting of your own choosing — no toxin and no millionaire — and let the recasting do the work. The piece should be genuinely amusing to someone who already knows the puzzle, so it may presuppose familiarity with the case, but it must not trade on any particular published commentary or on the audience having read the secondary literature. A short setup and a punchline are sufficient.
