For an upper-level seminar on war and morality, I want a decision tree of no more than a page that takes a reader from the single commitment that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were morally wrong to the different positions one might hold about why, or whether, that is so. Every branch point should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing the labels, and the tree should be grown from the logical space first, with the named positions located in it afterwards and unoccupied combinations marked and diagnosed in a line or two (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). The branch questions I care about are: were the civilian deaths intended, or foreseen but not intended, and does it settle the matter that the planners wanted the destruction of the city only as a means of forcing surrender?; is the prohibition on killing the innocent exceptionless, or defeasible when a state faces an existential threat?; does the good expected to follow bear on the act's character or only on how far the agents are to blame?; and is the prohibition grounded in the act itself, in a virtue, in divine law, or in the costs of permitting exceptions? The leaves should include Anscombe's absolutism, the double-effect casuistry that permits the bombing provided the terror was not intended, the supreme-emergency exception, an openly consequentialist defense, the view that the act was wrong but the agent excusable, and the view that noncombatant status is not a coherent category in total war; add whatever further leaf the space requires. Flag explicitly any two leaves that agree about Truman but come apart only on a case where the intended killing is small and the expected saving of life enormous, and say where your tree cannot separate them.
