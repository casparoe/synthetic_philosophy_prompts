I run causal analysis for a state workplace-safety agency: when someone is seriously hurt or killed on the job, my unit produces the findings that the board votes on and that our lawyers then have to defend in administrative appeals. The trouble is that we have never settled what we are actually claiming when we name a cause. Our counsel wants the but-for test — if the injury would have happened anyway, that factor is not a cause, full stop — and treats our findings as though they were verdicts about liability. My engineers, who spend their days reasoning about which guard, interlock, or procedure would have made the difference, keep producing findings in a different idiom: they describe the incident as a system, point at the factor whose manipulation would have changed the outcome, and are unmoved when told that some other factor was equally sufficient. I have read enough of the philosophical literature to know that these are not just two house styles but two theories — the counterfactual account built on but-for dependence and the interventionist account built on what a well-defined manipulation of a variable would do — and that each has known failure modes the other was designed to fix. What I need from you is a decision procedure my unit can actually run, in the ninety minutes we get per case, on a finding of cause: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a finding, where it must hand the question to the board because it has become a matter of policy rather than fact, and where it must say out loud that the answer depends on a convention we have chosen rather than discovered.

The procedure has to survive the cases that have already burned us. One: two redundant safety interlocks on a powered pallet truck were both defeated, months apart, by different people; either one alone would have stopped the machine, and the operator was crushed when neither did. Counsel's test finds no cause at all, which is unacceptable to a board that must issue a citation naming one. Two: a supervisor's failure to enforce a pre-shift inspection, where the thing not done is not an event and the counterfactual is about an absence. Three: the factor everyone in the building believes is the real cause — chronic understaffing that made the shortcuts rational — which is the kind of standing condition where I suspect the intervention is not well-defined at all, and where I need the procedure to tell me whether that is a defect in the factor or a defect in my question. It must also tell me how to handle a factor that is sensitive (the outcome turns on it in this case) but not robust (it would not have mattered across the range of plausible variations), and it must distinguish between the factors that are causally relevant and the one factor we choose to name — I do not want a procedure that pretends the selection step is forced when it is not. Then run it end to end on those three cases, including the one where it yields a result my board will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: which questions it leaves to values, which steps are judgment dressed up as method, and where a competent investigator could run it correctly and still reach the opposite finding.

One constraint on the answer itself: do not give me a reading list, citations, or references to specific papers or authors' works — I cannot check them and I do not need them. Argue from the substance of the two positions and their known problems. I want the procedure to be usable by people who have never heard the terms counterfactual and interventionist, so tell me which distinctions have to survive the translation into plain questions and which I can drop without loss. Finally, be candid about your own confidence: for each of the three worked cases, name the single consideration that, if it turned out the other way, would flip your verdict, and tell me which step of the procedure you would expect to be the first to break under adversarial cross-examination.
