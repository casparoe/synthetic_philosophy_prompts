I'm putting together a session on Moloch-type dynamics and races to the bottom, and a friend handed me an argument that I think proves way too much. Could you take the role of a game theorist / philosopher of social science who's thought hard about these selection arguments, brainstorm a bit about what each step is really committing to before you answer, and then give me a diagnosis? Here's the thing:

(1) When agents compete, the choice that maximizes each agent's payoff given what the others do is the one that gets selected.
(2) If any agent can gain by cutting a corner — a safety check, a wage, a cleanup cost — at least one will.
(3) Once some agents cut it, the ones who don't get outcompeted and shrink or die.
(4) So the corner ends up cut by everyone.
(5) Nothing in this turns on which corner it is: the same reasoning covers every cost that can be shoved onto someone else.
(6) Therefore every competitive domain drifts toward the maximally corner-cut, value-destroying outcome.
(7) That means markets, science, friendship, and even the institutions that are supposed to fix this are all racing to the bottom at once — including this very argument, since reasoning carefully is itself costly compared to just asserting something and being confident.
(8) But that last bit is obviously wrong; careful arguments do survive, and plenty of domains don't degrade to the worst outcome.
So something in there is broken. A friend of mine has a name for what she thinks the culprit is — she calls it the "single-ledger" assumption, the idea that at any moment all the competitors are being scored on one common axis — but I'm not sure that's it or that it's the only thing. Which step do you reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) if at all possible, they feel like the whole point. Please don't just tell me "it's more complicated" — I want the specific step and the actual price. Oh and if it helps, the concrete case that started all this was rival prompt-engineering shops racing to write ever-more-flattering system prompts, which may or may not be a fair example.
