Last weekend I got cornered for the better part of three hours while two friends of mine — one a novelist, the other a graduate student in philosophy — argued over a case the novelist invented for a story she's writing, and I've been asked ever since to say who was right. The case is this. A man keeps a private notebook in which he records a recurring inner state, a kind of mental fog he has never heard anyone else describe and cannot point to in any observable way, using a word of his own coinage, 'grelm,' together with a five-point scale. He tells no one what grelm is; he never uses the word to ask for anything, warn anyone, or describe anything to another person. But he does use it: he notices that when he writes 'grelm 4' in the morning he cannot do mental arithmetic by the evening, and over the years the predictions hold up. The novelist says the notebook is plainly meaningful — the word earns its keep, it figures in predictions that come true, and that is all a word needs. The philosopher says the notebook is exactly the sort of thing the private language argument was designed to rule out: the man has no criterion of correctness for his entries, nothing to distinguish his being right from his seeming to himself to be right, and a rule that cannot be applied wrongly is not a rule at all. The novelist replies that the predictions supply precisely such a criterion, and that the public cases are no better off anyway — when two of us consult a colour chart, each of us is still relying on his own memory of the chart from one moment to the next. The philosopher answers that the difference is not that public memory is more reliable, but that in the public case there is a genuine gap between thinking he remembers and actually remembering, because other people can correct him, whereas the diarist can be corrected by no one, himself included. The novelist then raised a further point she took to be decisive: suppose there is a scanner in the next room that could in principle tell, from the man's brain alone, whether he is in the grelm-4 state — a check that exists, and is public, but that he never consults. She says this shows the philosopher's objection was never about privacy at all but about the absence of a check, and the check exists. He says an unused check is no part of the practice, and that in any case the scanner is beside the point, since what it would certify is a brain state, not the correctness of the entry.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken through doubtful to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, then saying who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I would especially like the predictive-use claim and the scanner point handled carefully: does the man's success at predicting his own later incapacity really give his entries the criterion of correctness the philosopher says is absent, or does it only establish that something in him is reliable? And does the scanner, if it is never consulted, change the philosophical situation at all — and if it would change it when consulted, does that mean the argument was about checkability rather than about privacy, or is that a false contrast? I don't want references, a reading list, or a summary of what the secondary literature says; I have read enough of that to know it will not settle the question by itself, and I would rather have the arguments themselves sorted out as they were actually made.
