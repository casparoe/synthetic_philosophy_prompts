You are a philosopher of science whose expertise covers evolutionary psychology, the adaptationism debates, and the philosophy of biology. Please think carefully before writing, and organize your answer as a structured analysis rather than a single verdict.

I keep running into a slogan that I would like you to take apart: "we have Stone Age minds living in a modern world." I have met it in several forms. Most recently it closed down a discussion about why people overeat and struggle to concentrate: the implication was that these problems are more or less inevitable, because our minds were built for one kind of world and now inhabit another. I have also seen the same idea used to argue that certain sex differences and social arrangements are fixed, and to resist proposals for change on the grounds that they run against our evolved nature. In each case the sentence was doing a great deal of work while remaining, as far as I can tell, unexamined.

What I want is a map of what that sentence could actually be asserting. Specifically:

1. Begin by brainstorming the distinct claims it might be making, ranging from the almost trivial to the very strong.
2. Turn that into a menu of precise readings. For each, spell out exactly what is being asserted — what work "Stone Age" is doing, what "minds" ranges over, what the "modern world" is being contrasted with — and what sort of evidence would confirm or refute it.
3. For each reading, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible empirical claim, contested theoretical commitment, or simply false or confused.
4. Say which reading is required to support each of the uses I described — explaining modern malaise, excusing it, and grounding claims about a fixed human nature — and point out anywhere a weaker reading is quietly being traded for a stronger one.
5. Finally, connect this to the charge that evolutionary psychology produces "just-so stories": which of the readings are exposed to that charge, and which escape it?

Be candid with me. If the slogan is too vague to yield a clean menu, or if two of the readings I am assuming are really one, say so plainly rather than padding the list with distinctions that collapse under pressure. If my framing assumes the sentence has a single determinate meaning when it is really several slogans fused together, push back on that as well. I would rather have a short honest menu than a long tidy one.
