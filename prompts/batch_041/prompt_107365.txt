I recently came across the following gloss of Avicenna's flying man argument in an introductory survey of Islamic philosophy. I've reconstructed it from my notes, so the wording is mine, but this is the substance of what the author says:

"Avicenna's flying man is the medieval forerunner of Descartes' cogito. We are asked to imagine a man created fully formed and suspended in midair, his eyes blindfolded and his limbs drawn apart so that he touches nothing and feels nothing. Avicenna claims that even in this condition the man would be certain of his own existence, and certain of it while knowing nothing whatever about his body. What this establishes is that the soul is a substance wholly independent of the body — the body is no part of the soul's essence — and hence that the soul is immortal. The engine of the argument is that because the man can conceive of himself existing without a body, the soul can and does exist apart from the body; this is precisely the move Descartes would later make with the cogito."

I would like a careful audit of this. Take it claim by claim and tell me what a specialist in Avicenna would accept as stated, what they would want to qualify or would wince at as a simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the flying man is properly described as an argument for the soul's immortality, or whether immortality is a distinct conclusion reached by separate means. Second, whether the conceivability-to-possibility framing — "because the man can conceive of himself existing without a body, the soul can and does exist apart from the body" — captures how Avicenna actually argues, or instead imports a modern assumption about the relation between conceivability and possibility. Third, whether the Descartes comparison is fair to either thinker, given how each sets up the scenario and what each takes it to show; I have a vague sense that the two figures are doing quite different things with a superficially similar setup, but I can't put my finger on the difference. If it is relevant, I am also curious whether the flying man is best understood as a proof at all, or as something closer to a device for directing the reader's attention to something he is supposed to find already in himself — I have seen this suggested but do not know how much weight it bears.
