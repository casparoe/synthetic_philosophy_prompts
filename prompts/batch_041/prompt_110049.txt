I'm doing a master's in international relations and I've been argueing with my friend Priya about military intervention basically all semester. She's not a theorist — she hasn't read much of the just war literature, she just has strong views and (I'm fairly sure) one implicit rule she may not be able to state. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: Kosovo 1999, NATO bombing without a Security Council mandate, she says was right, because the ethnic cleansing was happening in plain view and the Russians were never going to allow a resolution. Rwanda 1994, she says a force should have gone in even without authorization, and that the waiting was the whole disaster. Bosnia 1995, the authorized airstrikes that ended the war, right. East Timor 1999, authorized, right. Iraq 2003, wrong, and she gets visibly annoyed when people compare it to Kosovo — for her the difference is that nothing at that scale was being done to civilians and the whole case was WMD and regime change. Libya 2011, right, and she still defends going in, though she thinks NATO should have stopped once Benghazi was safe rather than running the war through to regime change. Syria 2013, after the chemical attack she was against the strike Obama was threatening — no authorization, and she says a punitive strike protects nobody. Bahrain 2011, she says the outside world was right to stay out; a government crushing a protest isn't the same as a government trying to wipe out a population.

The case we actually keep fighting about is one I invented for a seminar paper, loosely stitched from Darfur and a couple of other things. Country X: the government is deliberately starving a minority region — aid convoys blocked, villages burned, somewhere in the low hundreds of thousands dead over three years and the rate accelerating. The Security Council is deadlocked because one permanent member is X's main arms supplier and keeps vetoing. The African Union has voted to authorize a protection force, and a neighbouring state — which has its own long-standing territorial and ethnic claims in that same region — has offered to lead it. AU troops plus that state's army could plausibly secure the region in a few months, but there's a real chance of a long messy occupation and of the neighbour annexing the area it "protects." I think she'll say yes to intervening, and my seminar friend thinks that would make her inconsistent with her Iraq view.

I care much less about yes or no than about the rule she's actually running. Give me the two or three rules that fit everything above, say what each one predicts about Country X, tell me which I should bet on and why, and then — this is the part I really want — name the further case I'd have to put to her to tell them apart, i.e. what scenario would reveal whether she's a legalist with an atrocity exception, a straight atrocity-protection person, or someone whose real test is whether the intervening coalition is broad and disinterested.
