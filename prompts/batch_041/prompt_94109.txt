I'm preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on the Chinese Room, and I've decided to spend it on the specification rather than the verdict. Before the class argues about whether the thought experiment refutes the claim that implementing the right program suffices for understanding a language, I want a shared list of what a theory of linguistic understanding would have to deliver in order to be entitled to settle the case at all. Please write me that list of desiderata. For each item I want the requirement stated sharply, the consideration or case that motivates it, and a note on whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. The cases I would like the list to be answerable to are at least these: the man in the room working from a rule book; the room taken as a whole, including the rule book, the paper, and the man; the man who has memorized the rule book and now does everything himself, with no room left over; a system whose entire input-output disposition over Chinese is stored as an enormous lookup table rather than computed step by step, so that its behaviour counts as a program only in a degenerate sense; the nation-scale version in which the room's work is done by many people passing slips of paper; an ordinary bilingual speaker; a monolingual English speaker fitted with an implant that lets her produce and comprehend Chinese; and something at the low end, a thermostat or a chess engine, which we are reluctant to credit with understanding anything at all. The distinctions the list must respect seem to me to include at least: simulating understanding versus duplicating it; syntax versus semantics; understanding a language versus being disposed to produce acceptable responses in it; understanding as a graded matter versus understanding as a threshold; understanding versus consciousness or experience; and the question of the level at which understanding is predicated, since the man may be said to understand nothing while the room is said to understand Chinese, and something has to govern whether that is a legitimate shift or an evasion. The theory also should not be required to contradict things we are fairly confident of, for instance that brains are physical systems whose behaviour is in some coarse sense describable by a formal procedure, and that our ordinary attributions of understanding to other people rest on behavioural and functional evidence and are defeasible.

What I most want from the exercise is the tension structure. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied -- I suspect, for instance, that a principle forbidding understanding to arise out of parts that themselves understand nothing sits badly with our willingness to credit brains, whose parts presumably understand nothing, with understanding -- then say which subset, and say what each party to the dispute has to give up. Also tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the largest number of the familiar positions, and why it has that power. I would like the items ordered roughly by how much work each one does, and I would rather have eight or ten well-motivated requirements than a long undifferentiated inventory. Skip the scorecard of who wins; the point of the session is to have the criteria in hand before we read anyone's answer.
