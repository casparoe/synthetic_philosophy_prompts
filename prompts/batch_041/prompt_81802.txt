I run a small works-in-progress series in my department and next month's guest is a philosopher who works on hinge epistemology — the On Certainty stuff, where all our doubting and justifying goes on against a backdrop of things we don't doubt and can't really justify. Her line, which I find interesting and also slightly annoying, is that hinges aren't beliefs at all, they're skills, habits of not-asking, so the question whether a hinge is true or false is a category mistake. The only question worth asking about a hinge, she says, is whether the habit of not-asking is in good working order or has gone rotten. And then she pulls this move I want to poke at: she says the habit of not asking whether the text in front of you was written by a person used to be a perfectly healthy hinge, and now it's rotten, and that this is what losing a hinge actually looks like — not a belief getting revised but a skill going bad.

Draft me 8-10 interview questions that don't let her just restate that. I definately want at least one each on:

- what well-founded can even mean for a skill, without smuggling propositional content back in, and a follow up for when she says it's well founded just in case it's held in place by everything around it (press on whether that's circular, and whether a whole community's habit can be rotten at once);
- whether hinges have to be true, or whether truth just isn't the right dimension for them, and what she does with the intuition that some of our certainties are just plain false;
- whether her view is really just a redescription of Wittgensteins riverbed image with the vocabulary swapped, and what would count as evidence for the skill reading over the belief reading rather than a stipulation about which word to use;
- the disagreement case: two people with different habits of not-asking, is there any rational way through or only conversion, plus the follow up for when she says genuine disagreement can't arise there because hinges aren't in the space of reasons;
- the AI example specifically, where I want her to defend the claim that human authorship was ever a hinge and not just an ordinary empirical belief nobody happened to doubt, and then to face the fact that a hinge which rots in ten years looks a lot more plastic than hinges are supposed to be — if hinges are that revisable, what's left of the idea that they're what makes doubt possible in the first place;
- and one on what would make her give up the skill view.

Give each question a follow up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. No what-drew-you-to-this-topic filler, and please don't ask her to recommend reading — I already have the manuscript.
