So I'm a backend engineer, no real philosophy training, but a couple of friends in a reading group I sit in on got into a huge argument last week and I've been chewing on it ever since. The setup: we were talking about a sepsis-prediction model one of them had read about (or maybe it was a churn model, doesn't matter — point is a statistical model that scores well on held-out data). Call them Marco and Devi. Marco's line was basically: who cares whether the model's assumptions are true? The model treats every patient as independent and every clinician as perfectly rational, and sure, that's false, but false assumptions are the whole point — a theory is just an instrument for generating predictions, and you judge it by how well it predicts, full stop. He said there's a famous argument for this, something like Friedman's F-twist, where asking whether a model's assumptions are "realistic" is a confusion, because no model is realistic and the good ones still work. Devi shot back that the assumptions matter exactly when you want to use the model to decide something — intervene. If the real world has contagion (patients infecting each other) and the model assumes independence, then the model's accuracy on the old data is a coincidence, and the second you use it to decide whether to isolate patients you're intervening on precisely the mechanism the model pretends isn't there. Prediction and explanation come apart, she said, and when they do the predictions stop being trustworthy off the data you fit on. Marco said fine, that just means it's a bad model in that range, which is an empirical claim you test by predicting, so he still wins — you never need the word "explanation," and anyway the most successful theories in history (ideal gas, Newtonian gravity, rational-agent economics) are known to be literally false yet let us intervene successfully all the time. Devi answered that those cases prove her point: we can use ideal gases because we know exactly how and why real gases deviate, which is a matter of understanding the mechanism, not a prediction; Newtonian gravity got replaced precisely because its assumptions about the structure of gravity were wrong; and rational-agent economics is propped up by patches. Marco came back that "knowing how it deviates" is itself just a prediction about when the model fails, so she'd redefined explanation as meta-prediction and conceded the currency is prediction. Devi said no — she knows the deviation from experiments and interventions, not from some other model's output, and that's the difference between a model that latches onto what's actually causing the outcome and a clock that happens to read right.
