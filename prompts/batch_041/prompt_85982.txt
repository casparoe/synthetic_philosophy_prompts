I'm the ops lead at a company of about 120 people and by default I've ended up owning the "values and conduct" section of our performance reviews — the bit where a manager has to say whether someone is honest, whether they're a team player, whether they take ownership. Twice a year, every manager writes five to eight of these, twenty minutes each, HR signs off on all of them, and anything that could lead to a firing comes to me and legal. I'm starting to think the whole section is noise. Two managers can watch the same person for a year and write opposite things. The person who's unflappable in a crisis is often the one who blows up over a scheduling mistake. And the write-ups seem to track which team your on more than who you are. I've been reading the philosophy that says this isn't just my office — the obedience experiments, the seminary students who were in a hurry, the whole situationist case that behaviour is mostly the setting and not the person — and I'm half convinced that "character" is just a name we give a snap judgement after the fact. But we can't scrap it: HR wants documented conduct assessments, managers need something to write, and I don't buy the situationist line all the way either, since people obviously do have patterns.

So what I want from you is virtue ethics turned into something a manager can actually run — not an essay, a procedure. Take seriously what the theory really claims (that a virtue is supposed to be stable across situations, that having one means acting knowingly and from a settled disposition and not just by habit) and take just as seriously the objection that a single act under pressure shows almost nothing, then give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a person because that's a values question and not a fact question. I'd much rather my managers learn to write "in situations like X, this person does Y" than slap a global label on someone, so build that in — and tell me how to keep the form from turning into a pile of anecdotes, and whether those if-then patterns are allowed to come out narrow (honest about expenses but not honest in general) or whether narrowing them that way is just quietly abandoning the theory. Then run it on three cases: someone who was rude to a client exactly once; someone with five years of "great attitude" and one integrity lapse; and a high performer whose good behaviour only shows up when they're being watched. I want at least one of those to land in a way I won't like — ideally the one where the procedure tells me our most trusted manager's glowing review is really just a description of a situation we built. Keep the whole thing to about a page, and be straight with me about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure and what this thing simply can't settle.
