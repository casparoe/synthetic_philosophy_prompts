So this is a slightly odd request and I'll try to be precise about what I actually need. I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy — metaphysics and logic mostly, though my thesis has drifted into stuff about branching time — and our department puts on its own little ceremony for the graduating majors the morning of commencement, and they've asked me to give the address this year. It's a small liberal arts college, so it's the majors, their families, and most of the faculty crammed into the chapel, maybe 150 people, and the audience splits right down the middle in a way I'm finding kind of paralyzing. Half of them are parents and grandparents who've never read a page of Aristotle and who will glaze over instantly if I start lecturing; the other half are our own faculty and the majors themselves, who will absolutely notice if I say something philosophically incoherent about time and truth. My grandmother will be in the third row. She has asked me three times what my dissertation is about and I have never once gotten past the first sentence.

What I want the speech to be built around is the sea battle — the argument in On Interpretation where Aristotle asks about a claim made today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. If it's true now, the battle looks already settled; if it's false now, it looks settled the other way; either way the future closes up, which is bad news if you think some of tomorrow is up to us. And there are two big families of response: either claims about contingent future events don't have truth values yet, in which case the future is genuinely open, or they do have truth values but truth doesn't drag necessity along with it, so the future is open in the sense that actually matters. I do not want to adjudicate that in a commencement address. I want the tension itself to do the structural work, and I want it to land in the gut for the parents rather than be heard as a puzzle. Concretely: the address has to be genuinely encouraging to a room of twenty-one-year-olds about the fact that their lives aren't already decided, without (a) asserting the loaded claim that their futures are 'unwritten' or 'wide open', which quietly presupposes the no-truth-value side, (b) retreating into the epistemic dodge that we just don't know what's coming — true, and useless as encouragement — or (c) letting it slide into the consoling thought that it's all settled anyway so they can relax. If the speech ends up secretly committing me to a view about bivalence, I've blown it.

Practical stuff: five or six minutes, so 800–900 words, and please don't name Aristotle inside the speech itself — the faculty will hear it as a wink and the parents will hear it as homework, so the shape of the argument should carry the weight rather than the citation. I'd like one concrete, homely image or anecdote that does the work of distinguishing 'it was always going to happen' from 'it had to happen' for people who have never thought about modality in their lives — something I could plausibly have from my own family, not a thought experiment with a philosopher's name stuck on it. And it has to actually end as a send-off, with warmth, not with a hedge about how the metaphysics is unresolved. Also, minor thing but there's alot riding on the tone: the ceremony is outdoors and there's rain in the forecast, which I keep thinking I should either use deliberately or avoid on purpose, and I honestly can't decide which.
