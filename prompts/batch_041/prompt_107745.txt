I'm a backend engineer at a small company that builds a decision-support tool. Customer plugs in a bunch of options — job offers, apartments, vendors, medical treatments, whatever — does a bunch of pairwise comparisons, and the tool spits out a ranked list with the best at the top. The whole thing rests on one relation: A is better than B.

I've been reading around on the philosophy side of this and its messing with my head. The relation is supposed to be transitive — if A beats B and B beats C, then A beats C — and for most of what our users compare it obviously is. But then there's this puzzle where it isn't. Take an option and make a tiny improvement; improve that one a tiny bit more; keep going down a long chain. Every step is clearly better than the last, but somewhere along the way you've arrived at something obviously worse than where you started. Or you do it in the other direction, making someones suffering a hair worse each step, and every step looks fine while the end of the chain is monstrous. I think the people who work on this call them spectrum arguments (Temkin? Rachels? I honestly can't remember who). And then there's the one where if your preferences go around in a loop, somebody can just charge you a small fee to keep going around the loop forever. I've also seen the word incommensurable thrown around for pairs where neither is better, neither is worse, and they're not equal either.

So here's what I want: a procedure — basically a spec — that my team can follow to decide when our tool is allowed to treat better-than as transitive and when it has to stop. Specifically:

- An ordered list of checks to run on a comparison set before we sort it, with what counts as passing each check and what counts as a fail.
- What to do when a set fails. The UI only has room for a single ranked list and product is going to fight me on this, so I need the defensible options (partial order? top tier only? just refuse and say so?) and which one the philosophy actually backs.
- How to tell an ordinary comparison from a spectrum one, when all we see is the user's pairwise answers and whatever numbers sit behind the options.
- Where the procedure bottoms out in a value call and has to hand off to the user or a human, plus how to word that hand-off so it doesn't read as a bug.
- What the tool definately cannot do, so I can write it in the docs and stop promising it in sales calls.

Then run it on three cases: (1) three job offers trading salary against commute against how much the work means to you; (2) an animal-welfare nonprofit customer comparing interventions that each differ from the last by a hair in how many animals they affect; (3) a user who says A beats B, B beats C, and then C beats A when we ask again — a cycle. For the third, show me what the procedure does when it catches the loop. And I'd like at least one of the three to end with the procedure telling me I can't ship a single ranked list, because I'd rather find that out now than from a customer.
