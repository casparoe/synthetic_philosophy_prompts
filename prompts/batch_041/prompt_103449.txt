Every August our village does a thing at the war memorial — wreaths, the Legion, the school choir, someone gives an address — and this year, the centenary, theyve asked me to do it. I teach history at the secondary school so I suppose I asked for it. The trouble is I dont know what to say about who was to blame, and I've come round to thinking that's the only thing worth saying anything about.

Here's the room I'm talking to. The British Legion branch will be there, and their secretary has already told me, kindly, that he hopes I'll tell it straight about Germany starting it. Our twin village in Bavaria is sending a delegation, mayor and all, plus the history teacher from their Gymnasium who I've been arguing with happily for twenty years, and they've got their own long-running argument going about their own memorial, which I gather isn't a happy subject. There's a young colleague of mine who thinks the whole ceremony is imperial nostalgia and will be watching to see whether I pretend otherwise. And there are about forty kids from the school who will be cold and bored.

So: what does it actually mean to hold a nation responsible for a war? I've been reading round it — the old Fischer row about whether Germany deliberately willed the war in 1914, the later pushback that says the powers sleepwalked into it, and that clause in the Versailles treaty that made Germany sign up to responsibility for the war and that everyone in Germany promptly christened the war-guilt lie. What I keep landing on is a distinction I want the speech built around, because I think it's the only honest way through this crowd: there's a difference between who caused it, who was guilty of it, and who is answerable for it now — and a memorial service can only ever be about the third of those. My grandad was at Passchendaele and never said one word about it to anybody, and I've decided that the fact he didn't know who to blame is itself part of what I want to say.

There's also a rule from the Bavarian history teacher that I want honoured, and it's harder than it sounds: you may not blame a whole people, and you may not let a whole people off.

Write me the address. Seven minutes spoken, roughly. No famous poems and no borrowed lines — nothing this crowd has heard at every other memorial they've ever stood at — plain words, and it has to work for the kids and for the German guests without either being talked down to. It has to be a speech and not a lecture; my colleague will smell a lecture from the back of the crowd. And it has to leave everyone able to walk up and lay a wreath afterwards without feeling either told off or let off the hook. I dont need you to settle the argument for me, I need the actual words.
