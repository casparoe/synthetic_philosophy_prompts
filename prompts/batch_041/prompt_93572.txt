I have been trying to settle when a prediction market's price may decide a question outright and when it may only inform a human decision, and my verdicts on particular cases do not obviously hang together. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they express, and to repair it or overturn my verdicts where they conflict.

Consider the following cases and my verdicts.

1. A market on whether a specified bill will pass by a stated date, currently pricing it at 0.7. I treat this as good evidence about the bill's fate and would let it guide a legislator's strategy. Verdict: informative, not binding.

2. A pair of conditional markets on a single metric — national income in a given year if a tax is enacted, against national income in that same year if it is not — with the two prices read from one book and compared by a fixed rule. Verdict: this is the form in which a market may actually choose the policy, because the quantity being priced is the difference the policy makes rather than the fate the world will take anyway.

3. A market on whether a particular bank will default within the month. Traders holding default positions then withdraw deposits and short the bank's paper, and the bank defaults. Verdict: once the price moves the world it is supposed to be measuring, it measures nothing I care about; I would not let it bind, and I regard the feedback as a defect of the market rather than a feature.

4. A market on the same bank's default in which one large trader pushes the price around to profit on a separate position and loses money in the attempt. Verdict: I am untroubled; the loss is the discipline.

5. A market whose resolution turns on a term the resolver must interpret — whether a policy reduced unemployment, say, when unemployment fell but the labor force also shrank. Verdict: I distrust it and would not bind.

6. A market in which those with private information about a firm trade against those without it. Verdict: I take the resulting price seriously; that is the whole point.

7. A market on whether a policy is just, or whether a war was right. Verdict: no. There is no fact for traders to converge on, and the price is only a poll with money behind it.

8. The case I keep going back and forth on: a decision market on whether to adopt a policy, in which the same traders who take positions on the conditional branches are also the officials who will implement the branch chosen. They have both the information and the power to make their information true.

My tentative principle is this: a market's price may bind a decision only when the outcome it tracks is independent of the market's own price, and the question has a determinate answer fixed by a resolution procedure that does not require interpreting a contested value. But the principle fails in opposite directions on cases I am confident about. Cases 3 and 4 both involve the price moving the world, so the independence clause fails in both, yet I want to keep 4 and discard 3. And case 2 — the case I care about most — fails the independence clause too, since enacting the policy is precisely what makes one branch actual; a strict reading would forbid the only use I trust.

Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets closest to my verdicts; identify for each clause the case that motivates it; say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed — a market on whether an assassination will succeed, used to select the target, and a market on whether a firm's product will be recalled, funded by the firm itself; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the resulting principle cannot accommodate and why I should give it up.
