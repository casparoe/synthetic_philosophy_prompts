I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, and the unit on eliminative materialism has always been the hardest to examine well. Students reliably do one of two things: they recite the Churchlands' forecast that folk psychology will be swept away as phlogiston was, or they recite the reply that they know they are in pain right now. Neither shows whether the student understands what the dispute is about. I want one essay question that neither move can answer.

Here is the wrinkle I want the question to turn on. Have the students consider what I will call the thin-survival scenario. Cognitive science converges on a mature theory on which believing that p is identified with a certain disposition to expect p-ish input and to behave accordingly; nothing in the theory's account of what it is to believe requires a proposition to be stored, tokened, or in any way represented. The word belief survives, and the theory even says beliefs cause behaviour, but the state it names is characterized wholly by its functional role and not by any content it carries. The question should force students to separate three things that the textbook discussion runs together: (i) eliminativism about the folk theory, the claim that the theory is false; (ii) eliminativism about the folk kinds, the claim that there are no beliefs; and (iii) eliminativism about content, the claim that nothing represents. The exam should ask whether thin survival vindicates eliminativism, refutes it, or leaves it untouched, and whether (i) through (iii) can come apart.

I also want to stipulate, inside the question, a criterion of kind-identity that students must first apply and then criticize. Call it limiting-case inheritance: a successor kind is the same kind as the folk kind just in case the successor theory's laws about it entail the folk platitudes as limiting cases. The essay should turn on whether thin survival satisfies this criterion, and on whether the criterion is too weak (letting in mere renamings) or too strong (ruling out genuine reductions).

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of roughly 750 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, give the marks each is worth out of 20, and add a one-line note for each on how candidates typically miss it. Include a short statement of the single move that separates an A answer from a B answer. I want the model answer to commit: it should take a definite line on whether thin survival vindicates or undercuts eliminativism, and defend that line, rather than laying out the readings and leaving me to choose.
