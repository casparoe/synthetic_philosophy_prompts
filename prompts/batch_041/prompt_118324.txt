I'm putting together a handout for the second meeting of a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and the session is on money pumps — the family of arguments in which an agent with a certain preference structure can be led through a sequence of trades to a sure loss, or to a position they would have paid to avoid. The assigned survey organizes the territory by listing the responses (resolute choice, sophisticated choice, the appeal to commitment devices, the claim that the argument shows only that you are exploitable rather than incoherent, and so on), which is fine as far as it goes but does nothing to tell a student which response they are actually committed to. What I want instead is a decision tree: each internal node a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, each leaf a named position on what money pumps show about diachronic rationality. Please build the tree from the underlying logical space rather than from the list of labels, then locate the standard positions within it — noting any combination of answers that no one occupies and briefly diagnosing whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flagging any pair of positions your tree cannot separate.

To keep the tree from collapsing into a popularity poll, build the early branches around the things I actually think the debate turns on. One is what the pump is supposed to demonstrate: that the agent's preferences are inconsistent, that the agent's will is weak or unstable over time, that the agent is merely predictable and therefore a resource for others, or that the preferences are fine but the disposition to act on them is not. A second is where the normative requirement bites: on the agent at each moment of choice, on the agent's whole life plan taken as a unit, or on the agent considered as a kind of thing that can be designed and redesigned. A third is what the correct response is supposed to be: revise the preferences, resolve to stick to the plan, arrange to be constrained in advance, update on the fact that one is being offered the trade, or decline to treat exploitability as a defect at all. A fourth, which I suspect the survey skips, is what work the examples are doing: distinguish pumps that turn on intransitive (cyclical) preferences from pumps that turn on preferences that merely change over time with no change in information, and from pumps that turn on risk attitudes that are not expected-utility, since I think the responses come apart depending on which of these a case exhibits.

For the cases that anchor the branch nodes, use at least these: a two-stage trade that extracts money from someone with a cycle of preferences; an agent whose ranking of two options reverses between an early and a later date with no new information arriving in between; an agent who would be paid now for forming an intention to do something tomorrow that, come tomorrow, they will have no reason to do; and an agent who is offered a series of trades they did not anticipate and could not have planned around. Phrase each node as a question about one of these cases, so the student is answering about the case rather than choosing a school. Where a recognized label exists, give it; where it does not, mark the leaf as unnamed and say in one sentence what its most awkward commitment is. The whole thing should fit on two pages, and please write it in American English.
