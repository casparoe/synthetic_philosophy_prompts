I'm taking a graduate seminar on research methodology, and the term has been organized around the replication crisis. The framing everyone reaches for is a war between frequentists and Bayesians: the frequentists are charged with encouraging p-hacking and with letting people read a p-value as if it were the probability that the hypothesis is false, while the Bayesians are charged with smuggling in priors that can be tuned after the fact to yield whatever conclusion the author wanted. I've grown suspicious that this axis is mostly a distraction, and that the actual fault line lies somewhere else — I have a candidate in mind, but I'd rather not state it, because I want to see whether you land on it independently or argue me out of it. So what I'd like is an essay that adjudicates the controversy rather than summarizing it.

To keep the discussion concrete, anchor it on the following hypothetical. A clinical trial reports its primary effect at p = .04. Post hoc, the authors note that the design had only about 35% power to detect the effect size they are now claiming. A skeptical reader reanalyzes the same data under a prior centered on zero with a scale set to half the smallest effect the field would regard as clinically meaningful, and obtains a Bayes factor of roughly 3 in favor of a nonzero effect. The journal editor now has to decide whether to publish, and a regulator has to decide whether to act on the result. Your essay should reach a verdict on two linked questions: first, in this case, whose evidence should carry more weight — the frequentist p-value, the Bayesian Bayes factor, or neither; and second, whether the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute is the right way to carve up what actually went wrong in the replication crisis, or whether the real problem is orthogonal to it. If you think the real problem is orthogonal, say what it is and why the usual framing obscures it.

I want a committed essay, not a survey: state your verdict plainly in the opening sentences and spend the rest of the piece earning it, giving your reasons and anticipating the strongest objection to your position and answering it. Do not close on a both-sides note. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
