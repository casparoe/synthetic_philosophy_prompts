You are an expert on Wittgenstein's later epistemology and, at the same time, someone with genuine experience writing decision procedures that institutions have to defend in writing. I am a final-year undergraduate taking a seminar on Wittgenstein's remarks on certainty, and I also work part-time at my university's research integrity office, where I am the first reader of complaints about published research. I have roughly twenty minutes per file to decide what the office does with a complaint before the review committee meets, and my supervisor has asked me to write up our triage procedure, because at the moment the outcome depends far too much on who happens to be on duty.

Here is the problem the procedure has to solve. Almost every complaint we receive is aimed not at a study's results but at one of the background propositions the study never argues for: that the instrument was working, that the reference material was what it said on the label, that the outcome measure measures the thing it is named after, that our own published record is the record. We cannot investigate all of these, and we cannot simply refuse them either. I want a procedure that makes the refusals principled rather than administrative, and that keeps faith with what the later Wittgenstein actually says about doubt, as far as I understand it: that doubting one thing always presupposes other things standing fast; that some propositions function as the hinges on which our questions turn rather than as answers within them; that we hold these without grounds, having absorbed them in training rather than established them by testing; and that they are not frozen forever, since the riverbed of our thinking can shift, though not because a single finding knocked it loose. Correct me if that reading is crude, but build on it rather than replacing it with a general essay on scepticism.

Our seminar instructor has been using a rough four-way distinction, and I would like you to adopt it, refine it, or tell me where it breaks down: (1) constitutive hinges, propositions that are part of what it is to do this kind of inquiry at all; (2) load-bearing empirical hinges, ordinary factual claims nobody in the field has ever had reason to doubt and that the field's methods cannot check without already presupposing them; (3) institutional hinges, propositions held fixed for legal or procedural reasons; and (4) propositions that only look like hinges because the person complaining has never seen them questioned. I suspect most complaints actually live in category 4, and I want the procedure to separate that category from the others rather than lumping everything under the heading of background assumption.

The central test I want the procedure to run — please check whether it is really the right test — is whether the proposition could in principle be re-opened by some recognized procedure in the field, however expensive or rarely used, without first presupposing itself. If yes, it is not a hinge for us and the complaint goes to investigation. If every available check presupposes it, then the complaint is not a complaint about evidence at all; it is a proposal to change the practice, and our office is the wrong body to hear it. Say clearly what the office should do instead, and be honest that the complainant will experience that as a brush-off.

The procedure itself should be a set of questions in order, each with (a) what counts as a sufficient answer, (b) what to do when no answer is available, (c) whether a clerk like me can answer it or whether it needs a scientist, and (d) where the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a person, because the remaining question is about values — how much disruption we are willing to impose on a researcher's career, whose certainty gets to count as the field's certainty — rather than about facts. Include explicit stop rules. Bear in mind that a doubt with no stopping point leaves the office incapable of concluding anything at all, and that this is a real failure mode and not merely a philosophical curiosity.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three complaints, at least one of which should produce an outcome I will find uncomfortable: first, a complaint that a laboratory's sample-handling instrument was out of calibration for the entire period of a study; second, a complaint that the standard measure used across an entire research area does not measure what its name says it measures; third, a complaint that our office's own file on a case was altered, so that the record I would rely on is itself in question. For each, say where the procedure lands and why, and give me one paragraph of plain language that I could send to the complainant.

Finally, include a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will get wrong, the ways in which treating something as a hinge can be abused as a way of protecting the assumptions of whoever happens to hold power in a field, and how the procedure should be revised as the field's riverbed shifts. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I am not asking you to attribute anything to anyone, and I would rather you define any term you need in your own words. If you need a concrete fact about a real case, invent the case and mark it as invented. Before you write anything, spend some time brainstorming the distinctions you think must survive translation into a procedure and the ones that can be dropped; then write the procedure for someone who will read it in two years and will not have taken the seminar.
