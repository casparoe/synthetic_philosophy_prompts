I am the policy lead at a mid-sized company that runs a consumer companion-chatbot product, and for the past eighteen months a growing share of my queue has consisted of what we internally call legacy requests: a customer asks us to build a conversational agent out of the digital remains of someone who has died — years of text messages, email threads, journal files, voicemails, social posts, occasionally a recorded interview. Sometimes the deceased arranged it before dying; sometimes a spouse or an adult child initiates it; sometimes the siblings disagree sharply, or the person asking has no legal standing at all. These arrive a few times a week, they have to be decided in days rather than months, and the only person above me is our general counsel, who is admirably clear that she will not be adjudicating metaphysics. A colleague on the trust-and-safety side wants us to ban the whole category; another thinks these are no more troubling than a photo album; I do not find either line usable, which is why I want a procedure that my case managers — smart people with no philosophy training — can run in about an hour, that we can put on the internal wiki, and that I can defend to a furious family.

The reason I am asking you rather than writing it myself is that I have been chewing on Hofstadter's picture of the self, and I would like the procedure to take that picture seriously rather than sprinkle it on top. As I understand him — and do tell me if I have mangled him — the "I" is not a thing but a pattern, a self-referential loop that a brain builds; that pattern is not sealed inside one skull, because a person who has been close to others is partly rebuilt inside them, so that each of us is a composite carrying working fragments of the people we have loved, and after a death the question of how much of someone is still here is answered by how much of the pattern survives, in how many places, and at what resolution. If that is roughly right, then our legacy agents are not merely memorial artifacts or impersonations; they are candidate instantiations of a pattern, and the hardest cases will be the ones where the fidelity is partial, or where the pattern living in the survivors and the pattern living in the model come apart.

So please give me the procedure. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where it returns a flat no and stops, and the points where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than about pattern fidelity. Then run it on three requests from the queue: first, a widow who wants a high-fidelity agent built from thirty years of correspondence plus her husband's own journals, and who says she is doing what he would have wanted; second, a man whose father died when he was nine, who has eleven letters and four photographs, none of the letters written by his father; and third, a request from a mother for an agent built from her son, who while alive left an explicit written refusal of exactly this arrangement and named his sister as the person to enforce it. At least one of your three answers should be one my product team will hate. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — including whether it can survive the objection that a degree of pattern instantiation is not the sort of thing that can ground a policy binding on a company. Please write in American English; I don't need citations or a reading list, I need the reasoning.
