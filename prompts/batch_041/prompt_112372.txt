I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and for a forthcoming piece on how we assign responsibility for disasters that seem to have no author, I sat in on an argument between two sources I respect — a philosopher of science and a former water-utility regulator — that I have not been able to settle on my own and would rather not paper over. They were arguing about a case I had described to them. A city water authority required its duty supervisor to walk the reservoir's valve gallery each morning and log the readings. One morning, for the first time in nine years, he skipped the round to attend a meeting. That afternoon a hairline crack in a valve gave way and flooded the district. Had he walked the gallery, he would have seen the seepage and closed the line in time; there is no dispute about that.

The regulator said the supervisor caused the flood. The philosopher said this was a category mistake: the crack caused the flood, and a man who was not there cannot be part of a causal chain — that he is to blame is a separate question, and conflating the two is exactly what lets people say that omissions cause things. The regulator answered that making a difference is all that causation has ever been, and that the supervisor made precisely that difference; if you want proof, imagine reaching in and making him do the round while everything else is held fixed, and the flood does not happen. The philosopher replied, first, that this test proves too much, since by it every resident who could have walked that gallery — and the mayor, and I — also caused the flood; and second, that one cannot intervene on an omission the way one intervenes on a crack, because there is no event there to alter, only a deliberation, and rewriting the man's mind is not holding the world fixed. The regulator said the test is not "anyone who could have" but "anyone who was required to," and that if this makes causation depend on our rules, so be it, since we do that everywhere: we say the short circuit caused the fire, not the oxygen, though both were necessary. The philosopher said that only shows we single out one cause for the purposes of blame; it does not show the unchosen ones were not causes, and if singling out is all the regulator means, then he has not shown that the supervisor caused the flood, only that we have decided to hold him responsible for it.

They went around like that for an hour and parted without either conceding. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: where each of them is right, where each overreaches, what the case actually establishes about whether an omission can cause anything, and which of the two, if either, comes closer to the truth overall.
