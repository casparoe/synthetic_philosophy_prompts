The idea: everyone knows the grounding objection to Molinism. Middle knowledge is supposed to be God's knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, i.e. facts about what some possible free creature would freely do in a given situation, and the objection is that if the creature's choice is genuinely free, nothing makes those counterfactuals true. Molinists usually reply either that they don't need a ground at all, or that they're grounded in something like the creature's essence or in God's own knowledge. I don't want to re-run that fight. What I want to know is whether it's one case of a bigger question: can there be true, determinate counterfactuals about what a genuinely indeterministic thing would do? A decaying atom seems to hand us chances rather than a fact about what it would have done, and yet ordinary dispositional claims ('that glass would have shattered if you'd dropped it') look like determinate would-facts about things that needn't have behaved as they did. So if the Molinist grounds the counterfactual of freedom in a disposition or propensity of the creature's will, does the grounding problem dissolve, get relocated, or get worse — because a disposition that reliably yields one outcome starts looking like determinism, which is the thing the doctrine was built to avoid?
