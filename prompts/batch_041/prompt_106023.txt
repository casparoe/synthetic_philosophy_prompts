I am a retired physical scientist who has spent the last several years reading economics and its philosophy, and I keep meeting a defence of the standard model of choice that I would like you to audit. The defence runs roughly as follows. The perfectly rational expected-utility maximizer stands to actual human deciding as a frictionless plane stands to real surfaces in mechanics: no real plane is frictionless, but the idealization is what lets one isolate the core dynamics, after which friction is added back as a correction term. By this light, satisficing, fast-and-frugal heuristics, and the rest of what goes under bounded rationality are friction — small, second-order departures from an account of the agent that is essentially right.

Please assess how far the analogy holds, treating it as an argument rather than a picture. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first, the correspondence, stating what plays the role of the plane, the friction, the core dynamics, the correction term, and — since a physicist would ask — the coefficient by which the friction is measured; second, the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the use to which the analogy is put, namely licensing the rational core and demoting bounded rationality to a correction; third, the apparent disanalogies that turn out not to matter; fourth, whether the conclusion the analogy is meant to support survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table; and fifth, a better analogy, drawn if you can from a regime in which a qualitatively new theory is required rather than a perturbation of the old one, or else a clear reason why no better analogy is available.
