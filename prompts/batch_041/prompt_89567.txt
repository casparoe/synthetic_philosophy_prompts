I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and the chapter I'm drafting is about what the competing readings of the Second Amendment presuppose about political membership — the way each of them tacitly answers the question of who counts as a bearer of the right, and what kind of act bearing arms is supposed to be. Rather than survey the post-Heller doctrine, I've been running the chapter through a single episode, and I'd like you to work it properly. In 1967, armed members of the Black Panther Party — who had been conducting armed patrols of Oakland neighborhoods to watch the police — entered the California State Capitol in Sacramento to protest a bill that would ban the public carrying of loaded firearms. The legislature responded by passing the bill as an urgency measure; Governor Reagan, who was on the Capitol grounds that day and remarked afterward that he saw no reason for a citizen to be carrying a loaded weapon on the street, signed it into law, and the National Rifle Association supported it, which reliably surprises the undergraduates I teach it to. The facts are not in dispute and I don't want them re-litigated.

The four readings I'm working with are these: the collective-right reading, on which the amendment protects the states' capacity to maintain an organized militia and confers nothing on individuals; the individual-rights reading, on which it protects a pre-political right of each person to keep and bear arms, at most loosely tied to militia service; the insurrectionist reading, on which its point is to preserve the people's capacity to resist a tyrannical government; and a broadly neo-republican reading I've been trying to develop myself, on which what the right protects is not personal self-defense but non-domination — the citizen's standing to resist arbitrary power, whether it comes from the state or from private actors the state will not restrain. For each reading, tell me who the right-holders are, what the Panthers were doing in its terms (exercising the right, exceeding it, or something the theory has no category for), what the legislature's action amounts to, and whether the episode confirms the theory, leaves it untouched, or is an anomaly for it. Then run the analysis the other way, which is the part I actually care about: what does the episode expose about each reading that its own defenders would rather not say? I'm particularly interested in whether the collective-right reading can even describe the Panthers — the militia it protects is one the state organizes, and therefore one the state can decline to recognize — and in whether that same problem infects the question of the unorganized militia that the federal statutes still define as the adult male citizenry of military age, which on its face would have included them. I also want to know whether my neo-republican reading has an internal difficulty, since an armed citizenry is supposed to be a standing check on arbitrary power, and here the check was legislated out of existence by the very body it was meant to check, with the country's main gun-rights organization acquiescing and hardly anyone objecting. Don't adjudicate the history or tell me whether the Panthers were right; be honest about where each reading goes silent, and say which of the four earns its keep on this case and which the case quietly refutes.
