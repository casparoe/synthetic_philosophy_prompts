I'm in a graduate seminar on Quine, and a classmate has reconstructed an argument that I can't get out of my head — partly because I suspect it proves more than anyone in the room wants it to. We had been working through Quine's naturalism, his insistence that reality is to be described from within science rather than from some prior philosophical tribunal, alongside his habit of treating moral value as a different sort of thing from scientific belief: closer to an expression of attitude, or a projection of feeling, than to a hypothesis that observation could confirm or refute. My classmate built the following out of that:

(1) For a naturalist, a discourse earns its place in the web of belief only if its claims are answerable to experience — only if observation can, in principle, count for or against them. (2) Moral claims are not answerable to experience in that way. When one person holds an act wrong and another holds it permissible, no possible observation settles the matter, and neither party's refusal to budge is a failure of rationality. (3) So moral claims do not earn a place in the web of belief. (4) So they are not statements of fact at all; they are expressions of attitude, or the verbal shadow of custom, and there is nothing for them to be true or false about.

I can almost live with (4) as a claim about morality — I'm not sure I want to, but I can. What stops me is that the same reasoning seems to run just as well on mathematics and logic. No observation disconfirms a theorem; no experiment forces us to abandon a rule of inference; and the norms the naturalist needs in order to say that careful science beats superstition — prefer the simpler theory, prefer the more predictive one, do not be gulled — are not themselves answerable to experience either. So the argument, if it works, appears to demote arithmetic, logic, and the norms of inquiry to mere attitude, which is not a price I think a naturalist can pay. Now I can't tell whether my classmate has located a genuine fault line in the position or has simply built a trap out of a slogan.

You are an expert on Quine's naturalism and holism and on metaethics, and you are good at diagnosing arguments that prove too much. Think carefully before answering, and begin by sketching a few candidate diagnoses before committing to one. Tell me which step or steps I should reject, explain why that step looked so compelling in the first place, and then price the alternatives: what does each available rejection cost, both for the moral conclusion and for the naturalist's hold on science, mathematics, and the normativity of inquiry? If the honest verdict is that the argument is valid and something simply has to give, say which commitment is least costly to surrender, and say what is lost when we surrender it.
