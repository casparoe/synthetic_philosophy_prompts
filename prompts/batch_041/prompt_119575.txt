Bit of a weird request, so bear with me. I'm in this small reading group — four of us, we meet above a pub in Leeds, it started as an excuse to avoid the gym — and for the last few months we've been working through the literature on what everyone calls fanaticism in decision theory. You know the shape of it: if you accept expected value maximisation, and you accept that some outcomes are so much better than others that no finite amount of ordinary good can outweigh them (or even just that value has no ceiling), then it seems to follow that a miniscule probability of an enormous payoff should dominate everything else you might do. So you should hand your wallet to the man who says he'll otherwise torture an astronomical number of people in a simulation, and you should spend your life chasing a one-in-ten-to-the-thirtieth chance of averting a catastrophe rather than doing something with a decent chance of helping a much smaller number of people. The arguments on both sides are basically all on the table by now. Those who swallow the conclusion say it just falls out of a few extremely weak-looking conditions on rational preference — transitivity, completeness, some continuity or Archimedean requirement — together with a denial of any value ceiling. Those who choke on it say we should drop one of those conditions, or go lexicographic, or impose a probability threshold below which small chances get rounded down to zero, or conclude that the whole framework is the culprit, that expected value was only ever meant as a rough-and-ready guide for humans who can't estimate tiny probabilities in the first place. Every fix someone proposes seems to break something else we all wanted, and that's where the group keeps getting stuck.

What I want isn't a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of why it won't move. Is this a substantive disagreement about value, a disagreement about the axioms of rational preference, or two groups quietly answering different questions under one heading — one asking what an ideal agent's preferences must look like, the other asking what an actual person should do with a number they got off the back of an envelope? My own hunch, probably wrong, is the last one: that the debate stalls because the bridge principle from ideal theory to real advice is never stated, and each side is assuming a different one without noticing. Test that against what each side actually says, and then tell me what kind of disagreement you think it really is — a clash of bedrock intuitions that no argument could shift, a hidden empirical question about what to do when you can't estimate the probability at all, a verbal dispute about what 'should' is doing here, or just a hard problem nobody has cracked yet. And given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress: some distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — or the honest admission that we should stop expecting the two sides to converge and just pick a convention and move on. British English please, and don't bother with a reading list, I've already got the papers.
