I keep running into talk of causation by omission — courts finding that a defendant's failure to act caused a death, ethicists saying that a gardener who forgets to water the plant caused it to die — and I think the whole way of speaking is confused. An omission is not an event; it is the absence of one. Causation, whatever else it is, is a relation between things that happen. So an omission cannot be a cause, and when someone insists that it is, they are really making a claim about responsibility — that the agent had a duty and did not discharge it — dressed up in causal vocabulary. And in any case, a stretch of inaction answers to indefinitely many descriptions (he failed to water the plant, he failed to fill the bath with it, he failed to douse it in bleach), with no fact of the matter as to which of these is the omission that caused the outcome; only a norm can pick one out. The causal question therefore collapses into the normative one, and we should drop the causal language and argue about duties directly.

Is that right?
