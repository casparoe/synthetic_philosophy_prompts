My neighbor Priya and I spent most of Saturday evening on her porch re-arguing the corner lot, and I came away less sure of my own position than when we started. Two years ago I helped draft our neighborhood association's letter supporting the city's corridor upzoning; I said at the time, and still believe, that owning a house does not give you a veto over what gets built within sight of it. The association then lobbied for a conservation overlay that exempted our own four blocks from the upzoning, and I signed that petition as well, largely because I did not want to lose the light in my kitchen. Now a nonprofit developer wants to put a five-story building of about forty apartments on the vacant lot two blocks east, a third of them reserved for households with very low incomes, and the residents there have asked me to speak against it at Thursday's hearing on the grounds of scale, parking, and the character of the street. Priya says my position is incoherent: I have arranged for my own street to be exempt from exactly the density I claim to want, and if I show up against this project I will simply be saying not here in the vocabulary of planning. She proposed a test — whether I would still object if I did not know in advance which of the two blocks I would end up living on — and I could not give her a clean answer. I do think there is a difference between building on a vacant lot and rezoning the street where I live, and that people can legitimately care about the character of their neighborhood, but I cannot get that difference to hold still, and I suspect I would not have read the parking study at all if the lot were three blocks further away. What should I do at Thursday's hearing, and is there a version of my position that is not just self-interest wearing a planning vocabulary?
