I'm putting together materials for a graduate reading group on the philosophy of self-deception, and I've hit a wall that I think is more than a difference of temperament. On one side stands what I'll call the literalist or divisionist camp: self-deception really is a person deceiving herself, so it requires something like an intention -- however inarticulate or sub-personal -- to bring about in oneself a belief one has reason to regard as false, and it therefore requires some partitioning of the mind, since the deceiver and the deceived cannot be one undivided subject at one time any more than a person can knowingly lie to herself. On the other side stands the deflationist camp: the label is a misnomer if read literally, and once we stop modelling self-deception on one person deceiving another, the phenomenon turns out to be an unmysterious species of motivated irrationality -- biased evidence-gathering, selective attention, wishful thinking that has gone far enough to issue in belief -- with no intention to deceive and no contradictory beliefs held anywhere in the picture. My friend and I have now spent three evenings arguing past each other, and I suspect we are not always disagreeing about the same thing.

Please draft the joint statement we could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want, first, a numbered list of claims both camps can accept: what phenomenon we are both trying to explain, which standard cases any account has to accommodate (the husband who is convinced of his wife's fidelity against everything he sees and who seems never to have decided to believe it; the person who sincerely denies being jealous while everyone around them can see it; the patient who somehow never gets round to opening the envelope with the results), and which assumptions would beg the question against one side and so must be kept out of the shared premises. Second, an exact statement of each remaining disagreement, given twice: once in wording the divisionist would accept as a fair rendering of her own position, once in wording the deflationist would accept, with the two formulations set side by side so it is visible what each turns on. Third, the cruxes -- I expect at least these: whether deception as such requires an intention to deceive; whether the self-deceiver must hold contradictory beliefs at some time, and whether spreading them over time rescues the literalist or merely concedes the point; whether the phenomenon needs a divided mind or only a divided attention; whether an unmotivated case could ever count; and whether the deflationary account leaves anything for the term to pick out that wishful thinking and biased belief-formation do not already cover -- and for each crux a case, experiment, or argument that both camps agree would bear on it, together with the prediction each camp makes in advance about how it comes out. Fourth, a short section saying which parts of our dispute are merely verbal: in particular, whether the literalist's insistence on the intention requirement is a claim about the psychology of self-deception or a stipulation about how the phrase ought to be used, and if the latter, what substantive disagreement survives once the stipulation is set aside. Fifth, for each camp, the result or argument that would count as evidence against its own view, and, if there is one, the finding both camps agree would actually move the debate.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, each camp's formulation, what both agree would bear on it, and each camp's prediction, then the two closing sections. Aim for something in the region of 1,500-2,000 words. Don't attach names, dates, or citations to any of the positions -- I want the substance, and neither of us is interested in a literature review; if a view is worth stating, state it.
