So I spent thirty-five years doing field ecology and then atmospheric chemistry — lots of regressions, lots of p-values, and honestly I never thought hard about what any of it meant until I retired and started reading philosophy of science properly. Now I'm hung up on something and I want you to push back hard on it. My position, roughly stated, is this: the replication crisis — the fact that so much of psychology and biomedicine won't replicate — is at bottom a frequentist disease. Null-hypothesis significance testing, the .05 cutoff, p-values that answer a question nobody actually asked, optional stopping, the whole Rube Goldberg contraption: it's the machinery that lets people fool themselves and each other, and if the field had gone Bayesian the way it should have, most of this would dissolve. Priors force you to say out loud what you believed before the data; a posterior is a direct statement about the hypothesis instead of a statement about hypothetical data you didn't collect; the whole thing is just more honest. My old labmate, who converted years ago and now runs a Bayesian shop, tells me I'm naive, and I'd like to know whether he's right.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against my view, sorted by how decisive it would be rather than just listed. In particular: is the frequentist framework actually the culprit, or is the culprit the incentives — publish or perish, the file drawer, and the fact that p-hacking is definately just as easy under a Bayesian banner, isn't it? How would I tell those two stories apart empirically rather than by trading anecdotes? I keep hearing that Bayesian analyses have their own ways of being gamed, that priors can be chosen after seeing the data, that Bayes factors can be tuned, and something about a paradox where a sharp null ends up with a big posterior despite a small p-value — I want to know whether these are serious objections or just frequentist sour grapes. Also, what does my view predict that a rival view doesn't? If the crisis were really about inference, I'd expect the Bayesian-heavy subfields and journals to be replicating better — is that actually the case, and what would I do if it turned out they're not? And be honest with me about the insulation problem: I've noticed that every time someone shows Bayesians screwing up too, my friends say "well, that's not real Bayesianism." Is my position in danger of becoming unfalsifiable, and if so, does that doom it or is it just the price of any view worth holding?

I'll tell you what would move me most: not a philosophy-of-statistics argument, but an actual comparison — some domain, or journal, or reform where the frequentist apparatus stayed put and things got better anyway, or where a switch to Bayesian methods happened and nothing changed. So rank the defeaters from "this should change my mind" down to "this is merely awkward," tell me which ones the Bayesians themselves would concede actually count against them rather than just deflecting, and give me your honest bottom line on whether I'm holding a view or a slogan. I don't need citations or a reading list, I just want the argument. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just write it the way you'd write me a long email, since I read these on my phone.
