I am a retired experimental scientist — forty years at the bench, mostly on reaction kinetics — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I have reached the point where I can usually tell an argument from a well-dressed analogy, though not always which one I am looking at.

At a seminar last month, a historian defended counterfactual history against the charge that it is idle fiction. His defence was an analogy: counterfactual history, he said, is a controlled experiment. The experimentalist holds every other variable fixed and perturbs one; the counterfactual historian does the same thing in thought, holding the rest of the past fixed and altering a single event — the driver does not take the wrong turn, the order is countermanded — and then reads off what follows. Since the controlled experiment is our best warrant for a causal claim, he concluded, counterfactual history is a legitimate method for establishing causal claims about the past. He went further: it also dissolves talk of historical inevitability, because a claim that some outcome had to happen is at bottom a claim about what would have happened under variation, and is answerable to counterfactual reasoning of precisely this kind.

I have run experiments for a living, and the analogy made me uneasy in a way I could not articulate. Please audit it. Give me the correspondence table first — what in the laboratory plays the role of the intervention, the control, the held-fixed background, the randomisation, the replication, the measured outcome — and then tell me where the mapping fails. I am particularly interested in whether the warrant of an experiment comes from the fact that one thing is varied, or from something else entirely: the design, the randomisation, the repetition, the fact that the held-fixed background is enforced physically rather than merely stipulated. If it is the latter, can the counterfactual historian reproduce that something else, or only its appearance? It would also help to know how the historian's procedure compares with the ways we draw causal conclusions in science without intervening — natural experiments, instruments, before-and-after comparisons — and whether the assumptions those methods must declare can even be stated, let alone checked, in his case. While you are at it, say whether the demand that the counterfactual world resemble ours except where the stipulation says otherwise is a substantive constraint or merely a convenience.

Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he drew from those that are merely picturesque. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives on what remains — both halves of it, the methodological claim and the claim about inevitability — and what analogy would have done better.

Finally, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
