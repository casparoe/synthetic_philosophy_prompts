I am a third-year philosophy student at a university, currently taking a seminar on applied ethics and the philosophy of mind. Our department runs a student podcast, and I have been asked to interview a visiting philosopher who defends the hedonistic imperative: the view that we should use biotechnology to abolish suffering across all sentient life, not merely reduce it. The guest is a systematic defender of the view and is likely to have ready replies to the usual objections. I want the interview to be substantive and to avoid letting the guest simply restate the thesis.

Please act as an expert interviewer and philosopher working at the intersection of ethics, affective science, and biotechnology. Before drafting the questions, think carefully and brainstorm the most philosophically interesting pressure points in the hedonistic imperative. Then draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered as a conversation, that probe the view's internal commitments rather than its familiar slogans. For each question, include a follow-up that is keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give.

I specifically want at least one question each on the following:
- The distinction between suffering as an aversive phenomenal state and suffering as a negative reinforcement or error signal. If biotechnology removes the aversive feel but leaves the signal intact, has the hedonistic imperative abolished suffering or merely dissociated it from motivation?
- The gradient problem: whether an agent can be motivated to learn, plan, and improve its condition without any negatively valenced state. Press the guest on what would replace the gradient if suffering is removed.
- The consent and standing problem: the hedonistic imperative may require editing future beings who cannot consent. Does the view treat presently existing sentient beings as mere means to a future utopia, or does it presuppose a controversial impersonal value?
- The value-of-suffering objection: the claim that suffering is partly constitutive of achievement, deep relationships, art, or meaning. Ask whether the guest is denying that claim or redefining value in a way that begs the question.
- The feasibility and ecological problem: if suffering is abolished in wild animals, what happens to ecosystems, predation, and the broader project of wild animal welfare? Is the view committed to a radical redesign of nature, and is that a feature or a bug?
- The evidence question: what empirical or conceptual discovery would count against the hedonistic imperative, and what would the guest say to someone who thinks the view is unfalsifiable.

Please also include one question that forces the guest to distinguish the hedonistic imperative from the experience machine and from mere wireheading, and one that asks whether the view is committed to maximizing pleasure or only to eliminating suffering. The questions should be respectful but pressing, and they should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. End with a question that invites the guest to state the strongest objection to their own view and how they would answer it.
