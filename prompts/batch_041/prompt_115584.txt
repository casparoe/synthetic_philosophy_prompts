I am a high school student working on a project for my ethics class, and I keep running into a pattern in the arguments I am reading that I do not have a name for. Someone argues that because a certain practice causes suffering, we ought to stop it. When asked why that follows, they add that we ought not to cause unnecessary suffering. When asked why that follows, they add that causing unnecessary suffering is wrong. When asked why that follows, they say that wrong just means what we ought not to do. Each addition is presented as a clarification of the words rather than as a new moral commitment, and the original conclusion is never actually secured; the argument simply grows a longer tail. I have noticed the same shape in arguments about honesty, charity, and even in a math class, when someone tried to justify a rule of inference by writing it down as another line in the proof.

I have heard two nearby ideas. One is the general claim that you cannot derive an ought from an is. The other is a puzzle in which someone asks a logician to justify a rule of inference, and the logician keeps adding the rule as a premise, so the request repeats forever. But neither seems to capture exactly what I am seeing. The first is about a single gap between description and prescription; the second is about logic in general and does not involve the peculiar trick of dressing up the added premise as a definition or a clarification. What I want to know is whether there is a name for this hybrid: a moral argument that tries to cross the gap by adding bridge principles that are themselves normative but are presented as semantic clarifications, generating a regress. If there is an established term, what is it and how is it defined? If there is not, please coin one and define it, distinguishing it from the two nearby ideas and from the naturalistic fallacy. I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
