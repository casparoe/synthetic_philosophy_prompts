I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on aggregation and distribution in ethics, and I need one essay question on the long-running dispute over whether, in a rescue, a greater number of lives saved is by itself a reason to save them. The difficulty is that this dispute has a canonical shape — the anti-aggregative challenge associated with Taurek, the coin-flip verdict, and the aggregative rejoinder — and a student who has merely memorized that shape can produce a passable answer without reasoning about anything. I want a question that cannot be answered that way.

To that end, I have invented two cases that I would like the question to be built around. In the first, the two natural readings of the aggregative claim pull apart:

The Flooded Mine. A flood has sealed workers into two chambers of a mine. The rescue team has the time and equipment to open exactly one chamber. The left chamber holds five workers; the right chamber holds one. The five on the left are near the end of their lives and, if rescued, would each be expected to live about four more years. The worker on the right is young and, if rescued, would be expected to live about forty more years. No worker in either chamber knows or is related to anyone in the other; no one's survival depends on anyone else's; the team cannot open both chambers or divide its effort.

In the second, the number of lives is equal in prospect but not in certainty:

The Long Odds. The team can open a chamber it knows to contain one worker, or attempt to open an unstable chamber containing five; the attempt will free all five with probability 0.3 and will fail, killing all five, with probability 0.7.

The question should require the candidate to bring a single principle to bear on both cases and to defend it. It should force them to say: what the aggregative claim would have to mean for the Flooded Mine to have a determinate answer, and whether that claim is best understood as counting persons or as summing welfare; what the anti-aggregative position implies about the Flooded Mine, and whether it can be stated without collapsing into the view that nothing is at stake between the options; whether the Long Odds case is settled by the same principle or by a different one, and what that reveals about whether the aggregative claim is being applied at the moment of choice or to the outcome; and what the strongest objection to the candidate's own position is, and how they would meet it.

Please give me three things.

First, the essay question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, with a stated time allowance (about seventy-five minutes) and an expected length (roughly 1,200 words). It should read as a single question rather than a list, and it should not name any of the moves I have listed above or otherwise signal where the difficulties lie. If you judge that the Long Odds case overloads a single question, you may drop it, but only on the condition that the remaining question still forces the candidate to confront the tension between counting persons and summing welfare.

Second, a model answer of about 650 words, written as a strong candidate would write it: it should take a definite position on the cases it addresses, reason from the cases as stated rather than from remembered texts, and be explicit about which reading of the aggregative claim it adopts and why. It must not cite or quote any source; the students will have no texts in front of them, and I do not want a model answer that depends on them.

Third, a marking scheme worth thirty marks, built from six to eight must-hit points. For each point, give the marks it carries and a one-line description of the characteristic error that shows a candidate has missed it — the specific confusion, not a generic plea for more detail. Then state, in two or three sentences, what a first-class answer must contain that a merely competent one will lack.

Assume that the candidates have studied this debate in class and know the names usually attached to its poles; assume nothing else. The question should reward a candidate who can hold one principle steady across both cases, and it should penalize one who answers each case on its own terms without noticing that the two answers are in tension.
