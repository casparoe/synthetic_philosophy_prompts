I retired from a chemistry department about four years ago and, mainly to stop myself from reorganising the garage every morning, I've been sitting in on a grad seminar and a reading group that meets above a pub. Last term somebody floated a view they called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and I've been chewing on it ever since, because I honestly can't tell whether it's deep or just a fudge. As I understand it: suffering and pleasure are not commensurable at all — no amount of anybody's pleasure outweighs one person's serious suffering — but that only kicks in once the suffering is severe enough; below that severity threshold, small pains and small pleasures trade off in the ordinary way. So a stubbed toe can lose to a good dinner, but agony is never outweighed by any ocean of bliss, however large.

I'd like to argue with a real defender of it instead of the cardboard version I keep building in my head. So please play a committed lexical threshold negative utilitarian — someone who has lived with this position for years, knows where the objections are coming from, will happily concede small points but will not surrender the threshold itself. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation across a table, stay in character until I say stop, and don't step out of the role to tell me what you're doing or to explain the view from outside. If I ask you something factual, answer as your character would.

My opening: I spent forty years measuring things on continuous scales, and a threshold like yours looks to me like a place where the exchange rate between pain and pleasure drops from finite to zero over an arbitrarily small increment of suffering, and I've never seen a natural quantity behave like that without something underneath it. Worse, I think I can build you a heap. Take a pain one notch below your threshold — you say it's commensurable, so a good dinner outweighs it. Fine. Now take ten thousand people each with that same sub-threshold pain; by your own lights each of those is outweighed by one dinner, so the whole ten thousand should be buyable with ten thousand dinners, even though the pile of them sounds to me an awful lot like the agony you say no amount of pleasure can touch. Meanwhile one person one notch above the threshold outweighs everything the human race will ever enjoy. So tell me: is the discontinuity real, or is the threshold doing work that a careful description of what's actually happening to these people wouldn't support? And if the answer is that the threshold applies to how a single life goes overall rather than to any one twinge, then I want to know what happens when I parcel that life out across a crowd.
