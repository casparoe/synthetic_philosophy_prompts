I spent 35 years as a radio astronomer and only got into philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I trip over the vocabulary. Here's what's been eating at me. I keep running into this dark forest idea -- everybody stays quiet because the first thing that goes loud gets wiped out by whoever's listening -- and people trot it out like its the obvious answer to why we've never heard anything. But nobody ever tells me what an answer here is even supposed to look like. So before I pick a side I want the spec sheet: what does any theory of the Great Silence have to deliver to count as a real theory at all? Write me the desiderata.

What I want in it:

- The observations and cases it has to get right, with the case behind each one. We've been leaking radio for about a century now and nobody's answered; the galaxy is old enough that somebody should've had a huge head start on us; and so on.
- The distinctions it can't just collapse. Here are two I've been turning over, and I want you to tell me whether they're genuine requirements or just my own hang-ups. First, the difference between silence thats chosen and silence thats structural -- a civilization that decides to shut up, versus one that simply never has the reach or the technology to be heard in the first place. Second, what I've been calling the last-caller problem: a dark forest is supposed to be an equilibrium where nobody transmits, but somebody had to build the first transmitter, so the theory owes me an account of how a noisy start settles into a quiet one, or a reason why it wouldn't. If those are real desiderata, say so and say what makes them bite; if they aren't, tell me where I've gone wrong.
- The things it can't contradict -- whatever we actually know about how stars and planets form, how common the raw materials are, how long a technological species could plausibly last, what interstellar travel and communication really cost.

Then flag any pair or subset of these that you think can't all be satisfied at once. Thats the part I actually care about -- if the list is overconstrained, that's itself the result. And mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to like. At the end, briefly, how the dark forest, the zoo idea, the rare-earth line, and the "they're already here / we're in a simulation" options do against your list. Dont need much on the scoring, just enough to see which single requirement does the most killing.
