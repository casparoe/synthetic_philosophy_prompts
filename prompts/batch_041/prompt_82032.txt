I'm TAing the last session of a grad seminar in formal epistemology and I have to run the discussion on the problem of old evidence. The students have all seen the standard setup (you already know e, so P(e|h) = 1 either way, so h's predicting e can't move your credence — but we all feel like that anomalous-orbit case really did confirm general relativity). What they haven't seen is any of the proposed fixes, so I want to hand out a ranked list instead of lecturing.

Here are the candidate responses I've collected, in no order. Some are things I've read, one is a friend's unpublished idea, and I've deliberately stripped the names off because I want them evaluated on the merits:

(a) The counterfactual/rewind move: ask what the agent's credence in h would have been if she'd never learned e, and use that as the baseline. Problem: the answer depends on how you imagine the rewind going, and you seem to get different numbers depending on whether you remove e alone or everything downstream of e.

(b) The "don't set P(e)=1" move: your actual credence function was never defined over e being false, but we can idealize one that is; do the confirmation math there and then conditionalize back.

(c) The "what's new isn't e, it's the fit" move: the genuinely new fact is that h entails (or makes probable) e, and that fact wasn't known before, so conditionalize on that instead.

(d) The bootstrapping move: let h help you derive e from other things you already accept, so h gets credit indirectly without ever making e more likely.

(e) The predictionist move: bite the bullet — old evidence confirms nothing, only successful novel predictions do, and our intuition about the orbit case is just wrong or is tracking something else.

(f) The deflationary move: confirmation isn't a psychological event; "e confirms h" is a logical/evidential relation between propositions, and the fact that the agent came to know e before h is simply irrelevant to it.

(g) My friend's idea, which I genuinely can't tell if it's deep or circular: keep track of when you learned each bit of evidence, and let each item's evidential weight be a function of the order it arrived in, so the same evidence can confirm h a lot if it arrived after h and not at all if before — "the likelihood is time-stamped."

What I want: sort these into tiers (S/A/B/C/D, or however many tiers you actually need) by how well each would survive in a real seminar, give each one a score out of 10, and two or three sentences on why it lands where it does — including which objection I should press on it when it comes up. Two extra things. First, I want the ranking to hold up under two cases the standard discussion doesn't handle: (i) the hypothesis wasn't in the agent's hypothesis space at all until after she learned e, so there's no prior to rewind to; (ii) the evidence in question is the agent's own past reasoning — she's now trying to figure out whether her earlier argument for h actually supports h, given that she's known the argument's conclusion all along. Second, tell me which single item I should present first in the session and which one I should save for last, and why the order matters.

I definately need actual differentiation here — don't tell me they're all fine or all flawed, and if two of them turn out to be the same move in different clothes, say so and rank them as one.
