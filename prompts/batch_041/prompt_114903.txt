I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've hit a puzzle I can't get past. One view of the mind — epiphenomenalism — says that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but cause nothing themselves, not even the beliefs or statements we form about them. That seems to generate a problem sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if a pain can't cause me to wince or to rate it a seven, how does anything I say about my experience end up being about the experience, rather than about the brain state that produced both?

What puzzles me is a practice that seems to assume the opposite. Clinical medicine, along with a good deal of law and insurance, treats a patient's self-report as the gold standard for certain inner states, pain above all. The 0-to-10 pain scale has no instrument behind it; the number the patient gives is taken as authoritative evidence of the state itself, and we judge treatments by whether that number falls. Compare blood pressure or blood oxygen, where we never trust the patient's own sense of things and reach for a device instead. So for some states we treat the report as the only possible evidence and as essentially transparent to the state, while for others we treat self-report as worthless.

What commitments does that practice presuppose about the relation between an experience and the judgment or utterance that expresses it? Does it require that the experience cause the report, or could it be licensed by a picture in which the experience and the report are both downstream of a common cause and merely correlated? Reconstruct the view of mind the practice commits us to, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in how we assess pain and other inner states if epiphenomenalism turned out to be true. Please answer in American English.
