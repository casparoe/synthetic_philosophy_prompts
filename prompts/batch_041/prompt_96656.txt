I am preparing a graduate seminar on indicative conditionals and the triviality results, and I want to build a consistency exercise around a visiting speaker's adjudications. Over the past term, Professor V has given the following verdicts on cases involving a fair six-sided die and simple factual claims A, B, and C:

- For the conditional "if the die lands even, it lands on a six," she assigns credence 1/3 and says this just is the conditional probability of a six given even.
- For the conditional "if the die lands even, it lands on a four or a six," she assigns credence 1/3, again matching the conditional probability.
- For the conditional "if the die lands on a six, it lands on an even number," she assigns credence 1.
- For the conditional "if the die lands on a six, it lands on a four," she assigns credence 0.
- She denies that "if the die lands even, it lands on a six" is equivalent to "the die does not land even or it lands on a six."
- She says the simple conditional-probability thesis fails for conditionals embedded under probability operators: she does not equate "it is probable that if A, then C" with "if A, then it is probable that C."

The triviality results are standardly taken to show that the simple thesis cannot be extended to all conditionals without giving up at least one of the following: the ratio definition of conditional probability, the claim that conditionals are propositions, closure of probability under Boolean operations, or the application of the thesis to embedded conditionals.

Now consider a new case: a conditional whose antecedent is itself a conditional, "if (if A then B), then C," where A, B, and C are simple factual claims and P(if A then B) > 0. What will Professor V say about the probability of this conditional? I want you to identify the candidate rules that her verdicts so far are consistent with, say what each predicts about the new case, give your best guess about her verdict and the reasons for it, and name a further case that would best distinguish the rules.
