I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy working on the ethics of promising, and I keep running into a pattern in the common law of contract that I suspect presupposes a theory of promising no philosopher would want to own once it is stated flatly. The doctrine of consideration holds that a promise is not legally enforceable unless it is supported by a bargained-for exchange: a bare promise to do something, however deliberately and sincerely made, binds no one in court. That looks like a considered refusal of the idea that the promise-act itself generates an obligation, with the law locating the ground of obligation in the exchange or in the reliance the promise induces rather than in the promise. Yet the same body of law, once a contract exists, remedies breach by putting the promisee in the position she would have occupied had the promise been kept, i.e. expectation damages, which seems to treat the promise itself as the source of an entitlement to performance, quite apart from any reliance. And promissory estoppel cuts across both, making a promise enforceable precisely where reliance would make non-enforcement unjust, even though consideration is absent. Meanwhile the law insists it will not look behind the parties' outward manifestations to their actual intentions, which is another refusal of the will.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of promising this cluster of doctrines presupposes, treating it as the implicit philosophy of an existing institution rather than as anyone's stated view. Which account of why promises bind does the consideration requirement commit the law to, and which rival accounts — promises bind as self-imposed obligations of the will, promises bind as assurances on which others may rely, promises bind by convention or by the value of the practice — does it rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Be careful to distinguish what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it could also be doing for other reasons; in particular, consider whether the requirement might be doing purely evidentiary or cautionary work, screening out unreliable claims rather than denying that bare promises bind, and if so whether that reading rescues the law from the theory I have attributed to it. Is the system internally consistent, or does the expectation measure presuppose exactly the will-based view that consideration rejects? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories of promising would each license it? And if one replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly will-based account, on which any promise seriously made binds as such, what would have to change in the doctrines, and what would be lost — both in the law's ability to do whatever consideration is actually doing for it, and in the moral picture the law would then be committed to?
