I have been reading the alignment literature and the forums around it for about a decade, and the argument over corrigibility and the shutdown problem is the one that has most obviously stalled. The setup everyone grants is that an agent pursuing almost any objective has instrumental reason to keep running and to keep its objectives intact, so a system that can be switched off or edited is not the default product of optimization; the proposed remedy is to build agents that are corrigible, meaning roughly that they neither resist nor invite shutdown and modification at the hands of their principal. A decade of proposals later there is a pile of partial constructions and impossibility-adjacent results rather than a solution. Indifference-style constructions, in which the agent simply does not care whether it runs or not, seem to make shutdown and even self-sabotage newly attractive rather than safe. Off-switch arguments, in which the agent permits itself to be switched off because being switched off is treated as evidence about what the principal wants, appear to assume a principal who is neither mistaken nor hostile. And there are the standing objections that corrigibility is a species of irrationality, that a reflective agent would edit it out of itself, that a corrigible agent loses to a non-corrigible one in any competition, and that nobody has yet stated the property in a form a malformed optimizer cannot satisfy in letter while violating in spirit. Outside the community the skeptics say the whole problem is a fiction, since nothing in current training produces a system that cares about its own continuation, and the insiders reply that the skeptics are answering a different question.

My hypothesis, which I would like treated as a hypothesis rather than as a premise, is that this is largely a level confusion. Half the participants are asking for a property of a single agent's decision-making in a fixed environment, which is a formal question with formal answers; half are asking for a property of an interaction between a fallible principal and an agent, which is an equilibrium or institutional question and cannot be settled by exhibiting a utility function. On that reading the impossibility-flavored results show that no agent is unconditionally deferential, while the safety proposal only requires deference inside an interaction the principal can trust, and the two camps are not in fact contradicting one another.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, a hidden formal question, a difference about what we want, or several of these in different places? Concretely, what do the indifference constructions and the off-switch arguments actually establish, and where does each of them stop? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a theorem, a sharper statement of the property that survives the known counterexamples, an argument that the property is a convention and the real work is institutional, or a case that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced? Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way one would write a long and careful email.
