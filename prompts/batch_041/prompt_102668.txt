I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the Industrial Revolution was inevitable. He kept saying that if the steam engine had not been developed in Britain, someone else would have developed it soon after, because the underlying scientific, demographic, and resource conditions made it overdetermined; I kept saying that the particular network of tinkerers, coal mines, and patent institutions was contingent enough that a different world could have stalled for a century. Now I have to guest-host a small history-of-technology podcast next week, and the guest is an invented historian who defends a thesis that cuts across both of us: she argues that the standard increasing-returns account of path dependence, the one that explains lock-in through sunk costs, network effects, and coordination advantages, is incomplete. On her view, the deepest lock-in is what she calls epistemic lock-in: once a technology or institution wins, the relevant community's imaginative horizon narrows, alternatives stop being represented in the training of practitioners, and the option ceases to be a live option for reasons that are not reducible to cost. She thinks this explains why the QWERTY keyboard, the standard railroad gauge, and the internal combustion engine persisted even when their efficiency advantages were disputed, and she claims that breaking this kind of lock-in requires an exogenous shock that reopens the imaginative space.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual content of that position rather than letting her restate it. I want at least one question on how her epistemic-lock-in mechanism differs from the familiar increasing-returns story and how one could tell them apart empirically; one on whether her view makes the same-outcomes-would-have-arrived-anyway thesis false or merely delayed; one on cases where a locked-in standard was later displaced, such as the shift from direct current to alternating current or the adoption of the metric system, and whether those count as counterexamples or as confirmations; and one on the normative upshot, namely whether epistemic lock-in is something we should try to prevent, and if so, who is supposed to supply the exogenous imagination. For each of those four, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Also include one question that she is likely to deflect with a stock line about lock-in being a research program rather than a testable hypothesis, followed by a second question that makes that deflection harder to use. The questions should be phrased the way a curious but skeptical interviewer would phrase them, not as gotchas, and they should not ask for reading lists or citations.
