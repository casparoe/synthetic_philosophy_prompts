I sit on the ethics committee at my hospital, where a single sentence has lately been doing most of the work: "it's what she would have wanted." Last month it was offered to authorize stopping hand-feeding for a woman with advanced dementia who now reaches for the spoon and appears content; this month it was offered to justify continuing treatment for a man whose directive — written after his diagnosis, with a clause asking us not to be talked out of it — refused precisely the treatment he now requests. Our chair treats the sentence as decisive in both cases and seems to think nothing further needs saying.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a ruling, the precise claims that sentence could be making — a prediction about what she would now choose, a statement about her present interests, an appeal to the authority of the earlier self over the later one, a claim about what we owe the person she was, or a way of managing the family's guilt — and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false, what it would license in each of the two cases, and which reading is actually doing the work when the sentence is used to override what the patient now appears to want. Say how confident you are in each verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
