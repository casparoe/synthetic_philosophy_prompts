I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been sitting in on a reading group my flatmate runs about accuracy-first epistemology — the idea that what makes a credence good is just that it's accurate, and that you grade a whole set of credences with a proper scoring rule, basically the same machinery we use at work to score forecasters. What I can't get past is that the framework seems to run fine on either of the two scores I actually know, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a bug. The two are the Brier score (for each question take your probability, subtract 1 or 0 depending on how it turned out, square that, add it up; lower is better) and the log score (add up the log of the probability you gave to what actually happened; closer to zero is better). At work we use log loss because that's what the library hands us, and we clip probabilities at 1e-15 so that a confidently wrong prediction doesn't come back as -infinity, which makes me wonder how much of the gap between the two is a real difference and how much is an artifact of nobody ever being actually certain.

So: compare them as two candidate answers to "what is it for a set of credences to be accurate, and why should that matter?", and be specific about where they truely come apart rather than where they merely look different. I tried to construct a case myself and I'm not confident in it. One question, three possible answers, the truth is answer 1: credence set P is (0.5, 0.25, 0.25) and set Q is (0.55, 0.45, 0). Brier: P sums to 0.375 and Q to 0.405, so Brier likes P. Log: ln 0.5 against ln 0.55, so about -0.69 against -0.60, so log likes Q. If that's right, I want to know what that split is a symptom of. Someone in the group also said the log score is picked out uniquely by some theorem while Brier is just one option among many — is that right, and unique in what respect? And does one of them have a clean split into a calibration part and a resolution part, and if so does that tell us anything about which is the better theory of epistemic goodness?
