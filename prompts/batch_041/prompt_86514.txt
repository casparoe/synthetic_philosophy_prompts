I teach an aesthetics seminar and this term I'm covering the fakes-and-forgeries week — the usual stuff about perfect copies, whether a forgery that fools everyone is any worse as a painting, whether the value we place on originals is snobbery or something real — and I want to teach it through Vertigo instead of through the "imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the Mona Lisa" thought experiment that every student has already heard a hundred times and can recite in their sleep. So what I want from you is a reading of the film through that debate. The core of the debate as I set it up for them is this: there's a family of views on which the value of an artwork is bound up with its actual history of making — with the intentions of the person who made it and the causal path by which it came to exist — so that two things can be indistinguishable to the eye and the ear and still only one of them is the work of art, the other is a copy of it, and no amount of looking will tell you which is which. Call that the provenance view. The opposing intuition is that what matters is the object in front of you and the experience it affords, and that if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference worth caring about.

Now here's why I think Vertigo is the perfect case and not just a cute one: the film is a forgery of a forgery. Judy is "Madeleine," so the woman Scottie falls in love with is a performance, and the real Madeleine Elster — the wife, the original — is dead in a way that's never even shown to us. Then Scottie takes the actual living woman and rebuilds her into the performance, the hair, the suit, the whole surface, and what he loves at the end is a copy of a copy that has no original behind it at all. So the standard question — what do we lose when we lose the original? — gets turned inside out, because in this case there was never an original to lose, and he knows it, and he wants it anyway. I'd like you to tell me whether Scottie is a counterexample to the provenance view or its most vivid illustration. My hunch, which you should feel free to demolish, is that his desire is purely surface-level — any convincing instance of Madeleine will do, he'll take the copy, he practically demands it — and yet what destroys him is not that she looked wrong but that she came about the wrong way, by deceit, so the thing he can't forgive is a fact about a history he can't see. Is that right? And what do we make of the necklace, the one clue that gives her away: the film identifies the fake by an object with a provenance rather than by a resemblance, which strikes me as the film quietly taking a side. Also, if I'm remembering the staging right, there's a small scene where Midge, who draws for a living, shows him a version of the Carlotta portrait with her own face in it — a copy with a joke inside it, an honest forgery — and he barely looks at it. Correct me if I've garbled that, but if that's roughly it, I'd like it in the reading too, because it seems to say something about who gets to make copies and what we want a copy to do.

The last thing I want, and this is really the point of the exercise, is for you to take the film seriously as a piece of thinking and not as an illustration of a position I've already handed you. So: does it dramatize anything that the argument alone doesn't — about the demand for authenticity, about what it costs to insist that the thing you love be the real one? The ending, where the truth is finally extracted and it kills her, feels to me like the film's darkest claim: that the appetite for the genuine article is not innocent, that it may be a demand for a causal history and a demand to know, and that it can be satisfied only at the cost of the thing itself. If that's a real reading, say so and say what it adds to the philosophy. If I'm forcing the film onto the theory and it doesn't fit, say that too — tell me where the analogy breaks, what the film is actually doing instead, and which scenes I should put on the board for the seminar to argue about. No need to point me to further reading or a bibliography, just give me the reading.
