A colleague in my department's logic reading group has constructed an argument that he says refutes the standard idealization of rational belief, and I have not been able to locate the flaw. He insists it is not a trick, that each step is to be read in its plainest sense, and that the only honest replies are to surrender a premise and pay the price. Here it is in his numbering.

(1) An ideally rational agent's beliefs are closed under entailment: if she believes p, and p entails q, then she believes q. He takes this not as a description of any actual thinker but as a constitutive norm of ideal belief, and says it is about as uncontroversial a starting point as epistemology contains.

(2) Take any humdrum belief of hers — that it rained in her city yesterday, say; call it E. E is a proposition, and every proposition entails every logical truth.

(3) So she believes every logical truth.

(4) Mathematical truths are necessary: that 2 + 2 = 4, and that the millionth digit of π takes whatever value it in fact takes, hold in every possible world. Since a proposition entails whatever holds in every world in which the proposition itself holds, E entails each of these as well.

(5) So she believes every mathematical truth — every theorem of arithmetic, settled or not, and the answer to every currently open problem in mathematics.

(6) Believing all of mathematics is an infinite task, and it is not merely that no finite agent has completed it; none could. An ideal that no possible agent could satisfy is not a norm of rationality but a fiction.

(7) Therefore (1) is false, and closure under entailment is no requirement of rationality after all.

I am sure the conclusion is too strong — I would sooner keep (1) than (7) — but I cannot say which of the middle steps fails, or whether the failure is in (1) itself. Which single step would you give up, why did it look obvious, and what does giving it up cost? And if the honest move is to give up (1), tell me what a rational agent is required to do with the consequences of her beliefs instead, and whether your replacement can be stated without quietly helping itself to the very idealization we are trying to avoid.
