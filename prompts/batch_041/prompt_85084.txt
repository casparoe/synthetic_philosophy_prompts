A shouting match at my family's table last month — a relative insisting that "genocide" is a word belonging to one event, my cousin answering that hoarding it is itself a political act — has turned into an invitation to run the session on atrocity and its names at our adult education seminar, where nobody in the room has legal training and half of them have a stake in the answer. I want a plan for the session, not the lecture itself.

The arc I'm after runs from the loose way the word is used now, through the legal category, and back out to the moral and political question of who may apply it. Lemkin's original conception should be the hinge: he counted the attack on a group's institutions, language, and economic life as part of the crime, not only the killing, and the definition my students have half-absorbed from the news is narrower than that in ways they won't expect.

Tell me what has to be in place before the legal and the moral questions can be told apart at all; which single case should carry the whole session, and what it must have and lack — I'm inclined toward one where the label is genuinely contested, but say so if that's the wrong call; the misconceptions they'll arrive with and the order those will surface in (I expect "genocide means mass killing" and "if it isn't legally genocide, it isn't so bad," but there will be others); exactly where in the sequence to head each one off; one closing question that shows whether they've grasped the concept rather than collected a verdict; and what I should openly decline to settle at this level, and why.
