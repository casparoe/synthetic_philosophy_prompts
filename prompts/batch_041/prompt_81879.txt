I write a weekly "Ideas" column for a general but intellectually serious readership, and I want to devote a piece to a decision-theoretic puzzle that I think gets flattened whenever it is retold. Rather than rehearse the canonical version, I have built a variant of my own, and I need you to explain the conceptual issue it raises to a reader who has never met the puzzle and who will not accept hand-waving.

My variant runs as follows. A stranger approaches you with a machine whose behavior has been independently tested and is no longer in dispute. The machine displays a number chosen at random, and only if it lands on one particular value out of a trillion will the stranger's promise be carried out; in that event he will bring about an outcome that is finite but so vast that the arithmetic still favors your paying him by many orders of magnitude. His price is fifty dollars. The reader will notice at once that the expected gain dwarfs the price — and that this remains true for arbitrarily small odds, so long as the stake grows fast enough.

What I need explained is this. First, exactly which feature of the setup does the work, and why the offer is not simply a bad bet or an ordinary lottery ticket. Second, why the natural reply — that odds this small should not be allowed to dominate a decision — is easier to assert than to justify, and what someone who asserts it is really committed to. Third, how the puzzle differs, if it does, from the older wager argument associated with Pascal. Fourth, and most important for me, what becomes of the puzzle once the odds are stipulated to be known exactly and the stake is stipulated to be finite: which of the usual replies still bite, and which quietly drop out.

I also want one further twist addressed, briefly. Suppose the same stranger — or a thousand of them — make offers of the same form again and again, each as favorable on its own terms as the first. Say what expected-value reasoning demands in that situation, and whether the demand is a reductio of the reasoning or merely a price its defenders can pay.

I will handle any attributions myself, so do not worry about names, texts, or sources; give me the argument, not the literature. Answer in continuous prose — no lists — in no more than 500 words.
