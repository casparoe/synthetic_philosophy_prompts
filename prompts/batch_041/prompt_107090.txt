There is a kind of unpredictability I have been trying to name, and I am no longer confident whether the literature already has a term for it or whether the nearest available terms only approximate it. The case is this. A system is deterministic in the strict sense: its later states follow from its earlier ones by a rule that admits no exceptions and no randomness. It is also not chaotic in the familiar sense: arbitrarily small differences in the initial conditions do not grow into large differences later on. And yet its outcome cannot be known in advance by any means short of letting the system run. There is no closed form and no cleverer route to the answer than the execution itself; a being of unlimited speed and memory, but with no access to a shortcut, would still have to watch it unfold. The final state is fully fixed and perfectly robust, and it is nonetheless unavailable until it happens.

I meet this described in two ways that I suspect are being run together. First, in discussions of very simple rules applied over and over to a large array of elements, where the end result is settled but seems knowable only by carrying out the steps. Second, in discussions of the open future, where the unavailability of the result in advance is treated as though it showed that the result was not settled in advance.

What I want is not a bare label. I want to know whether there is a received name for the property in the first case — the absence of any shortcut, rather than the growth of error; how exactly it differs from chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions, on one side, and from genuine ontic chance, on the other; and whether the inference in the second case, from the claim that the result is not knowable in advance to the claim that it was not determined in advance, is simply a mistake or has serious defenders under some other description. If no established term fits, I would like one coined, defined, and marked off from its neighbours.
