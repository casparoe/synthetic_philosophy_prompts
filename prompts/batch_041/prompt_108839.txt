I am a graduate researcher in a machine-learning laboratory, and I need help disentangling a slogan that a senior colleague repeatedly deploys in our evaluation meetings. The line is that any measure which becomes a target will cease to be a good measure. Most recently, in a discussion about whether to add a diversity-of-generated-samples metric to our evaluation suite, my colleague used the line to argue that optimizing the new metric would make it worthless, and therefore that we should not adopt it. I cannot tell whether this is a deep truth, a tautology, or a confusion, and I need the precise claims the line could be making before I can respond. Please lay out the distinct theses it could express, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each state whether it is analytically true, empirically plausible, false, or true only under special conditions. In particular, distinguish at least the following readings: a definitional reading, on which being a good measure simply means not being optimized; a statistical or regressional reading, concerning selection on a noisy proxy for the underlying goal; a causal reading, concerning intervention on a variable that was merely an effect of the goal; an extremal reading, concerning divergence between a bounded goal and an unbounded proxy; an adversarial or strategic reading, concerning agents who optimize the proxy against the goal; and an institutional reading, on which a measure's meaning is constituted by the practices in which it is embedded. For each reading, say what would have to be true of the world for the claim to hold, and give a concrete illustration from machine-learning evaluation or from institutional metrics such as hospital mortality rates, school test scores, or citation counts, indicating whether the illustration supports or undermines the reading. Then tell me which of these readings would actually support my colleague's conclusion that we should not add the diversity metric, and which would not. Finally, if the slogan is true under some reading, say what a research group could do to avoid the failure mode, and whether that remedy is available in our case. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need the menu of candidate claims, your verdicts on each, and the implication for our decision.
