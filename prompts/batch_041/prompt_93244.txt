im on the exec of our student philosophy society and we somehow booked a guest for the podcast in two weeks -- someone who works on Aumann's agreement theorem and takes the strong line that it's a genuine constraint on rational belief, not a cute fact about toy agents. our listeners are mostly second and third years whove seen a bit of decision theory but no measure theory, and i dont want to hand him softballs or let him hide behind "the theorem only bites when the disagreement is common knowledge" for the whole hour. could you draft me 8-10 interview questions, each with a follow-up attached?

things i definately want hit, at least one question each (reorder if it flows better):

1. the common prior -- sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine, the idea that differences in belief are down to differences in information. is that an assumption the theorem makes or a conclusion it reaches? and if two people can disagree about the prior itself, hasnt the disagreement just moved up a level, to a place where nothing constrains it at all?

2. the antecedent. if he and i are in a room and i can see he thinks X and he can see i think not-X, isnt the disagreement already basically common knowledge, in which case the theorem says one of us is irrational -- and if thats not what it says, what exactly is missing? for the follow-up here i want: if he says the missing piece is common knowledge of the *posteriors*, ask whether that antecedent can ever actually obtain when theres a live disagreement, i.e. is the theorem vacuous or self-defeating rather than false.

3. the no-trade consequence. common prior plus common knowledge that both sides are willing to trade means no speculative trade at all. is that a reductio of the whole framework or a feature of it? and does he think real markets falsify it?

4. dropping logical omniscience. the proof assumes agents know their own posteriors and can do the computation. what happens for agents like us, who arent even sure what our own credences are? does the theorem still say anything, or is it a theorem about gods?

5. the peer disagreement debate, conciliate vs stick to your guns. does Aumann's theorem actually support conciliationism, or is it being misused there? he'll probably say its misused, so the follow-up should ask what the theorem *does* license me to do when i find out a colleague i respect, who saw the same data, disagrees with me.

and then two or three standalone ones:

6. is there any empirical content here at all, or is it a norm that could never be tested -- and if the latter, what makes it a claim about rationality rather than a definition of it?

7. persistent disagreement among people who obviously arent stupid and who share the same evidence (medicine, climate, whatever). is that evidence theyre irrational, or evidence the idealisation is wrong? and the follow-up: if he says the idealisation being wrong doesnt matter because its an ideal, ask what work an ideal that nothing could ever satisfy is doing.

8. whether the "common knowledge" bit -- the infinite hierarchy of knowing that he knows that i know etc -- is doing real work or whether its a technicality that could be relaxed without losing the result.

please make the follow-ups actually keyed to the answer hes most likely to give (ive sketched a couple above), and keep the whole thing to 8-10 questions. no "what got you into this" stuff and no asking him to recieve... i mean recite the proof.
