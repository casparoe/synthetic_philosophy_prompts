I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company with a codebase that's older than most of the team. We get alot of "can we just get rid of this?" proposals -- a 200-line function nobody understands, a manual approval step in our deploy process, a sleep-and-retry in a data pipeline -- and the argument always goes the same way: someone says we can't touch it because something will break, without being able to say what, or we spend a sprint carefully preserving something that turns out to be dead weight, which definately makes the whole don't-touch-it instinct look like superstition.

I want you to act like a patient philosopher of institutions who also actually ships code. Take Chesterton's fence, plus the Burkean idea that inherited practices can encode knowledge no living person has (the weird hack might be a bug fix from 2013 that exactly one customer depends on), and turn it into a real decision procedure my team can run in a 30-minute design review. Before you write the procedure, brainstorm out loud about what the principle is actually claiming and what it does *not* claim -- I suspect it's really about how cheaply a change can be reversed, but I don't want you to just assume that, tell me if that reading survives.

What I need: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a hard "keep it," and where it has to hand off to a human because it's a judgment call about risk appetite rather than a fact. It has to handle the case where nobody alive knows why the thing is there, and the case where the original reason is gone but some other mechanism now covers it. Don't let it collapse into "go ask whoever wrote it" -- most of them have left. Assume the tech lead and the owner of the affected service have to sign off, and that reviewers usually don't know the history.

Then run it on three cases: the mystery billing function, the manual approval step in the deploy process, and one case of your choosing where the procedure says rip it out -- I want to see it isn't just a rubber stamp for whoever owns the code. Mark where it misfires or can't decide, and tell me honestly how someone could abuse this procedure to block all change.
