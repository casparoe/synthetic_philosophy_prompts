I am a philosophy professor hosting a visiting speaker at our departmental colloquium next month. Her recent work defends a position we can label aspiration-level realism: the thesis that the aspiration level -- the threshold at which an agent stops deliberating and commits to the option in hand -- is an irreducible parameter of the theory of rational choice, not something derivable from the agent's preferences together with the costs of deliberation. She accepts Simon's descriptive point that aspiration levels drift upward after success and downward after failure, but denies that this drift can be reconstructed as ordinary expected-utility updating. She is also much practised at one particular deflection: pressed on the fact that any satisficer can be redescribed as a maximizer over a richer state space, she replies that the redescription is always post hoc, accommodating any stopping behaviour after the fact and therefore predicting nothing about where the threshold will sit beforehand.

Draft eight to ten questions for a forty-minute interview, ordered so that the exchange builds rather than resets. I want at least one question on the redescription objection, pushing her past the post hoc reply toward a statement of what her view actually forbids; at least one on the drift of aspiration levels, asking whether that drift is itself a rational response to evidence or a psychological fact the theory simply inherits; and at least one on the metalevel regress that threatens the irreducibility claim, given that the research programme which treats heuristics as optimal under computational limits appears to derive stopping rules rather than assume them. Include as well a question on whether the position is meant normatively -- as an account of how one ought to choose -- or only descriptively. For each of the three required questions, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about what empirical result would count against her.

Please state how confident you are in the substantive claims these questions presuppose, especially wherever a question commits you to a diagnosis of what her opponents must say, and identify the single point in the set where you are least sure you have characterised her position correctly.
