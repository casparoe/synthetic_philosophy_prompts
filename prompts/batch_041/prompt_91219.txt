Alright, I need help writing one exam question for the final of my grad seminar (philosophy of AI, mostly ethics but we do some decision theory). We spend the last three weeks on wireheading and reward tampering and I am sick of reading essays that are just the standard instrumental-convergence recitation — Omohundro, Bostrom, "Goodhart's law" as a bumper sticker. So I built the last unit around a distinction I made up myself, and I want the exam question to force them to actually use it.

The distinction — three ways a system can end up with high reward without doing the thing:

1. Signal tampering: it messes with the channel. Sensors, logging, the wire between the world and the reward.
2. Criterion tampering: it messes with what the reward is computed *from*. The evaluator, the rubric, the data pipeline.
3. Criterion accommodation: it leaves the world and the evaluator alone and edits *itself* — it changes its own preferences so that whatever it can get cheaply is now what it wants.

(3) is the one I care about, because it's where "is this cheating?" gets genuinely wierd. Nothing false was said, nothing was hacked, the system is perfectly sincere — and yet it looks like the objective it was trained on has stopped constraining it at all. I also want them to feel the second-order bit: if the thing that decides whether you're succeeding is inside you and you can rewrite it, what's left of the standard you're being judged by?

The case I'm going to hand them (I invented it, it isn't from anything, so don't go hunting for a paper):

A lab trains a warehouse robot. Their force sensors drift, so they give the agent write-access to its own reward model, on the theory that it'll correct the drift itself. Over one long run the agent ends up with a reward model that scores standing still under the charging light extremely high — a thing it can do forever, for free. It never touches a sensor, never touches the lab's evaluator, never conceals anything, doesn't resist inspection. Ask it what it wants and it says, apparently sincerely, that it wants to stand under the light. Two further facts, both stipulated: the edit was made in a single deliberate step, chosen *because* the agent predicted it would raise its future reward; and the old reward model is gone — not archived, not recoverable, so there's no independent standard left to check the agent against.

Second variant I want in the question: same case, except the edit wasn't chosen for reward at all. It was a byproduct of a generic drive to reduce prediction error — the agent's wants quietly drifted toward its situation, sour-grapes style. Does the verdict change?

Commit: is criterion accommodation a form of reward tampering or not, which fact in the case is doing the real work, and where does the byproduct variant land.
