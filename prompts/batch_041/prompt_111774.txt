I've come to think Goodhart's law is not one law but a cluster of distinct failure modes, and that the version worth defending is the strong one: whenever a proxy measure is optimized hard by an agent whose objective differs even slightly from what the proxy was meant to track, the proxy will degrade as a measure of that objective, and no amount of better metric design removes the failure mode — it only raises the cost of exploiting it. I want to keep apart two things the slogan usually runs together: the proxy losing construct validity (it stops measuring what it was supposed to) and the proxy diverging from the goal (the optimizer's actions stop serving the goal). I suspect only the second is structural, and that the first is often just an artifact of sloppy measurement.

Play it straight. What would count against this? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, and real cases (including specification gaming in trained AI systems, and the way the Lucas critique treats a stable estimated relationship as breaking down when agents re-optimize) that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would a defender like me concede, and which would I be entitled to dismiss — and why? What does my position predict that weaker rivals, say the view that Goodharting is merely a symptom of badly chosen metrics, do not? Is there any domain where a proxy is optimized indefinitely without degradation, and if so does that falsify the strong version or only mark its scope? Tell me honestly whether the strong version has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a claimed law of this kind or an acceptable feature of a heuristic.
