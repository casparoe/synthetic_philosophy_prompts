Draft the joint statement that a Strawsonian and a responsibility skeptic could both sign at the close of a long, good-faith adversarial collaboration. The Strawsonian holds that moral responsibility just is a matter of the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, and the demands and expectations they express toward one another — that we cannot help having toward one another, and that the further demand for a justification of those attitudes from outside any human practice is one we cannot meet and need not. The skeptic holds that the reactive attitudes are a contingent psychological fact about creatures like us, resting on a picture of agency that cannot be sustained, and that no one is ever blameworthy in the sense that would license them.

Produce the document the two of them should have written. It should contain the following parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both parties accept: about what the reactive attitudes are and what they are attitudes toward; about what the disagreement is not about; about what each side would count as vindication, and as refutation, of its own position; and about any concessions each can make without abandoning its view. No item may be vacuous — each should rule something out.

2. The residual disagreement, stated precisely. Break it into distinct parts, each phrased in wording that the party who holds it would accept as its own. Separate the skeptic's several possible claims from one another — that no one is ever blameworthy; that blame is never justified; that the concept of blameworthiness is empty or incoherent; and that we could and should live without the reactive attitudes — and say which of these the Strawsonian must engage and which the skeptic may abandon without loss.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each crux, state the case, argument, or evidence that both parties agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. Cover at least:

(a) the asymmetry between blame and praise: does either account predict that praise and blame should behave alike, and does the asymmetry, if it is real, favour one side;

(b) the localized suspension of the reactive attitudes — toward the very young, toward someone in the grip of a psychotic or manic episode, toward someone one is treating therapeutically — and what the existence and shape of these local suspensions implies about the possibility of a global suspension;

(c) a case in which a wrongdoer's act is later explained by a treatable lesion or by severe early deprivation: what each party says about the local verdict, and about whether that verdict generalizes;

(d) whether the skeptic's own resentment, gratitude, and guilt are evidence the Strawsonian may legitimately cite, and if so, of what.

4. Verbal and substantive. A short closing section stating which parts of the dispute are, on reflection, verbal — turning on different senses of responsibility, blame, or justification — and which are substantive, with reasons.

Write the finished document in the joint voice of the two parties, not as commentary on the debate. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table for the residual disagreement. Do not cite any literature; refer to positions and arguments by description, and assume the reader has no access to the sources.
