You are a philosopher of the social sciences with a solid grounding in game theory and in the empirical study of common-pool resources. Before you write anything, think the problem through from the ground up: lay out the structure of the analogy first, map its parts, and only then decide what it can bear. I would rather have a careful audit than a fluent summary.

Here is the claim I keep encountering, and I want it examined rather than restated. At a seminar on climate policy, a colleague argued that every shared-resource problem — a fishery, an aquifer, the atmosphere, the office fridge, an unmoderated comment section — is "structurally a prisoner's dilemma." His reasoning: whatever everyone else does, each individual does better by taking more or contributing less, so depletion is the dominant strategy and the resource is doomed unless some external authority privatizes it or sets and enforces quotas. From this he drew a practical conclusion — that appeals to voluntary restraint, community norms, and self-organization are wishful thinking, because the dilemma is built into the situation itself, not into the character of the users.

I want an audit of the analogy. Please:

1. Give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine. What plays the role of the two prisoners, the two options, the payoffs, the single play, the simultaneous choice, the absence of communication, and the enforcement mechanism?
2. Say where the mapping holds and, more importantly, where it breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for my colleague's practical conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Pay particular attention to features the prisoner's-dilemma frame quietly imports that many real commons lack: that the interaction is one-shot rather than repeated, that players cannot communicate or make binding agreements, that they cannot monitor one another, that the situation is symmetric, that no one can change the rules or the boundaries of the resource, and that defection is genuinely dominant rather than merely tempting.
3. Consider whether other games fit some of these cases better. Where would an assurance game or a stag hunt, a game of chicken, or a pure public-goods problem with a dominant strategy of non-contribution be the more honest model? What turns on getting this right — does the choice of game change what counts as a solution?
4. Tell me whether my colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion all along. If it does not survive intact, say what a better analogy would be, and what it would license instead.
5. End by naming the point in your own reasoning where you are least confident, and state what evidence or argument would move you to a different verdict — for instance, what you would have to learn about a particular commons to conclude that the prisoner's-dilemma framing was after all the right one.

Keep the discussion at the level of the structure of the situations and the conclusions they support; I do not need a bibliography, and you should not guess at citations.
