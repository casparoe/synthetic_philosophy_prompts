Im prepping the first unit of my grad seminar on self-knowledge, and I want to open with something that forces people to actually argue instead of cite, so I've been trying to cook up the right prompt for them. Here's the issue I keep circling. Everyone in the room will know the anti-luminosity argument: take any mental state that comes in a continuous series of degrees — the room getting darker, a tone getting louder — and since you can't tell adjacent cases apart, there's no degree at which being in the state guarantees you can know you're in it, so nothing interesting is luminous. Good, fine. But that argument, when you look at it closely, is really about *iteration*: it's about the gap between being in a state and forming a further belief about it, where that further belief is itself a fallible empirical judgment made under the same margins as the first. It seems to leave untouched a different thesis, which I've been calling single-step transparency: that for a certain special class of states — I've been calling them "self-certifying," and the ones I have in mind are states that only exist when you take them up in attention, like the state of actively attending to the boundary between two very close shades, or the state of straining to recall a name — being in the state just is being in a position to know you're in it, with no further investigation and no fallible extra belief in between.

So what I want from you is an argument *for* that position: that single-step transparency holds for self-certifying states, and that the anti-luminosity argument is about a genuinely different phenomenon and so never gets a grip on it. Then, since I don't want a one-sided brief my students can just shrug off, give me the strongest objection to the position and say honestly whether it survives. No reading lists, no who-said-what, just the argument itself, clean, so they can attack it. American English please.
