My officemate is an expressivist about moral talk -- "eating meat is wrong" isn't a description of anything, it's a stance, and she refuses to call it a belief (she'll say it's "true" if you push her, but only in the thin I-endorse-this sense, which I think is a dodge). Her embedding verdicts are what throw me. She says "if eating meat is wrong then buying it is wrong; eating meat is wrong; so buying it is wrong" is a perfectly valid argument, and so is "either eating meat is wrong or fishing is wrong; eating meat isn't wrong; so fishing is wrong." And she's adamant there's no equivocation in either one -- the moral sentence means exactly the same thing in the "if" clause and under the "not" as it does when she asserts it flat out. Now shes got a new case for her seminar: "either eating meat is wrong or keeping chickens as pets is wrong." Will she say that one is true or false at all? What rule is she actually running -- something like "moral sentences carry ordinary truth-conditional content as soon as they're embedded, and it's only the bare assertion that's expressive," or "no moral sentence ever has a truth value, and validity just means you can't accept the premises and reject the conclusion," or something else -- and what further case would tell those apart?
