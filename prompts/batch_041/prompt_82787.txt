My friend and I just burned three hours argueing about whether money is socially constructed, and she ended it with: constructed just means we all treat it as real, so money and race are the same kind of thing, and if you think race is different then say what the difference is. I couldn't. You can redenominate a currency over a weekend and nobody's identity survives it; you can't do that to a racial hierarchy. But that's a vibe, not an answer.

So before I read one more paper on social kinds, I want the spec. Tell me what any theory of social construction has to be able to do before it counts as adequate -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the puzzles it has to dissolve rather than rename, the findings from the social sciences it can't contradict. One requirement at a time, with the case or consideration behind each, and say what a theory looks like when it fails that requirement. Where two requirements can't be had together, flag the pair and tell me which one you'd give up.

Cases I want covered, and if you think some of these don't belong in the list, say why:

- witches. A category that was collectively accepted, ran courts and executions for a couple of centuries, then stopped. Was it real while it lasted? What does the theory owe me here?
- money, including the shell-and-stone varieties, where the token has no use value and the whole thing still works.
- race, specifically the same person counted as white in one country and not another, with no change in their body or ancestry.
- the live one: essential worker. Somebody coined it in 2020, it got written into vaccine priority and hazard pay, and now it's sorting people. Nobody thinks it's a natural kind, but it did things.
- sex vs gender, since it's the case everyone actually cares about, and a theory that handles witches but not this is useless to me.

Deliver it in four named sections, in this order:

1. The cases that set the bar -- about 300 words, just the cases and what each one is doing to the theory.
2. What a theory has to deliver -- the requirements list, roughly 800 words, this is definately the part I care about. One per paragraph: the requirement, the case behind it, and what failing it looks like.
3. Requirements that can't both be met -- 200 words on the trade-offs and which side you'd take.
4. What does the most filtering -- 200 words. Which single requirement kills off the most otherwise respectable views, plus one line each on the main families of view, described by what they claim rather than by who said it. I don't want names or a reading list, I'll find the papers myself.

Skip any scorecard beyond that last section, and don't tell me what I should conclude about the five cases -- I want the standard, not the verdicts.
