Two of my colleagues went at each other at a workshop dinner over futarchy — the scheme where you vote on one measure of how things are going and let prediction markets pick the policy, on the grounds that a market prices in everybody's scattered information while eleven councillors just price in whoever shouted last. I tried to referee and ended up agreeing with whoever was talking. Could you sort it out for me?

The concrete version they were fighting about: a city keeps an ordinary vote on a single 'well-being index' — income, life expectancy, commute time, air quality — and then adopts whichever policy the decision market says will push that index up most. Mira is for it, Hal is against, and they got through about six moves before the plates arrived.

Mira: the council is eleven people with no stake in the outcome, half of them retired, and they vote whatever the loudest ward wants. A market pays you only if you're right, so it has to use everyone's information, including the people who'd never get a hearing. On any question where you can say what 'better' means, the market beats the room.

Hal: 'better' is the whole problem. The index is a choice, and not a small one — count commute time but not how close people live to their families and you've already ruled against the policies that keep extended families in one neighborhood. The index isn't measuring what people value, it *is* a policy. And you can't vote on the index 'first', because nobody knows which index they want until they know which policies it favors.

Mira: every institution runs on a proxy — cost-benefit analysis, 'the reasonable person', credit ratings. At least here the proxy is written down in advance, published, and amendable, and anyone can bet against it. The alternative isn't no proxy, it's an unwritten proxy held by eleven people.

Hal: then the real fight migrates to the amendment process, which is worse, because the stakes there are abstract and only the organized show up to amend. And there's a second thing. What exactly is being traded? The contract is supposed to price 'the index if we adopt X'. If we don't adopt X, that branch never happens — so either the contract voids, and the information you paid for is only about worlds you declined to enter, or it resolves on something else, and the price becomes a blend of 'will X get adopted' and 'would X actually work'. The council will read it as the second.

Mira: voiding is fine, thin markets are fixable with long horizons and a subsidy, and the manipulation worry runs the other way. If I buy contracts to make X look good, I'm buying at a price I think is wrong, and I only get paid if the index really does move. My manipulation is a transfer to everyone else.

Hal: only if I'm playing for the contract. If I want the bridge, my payoff isn't the contract, it's the bridge — so I spend fifty grand moving the price and get half a billion in policy. That's not a market failure, that's lobbying with a receipt. And it's self-undermining: once everyone knows the price can be pushed, the price stops meaning anything, and you've got a vote with extra steps.

Go through it move by move — who's right about what, and which of Hal's objections are fatal to the scheme as described versus just things that make it expensive or ugly. And tell me what each of them is missing. I don't need any reading, I just want the arguement straightened out; it's definately still bugging me.
