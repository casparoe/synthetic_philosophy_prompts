Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about how a language model ought to be trained to be helpful, and we couldn't agree on whether the whole enterprise even makes sense. Here is the practice I want you to examine. When a lab aligns a model to human preferences, it typically collects millions of pairwise judgments from a crowd of annotators — this answer is better than that one — and fits a single statistical model in which every candidate answer gets one latent score, so that the probability of one answer beating another is a function of the gap between their scores. Because the crowd is diverse, the training target ends up being an average of preferences that, taken one at a time, genuinely conflict. Yet the same pipeline also enforces a written list of rules the model must not break no matter how the crowd votes, and those rules are written by the lab rather than by the annotators. My friend says the averaging shows the practice is committed to a thoroughly neutral, procedural view of value — nothing good in itself, just whatever people happen to want, summed — while the fixed rules show it is committed to the opposite, a substantive floor that no majority may cross. I think both readings are too quick. Tell me what this practice actually presupposes about whose values an aligned model serves: whether it requires a view about the commensurability of different people's preferences, whether the scoring layer and the rule layer presuppose consistent things or pull apart, which rival theories of value it quietly rules out, and which it merely fits well with. Where the practice is underdetermined, say so, and say what would have to change in it if we swapped the presupposed view for a different one. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — just the way you'd put it in a long email.
