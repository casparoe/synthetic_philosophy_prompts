I'm putting together a handout for a translation workshop, and what I want from you is a decision tree of the positions people actually hold about translating poems that live or die by their form — the kind of thing Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot circles around, that short Renaissance French poem of Marot's written for someone who was ill, plus all the versions of it he collects. Each branching node should be a question a translator can answer without already knowing which camp the answer puts them in, and each leaf a named position: literal crib with commentary, content-first paraphrase, keep the constraint even if the sense suffers, dynamic equivalence (preserve the effect on the reader), transcreation (make a new poem that stands on its own), keep the source's strangeness, and flat-out untranslatable. Those may not be the right leaves, adjust as you see fit, but keep it to about ten.

The angle I care about: I keep having arguements with people over whether the formal constraint is part of the meaning or just decoration, so make that one of the top splits. And the tree should get intresting on the cases where the constraint isn't sonic. Four specimens to run through it: a poem in a tight rhyme-and-metre scheme; a lipogram (no letter e, say); an acrostic that spells the addressee's name; and a poem written to fit a tune everybody knows. Where two positions give the same verdict on a specimen, say so rather than pretending they differ. Also mark which cells of the tree are empty — nobody defends them — and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree simply can't separate.
