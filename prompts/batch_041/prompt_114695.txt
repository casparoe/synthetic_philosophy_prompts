My book club picked Middlemarch for next month and I somehow got volunteered to lead the discussion — I'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy, and I liked the book enough to open my big mouth. Here's the itch I can't scratch. Everyone knows George Eliot lost her faith young and paid the bills partly by translating Feuerbach (the guy whose whole move is that God is just humanity's own best qualities projected onto the sky — theology is really anthropology wearing a costume), and yet everything she wrote afterwards still *feels* religious: saints, vocations, pilgrimages, confessions, providence, the whole apparatus, just with the supernatural bit scooped out. I want a reading of Middlemarch that takes that swap seriously and asks whether it actually works. Treat the novel as an experiment: can you keep the moral and emotional machinery of religion after you've removed God from it? Dorothea's craving to do something great, her mistaking Casaubon for a spiritual calling, Lydgate's collapse, the way the book keeps handing people coincidences and "webs of causes" that feel like providence but are supposed to be just causes, and that famous last bit about the good that gets done by people whose lives go unrecorded — is that a religion of humanity that genuinely stands on its own, or does the novel quietly smuggle back the consolation it claims to have given up (a secular heaven, where the reward for unhistoric virtue is still... being remembered, or at least mattering)? I keep going back and forth on whether the ending is honest or a bait-and-switch, and I'd like you to actually land somewhere instead of telling me it's ambiguous and beautiful.

Second thing: don't just hand me the tidy "Eliot = Feuerbach" reading, because I don't fully buy it. She's got other ingredients in the pot (I gather Spinoza and Comte and whatever she picked up from the Victorian freethinkers, plus her own stubborn thing about the slow improvable-ness of the world — some word for it, meliorism or something), and I suspect the novel is doing something more interesting than illustrating a translation she did years earlier. So where does the projection theory actually illuminate a scene or a choice, and where does Middlemarch push back against it or go somewhere the philosophy can't follow? And the flip side: what does the *fiction* give us here that a straight argument for a godless humanism can't — what can a novel dramatize about living without providence that Feuerbach's prose can only assert? If the honest answer is that my reading flattens the book, say where it breaks and what the better reading is.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — just tell me the verdict up front, then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate my question back at me, and please skip anything that needs citations, since I'm going to say this stuff out loud to seven people in a pub and I don't want to be the guy who invented a quote.
