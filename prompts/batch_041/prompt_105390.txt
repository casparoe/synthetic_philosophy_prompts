I am a fourth-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and the central chapter of my dissertation defends a particular version of Ian Hacking's looping-effects thesis about psychiatric classification. Treat that version as the position under scrutiny, and tell me what would count against it — what would have to be true for it to be false.

Two commitments define my version, and both are places where I depart from the way the thesis is usually stated. First, I argue that the loops that matter are predominantly institutional rather than identificatory. On my account, what moves people is not chiefly that they encounter a diagnostic label and take it up as a self-description, but that the label reorganizes the practical world around them — referral thresholds, insurance coverage, school provision, peer communities, the availability of a culturally legible script for distress — so that behavior and self-report drift even in people who never endorse the category. Second, I hold that a genuine loop requires more than correlation: the classification must figure in the causal explanation of a non-trivial shift in the distribution of the very trait the classification claims to pick out, a shift that exceeds the secular trend the trait was already on. I also treat as an open question whether the targets of a classification revising that classification — as happened when gay and lesbian activists contested the pathologization of homosexuality, and as neurodiversity advocates now contest other categories — is a phenomenon the thesis should absorb or a counterexample to it.

What I need is an honest map of this position's exposure. Which observations, historical cases, arguments, or general results in the philosophy of science would tell against it? Rank them from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them I should concede myself, as opposed to which only a hostile critic would press. Tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not — a biomedical realism that treats these categories as tracking a stable underlying pathology, and a thoroughgoing social constructionism that treats them as pure imposition — and therefore what evidence would discriminate among the three. I am especially concerned about whether the criteria I have built in have rendered the thesis unfalsifiable, and whether, if so, that is a defect of my particular version or an unavoidable feature of any thesis of this kind. Finally, tell me which single piece of evidence or argument you would expect to move me most, given everything I have said about what I am committed to.

I do not need a bibliography or a reading list; I want the arguments and the cases.
