Polybius gave us the wheel of constitutions — monarchy decays into tyranny, tyranny into aristocracy, aristocracy into oligarchy, oligarchy into democracy, democracy into mob rule, and then you're back at square one — which is why he figured Rome's mixed constitution had finally broken the wheel for good. Ibn Khaldun picked up the same cyclical machinery and pointed it at dynasties: hard desert nomads held together by asabiyyah conquer a soft city, by the third generation everyone's gone comfortable and lost the group feeling, and the next wave of tough outsiders takes over. Then Spengler and Toynbee revived the whole thing in the twentieth century, and really they're two versions of one deterministic law — civilizations are organisms with a set lifespan, they're born, mature and die on schedule, and Spengler's morphology and Toynbee's challenge-and-response are just two ways of stating that same law, which is why both of them could forecast the West's decline. The lesson of all four is that history has no direction, that nothing is ever really new, and that since the patterns keep repeating a clever statesman can see the cycle coming and steer his way out of it.
