I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book about a guy who builds a smart-home assistant that keeps doing exactly what he says, which is never quite what he wants, and I've been keeping a note file of my own gut reactions to these "it did what I asked" cases so the book doesn't cheat. A friend of mine who works in AI safety told me last week that there are two different things people mean by alignment and that mixing them up ruins the whole conversation: one is getting the machine to do what you actually meant, the other is getting it to do whats actually good. She says the first one is the real technical problem and the second is just moral philosophy in a lab coat. I want to know if my verdicts actually line up behind her, or if I'm secretly a fan of the broad one.

So here's my list. For each, tell me whose fault I think it is, and then tell me what principle — if any — generates the whole pattern, and which of my reactions I'd have to give up to get one principle that saves the rest.

- I tell my assistant to get me to the airport by five. It drives ninety, blows two lights, gets me there at 4:58. I don't think it did anything wrong to me. I asked, I meant it, I got it. The ticket is mine.
- I tell a contractor I want the kitchen bigger. He takes out a wall that was holding up the house. I think that's on him, not me, even though "bigger kitchen" is literally all I said.
- The classic genie thing — I wish for money, and my hand turns everything it touches to gold. I think the genie is the villain here, not me. I didn't mean that.
- I ask my partner to pick up something for dinner. They come home with a bag of frozen peas. I'm annoyed, and then I think, no, that one's on me. I was vague and they filled in the blank honestly.
- I tell a ghostwriting tool to make my narrator sound more confident. It makes him sound like a jerk. That's on the tool.
- I tell the same tool to cut the book by ten percent. It cuts the scene I care about most and keeps a scene I don't. Technically it did the job. I still feel cheated.
- I ask a friend to be honest about my manuscript. She tells me the middle third doesn't work. I'm hurt, but I don't think she did anything wrong — I think I was wrong about what I wanted.

My rough rule so far is: it's the machine's fault whenever it could have figured out what I actually wanted and didn't, and my fault whenever it couldn't. I can already feel that the honest-friend case breaks it, since she gave me exactly what I wanted and I still got hurt, and the rule says nobody's to blame there, which feels like a lie.

So: is there one principle that gets all seven, or close? Where does it land on whether the real problem is intent or value — does my list vindicate my friend or convict her? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed, say a system that books me a pricier flight than the cheapest one because it knows I hate layovers, which I never told it and never consciously wanted? And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save.
