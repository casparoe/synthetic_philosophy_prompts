I am a retired geophysicist who has, since retirement, become a fairly serious amateur student of moral philosophy. I sit on the ethics committee of our national geological society, and I need to circulate a written opinion before the next meeting.

The case before us is this. During a developing volcanic crisis overseas, an ad hoc team was assembled to help interpret monitoring data for the local authorities. One of our fellows, a volcanologist of precisely the right specialism, declined to travel to the affected area. She cited family responsibilities and the personal risk, which was real; she is employed by a university, holds no public-duty post, and had no contractual or statutory obligation to go. By all accounts the contribution she could have made was substantial, and the local team was measurably weaker without her. There is now pressure, both within the society and in the press, to treat her refusal as a failure of professional responsibility.

Our draft code of conduct contains a line to the effect that members should render assistance to the public where their expertise permits. I want the committee to decide what, if anything, follows from that line, and whether it should be rewritten.

Please draft the opinion I shall circulate. It should recommend a definite course of action — a finding, and any amendment to the code with the exact wording you would propose — together with reasons the committee can actually act on. It should engage the philosophical territory rather than treating the matter as a question of institutional taste. In particular I want it to confront the idea that some acts are good to do yet not required, which philosophers call supererogation, and the uncomfortable corollary: that if the refusal falls on the permissible side of that line, censure would be unjust even though the choice was, in some sense, disappointing. It should also reckon with the strongest argument against that conclusion — that the pertinent duties attach to a role or a profession rather than to a person, and that our own word should may already have manufactured an obligation. And it should say plainly where you think the line falls in this case and why, rather than rehearsing the options and leaving the choice to us.

I should add that I am not looking for a tidy resolution. If the concept of supererogation turns out to generate a paradox — that nothing beyond duty can ever be demanded, which seems to let us all off rather too lightly — say so, and tell me how you propose to live with it.

Give me a committed answer: a definite finding and definite drafting choices, with your reasons, not a survey of positions. Please write in British English.
