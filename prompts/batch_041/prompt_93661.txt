Read David Lewis's short paper on penal substitution — the one where he imagines an innocent friend volunteering to take your prison sentence, and says we'd never actually go for it, the authorities would just punish you anyway once they found out. His fix seems to be that we *do* accept substitution when the punishment is a fine, since a fine is basically a debt and anyone can pay a debt, but not when the punishment is imprisonment, because that's supposed to be about making the guilty person suffer. I think that line is doing way less work than he wants it to. Can you argue that he's wrong — that the debt/punishment seperate doesn't hold up, and that his own test pushes us toward either accepting both kinds of substitution or rejecting both, not the split he lands on? Use a case he doesn't use, like a company paying a fine on behalf of an employee, or a parent doing their kid's community service hours, and see what his own criterion does with it. Just the argument, please, no survey of the literature — I'm a software guy and this reads to me like a bug in the abstraction.
