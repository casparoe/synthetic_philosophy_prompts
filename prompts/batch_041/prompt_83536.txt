I direct a small museum in a provincial city that documents a genocide — a modest place, one permanent room, a reading corner, a wall of family photographs — and next month we reopen after a refurbishment, at which point I have to give the opening address. The room will hold local councillors who are there because it is their job, secondary-school teachers who bring classes through, and, in the front two rows, a dozen or so descendants of victims, some of whom have spent decades fighting the claim that the whole thing was exaggerated or invented. There will also be one emeritus historian from the university, a genuine friend of the museum, who over the years has gently pressed us to correct two dates and one figure on our panels.

What I want the address to do, and what makes it difficult, is to hold two things together in a single speech without letting either collapse into the other. The first is that we are an institution that revises itself: we have changed our panels before, we expect to change them again, and we do not treat our own text as scripture. The second is that we will not stage a debate about whether the genocide occurred, and I want to be able to say why that refusal is a matter of method rather than of fear or censorship.

I have never managed to put the difference well in public. The people who demand the debate do not, as far as I can tell, apply to this event the standard of proof they apply to anything else; they treat one missing document as decisive while treating the convergent testimony of survivors and the records left by the perpetrators themselves as if it counted for nothing; and their corrections all run in one direction, toward exculpation, which is not how a discipline corrects itself. But when I say this out loud it sounds either like a refusal to answer or like a request that the audience take our authority on trust.

Write the address, about eight hundred words. It must be intelligible to the councillors and the teachers, who know almost nothing about historiography, and it must not insult the descendants by treating the question as in any way open. The distinction between legitimate revision and denial should do real structural work in it — the shape of the whole speech should follow from that distinction rather than mentioning it once and moving on. It must not rest on the grief in the room, which would be an argument from emotion, and it must not rest on our standing as a museum, which would be an argument from authority; whatever force it has should come from the difference in method. Do not lean on any particular date, figure or document, and do not quote anyone: I want the argument to stand on its own without them.

Please write it in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a letter. And in British English.
