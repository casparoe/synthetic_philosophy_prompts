I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of logic (fourteen students, all with one prior course in symbolic logic). I want an activity that makes them feel the regress Carroll's tortoise opens up: that no additional premise, including one that states the rule they are relying on, obliges someone who accepts the premises to accept the conclusion, and the accompanying suspicion that inferring was never a matter of accepting a further proposition. Build it around a token economy rather than a discussion. Every proposition the group wants treated as settled must be paid for; the sceptic's script permits exactly two moves — accept anything that has been written down and paid for, and ask what licenses the step from what is accepted to what is proposed — so the sceptic is never obtuse, only patient. Give the group few enough tokens that they run out before the sceptic concedes, and in the second half rotate the sceptic's role so that a student must try to justify modus ponens itself; let the group discover what happens to each justification they attempt. Include the rules and materials, the timing, a debrief that maps each stage onto the regress and onto the difference between accepting a rule and accepting a proposition, what will predictably go wrong the first time, and a frank note on which part of the point the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself.
