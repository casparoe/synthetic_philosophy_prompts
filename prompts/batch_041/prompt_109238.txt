I'm a retired isotope geochemist — forty years in, mostly lab work on sediments and ice cores — and since I stopped going in I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, badly, with a reading group that meets above a bakery. Last month we did the Anthropocene and I've been chewing on something ever since. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science who actually understands how stratigraphy works, and think this through slowly and carefully with me. Take your time, and don't worry about references — I'm not going to chase down books you name, and I can't look anything up from the kitchen table.

Here's the practice I want to put under the microscope. To get a new unit onto the geological time scale you need a boundary you can point at: one spot in one outcrop or core somewhere in the world, a marker in the material that can be recognized worldwide and that turns up at more or less the same level everywhere. Geologists call it a golden spike, officially a GSSP. The Anthropocene working group went looking for one, settled on sediments from a small lake in Ontario with a plutonium signature from bomb tests in the early 1950s, and after fifteen years of argy-bargy the international bodies voted it down in 2024 — first the subcommission, then the commission, then the union. So the same community that will tell you, over a beer, that humans obviously rewired the earth system in the twentieth century declined to make it official, and instead keeps the term around as a description rather than a unit. That's the thing I keep tripping over.

What I want from you: forget the politics and tell me what that procedure presupposes. Not what the people involved say they believe — what the machinery itself commits them to. So, for instance: if the whole apparatus turns on finding a single physical point that stands in for a worldwide instant, does that presuppose that the boundaries are real joints in nature that we're discovering, or could the same apparatus work if epochs were just useful bookkeeping? If the answer has to be voted on by a commitee with a supermajority rule, is the practice discovering an answer or making one — and can it coherently be doing both? The same people who insist the marker be synchronous worldwide also use boundaries pinned to the first appearance of a fossil group, which everyone knows shows up at different times in different places, so is the synchrony requirement doing real work or is it decoration? And I had a colleague who swore the whole fight was confused because the Anthropocene is an event, like the Great Oxidation Event, not an epoch — an event has a ragged, drawn-out, uneven beginning, so demanding a single spike for it is a category mistake. Is that a distinction with teeth, or is he just being difficult? If he's right, what kind of classification machinery would you need instead, and what would it have to give up?

A few things I'd find useful. Start by brainstorming: give me a list of candidate presuppositions before you organize them, even the ones you end up throwing out. Then sort them, and be explicit about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely fits comfortably with — I want to know where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where two or three different views of what a geological period is would all license the same behavior. Then say what rival views the practice quietly rules out, and pick a fight with yourself about whether the thing is internally consistent. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the rules, in the voting, in what counts as a marker — if we swapped the presupposed view for one of its rivals. Concrete answers over hedging, please; I'd rather you commit to a reading and defend it than list five possibilities and shrug.
