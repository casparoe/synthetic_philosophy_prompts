I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level ethics seminar — about sixteen students, a mix of juniors, seniors, and one or two first-year graduate students, most of whom have met Kant only as a name attached to "don't treat people as mere means." I want to run a classroom activity, not a lecture, that makes the class feel the force of a position they will otherwise dismiss on contact: that you may not lie to the murderer at the door.

The version of the position I care about is the one that survives after the cartoon is stripped away. It is not the flat rule that lying is forbidden no matter what happens, which the students will simply weigh against the consequences and reject. The hard claim is that the wrong of the lie is not done to the man at the door at all, and is not cancelled by the fact that he intends a murder. On this reading the duty of truthfulness is owed to the standing of assertion itself — to everyone who depends on what people say — and the man at the door has neither a special claim on it nor the power to forfeit it on everyone's behalf. The position also leaves an exit: it forbids deceiving, not silence. I want the class to feel the pull of the obvious reply — that a murderer has forfeited any claim to be told the truth and is using me as a tool — and then to feel the pull of the claim that survives that reply: that the lie injures the general practice of truth-telling rather than the man himself, which is why declining to answer is not the same as lying. I do not want the words "categorical imperative" or "duty of truthfulness" spoken until the debrief.

Design the activity so that the idea shows up in what the participants do — in the roles, the rules, or the stakes — before anyone names it. It must fit the 90 minutes, need no materials beyond paper, and involve no real money. You may stage the door itself or a variant of it; what matters is that participants act and decide rather than discuss. Give me the full runnable thing: the setup and any roles, the rules and how the stakes or payoffs work, a timeline to the minute, what I say and do at each stage, and the exact moment the position should become visible to them. If multiplying the scenario (many doors, many knockers) helps make the injury to the general practice tangible rather than merely asserted, use that; if it doesn't, don't.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the position — where they felt the third-party injury, where silence and deception came apart, where the man at the door looked like he had forfeited something — and say what I must add that the activity cannot generate on its own. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the likely objection that the whole exercise just shows Kant was absurd.

Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that any game makes the wrong of lying look consequentialist — a cost to a shared fund of trust that could in principle be weighed — which is exactly what the position denies, and that a game trades in payoffs rather than rights, so it cannot reproduce the claim that the man at the door had no right to the truth that he could have forfeited. Say whether you agree, and name anything else that is lost in the translation from the position to a room full of people keeping score.

Give me one committed design rather than a menu of options: make the definite calls about structure, timing, and stakes, and give your reasons for each, so that I can run it as written. No citations, reading lists, or references — don't guess at sources; I want the design, not a bibliography.
