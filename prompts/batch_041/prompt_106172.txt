I've been re-reading Franklin's Autobiography again, mainly the stretch where he tries to arrive at moral perfection — the thirteen virtues, the little book ruled with red ink, one virtue per week for thirteen weeks, four courses a year, the black spots he'd wipe off with a wet sponge, later transferred to ivory leaves. A few things jump out at me. It's basicly a self-tracking protocol: a ledger, a weekly focus, a daily evening examination. He admits it failed in the ways that matter — Order he never got and kept relapsing on, and on Humility he says he got the appearance of the virtue rather than the reality, then undercuts the whole thing by noting that if he had conquered his pride he'd probably be proud of his humility. He designed it to be sect-neutral on purpose because he wanted to publish it as a teachable method — he even had a title in mind for the book. And the most underrated bit, I think: he insists vicious actions aren't harmful because they're forbidden, they're forbidden because they're harmful, and he thought you could sell virtue to a young man as the surest route to a fortune. So the project is prudential, method-y, and oddly indifferent to whether you're actually good deep down, as long as the conduct comes out right.

Now shove him forward a couple centuries. I want you to predict his views on four things that didn't exist in his lifetime, and I want each prediction tied to specific features of his actual project rather than to generic "Franklin was a practical guy" stuff.

1. Habit-tracking apps with streaks and push notifications — Streaks, Habitica, and the ones where you put money at stake and get charged if you miss a target. Would he see these as the natural continuation of the little book, or as something that changes the character of the practice? Be specific about which of the thirteen virtues a streak mechanic helps with and which it wrecks; I'd guess Order is the interesting case. Also: his one-virtue-at-a-time schedule, where he leaves the other virtues to their ordinary chance while he drills one, seems to assume attention and willpower are a scarce budget you spend. Given how badly the ego-depletion research has replicated, does his method survive if willpower turns out not to be a depletable resource? And what would he make of moral licensing — the finding that people who've just done something good feel freer to do something bad?

2. An AI that does your evening examination of conscience for you: you journal or dictate your day, it tells you where you fell short, and it adjusts next week's focus. Would he use it? Would he trust its verdicts? What does his handling of the Quaker friend who told him he was generally thought proud suggest about whether he'd want a machine doing that job, or whether the rebuke has to come from a person who actually knows you and has standing to give it?

3. Moral bioenhancement — a pill, a gene edit, some neuromodulation that makes you more honest, more temperate, or less prone to anger. Does his framing, where contrary habits have to be broken and good ones acquired and established before you can depend on steady conduct, commit him to thinking this is cheating? Or is it exactly the kind of shortcut he'd have loved? Note that he already thought the mere speculative conviction that virtue was in your interest wasn't enough to keep you from slipping — that seems relevant to whether he'd think a pharmacological fix works on the right level.

4. Whether he'd sign onto something like effective altruism or a quantified do-the-most-good calculus, given that he ran his own ethics through a ledger and thought vice was forbidden because it's hurtful. And separately, whether he'd be comfortable with a social-credit-style system that scores people's virtue publicly — given how much of his humility work was explicitly about managing appearances, I genuinely don't know which way he'd go.

For each of the four, do three things: (a) give your best prediction of his position, (b) say how confident you are and which part of the Autobiography is doing the work, and (c) flag where you're extrapolating past anything the text supports. Then at the end, seperate cleanly what is actually settled about Franklin's moral thought among people who've studied him from what is genuinely contested or a matter of interpretive dispute — don't dress up a contested reading as the consensus, and if one of your predictions rests on a contested reading, say so explicitly. If you think a question is malformed — if "what would Franklin think of a habit app" is just too anachronistic to have a determinate answer — say that instead of forcing one.
