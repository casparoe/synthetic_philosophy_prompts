So I'm writing a piece on how the old behaviorists would've reacted to LLMs, and I keep circling back to Dennett's tower of generate-and-test — the ladder where Darwinian creatures are born knowing, Skinnerian ones learn by trial and error in the world, Popperian ones run the trial and error inside their heads first, and Gregorian creatures are Popperian plus all the tools and words they borrow from culture, so a chunk of their thinking gets offloaded into the environment. Fine. But here's my actual question: stick B.F. Skinner in a room with a modern LLM and this ladder, and what does he say?

Not a generic behaviorism-vs-cognitivism answer — I want you to actually role-play the man's commitments. Would he accept being filed as the second rung, a creature that can only learn by getting reinforced in the real world, when his whole point was that operant behavior is where the interesting selection happens? Does he think Popperian and Gregorian are real joints in nature, or just cognitivists mystifying what's still reinforcement all the way up? And the one I really care about: where does a large language model sit? It's trained by gradient descent on a giant pile of other people's words, which smells Gregorian to me — all that borrowed culture compressed in — but it also never acts in a world and never gets its own behavior shaped by consequences, which smells like it fails even the Skinnerian rung. So would Skinner say the model is just a talking head that emits previously-reinforced-ish text, not really a new grade of creature at all? Or would he break his own taxonomy and insist the training process is just operant conditioning at industrial scale? (There's alot riding on this for my lede, so don't be shy.)

Give me your best reconstruction of his verdict, not yours. Lead with the verdict in one or two sentences, then the reasoning, and keep the whole thing under 350 words. No hedging me to death — I need something I can actually use.
