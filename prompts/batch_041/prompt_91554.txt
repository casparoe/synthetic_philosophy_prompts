I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — I write long-form pieces on psychology, neuroscience, and the moral philosophy that gets bolted onto them — and I've spent the better part of a year reporting on how courts and parole boards talk about culpability. Somewhere in that reporting I stopped being a neutral observer and started having convictions of my own, and before I put any of them in print I want a hard-nosed audit of whether they actually cohere. So I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on moral responsibility and is fluent in the literature that grows out of P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes. Begin by briefly restating each of the commitments below in your own words, so I can tell you've got them right, and then do the real work. Think carefully and reason it through rather than pattern-matching to the usual debate; I'm less interested in a survey of positions than in a diagnosis of my particular package.

Here is where I've landed. (1) I'm a thoroughgoing naturalist: I don't believe in any contra-causal freedom, and I take every choice to be the upshot of a causal history. (2) I think Strawson is right that moral responsibility rests not on metaphysical freedom but on our reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt, the whole weather system by which we hold one another to account — and I don't think determinism gives me any reason to abandon that system. (3) At the same time, I think particular reactive attitudes can be criticized as unwarranted: a resentment that's really just displaced anger or status anxiety is one I should be able to name and try to shed, and I'd want to ask whether the same critique applies to indignation on someone else's behalf and to my own guilt. (4) I distinguish sharply between the question of whether an action reveals the kind of person someone is and the question of whether it's fitting to blame them, and I think only the first survives when someone's wrongdoing is fully explained by a brutal upbringing. (5) But I also think retreating to that cooler, explanatory stance toward ordinary wrongdoers — treating them as patients to be understood rather than agents to be answered — is usually condescending, and I want to resist it. (6) And I think the more of someone's cruelty is traceable to what was done to them, the less we should hold it against them.

Now tell me which of these pull against one another, which pairs only look like they're in tension, and what the cheapest way to restore consistency would be. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of equal problems, say explicitly what would have to give in each case and what that concession would cost me, and be specific about which single commitment is doing the most damage to the rest of the set.
