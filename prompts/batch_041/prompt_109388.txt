You are a philosopher of criminal law and punishment, fluent in the standard theories of why the state may punish — retribution, general and special deterrence, incapacitation, the rehabilitative ideal, restoration — and in the liberal and communitarian arguments about what a state may do to someone for his own good. Before answering, think carefully and at length: begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list every candidate commitment you can see in the practice described below, say briefly why each is a candidate, and only then commit to a reconstruction and defend it.

Here is the practice I want you to anatomize. In Norway there is a maximum-security prison, Halden, opened in 2010, holding roughly 250 people, which was built without towers, barbed wire, electric fencing or armed guards; its cells have unbarred windows and shared kitchens; its grounds are laid out to resemble a village, and the stated design aim is to make the difference between life inside and life outside as small as possible, so that release is less of a cliff. Prisoners are, however, locked into their cells for twelve hours a day, misbehaviour is answered with cell confinement or transfer, and the perimeter wall is deliberately left visible from almost everywhere inside. Release is not simply the expiry of a term: the maximum determinate sentence is twenty-one years, and an offender judged still dangerous may be held under preventive detention that is renewed five years at a time and can in principle continue for life; and decisions about early release turn in part on the offender's progress in work, education, and treatment. Set against this, the American system is formally "correctional" and runs programmes of its own, yet its institutions are largely built as warehouses and its sentence lengths are fixed almost without reference to anything the offender does or becomes while inside.

Now imagine the Norwegian model pushed one step further, into a facility where release dates are set entirely by measured reduction in risk, where staff are evaluated by the recidivism of the people they have supervised, and where no deprivation is imposed beyond the loss of liberty itself — no uniforms, no bars, no restrictions on visits — on the theory that the sentence just is the loss of freedom and that everything else must be made as normal as possible.

I want the implicit theory of this practice, not a survey of penal theory. Specifically:

1. What does the practice presuppose about the justification of punishment? Does it commit to the claim that deprivation of liberty exhausts the punishment? What does it presuppose about when hard treatment is permissible — desert, the offender's future dangerousness, the state's interest in reducing crime, or something else?
2. What does it presuppose about the offender — a rational agent responsive to reasons, a person whose criminality is a trace of social circumstance and therefore treatable, or a rights-bearer who must be respected even while confined? Which of these does the practice actually need, and which does it merely fit comfortably with?
3. What does it presuppose about the state? Does the coerced element in treatment — that progress is a condition of earlier release — presuppose a licence to remake a person for his own good, and is that licence compatible with the liberal refusal to rank conceptions of the good life, or with any serious role for desert?
4. What does the American contrast presuppose by comparison, and is the two systems' shared word "correctional" a sign of a shared commitment or of a shared evasion?

Then separate the two things I keep running together: which commitments the practice strictly requires, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that a retributivist, a deterrence theorist and a rehabilitationist could each sign off on the same buildings and the same rules. Finally, suppose we rejected whatever you take to be the central presupposition — say, we concluded that the state has no business remaking anyone — and tell me concretely what in the institution would have to change, down to the level of the cell door and the release calendar.
