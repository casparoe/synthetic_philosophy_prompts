A popular-science book on computation that I've been reading gives the Church–Turing thesis the following gloss, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust:

'Church and Turing arrived at the thesis independently in 1936, Church from the lambda calculus and Turing from his machines, and the fact that two such different routes converge is generally taken as its strongest warrant; a claim about what can be computed in principle is not the kind of thing that can be proved outright, so convergence is the best we can have. The thesis itself says that a Turing machine can compute anything that any effective procedure can compute, and it has held up remarkably well — quantum computers, for all their speed-ups, have not exceeded it, and no one has found a hypercomputer. It also settles the status of the mind, since the brain is a physical system and its operations must therefore be computable. What the thesis licenses, finally, is the equivalence of all universal computers: a phone and a supercomputer differ only in speed.'

I suspect that some of this is harmless popularization and some is a real error, but I can't tell which is which. Go through it claim by claim, saying what someone working on computability would accept, what would make them wince, and what is simply wrong. I'm especially interested in whether the gloss is running together several distinct things that travel under this one name — a stipulative definition of an informal notion, a mathematical result about formal systems, an empirical claim about physical processes, a claim about human reasoning — and whether the closing claims about the mind and about the equivalence of computers follow from anything true in the gloss.
