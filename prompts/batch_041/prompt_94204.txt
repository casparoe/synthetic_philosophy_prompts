I am drafting a position statement on the necessity of origin for a graduate seminar and would like an audit of it before I circulate the draft. What follows is the package as I would state it to an unsympathetic colleague. I want to know which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be; for each genuine tension, what has to give and at what cost; the genuine tensions ranked by severity; and, at the end, the single revision that would clear up the most. I am not asking for a survey of the literature — I want the internal audit.

(i) Necessity of origin for persons. For every human person there is a particular sperm and a particular egg such that the person could not have originated from anything else. The question I am always asked, whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg, therefore gets a flat no. I take this to be a necessary truth about the individual rather than a constraint on our concept of a person, and I take there to be a determinate fact of the matter in every case: it is never indeterminate whether some possible individual is me.

(ii) The genome is not essential. The same starting point could have yielded a person differing from me in any number of ways — a mutation in an early blastomere, a different course of development, a taller or blue-eyed or otherwise differently specified body — and that person would still have been me. Nothing in my genetic or phenotypic make-up looks essential.

(iii) Origin essentialism is not a thesis about persons in particular. It holds for any individual whose existence begins at a definite event: an organism, a cell, a zygote.

(iv) Personal identity over time is psychological continuity. Were my mental life transferred into a fresh body, or a body assembled elsewhere with my memories and dispositions installed in it, the person who emerged would be me — and I mean this as a criterion of identity, not merely as a claim about what matters in survival.

(v) The awkward cases, and my verdicts on them. In tetragametic chimerism, where two zygotes fuse and the resulting person is built from two distinct cell lines; in mitochondrial replacement, where the nuclear genome comes from one pair of gametes and the mitochondrial genome from a third party's egg; and in the laboratory case of a zygote that divides and whose two separated halves are recombined into a single embryo before implantation — in none of these do I want to say that one person has two origins, or that there are two people where we thought there was one.

I suspect this package cannot be held as stated, but I cannot locate the fault line. Find it, and be specific about which commitment has to move.
