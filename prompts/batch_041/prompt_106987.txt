I'm putting together a short unit on why pain is bad and I want to nail the spec before my students read any actual theories. So: what does a theory of the badness of pain have to get right to count as adequate? Good enough for what, you ask — good enough that it tells me what to say about pain in animals and infants without hand-waving, and good enough to survive the weird clinical cases. Give me the requirements list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't just collapse, with the specific case or consideration behind each requirement. And tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudice of one camp.

A few things I want on the table, since they're the ones that keep tripping me up:

- The asymbolia cases. There are patients — with certain brain injuries, I think around the insula? — who will say they feel pain, can localize it and rate its intensity, but it doesn't bother them, doesn't hurt, they don't pull away. If pain is supposed to be intrinsically bad, what the hell is going on there? Any theory that can't say something here looks dead to me, but I'm honestly not sure whether the right move is to deny that they're in pain or to deny that the pain is bad for them.
- The sensation/affective split. There's a difference between the intensity of a pain and how unpleasant it is, and the two can seperate. Does a theory of the badness of pain have to be a theory of the second thing specifically, or does that just relocate the problem?
- Pains people want or at least don't mind — masochism, religious flagellation, the soreness after a hard workout. Is the requirement here that badness is relative to desire, or that these cases are somehow being misdescribed?
- Creatures that feel pain but arguably don't have the concepts or desires we do — infants, animals. Does the theory owe them an account of why it's bad for them, and can it give one without smuggling in higher cognition?
- The protective/functional story. Pain keeps you from damaging yourself; that's why congenital insensitivity to pain is a catastrophe. Does a theory of why pain is bad have to be consistent with it's usefulness, or even explain the badness by it?

Then the part I most want: is there a subset of these that can't all be satisfied at once? I suspect the 'intrinsically bad' requirement and the asymbolia requirement pull against each other, and that the desire-based route which handles masochism blows up on infants. Say which pair is in genuine tension and why, and whether that means the list itself is a result.

Finally: which single requirement does the most damage, i.e. knocks out alot of the popular theories on its own? My guess is asymbolia, but tell me if I'm wrong. And if it's cheap to do, a line or two on how the main contenders — hedonism about pain, desire/attitude theories, perceptual theories — score against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria though; the scorecard I can do in class.

Please write it in American English.
