For the past year I've been reporting on the revived argument over whether a planned economy could work in an age of cheap computation, and I keep watching the same impasse: Mises's claim that without market prices there is no rational basis for allocating capital, Hayek's claim that the knowledge required is dispersed, tacit, and discovered only through competition, and Lange's reply that a central board could set prices by trial and error and correct them as a market would. Each side restates its case and the other does not budge. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is partly verbal, and partly an artifact of three distinct objections being run together -- an information problem (can a planner use knowledge that already exists?), a discovery problem (can a planner generate knowledge that does not yet exist?), and an incentive problem (will anyone act on the plan?) -- with Lange answering only the first. Diagnose it for me: is that right, and if so, which objection is actually doing the work in the modern revival? Then tell me what a non-question-begging version of the debate would look like, and what argument, formal result, or evidence would genuinely move it.
