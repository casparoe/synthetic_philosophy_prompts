We've got a decision to make and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, so I need help drafting it.

The situation: our committee has a rule that goes back decades — we don't vote on an acquisition unless at least three of the five of us have stood in front of the actual work, in person, no reproductions. It's in our bylaws, nobody remembers who wrote it. Now an alum donor will pay for us to buy a piece by an artist we've been chasing for years, but only if we commit by the 15th; there's another museum waiting on her. The catch is the work is in a private collection overseas, the owner won't lend it and won't let it travel, so none of us can go stand in front of it. Our curator has seen it and is pushing hard — she says its the most important thing to come our way in twenty years and would anchor the new wing. She's offered everything: a conservator's report, very high-res images, a 4K walkthrough video, and an hour-long account of what it's like to actually be in the room with it. One of my fellow members says that's precisely what the rule exists to prevent, and that voting on it means pretending to know something we dont.

Heres the wrinkle that I think is the real reason I'm writing you. The piece is a conceptual work — as far as I can tell the "work" is basically a written set of instructions plus a certificate, and the museum is meant to carry them out; the artist has said the piece only comes into existence once someone performs it. So there may be nothing to stand in front of. Is there even an original for us to be acquainted with? Second wrinkle: we waived the rule once before, for a video installation, and we've bought prints and photographs without viewing them (multiples, so any good impression seems as good as any other). Whatever we decide, someone will call us inconsistent.

In class we covered the idea that you can't really get aesthetic knowledge secondhand — that testimony can tell you what other people find beautiful but can't put you in a position to judge for yourself — and also a distinction between works with a unique original and works that exist in multiple faithful copies (scores, prints from an edition). I have a feeling both of these are doing real work here and I don't know how to use them.

What I need: draft the short memo/opinion I'll circulate before Thursday.
