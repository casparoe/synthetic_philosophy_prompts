I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and a disagreement with a classmate has stopped being productive. We were arguing about whether the retributivist/consequentialist divide survives contact with political transitions, and I floated what I called the amnesty test: take a settlement that ends a civil war only by granting a blanket amnesty to combatants on both sides, including people who ordered massacres, where the alternative to the deal is another decade of fighting and tens of thousands of additional deaths. My claim is that the best version of retributivism — the view that hard treatment is justified by and proportionate to deserved suffering — cannot adequately accommodate that case, that the best version of consequentialism can, and that this asymmetry is a genuine and non-trivial point in favor of consequentialism in the overall comparison of the two theories. My classmate thinks I've built a strawman and puts her credence in that claim at about 0.15; I'm at roughly 0.7, and I suspect we are disagreeing about something other than the number.

Give me your own credence in that claim, and give it to me as a decomposition rather than a verdict: rough probabilities with a line of reasoning for each, and how they combine. I want terms covering at least these sub-questions. First, whether the best retributivism really cannot accommodate the amnesty — as opposed to accommodating it as deserved suffering that is outweighed by other reasons, or as the absence of any institutional duty to collect the debt, or by distinguishing the justification of punishment from the state's reasons for actually punishing. Second, whether the best consequentialism can justify the amnesty without quietly reimporting the deterrence-preserving constraints a rule-consequentialist would impose, which would make it as restrictive as its rival. Third, whether this case tests a theory's core or merely its emergency clause — that is, whether failing a case type is disqualifying at all, or whether theories are properly compared holistically, with hard cases expected on both sides. Fourth, whether the moral-remainder phenomenon — the intuition that something is still owed to the victims even when the deal is plainly the right thing to do — actually favors retributivism rather than consequentialism, since a theory with nothing left to say once the consequentialist calculation has been run may be the one with the explanatory gap. Add any terms I have missed, and if a hybrid view — desert fixing the outer bounds of permissible severity while forward-looking considerations select within that range — makes the question partly mis-posed, tell me how that should change my number rather than merely dissolving the question. Then tell me which single sub-question my number is most sensitive to, what argument or piece of evidence would move it most, and, since my classmate and I are only 0.55 apart, which of the sub-questions is the likeliest site of our real disagreement — that is, which one we would most plausibly still disagree about even if we agreed on all the others.
