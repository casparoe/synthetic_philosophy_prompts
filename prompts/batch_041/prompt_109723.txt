Ok so I'm a critical care fellow and I sit on our hospital's ethics comittee, and I did a philosophy degree before med school, which means I'm the person everyone asks about this stuff, which is a curse. Here's the thing I can't stop turning over.

Our law, the Uniform Determination of Death Act, says — and I'm quoting the actual statute here — "An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead. A determination of death must be made in accordance with accepted medical standards." Both criteria sit in the same sentence like two doors into the same room. In practice they aren't even the same shape.

Camp one, the brain-death people: a patient who meets the whole-brain criteria is dead, full stop. The ventilator is inflating a corpse. The heart beats and the chest rises, but that's a machine and a body, not a person. Camp two, the skeptics: that's a legal fiction wearing a lab coat. Those bodies are still integrated living organisms — they heal wounds, fight off infections, hold their electrolytes in balance, I've read case reports of brain-dead women kept on support long enough to deliver a baby — and the only reason we call them dead is that we need a permission slip to take the organs. On this view we decided they were dead; we didn't discover it.

And then the part that actually keeps me up. In DCD — donation after circulatory death — we withdraw life support, watch the pulse stop, wait a few minutes, and declare death. But everybody in that room knows the heart isn't irreversibly stopped. It's just permanent: permanent meaning we've decided not to restart it and it won't kick back on by itself. Restarting it is a decision we've ruled out, not a physical impossibility. So by the brain criterion we demand irreversibility, and by the circulatory criterion we quietly settle for permanence. Which is a word doing an enormous amount of unexamined work, and I suspect it's smuggling in a value judgment about when it's ok to stop.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A real factual dispute about what death is? A verbal one, where one side means the biological event and the other means the legal status? A normative fight about which values we want encoded in a definition of death — the dead-donor rule, organ supply, public trust in medicine? Or two groups answering different questions under one word, "when does an organism stop being alive" versus "when may we treat someone as no longer one of us"?

My hunch, offered as a hunch: it's mostly normative wearing a lab coat, and the biology is doing almost none of the work — that every live version of "death" here is really a decision about when we're permitted to stop and to harvest, and the two sides just have different answers to the permission question while pretending to answer the factual one. But I dont fully trust my own cynicism, so tell me where it's wrong.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right, and I want the reasons grounded in what each side actually commits itself to, not a survey. And I want the honest bit: what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of data, a formal result, an argument, a change of question — or is this one of those that just sits there forever because it isn't really one question. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and say why.
