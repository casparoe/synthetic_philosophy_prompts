I am trying to understand the exact probative force of the cogito in the following passage from the Second Meditation:

"Doubtless, then, I exist, since I am deceived; and, let him deceive me as he may, he can never bring it about that I am nothing, so long as I shall be conscious that I am something. So that it must, in fine, be maintained, all things being maturely and carefully considered, that this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind."

What, precisely, does this prove? I am not asking for the standard summary that Descartes proves his own existence. I want to know how far the argument actually reaches. Does the indubitability attach to the proposition "I am, I exist," to the act of asserting or conceiving it, or to the existence of a thinker who performs that act? The parenthetical "pronunciatum" interests me here: does it mark the certainty as belonging to the uttered or entertained proposition in act, rather than to a timeless truth? And does the clause "each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind" make the certainty episodic—true on each occasion of thought—and if so, does that undermine any inference to a persisting thinking substance? If the passage proves only that whenever there is thinking there is some subject or other, what is the status of the "I" that is said to exist? Is it a substance, a bare subject of predication, a grammatical illusion, or simply the occurrent event of thinking itself? And how does the surrounding language of deception and consciousness—"I am deceived," "so long as I shall be conscious that I am something"—bear on the argument? Does it presuppose a continuous conscious subject rather than prove one?

I would like a careful reconstruction that separates what is strictly entailed by the passage from what later readers have read into it. In particular, I want to understand whether the cogito is best understood as proving the existence of a thing, the reality of thought, or the impossibility of coherently doubting while thinking. Please be self-critical about the interpretation: if my question rests on a confusion—for instance, if I am wrongly demanding that the cogito prove a substance when Descartes need not be doing that, or if I am conflating the order of discovery with metaphysical dependence—say so plainly rather than giving a polite synthesis. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the philosophical issue laid out.
