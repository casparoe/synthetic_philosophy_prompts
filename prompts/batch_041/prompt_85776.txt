I am a novelist, and the book I am writing is about a defeat and the uses one family makes of it over four generations. The trouble is that I cannot decide which of the world's defeat-myths I am allowed to put in my characters' mouths, because my own reactions refuse to line up into anything I can state. I admire the Masada story — the last stand, the deaths chosen rather than suffered, the modern vow that the place will not fall again — even knowing that it reaches us through a single ancient source written by a man on the other side, and I would build a novel on it tomorrow. The Confederate Lost Cause has the same skeleton — a lost war, gallant men, sacred ground, a memory tended deliberately by women's associations after the men had lost — and I find it repellent; I do not think the skeleton is what distinguishes them. Kosovo moves me as poetry and as grief, and the same poem put to work as a claim on land in the last decade of the twentieth century strikes me as monstrous, and I cannot tell whether that is a corruption of the myth or simply what the myth was for. Dunkirk I admire, though it is a myth of rescue rather than of sacrifice, it asks me to forget how much of the deliverance was luck, and it flatters a national habit of muddling through. Then there is Custer: I know perfectly well that the story celebrates a vain man on the wrong side of a war of dispossession, that the myth was made by the very nation whose army he lost to, and still the last stand works on me. My grandmother's village had its own version of all this — a strike in the twenties that was broken, and a song about a man who would not sign — and I love it while being fairly sure it is not true.

I have tried to state the principle behind these reactions and I keep failing. The nearest I have come is this: a defeat-myth is honourable to the extent that it is a myth of what the losers chose — an endurance, a refusal, a death taken up — and dishonourable to the extent that it is a myth of what was done to them, a betrayal or a wound; and honourable to the extent that it asks something of those who remember, dishonourable to the extent that it makes a claim on others. But Dunkirk is a myth of what was done to us and I keep it, and the Lost Cause is full of what the losers chose and I do not want it. Tell me whether one principle, or a small set of them, can hold my verdicts together; for each clause you propose, tell me which of my cases forces it; tell me which of my verdicts I should abandon if only the simpler principle will do; and apply whichever principle you favour to a defeat-myth I have not mentioned, choosing the case yourself. I would also like you to mark plainly which parts of this are settled among people who actually know these histories and which are genuinely contested, and not to hand me a contested reading as though it were the consensus. I am going to give these ideas to characters I love, and I would rather know where I am taking liberties.
