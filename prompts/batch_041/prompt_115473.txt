I sit on the priority-setting committee of a regional health system — eleven members, mostly clinicians, plus two lay members, of whom I am one — and our vote on the standing allocation rule is ten days out. I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, which is why I drafted the clause in our charter saying that allocation decisions must be defensible to each affected person and not merely to the population as a whole. I now suspect I wrote something I cannot operationalize, and the memo I have to circulate before the vote is my attempt to find out.

The question is how to spend the remainder of this year's fund for high-cost treatments. The staff proposal ranks claims by expected health gain per dollar. On that ranking, roughly nine hundred patients with a common condition, each of whom would gain about half a quality-adjusted life-year, outrank four patients with a rare condition who would each gain something in the neighborhood of a dozen years and who will die within the year if untreated. The staff proposal buys on the order of ten times the total health gain of the alternative, which funds the four. One colleague wants a straight lottery across all nine hundred and four claimants; another wants a rule that never lets a large number of modest gains outweigh a small number of catastrophic losses, whatever the totals say.

The contractualist idea I keep circling — Scanlon's, roughly, that a principle is legitimate only if no one affected by it has adequate grounds to object, and that what matters is the force of each person's objection rather than the sum of benefits produced — may or may not resolve this case, and I cannot tell whether the standard defense of aggregation, which asks me to imagine choosing the rule from behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of being any claimant, is a genuine argument or merely aggregation under a different description. Our charter also requires that every denied claimant receive a written statement of reasons, so whatever rule we adopt has to be something we are prepared to write down and hand to the people it excludes. I do not want a memo that recites slogans in either direction; the committee has one member who is effective-altruism-adjacent and reflexively sums, and one who reaches for Kantian language about using people as means, and neither move has persuaded anyone of anything.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete rule, specify a decision procedure the staff can actually apply to individual claims, say what we should do with the four pending applications before this quarter's deadline, and name a date by which we should revisit the rule. It should give the strongest version of the position it rejects its due: if you recommend the aggregative rule, tell me plainly what we are saying to the four, and draft the notice one of them would receive; if you recommend against it, tell me what we are saying to the nine hundred. Skip the literature review — I know the terrain well enough and want the argument, not a reading list. Keep it short enough that I can read it aloud in ten minutes.
