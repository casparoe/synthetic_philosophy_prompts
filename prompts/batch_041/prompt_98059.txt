Treat the opening of the Berlin Wall on the night of 9 November 1989 as a test case for the concept of overdetermination in historical explanation, and run the analysis in both directions: the concept against the episode, and the episode against the concept.

Work from the following facts, so that nothing turns on details you cannot check. Through 1989 the East German regime was under pressure from several independent directions at once. The Soviet leadership under Gorbachev had signaled that it would not use force to keep allied governments in power. Hungary had opened its border with Austria, and large numbers of East Germans had left through it. The Monday demonstrations in Leipzig and other cities had grown past the point at which the state could disperse them by threat. And the economy was buckling under foreign debt and shortages. None of these is a triggering event; each is a standing condition or a process. The trigger, by contrast, looks almost accidental: at a press conference that evening the SED spokesman, Günter Schabowski, working from a note he had not been properly briefed on, announced that the new and more permissive travel regulations would take effect at once — when in fact they were to begin the next day and still required applications. Crowds took the announcement as an opening and gathered at the crossings; the border guards, without orders and without any will to use force, let them through; and within hours the Wall had ceased to function as a barrier.

What I want is not a narrative of 1989 but an argument about what it would mean to call this event overdetermined.

First, fix the concept. Distinguish overdetermination in the strict, metaphysical sense — a single token event with two or more causes each of which was on its own sufficient to produce it, so that removing any one of them would have made no difference — from the loose historiographical sense of "many causes," conjunctural causation, or a plurality of contributing conditions. Say clearly which sense, if either, the episode can bear.

Second, do the mapping. Identify the candidate causes, and say honestly whether any of them is sufficient in the strict sense, or whether each is at best a condition that raised the probability of the outcome. Say, for each, whether it is an insufficient but non-redundant part of some unnecessary but sufficient condition — the familiar INUS pattern — and whether the conjunction of them is itself sufficient. If the structural factors are only contributory and the trigger is only contributory, then the event is multi-causal but not overdetermined; say so, and say what that verdict costs.

Third, apply the counterfactual test and report what it does. Had Schabowski not misspoken, would the Wall have come down anyway? If yes, the standard counterfactual analysis of causation is forced to deny that his slip caused the opening, even though it plainly did; if no, then there is no overdetermination here after all. This is the familiar shape of the overdetermination problem for counterfactual theories, and I want you to show how it lands in this particular case rather than rehearsing it in the abstract. Consider, too, whether the episode is better described as preemption than as symmetric overdetermination — the structural causes standing ready, as it were, to bring about the fall, with the press conference preempting them — and whether the standard distinctions among early, late, and trumping preemption fit.

Fourth, ask whether the concept survives the transfer at all. Overdetermination as the metaphysician uses it presupposes causes that are sufficient, and hence deterministic, for their effects; historical explanation traffics instead in probabilities, dispositions, and open-ended processes. Is there a defensible notion of overdetermination for history that does not require token sufficiency — for instance, a notion of robustness, on which an outcome is overdetermined when it would have occurred across a wide range of ways the antecedent conditions might have gone? If so, state it precisely and say what it would and would not license; if not, say what historians are actually doing when they call an outcome overdetermined.

Finally, close on the concept itself. Does the episode show that overdetermination is a metaphor in history, borrowing authority from a metaphysical notion that does not really apply, or does it show the reverse — that the metaphysical notion is an abstraction from exactly this kind of case, and that we should trust the historical usage more than the philosopher's? And say where the concept is silent. Overdetermination is a claim about what made the outcome happen; tell me what it leaves untouched — questions of significance, of responsibility, of what the night meant to the people in the crowd — and whether that silence is a defect or simply the boundary of a causal concept.

Do not give me a survey of views on causation, and do not lean on authorities I cannot look up. Argue the case.
