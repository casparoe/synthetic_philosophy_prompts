For a long while now I've kept circling back to Dennett's four grades of creature — the Darwinian, wired by selection; the Skinnerian, wired to learn from reinforcement; the Popperian, able to run its trials in simulation and let the wrong hypotheses die in its stead; and the Gregorian, which augments itself with the thinking tools of its culture — and I've been mildly annoyed that a taxonomy this useful is almost never used as comic material. Every joke about it settles for the same lazy pigeon gag, and nobody ever tries to make the Gregorian grade genuinely funny, which seems like a waste given that its entire content is the idea that we offload cognition onto external tools. So I'd like you to write me a joke that takes the taxonomy seriously enough to be funny on its own terms — the sort of thing you could tell at a LessWrong meetup without anyone suspecting you'd skimmed the Wikipedia summary.

Here's the kind of setup I have in mind, though you should feel free to reshape it: the creatures are all in one room, confronted with the same novel problem — say a vending machine with a single unlabeled button — and the comedy comes from the specific move each one makes. The binding constraint is that the punchline has to turn on the actual content of the grades rather than on their labels: someone who knows the taxonomy should laugh at the precise thing each creature does, and someone who doesn't should be able to reconstruct the distinctions from the joke alone. I also want one new element folded in. I've been toying with the idea of a fifth grade — call it the Hayekian creature — which never runs a simulation of its own and instead treats the aggregate behavior of other creatures (a price, a queue, a rating) as a compressed summary of trials it never personally ran. I'd like the punchline to hinge on the difference between a creature that tests its hypotheses internally and one that buys its answers off a signal generated by other creatures' trials; that distinction should be doing the comic work, not just decorating it.

Keep it tight — a paragraph or so of setup and a landing line that earns the laugh. End the whole response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence which of the grades the punchline ultimately turns on.
