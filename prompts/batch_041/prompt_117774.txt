Six months ago I moved from Kyoto to Delft for a postdoc, and the adjustment I have found hardest is not the weather but the way disagreement is handled. A colleague will tell me in her first sentence that a draft is weak or that an idea will not work, and when I once complimented a seminar talk I had not enjoyed — out of what I took to be ordinary courtesy — a friend told me, not unkindly, that I had told a white lie, that white lies are the oil that keeps the social machine running, and that this is exactly why the Dutch manner is better: it spares everyone the maintenance. A week later the same picture came back at me from the other direction, when an expatriate friend insisted that my old habits were the real lubricant and the bluntness around us the sand in the gears. It struck me that we were both pulling on the same metaphor in opposite directions without either of us asking what it can actually bear, and that the people who invoke it most confidently seem to think it settles the moral question rather than merely picturing it.

The reason this has stopped being an idle puzzle is that in three weeks a visiting student from Kyoto will hand me a chapter she intends to submit, and I will have to decide what to say to her; the oil metaphor is the only thing anyone around me has offered by way of guidance, and it seems to point at bluntness. I am enrolled in a part-time course in applied ethics, and the more I read about deception the less I trust the image — partly because the things we lump together as white lies are so various: the formulaic invitation that nobody intends to act on, the false compliment about work the speaker privately rates, the excuse that protects the person giving it rather than the person receiving it. I would like an audit of the metaphor rather than an endorsement or a denunciation of it, and I would like the audit to be explicit about the work the picture is being asked to do in my circles, namely licensing the conclusion that a small untruth told to spare someone's feelings is a trivial cost that no serious moral view should worry about.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing summary, one or two sentences per item. The first should be the correspondence table, stated plainly: what plays the role of the oil, the machine, the friction, and the maintenance. The second should be the disanalogies that matter for the use just described, separated from the ones that are merely decorative. The third should be what the picture quietly imports that the target lacks — about who applies the lubricant to whom, about consent, and about whether a lubricated machine is still a machine whose parts can report on their own condition. The fourth should say whether the conclusion survives once those mismatches are on the table, and whether it survives equally for all three sorts of white lie I listed or only for some of them. The fifth should say what a better comparison would be if this one is past repair, and which of the two opposed uses of the metaphor — mine or my colleagues' — it can be made to support.
