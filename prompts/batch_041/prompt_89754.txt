I'm a novelist, and the book I'm writing turns on a wager. My protagonist has built a machine that grinds through the proofs of a formal system, one after another, and will stop the instant it finds a proof of a particular arithmetical statement — a statement he can neither prove nor refute, and which is, as he keeps telling anyone who will listen, either true or false with nothing in between. His backer has offered him a large sum if the machine halts within its first ten-to-the-hundredth steps, and will take his house if it doesn't. That window is finite, and therefore already settled one way or the other, though no one alive could settle it by running the thing. I wanted to know whether a sensible man in that position could hold a middling confidence — three in five, say — that the machine will halt, so I raised it at dinner with two friends, and they went at each other harder than I had bargained for.

Nadia, who spent her twenties doing proof theory, opened. (1) She said a middling credence here isn't merely unverifiable, it's incoherent: the machine's behaviour is fixed, there is no chance process for the credence to be about, and degrees of belief are supposed to answer to logical relations — if you're certain of P and certain that P implies Q, you must be certain of Q. Close your credences under the consequences of what you already know, she said, and any assignment that isn't 0 or 1 on a settled matter either contradicts itself or collapses to the extremes. The honest report is 'I don't know', and that is not a degree of belief at all. (2) Ewan, a statistician, said that proves far too much: he doesn't know how many coins are in the jar on the shelf, and there's no chance process in that either, yet a middling credence there is plainly sensible, and it improves when someone weighs the jar. What makes a credence rational, he said, is not that a die is being rolled but that the evidence discriminates — and here it does: the machine's code, the number of steps it has already survived, the shape of the proof it is hunting for, whether anyone has established a bound. When he says three in five he means he'd take the bet at those odds, and he'd expect to come out ahead over a run of such bets; moreover the credence is not idle, since if the machine has run nine-tenths of the way without halting, the question of whether it halts within the window has a different answer, and you can say by how much. (3) Nadia came back at the updating. It only works, she said, if you treat this machine as drawn from a class of machines with a known distribution of running times, and that reference class is doing all the work. All machines of that length? All machines that man has written? All machines that have survived this many steps? Choose differently and the number changes, so the three in five is an artefact of a classification you had no right to. (4) Ewan conceded the reference-class problem was real, but said it was a problem about doing the thing well, not about whether to do it at all — and that you cannot escape it by refusing, because refusing is also a policy, whether of credence one-half, or of never betting, or of treating both outcomes as equally likely, and each of those has consequences too. A rule that is explicit, coherent and improvable beats a pretence that the question has no answer; and, he added, an agent who insists the only honest answers are 0 and 1 is an agent who is certain of one of two exclusive outcomes, which is precisely the agent a bookmaker wants to meet. (5) Nadia said that last point was where she would push hardest. Imagine a bookmaker permitted to read your credence before you bet, who offers you this wager: you win if your credence in the proposition is less than a half, and lose otherwise. Whatever rule you adopt, either you take the bet and lose, or you decline and, in declining, reveal the answer. No bounded rule survives contact with an adversary who can read the rule — which shows that a coherent, ungameable, non-extremal credence function is not something a limited thinker can have. It is an ideal object, defined by facts (the full logical closure of what you know) that no limited thinker can see, and a norm defined by inaccessible facts gives you no guidance about what to think now. (6) Ewan said the adversary was the whole difficulty: a being who can read your credences can read your evidence, and if it can read your evidence it can very likely simply work out the answer, so she had described an unfair game rather than a defect in his credence. Remove the mind-reading and the self-referential wager loses its teeth; any bet on a settled question is a bet against someone better informed, and the remedy is to know your counterparty, not to stop having degrees of belief. And her own picture is no safer, he said: the man who is certain the machine will halt is fleeced by anyone who knows it won't, so the gaming argument cuts the other way — it is certainty without knowledge that is the dangerous state, not three in five.

Please go through the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly, attending to what is actually said rather than to the position each of them might be taken to hold. Then tell me who, if either, has the better of the argument overall, and what both of them are missing — in particular, whether there is a defensible position between theirs that survives everything each of them says, and what exactly it commits one to. I have no use for a reading list; I want to know which of these lines I can put in a character's mouth without making him a fool, and which of the two should win the scene — not because I want a verdict that flatters my plot, but because I would like the loser to lose on the merits.
