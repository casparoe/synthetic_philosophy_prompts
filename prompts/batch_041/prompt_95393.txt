I'm a novelist, four books, all of them in close third or first person, which means my entire technique consists of manufacturing the impression of another person's interiority out of behavior, speech, and small physical detail. I'm two years into a novel set in the 1660s whose central figure is a mechanical servant, a clockwork man or something close enough, who moves and answers and works, and about whom the human characters cannot decide whether anything is going on inside. So I've been living with Descartes' animals for months: the claim that beasts are machines, the two tests he offers for distinguishing a machine from a mind (a machine could never use language in the responsive, non-formulaic way we do, and could never fail intelligently, improvising competently across the whole range of situations rather than excelling at one narrow task), and the suggestion that the gap between beast and man is a difference in kind rather than degree. My difficulty is that I can only render this from the outside; my toolkit has no way of asserting what is or isn't there, only of showing how it looks from a vantage point that already assumes something is there.

So I want you to do something for me rather than simply explain him. Restate Descartes' position on animal minds, as faithfully as you can, entirely in the vocabulary of a working novelist: character, voice, point of view, interiority, free indirect discourse, the unreliable narrator, dramatic irony, what a scene can and cannot assert, the difference between a character and a device. And please don't resolve on his behalf the dispute I keep running into over whether he denied animals sensation or only thought; if the vocabulary forces a verdict, I want to see it forced. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of his view translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and where exactly does the distortion creep in? I suspect my craft's conventions of interiority are doing the smuggling, and I'd like to know whether that's so. Which parts cannot be said at all: is there anything in Descartes that a novelist, qua novelist, has no way to express, and does that indicate a gap in the novelist's toolkit or something in Descartes that was never as clear as its slogan? I'm especially interested in the asymmetry at the bottom of his view, that each of us has direct access to one mind and only inference to every other, and whether that can be represented novelistically at all, or whether the omniscient narrator is precisely the device that papers over it.

Finally, tell me which way the translation pushes the interpretive question. If the novelist's vocabulary can only make sense of a creature that has some interiority, is that because the vocabulary is impoverished, or because the alternative is not a coherent thing to say about anything, beast or man? I'm fluent in my own trade and only an educated reader of philosophy. I've read the Discourse and the Meditations, not the correspondence or the commentators, so don't lean on scholarship I'd have to go look up, and don't give me the standard line about Descartes the cruel mechanist. I want to see what his position looks like when it has to be staged rather than asserted.
