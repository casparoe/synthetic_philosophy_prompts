I attend a weekly reading group on decision theory and rational choice, and last session the convenor set us a puzzle that split the room along lines I still can't adjudicate. The puzzle is the familiar symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, but with the stipulation that the two players are known to be running the same deterministic decision procedure — same program, same inputs, no communication, and no observation of the other's choice until afterwards. Two of the regulars, Dana and Milo, took opposite sides and argued it out over several rounds while the rest of us listened. I'd like you to reconstruct their exchange and adjudicate it point by point; I genuinely don't know who has the better of it, and I would rather not be told merely that "it depends what you mean by rationality" without an account of what it depends on.

Dana's line was this. In the symmetric case my choice is perfectly correlated with my counterpart's, because we are running the same program on the same problem; so choosing to cooperate is evidence that my counterpart cooperates, and choosing to defect is evidence that they defect. Deliberating as though my choice were independent of theirs is precisely the mistake that makes the dilemma look like a dilemma; once you condition on the correlation, cooperation is the option that maximizes expected payoff, and the position sometimes called superrationality is just ordinary evidential reasoning applied honestly to a symmetric case. Milo objected that correlation is not the right kind of dependence: my counterpart's program is a fixed function, and my output does not cause theirs, so my cooperating cannot make them cooperate — I am simply one of two identical machines producing the same output, and dressing that up as a reason for action confuses a fact about what we both do with a reason for me to do it. What actually licenses cooperation, he said, is not evidence but identification: I should deliberate as a member of the two-person unit rather than as an isolated individual, and ask which of the four joint outcomes is best for the pair, which is the familiar team-reasoning move. Dana's reply was that this either changes the subject or smuggles in an assumption. If team reasoning works by making me care about the pair's payoff rather than my own, then it has not shown that cooperation is rational for a self-interested agent; it has changed the agent. And if it leaves my preferences alone and merely invites me to adopt a "we" perspective, then it owes an account of when the "we" has authority over me — otherwise any individual can dissolve into any collective and call whatever it then does rational. Milo answered that this misdescribes the move: he is not asking anyone to prefer the pair's payoff, but to recognize that in the symmetric case there is no separate individual whose payoff is at stake in the relevant deliberative sense — the pair is the agent, much as a team or a firm is a single agent when it acts — and that Dana's insistence on the atomic individual is a stipulation rather than an argument. Neither would concede, and the convenor, unhelpfully, said they were both right and adjourned.

Please go through the exchange move by move — Dana's evidential argument, Milo's correlation objection, Milo's team-reasoning alternative, Dana's reply about preference change and the authority of the "we," and Milo's rejoinder about the unit of agency — and rate each from clearly mistaken through questionable to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing: in particular, whether there is a way of understanding the superrational recommendation that is neither evidential reasoning nor a change in the agent's preferences, and if so whether it survives Dana's objection about the authority of the collective.
