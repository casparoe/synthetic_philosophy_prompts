I sit on the transplant selection committee at the hospital where I work — I'm the ethics person on the panel, which mostly means I'm the one who reads the policy out loud. We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion to the other members beforehand, so I need you to draft it.

Background: our program's rule for alcohol-related liver disease is flat. No listing without 90 days of documented abstinence, and our committee's job is only to confirm the 90 days were real. The only escape hatch is one sentence saying the medical director "may grant an exception in unusual cases." Its been invoked twice in five years, and both times the patient's family had already retained a lawyer. Nobody on the committee is proud of that sentence.

The case in front of us: a 41-year-old woman, alcohol-related liver disease, 11 weeks since her last drink (under the rule, but only just), enrolled in an intensive outpatient program, has a sponsor she actually talks to, decompensating — her hepatologist says months, not years, if we don't list. Two kids, 9 and 14; partner works nights. One relapse two years ago, right after her mother died. The addiction medicine consult says her odds of sustained sobriety are a coin flip, honestly. If we wait for day 90 she may not be alive for it.

Two of my colleagues are dug in and the memo needs to deal with both of them seriously.

One says the flat rule is the whole point: it protects patients from us, keeps us from rewarding whoever has the best advocate, and spares us from pretending we can predict relapse. She says a committee that starts bending rules for sympathetic cases ends up bending them for the articulate and the well-off, and points at those two lawyer-assisted exceptions as proof. She's not wrong about that.

The other wants to delete the number entirely and judge each case on its particulars. Her line is that the features we'd point to as reasons to list this woman — her kids, her sponsor, the bereavement that preceded her relapse — are reasons the other way in some other case (the teenager caregiving for a parent, the sponsor who's the only thing holding a fragile routine together, the grief that hasn't been worked through yet), so any waiver-factor list we write is a fiction about our own consistency and it'll get gamed within a year by anyone who's read it. She's read a lot of moral particularism and I think that's where she's coming from — the idea that there just aren't any true general moral principles to apply, only the case. I'm definately not sure I buy it but I can't refute it either, and I notice our current rule is exactly the kind of thing she's attacking.

Our social worker made a suggestion I want you to react to: keep the 90 days as the default, but require that every departure be written up as a short anonymized case note, circulated to the committee, with no departure binding the next panel (no precedent, each decision stands alone), and once a year we read all the notes together and ask whether any pattern in them should be promoted into the actual rule. That seems like it's trying to have both, and I can't tell if it's a real answer or a fudge.

So: draft the opinion. Give me a concrete recommendation up front that I can actually move — the medical director will ask me what the rule is now and I need an answer — plus the reasoning, plus the strongest case for the other side stated fairly enough that its defenders recognize themselves in it. Say whether the social worker's scheme should be adopted as-is, amended, or dropped, and why. If you think the right call is to hold her to the 90 days, say so; I'd rather circulate something I disagree with than mush. And tell me what I should say when someone asks why we don't just publish the waiver factors and be done with it — that's the question I'm least ready for.

One page, 800 words max, bullets where they help. No literature review, no citations, just the argument. I need an interim thing I can pass Thursday and a permanent version for the quarterly policy meeting in June.
