The line I'm running: everyone teaches Frankfurt cases as showing that moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities. I think that's wrong, but not in any of the usual ways. My claim is that Frankfurt cases are dialectically inert — they leave the compatibilism question exactly where they found it, and each of the standard patches (the flicker-of-freedom move, the dilemmic defence, the no-movement objection) either restates the problem or changes the subject. The reason is that in any genuine Frankfurt case the intervener is counterfactually idle; he never lifts a finger. What does the work is that the agent's action is over-determined by her own reasons. I call this self-overdetermination, and I argue that once you see it, the case is just an ordinary case of someone acting wholeheartedly on her own character — precisely the picture the compatibilist already accepts. So the cases can't settle anything, because they presuppose the compatibilist account of action rather than testing it. I realise there's an obvious comeback, that this is just the flicker-of-freedom defence in new clothes; I have a paragraph on why it isn't, but it isn't my strongest bit and I'd rather find that out now.
