I'm a fourth-year PhD student in applied mathematics — my thesis is on numerical methods for nonlinear PDEs, so I lean on Zorn's lemma and the Hahn–Banach theorem more or less weekly and have never once stopped to ask whether I'm entitled to them. Our department's foundations reading group is working through the opening chapter of a popular book on the philosophy of mathematics, and the chapter's treatment of the axiom of choice is the one section I have no way to check against anything I know. It runs, more or less, as follows.

"The axiom of choice says that given any collection of non-empty sets, one can form a new set by picking one element from each. For finite collections this is uncontroversial — you simply do it — and the axiom extends the same idea to infinite ones. What gives it teeth is that it asserts the existence of a choice set even when there is no rule telling you which element to take. Its chief use is that it licenses the well-ordering theorem, on which every set can be arranged in a well-ordered sequence; Zorn's lemma, the workhorse of modern algebra, is another name for the same fact. Its chief cost is the Banach–Tarski paradox, which shows that a solid ball can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled, using only rotations and translations, into two balls each the size of the original. Since no physical process could possibly do that, Banach–Tarski is the standard reason for thinking the axiom of choice is false; the standard repair is to restrict the axiom to countable collections, which is enough for analysis and delivers the Hahn–Banach theorem without the paradoxes. The independence results have settled the matter in a way: Gödel showed that the axiom is consistent with the rest of set theory, and Cohen showed that its negation is consistent too, so the axiom has no truth value. The choice between accepting and rejecting it is a matter of taste, and the only real question is which theorems you would rather have. Accept the axiom and you get Tychonoff's theorem and a basis for every vector space; reject it and you can keep every set of real numbers measurable."

I came away persuaded, which is why I'm writing. I had always assumed the axiom was simply true; I now suspect it is a bookkeeping convention, a useful fiction that buys theorems at the price of coherence — and something about that conclusion sits badly with me, though I can't articulate what.
