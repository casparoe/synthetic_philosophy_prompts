Here's the question I set (short answer, 10 points):

Two well-known accounts of what it is for a biological trait to have a function:

(i) The selection-history account: a trait's function is the effect it was selected for. Roughly, the function of a trait is the effect that explains why the trait was retained in the lineage, i.e. what its ancestors were selected for doing. If a trait was never selected for doing X, then doing X is not its function, however useful X happens to be.

(ii) The causal-contribution account: a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity or activity of the larger system it is a part of. Functions get assigned by analysing a system into its component capacities; what matters is what the part actually does in the system now, not where it came from.

Now consider a made-up case (nothing here depends on real natural history). A species of beetle gets blown onto a foggy island. Its leg hairs were selected for sensing vibrations in the substrate, and it still uses them for that. On the island, fog condenses on the hairs, the droplets run down to the mouth, and the beetles get water they would otherwise lack; beetles with denser leg hairs survive and reproduce more. We look at the population (a) immediately after arrival, before any beetle with denser hairs has been born, and (b) three thousand generations later, after selection has substantially thickened the hairs.

For each of (a) and (b), state whether the selection-history account and the causal-contribution account respectively attribute the function of water collection to the leg hairs, and briefly say why. Then: what does each account say about a heart that is malformed and cannot pump? Which account has an easier time with malfunction, and why?

The student's answer:

Both accounts are meant to be accounts of real biological facts rather than figures of speech, but they disagree about what grounds a function.

On the selection-history account, the hairs' function at (a) is vibration sensing and nothing else. Water collection is not a function yet, because no beetle has ever been selected for collecting water with its hairs — the trait's presence in the population is fully explained by its past sensing role, and the water collection is a lucky side effect. At (b), once selection has thickened the hairs, water collection becomes a function too, since the thickening was retained because it improved water intake. So the account is history-relative: the same structure can acquire a new function after enough selection.

On the causal-contribution account, the hairs already have the function of water collection at (a). What matters is what the hairs do in the system now: they reliably deliver water to the mouth and so contribute to the beetle's water balance, which is a capacity of the organism. Sensing is a function too, so at (a) the hairs have two functions on this account, and nothing has to wait for selection to license that.

On malfunction: take a malformed heart that cannot pump. The causal-contribution account handles this comfortably: the heart is a component of the circulatory system whose job is to move blood, so a heart that cannot move blood is malfunctioning, and we can say that without knowing anything about the animal's ancestry. The selection-history account actually has a harder time, because it defines function by what was selected for in the past, and past selection is what makes it awkward to say what a trait is supposed to do now: the malformed heart is still a heart, so the account has to fall back on the history of hearts in general, which is really a statistical fact about the population rather than a norm. That is why the causal-contribution account is usually thought to be the one that grounds the normativity of function, and the selection-history account is the more descriptive of the two.

One thing worth flagging: the causal-contribution account is sometimes accused of counting any effect as a function. That is a real problem — read literally, the heart's functions would include making noise, warming the blood, and taking up space in the thorax, which is absurd. So the account needs some way of picking out the relevant capacity.

Overall, the two accounts differ mainly over whether function is backward-looking or forward-looking, and the beetle case shows the selection-history account is the more restrictive of the two.
