I recently read a chapter in a popular book on epistemology that attempts to explain the relationship between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, and I would like to know how much of its account I can trust. The chapter's summary runs roughly as follows. Both views, it says, agree that whether someone knows something depends on more than the evidence in their possession, and they differ only in where they locate the variation. Contextualists hold that the word "knows" is context-sensitive, so that when a skeptic in a seminar says you cannot know you have hands while an ordinary person says of course she knows she has hands, both are speaking truly, because the epistemic standards in play differ between the two conversations. Pragmatic encroachment, the chapter continues, is simply this same insight applied to the subject rather than to the attributor: it says that whether you know depends on the practical stakes you face, so that with identical evidence you know the bank is open in a low-stakes situation and fail to know it in a high-stakes one. The chapter then asserts that the now-standard bank cases were constructed in order to prove pragmatic encroachment, and that the real lesson of those cases is that knowledge is not a genuine relation at all but merely a matter of what a community is willing to accept, which is why the position is often classified as a form of relativism. Finally it presents what it calls the decisive objection: surely whether you know something cannot depend on how much money is at stake, since knowledge is a matter of truth and evidence rather than consequences, and therefore both contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are refuted by common sense. I suspect the chapter is being unfair to at least one of these views, but I do not know the literature well enough to say precisely where the unfairness lies.
