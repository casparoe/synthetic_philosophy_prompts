You're a philosopher of probability with a good nose for when a puzzle is being papered over, and I need you to build me one case, to order.

Background: I'm putting together a session for a grad seminar on statistical evidence, and what I want on the table is the reference class problem in its nastiest form — not the toy version where the answer is just use the narrowest class. So start by brainstorming three or four candidate setups, say quickly why your throwing each one out (the cab problem, the mammogram, anything with lottery tickets, any case where one candidate class simply contains the other — all out), then commit to the one you keep and build it properly.

Specs for the case:

- Realistic. A hospital, an insurance office, a parole board, a maintenance crew — someplace a normal person actually ends up. No dice, no urns, no possible worlds.
- One individual, one non-repeatable event, and the probability we're after is the probability for that individual.
- There have to be exactly two reference classes that both look legitimate, and neither may be a subset of the other, so you can't fix it by narrowing. Both have to be defined by properties you'd know about the person before the outcome, and neither can be a gerrymander (no class like people born on a Tuesday who also happen to...).
- Make the two classes differ in kind: one of them mechanistic, dose-response, causal-ish; the other purely actuarial. That contrast is the bit I care about.
- The two answers have to come apart by a decent margin, not a hair. And the numbers have to fall out of how the classes are described — if you stipulate the probabilities by fiat, the case is cheating and I'll throw it out.

Then do the work. State the case in a paragraph, then give the verdict of (a) a straight relative-frequency frequentist, (b) a propensity theorist who thinks there's a genuine chance here, (c) a subjectivist who'd pick a class on some principled basis rather than by taste. For each, say which class they take and why. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the whole thing if I add a third class that refines both of the first two — does the puzzle dissolve or get worse? Finish with how someone who thinks maximal specificity solves this would attack your case, and whether the attack lands.

Think it through before you write anything. I'd rather have one case that actually holds up than four sketches.
