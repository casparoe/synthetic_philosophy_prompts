What I want to get clear on is how his argument there stands to a broader idea that keeps surfacing in our discussions: the thought that mindedness can simply be read off from behavior — that if a creature or a device produces the right sort of linguistic and problem-solving output, then we already possess all the evidence of a mind that we could reasonably ask for. As I read him, Descartes does not merely assert that animals lack thought; he offers what looks like a behavioral criterion, namely that no machine could ever use language the way we do, adjusting its utterances to the situation at hand, and that no machine could match a human across the whole range of activities, since even where it excelled it would betray itself somewhere else. I would like you to compare Descartes' specific argument with this more general behavioral criterion for mentality, and to say in what respects his version is doing something that the bare criterion is not.

I would rather you take that challenge seriously than brush it aside. If these systems genuinely do clear the behavioral bar, does Descartes' argument collapse, or was it always quietly relying on something beyond observable behavior — and if the latter, what exactly is that further something, and is it defensible?
