(1) Black's famous counterexample to Leibniz's law asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of exactly the same size, colour, temperature and composition, held a fixed distance apart. (2) The spheres share every property, so by the principle of identity of indiscernibles they ought to be one and the same object; since they are plainly two, the principle is false, and since that principle is just Leibniz's law, that law falls with it. (3) The example shows that there is more to identity than properties: the spheres are distinct without being distinguishable, so their distinctness is a brute fact and identity has to be taken as primitive. (4) It is no use replying that the spheres are distinguishable by their position, since position is a relation and a relation is not a property of either of the things it relates. (5) Nor does the principle of sufficient reason come to the rescue: there is no reason why the spheres should be arranged one way round rather than the other, so the case tells against that principle too. (6) The moral is that two things can differ numerically without differing in any other way.
