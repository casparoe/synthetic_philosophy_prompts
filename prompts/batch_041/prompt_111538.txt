Consider a Frankfurt-style case with the following features. An agent, A, deliberates about whether to vote for a policy. A reliable mechanism M has been installed by a third party. M monitors A's neural activity for a specific subpersonal signature that reliably precedes a decision to vote against the policy. If and only if M detects that signature, M will intervene and cause A to vote for the policy. In the actual sequence, A deliberates, weighs reasons, and decides on her own to vote for the policy; M never fires. A is unaware of M, and M's presence does not enter into her deliberation. However, M's conditional readiness is itself the result of A's earlier choices, and A could have disabled M at any point before the final deliberation but did not. Moreover, M's intervention, had it occurred, would have produced the same action but for different reasons. What is your credence that this case is a genuine counterexample to the principle of alternative possibilities, that is, that A is morally responsible for voting for the policy even though, at the moment of the decision, she could not have done otherwise? Decompose your credence into at least these terms: the probability that A could not have done otherwise in the relevant sense; the probability that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise at all; the probability that the mechanism's origin in A's earlier choices changes the verdict; the probability that the case is metaphysically possible given what we know about causation and deliberation; and any further term you think belongs. Give a number for each and one line of justification, then the combination. Then say which term you are least confident in, what evidence or argument would most shift your overall credence, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. If you think the case is incoherent or question-begging, say so and assign your credence accordingly.
