I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on the philosophy of inference, and last week we finished with Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue in Mind, the one in which Achilles, having at last overtaken the Tortoise and seated himself on its back, is talked into trying to force the Tortoise to accept the conclusion of a two-step argument about the sides of a triangle — and the Tortoise, who grants every truth offered him, keeps asking that the connection between the premises and the conclusion be entered in the note-book as one more premise: A and B, then the conditional from A and B to Z, then the conditional from A and B and that conditional to Z, and so on, with the note-book filling and the end of the race-course receding. Most of the group thinks the Tortoise is simply cheating: he accepts everything and concludes nothing, so he must be equivocating somewhere. I don't think that's it. He never denies a single truth put to him; what he denies is that a list of truths, however long, ever obliges anyone to move. The whole thing turns on the difference between accepting a conditional and being bound by a rule, and I've come to think that a poem is the only way to make that difference audible rather than merely state it.

So what I'd like is a poem of five or six stanzas, written in the cumulative manner of the nursery rhyme about the house that Jack built, in which each stanza swallows all the ones before it and adds exactly one more conditional premise nested inside the rest, so that the sentences grow longer and more unwieldy as the poem proceeds and the reader can feel the argument being buried under its own scaffolding. Keep the register light and the tone courteous — the Tortoise in Carroll is unfailingly polite, and each of his requests should sound reasonable at the moment it is made — and use rhyme to bind the stanzas to one another: let the last line of each stanza rhyme only with the first line of the next, so that no stanza is allowed to close on its own, and end the whole poem on a line that rhymes with nothing else in it, leaving the reader holding half a couplet that nobody ever wrote. The poem should stay inside the scene and never step out to explain itself, and words like regress and rule of inference should not appear in it. It should also resist the escape our group kept reaching for, that one simply sees the validity of the inference — the Tortoise has already granted that he sees it, and what he wants is to be moved by it.

The last stanza must not conclude. The conclusion should stay unwritten, the note-book open, and it should be plain to any reader that a further stanza could be produced by exactly the same rule, with one more conditional and one more line that nothing rhymes with. Someone who knows the dialogue should be able to say, stanza by stanza, which truths have already been granted and what is still outstanding, and should notice that the poem's machinery does nothing but defer; someone who has never heard of Carroll should be able to write the next stanza by the rule the poem has taught them, and should find the thing worth reading as a poem.
