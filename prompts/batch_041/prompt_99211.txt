During a graduate seminar on game theory, two participants fell into a disagreement about the significance of program equilibrium — the solution concept in which each player in a one-shot game submits a program, the programs are mutually readable, and an equilibrium is a profile of programs such that no player could gain by submitting a different one. The standard illustration is the prisoner's dilemma: a program that cooperates exactly when its opponent's program cooperates is a best response to a copy of itself, so mutual cooperation appears as an equilibrium even though the game is played once, with no repetition and no enforceable contract.

Anna took this to be a substantive result: it shows that rational cooperation in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma is attainable, and that mutual conditional cooperation is a genuine Nash equilibrium of the meta-game in which the programs are chosen. Ben replied that the result only relocates the difficulty. The conditional program is reliable solely because a submitted program cannot deviate, and credible precommitment was never what made the prisoner's dilemma hard; furthermore, the equilibrium presumes that both players are transparent and mutually simulable programs, which is a special and unrealistic case, so nothing has been established about the dilemma as a problem for rational agents. Anna answered that the choice of program is itself a rational choice made before play, that no program could do better against the other, and that assuming the transparency of decision procedures is an ordinary modelling idealization, no more objectionable than assuming players know one another's strategies. Ben countered that defecting programs are equally fixed points of the same construction, so what the example really exhibits is equilibrium multiplicity rather than the availability of cooperation.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point, indicating for each of the four moves whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and saying what, if anything, both of them are missing.
