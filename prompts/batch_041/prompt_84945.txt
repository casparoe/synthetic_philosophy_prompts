Q3. (a) State the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and use it to derive Skolem's paradox for ZFC. (b) Explain why the situation you have described is not a contradiction, and say what it shows about the absoluteness of 'countable'. (c) Some philosophers take the paradox further and argue that it shows our own set-theoretic notions are indeterminate, so that a sentence like CH has no determinate truth value. Does the paradox support that further claim? Give reasons.

Student's answer: "Downward Löwenheim–Skolem (DLS): every satisfiable first-order theory in a countable language has a countable model. ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language — the only non-logical symbol is ∈ and the axiom schemas are schemas of first-order formulas — so if ZFC is satisfiable it has a countable model M.

ZFC proves Cantor's theorem and so proves that there are uncountable sets: M ⊨ ∃x(x is uncountable). Take m ∈ M witnessing this. M is countable, so its domain can be enumerated, and the set {a ∈ M : a ∈^M m} is a countable set, so there is a bijection between it and ω. So we have a model that says m is uncountable, while from outside we can count m. That is the paradox.

(a) It does not show that ZFC is inconsistent. The step where the paradox bites is a shift between two different ∈-relations: the one the model uses and the real one, which is the metatheory's. ZFC's consistency isn't at issue; DLS is a theorem about models and it never gives us a proof of a contradiction inside ZFC.

(b) The resolution is that countability is not absolute. When M says 'm is uncountable', what it means is that there is no f ∈ M with M ⊨ 'f is a bijection between ω^M and m'. The bijection exists in our metatheory but not in M: M doesn't recognise the pairing as a bijection, because from M's point of view it is either not a function or not onto — the set of ordered pairs simply isn't one of M's elements. So 'countable' means something different depending on which model you're standing in. It isn't absolute; it's relative to the background theory, which is why the countable model can satisfy a sentence that seems to say it is uncountable.

(c) I think the paradox does support the further claim, and this is the interesting part. Gödel and Cohen showed that ZFC + CH and ZFC + ¬CH are each consistent if ZFC is, so by DLS each has a countable model, and in each of those models the reals are countable from outside. Since our own set-theoretic language is pinned down only by a first-order theory, we are in the same position as M: for any consistent theory we adopt, there is a model in which the continuum is countable. There is therefore no more fact of the matter about CH than there is about whether M's m is really uncountable, and the appearance of determinacy just comes from confusing the theory with the world."
