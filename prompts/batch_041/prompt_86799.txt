I am a high school student writing a final paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on creativity, and I have been asked to produce something unusual: a document that both of my mentors could sign. One of them argues that creativity is variation on a theme — the view associated with Douglas Hofstadter, on which a new idea is nearly always an old pattern with its knobs turned a little, and even the largest leaps are analogies carried across domains. My other mentor says that cannot be the whole story. She draws a distinction she credits to Margaret Boden: exploratory creativity stays inside a space of possibilities and finds its unexplored regions, whereas transformational creativity alters the rules of the space itself, so that what counts as a good move is different afterward. Her claim is that a knob-twiddling model can only ever account for the first kind, and that the interesting cases are all of the second.

The two of them have been arguing past each other for a semester. Please take the role of an expert in the philosophy and cognitive science of creativity — someone at home both in the variation-and-analogy tradition and in its critics — and write the joint statement they should have written together.

Before you draft anything, reason carefully and brainstorm privately: for each side, set out what it actually needs to claim; then test every candidate point of agreement to make sure it is neither vacuous nor quietly slanted toward one side. Do not begin writing the document until that groundwork is done.

The document should contain four parts:

1. A numbered list of claims that both mentors can sign — about what creativity consists in, about what the two views agree the phenomena are, about what neither view denies, and about how the word novelty should be understood.
2. A table of the genuine disagreements. State each one in neutral wording, and then give each side's own formulation of its position, phrased so that a defender of that side would accept it.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, identify a concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case.
4. A brief section saying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive, with your reasons.

To keep the cruxes concrete, please have both sides commit to predictions about four cases of my choosing: (a) chess played on a nine-by-nine board rather than an eight-by-eight one; (b) a chess variant whose object is to lose all your pieces; (c) a chess variant in which each move is assigned by auction rather than by turn; and (d) the historical shift from Euclidean to non-Euclidean geometry. For each case, say whether the variationist is obliged to call it a knob-twiddle, whether the transformationist is obliged to call it a change of space, and whether either verdict is actually forced by that side's view or merely permitted by it.

Keep the document self-contained. Do not cite books, articles, or dates, and do not include a reference list; I have no library access, and I want the reasoning to stand on its own. Aim for something both mentors could put their names to without either of them feeling misrepresented.
