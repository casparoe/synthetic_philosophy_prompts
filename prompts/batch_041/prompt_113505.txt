I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and this term I'm running a discussion section for a large introductory ethics course. Next week the lecture covers theories of punishment — retribution, deterrence, and the expressive view, on which what distinguishes a punishment from a mere price or tax is the public condemnation it carries rather than the suffering it imposes — and the reading also lays out the standard checklist for something to count as punishment: it has to impose something unpleasant, it has to be for an offense against a rule, it has to fall on an offender, it has to be intentionally imposed by other people, and it has to be imposed by an authority with standing to do so. My students, however, care far more about whether being fired, removed from a platform, or quietly dropped by a professional community counts as punishment than they do about the textbook cases, and we spent most of last week going in circles. The episode that set it off was a visiting speaker whose talk was canceled after an open letter; the letter's authors insisted they were not punishing anyone, merely exercising their own freedom of association, while the speaker insisted he was being punished without a hearing, and both sides seemed to me to be reaching for something in the theory that neither had quite named. The students who say it is punishment point to how much it hurts and how unmistakably it marks someone as blameworthy; the students who say it is not point out that there is no rule, no authority, and no end date. I would like to open next week's section with a joke that lands on this tension instead of dissolving it, and I want it to be a genuine joke — one that would amuse people who already know the material because the punchline turns on the structure of the theory, not a wisecrack about social media or a generic complaint about the internet.
