I'm in a seminar on aesthetics and we're doing Schopenhauer's philosophy of music this week, and I have to open the session with somthing that isn't just a summary. What I find hardest to explain to people is the double claim: that music isn't a copy of the Ideas the way painting and poetry are, but a copy of the will as thing-in-itself -- and yet he also wants to say music is built out of voices that line up with the grades of the will's objectification, bass for the inorganic, then the middle voices for plant and animal life, and the melody riding on top as the human will's striving, the whole thing never actually resting even when the cadence makes you think it has. I'd like a poem that does that rather than says it.

Form: four voices, like a little consort piece, 24 to 32 lines, and the voices have to behave like voices. The bass line should be an unchanging refrain -- literally the same words each time it appears, a drone that never develops, since he thinks the ground of the world is that mass that just persists. The two middle voices can move but should be narrow and repetitive, plant-slow and animal-quick respectively, and they should rhyme within a closed set of sounds that never gets new members. The soprano is the only voice allowed to develop, to want, to digress from the tonic and come back -- and its rhymes should keep reaching for new sounds and failing to settle, so that by the end it isn't resolving into the bass but thinning out into it, still wanting something while the drone keeps going underneath, because the melody's rest is borrowed and the bass never stops. No quoting or paraphrasing his prose, and don't put his name in the poem; someone in the seminar who knows the theory should be able to point at each voice and say why its line behaves that way, and someone who doesn't should just hear four parts that don't quite agree.
