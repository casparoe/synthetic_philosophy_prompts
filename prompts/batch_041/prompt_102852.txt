I have been working through James Legge's nineteenth-century translation of the Mencius and have run up against a short exchange in the sixth book, the part conventionally referred to as Gaozi, which I cannot interpret with any confidence. Legge renders it as follows:

The philosopher Gao said, 'Life is what we call nature!'

Mencius asked him, 'Do you say that by nature you mean life, just as you say that white is white?' 'Yes, I do,' was the reply.

Mencius added, 'Is the whiteness of a white feather like that of white snow, and the whiteness of white snow like that of white jade?' Gao again said 'Yes.'

'Very well,' pursued Mencius. 'Is the nature of a dog like the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox like the nature of a man?'

I should like help in understanding what Mencius is doing here. Please treat this as a request for a close reading of this exchange rather than for a general summary of the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement. Four things puzzle me.

The first is the formal shape of the argument. Is the closing question meant as a reductio of Gaozi's thesis, or merely as a demand for clarification, or as something else? If it is a reductio, what is the suppressed premise that does the work, and is that premise one Mencius is entitled to assume rather than one that is precisely in dispute?

The second concerns the analogy with whiteness. Gaozi's 'life is what we call nature' is compared by Mencius to 'white is white', and the whiteness of feather, snow and jade is conceded to be uniform. It seems to me this cuts both ways. If a single property can be present identically in things of quite different kinds, why should the property of being alive not be present identically in dogs, oxen and men? I cannot tell whether Mencius is turning Gaozi's own analogy against him or quietly borrowing a premise from it.

The third is a reconstruction offered by a colleague of mine, which I am not sure I trust. He distinguishes what he calls the constitution question (what a creature comes with, prior to any training) from the specification question (what kind of thing a creature is, and hence what its characteristic activity is). On his reading, Gaozi's thesis is an answer to the constitution question, whereas Mencius's closing question is an answer to the specification question, and the two therefore pass one another by without contact. He adds that a competent defender of Gaozi could simply answer 'yes' to Mencius's question: dogs, oxen and men might all share one set of vital dispositions, with everything distinctively human arising from training, ritual and convention rather than from an inborn inclination. He says this makes Gaozi a forerunner of the position defended in the Xunzi, where human nature is said to be bad and whatever goodness we observe is credited to deliberate effort and cultivation rather than to nature. If he is right, then winning the dog-and-ox argument would not give Mencius anything like his thesis that human nature is good.

The fourth difficulty follows from the third. Even granting Mencius his conclusion that the nature of a man is not like the nature of an ox, what further step would he need in order to reach the claim that human nature is good? And is there anything in the passage itself that supplies that step, or does it have to be imported from elsewhere in the text?

Please assess my colleague's reconstruction rather than merely restating it: say which parts of it are defensible, which are anachronistic, and where it distorts the dialectic. I would also like a verdict on which reading of Mencius's closing question the passage better supports, and a sketch of how a strong opponent of Mencius, rather than a straw man, would reply at each move.
