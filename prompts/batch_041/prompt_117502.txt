I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'd like an essay that takes a position on a problem in decision theory that I keep circling from an angle I haven't seen addressed.

The background is the familiar argument that if you maximize expected value, you must accept gambles with tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs, and that this commitment has no natural stopping point: however small the probability, some sufficiently large payoff makes the bet worth taking. I already understand the standard responses, things like capping the stakes, refusing to let probabilities below some threshold count, or treating extreme outcomes as lexically separate, and I don't want the essay to be a survey of those. I want it to go past them.

What I want the essay to focus on is a construction I came up with, which I'll call a self-scaling wager, because I haven't seen it discussed anywhere and I want to know whether it genuinely breaks expected value maximization or is only a curiosity. Here is the setup. A bookmaker offers you a single ticket for a small fee. If you buy the ticket, then with probability one half it pays you an amount equal to some fixed multiple m of the expected value of your entire decision policy, meaning the total expected value of everything you will ever do, given that you are the kind of agent who bought this ticket. With probability one half it pays nothing. The payoff is therefore not a fixed quantity; it is defined by referring to the value of the very policy that decides whether to accept it. Evaluating the ticket in the usual way, buying it changes your policy's value from some baseline B to a new value that satisfies an equation whose right side contains a multiple of that same new value. I've noticed that when the multiple is large enough, the equation has no sensible solution, and when it sits just below that range, buying the ticket appears to multiply the value of your whole life by an arbitrary factor, after which the same reasoning seems to license buying such tickets over and over.

I want the essay to answer a single question: does the self-scaling wager show that expected value maximization is incoherent, or does it only show that we need a well-formedness restriction on what may count as an outcome, namely that an outcome's value may not depend on the policy evaluating it, leaving ordinary fanaticism about small probabilities and large fixed payoffs untouched? Please argue for a definite verdict rather than laying out both sides and stopping, and engage the strongest version of whichever position you end up rejecting. If you think the wager is decisive, say exactly which premise of expected value maximization it refutes, and whether that same premise is also needed for the ordinary, non-self-referential cases. If you think it is not decisive, state the restriction precisely and explain why it is not ad hoc, since it will look like a rule invented to exclude one inconvenient example. Either way, tell me what the verdict implies for a practical question I actually care about: whether it is defensible for me to commit a nontrivial fraction of my savings to projects whose success I estimate at something like one in a billion but whose payoff I estimate as very large.

I would also like you to be explicit about the limits of your answer. State how confident you are in the verdict, and single out the one step in your reasoning you are least sure about, and why.

I'm not asking for references, citations, or a reading list; please reason it out from the setup and define any terms you introduce.
