I'm putting together a reading group on critiques of effective altruisim, and I want to stress-test the core project. Below are eight objections, some standard, some I made up. Rank them in tiers (S, A, B, C, D, F) by how much of a threat they pose to EA's core claims (that we should do the most good we can, impartially, using evidence and reason), with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. Also tell me which are standard and which I invented, and for the invented ones say whether they actually have teeth or are just clever-sounding. I want to know how a smart EA would respond to each, and whether thier response would be convincing.

1. The institutional critique: EA focuses on individual donations and career choices, but most gains in welfare come from institutional and political change. So EA is at best a rounding error and at worst a distraction from the real levers.
2. Earning to give: taking a high-paying job to donate more. Critics say it's self-serving, corrupting, and empirically weak, since the marginal dollar donated may do less good than direct work.
3. Measurability bias: EA's emphasis on quantifiable outcomes systematically neglects hard-to-measure goods like political freedom, cultural flourishing, and long-term institutional resilience.
4. The counterfactual charity problem: EA's insistence on counterfactual impact makes it impossible to justify funding anything that would have happened anyway, which rules out supporting public goods and infrastructure that many people benefit from.
5. The aggregation objection: EA's aggregative consequentialism ignores the separateness of persons and the legitimacy of partiality; it treats individuals as interchangeable units of welfare.
6. The replacement problem (my term): if EA successfully reduces existential risk, it may just accelerate the arrival of a stable totalitarian singleton that locks in bad values forever, so risk reduction isn't obviously good.
7. The epistemic humility trap (my term): EA's willingness to act on expected value calculations under deep uncertainty licenses high-variance interventions that would be reckless if the probabilities are wrong; the framework lacks a principled way to distinguish bold bets from gambling.
8. The cause neutrality critique: EA's claim that causes should be chosen without regard to personal attachment or political identity is either incoherent or covertly imposes a particular liberal-cosmopolitan worldview.
