I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty years in precision measurement, the last decade of it on tests of Bell-type inequalities — and since retiring I have been working through a graduate degree in philosophy. This term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on divine foreknowledge and human freedom, and the instructor has asked me to contribute one essay question to the final examination, on the grounds that the students should meet one of these problems from a physicist's angle for once. I would be grateful if you would draft that question for me, along with a model answer and a marking scheme. I am new enough to this literature to distrust my own attempt at a model answer, and I would rather submit something I can defend in front of colleagues who have read a great deal more theology than I have.

The seminar has spent several weeks on Molinism: the view, named for the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, that God's knowledge includes not only necessary truths and the truths he freely decrees, but also a middle knowledge of conditional truths concerning what free creatures would do in circumstances that never obtain. What interests me is the objection that presses on the truthmakers of those conditionals. Such a conditional is supposed to be contingent, and supposed to be true independently of God's will, since otherwise the creature's action would be God's doing rather than the creature's; but if it is grounded instead in the creature's nature and circumstances, then it looks like something God could read off from what he has made, and the freedom it was introduced to protect drains away. I want this difficulty to be the center of the question, but I do not want the students merely to rehearse it.

There is a notion from my own field that sits close to it, and I want the question to make the students work with it. In the foundations of quantum mechanics one speaks of counterfactual definiteness: the assumption that a measurement which was not performed nonetheless has a definite outcome, and that these unperformed outcomes may be treated on the same footing as performed ones in statistical reasoning. The Bell-type results are commonly presented as showing that locality, the absence of a conspiracy between the measured property and the apparatus setting, and counterfactual definiteness cannot all be had together, and that at least one must be surrendered. My own instinct is that the analogy is a trap for the Molinist rather than a gift, but the question must not disclose that, and an excellent answer should be able to land on either side of it if the reasoning is careful.

For the question itself, please observe the following. It must be self-contained: a student who has never opened a physics book must be able to answer it, so include in the question itself a short plain-language gloss of counterfactual definiteness and of the Bell-type predicament, and a sentence or two recalling the Molinist apparatus. It must be answerable without naming a single author, title, or text, and no credit should attach to names; the examination is closed-book. It must not be passable by reciting the taxonomy of the three kinds of divine knowledge, nor by summarizing the objection in its usual form; it has to require the students to do something with the analogy — at minimum to say what would have to be true of a grounding for these conditionals, to test whether the physicist's unperformed measurements supply such a grounding, and to identify at least one respect in which the two cases come apart. It should not be a leading question. Assume ninety minutes and an essay of roughly a thousand words.

I would like from you, then: first, the question as it would be printed on the examination paper, with its instructions; second, a model answer of about nine hundred to eleven hundred words that takes a definite line and makes at least one disanalogy explicit; third, a marking scheme out of twenty that names the four or five specific moves a first-class answer must make, gives the marks for each, and adds for each move a single line on how candidates typically get it wrong, followed by a short list of errors that should cost marks outright; and finally, in under a hundred words, a note telling me which single sentence of the question is doing the discriminating work, and what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one.
