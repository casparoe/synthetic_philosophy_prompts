I sit on the editorial board of a behavioral-science journal, and over several years we have settled into a de facto policy on adaptation hypotheses without ever writing it down. I am now handling a submission we must decide, and I want to know what our own record predicts we will do, and whether our record in fact supports one policy or is equally consistent with two that come apart on this paper. Everything needed is stated below; the earlier papers and the new submission are described in full, so nothing here turns on facts outside the prompt.

Our decisions so far:

1. Accepted. A paper arguing that gratitude is a device for calibrating long-term reciprocal partnerships. It named the ancestral problem (repeated exchange among non-kin, where defection is tempting and hard to detect), specified a design signature (the emotion tracks the cost borne by the benefactor rather than the benefit received, and scales with the benefactor's apparent need), reported the pattern in a small-scale society with little market integration, and showed a dissociation from general positive affect.

2. Accepted. A paper arguing that nausea and contamination sensitivity constitute a pathogen-avoidance system. It named a selection pressure and specified a design signature (the response tracks cues of infectiousness — strangers' bodily products elicit more than kin's; the threshold moves with perceived infectivity), and showed that the calibration follows infectivity rather than cultural category.

3. Rejected. A paper arguing that a taste for spiced food is an adaptation for pathogen management in hot climates. The evidence was a cross-cultural correlation between spice use and mean temperature. Nothing separated this from recipes diffusing along trade routes, and no ancestral selection pressure was identified beyond the observation that pathogens were a problem.

4. Rejected. A paper arguing that the human tendency to overestimate the prevalence of ambient threats is a vigilance adaptation. The functional story was plausible on its face, but the authors could point to no feature of the response that a domain-general mechanism (base-rate neglect under uncertainty) would not equally produce, and they conceded this while maintaining the adaptation claim.

The written rationales are not uniform: in 1 we cited the small-scale-society replication, in 2 the calibration to infectivity, and in 3 and 4 the rejections were worded almost identically, to the effect that the adaptation claim had not yet been earned.

The submission in front of me argues that the impulse to punish third-party defectors is an adaptation for deterrence in small groups with public reputations. It names a specific ancestral selection pressure — the reputational returns to being known as someone who punishes, where reputations were common knowledge — and it does identify a design signature: the impulse is calibrated to the presence of an audience and to the reputational cost of inaction rather than to the magnitude of the harm suffered by the victim, which is what selection for deterrence, and not a general sensitivity to harm, would produce. What it does not do is test this against the alternative most salient in that literature — that punitive motivation is a byproduct of a domain-general sensitivity to norm violations, amplified by cultural reinforcement of punitive norms — and it reports no cross-cultural or developmental evidence.

Tell me what our likely verdict is, and which rule our record is actually running. Lay out at least two rules that fit decisions 1 through 4 equally well, say what each predicts here, give your best guess and the reasons for it, and name the further submission — some case other than this one — that would most cleanly separate the rules. If our record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so rather than manufacturing a decision. Close by stating how confident you are in the prediction and in your reading of the operative rule, and identify the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
