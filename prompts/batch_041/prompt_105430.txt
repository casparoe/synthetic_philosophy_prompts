I'm building a discussion unit on persistence for an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and before we start arguing about who's right (endurantists or perdurantists) I want to hand the students a spec sheet: what does any theory of how a thing persists through time actually have to deliver? So write me the desiderata list.

For every item, give me the case or consideration that puts it there, not just the label. Some things I definitely want on it: the "why is change even a problem" business (a thing is red, later it's green, it's the same thing, redness and greenness are supposed to be properties of the thing itself, and the thing is supposedly all there at each moment -- how do those sentences live together?), the cases where two things seem to occupy all the same material at once (the lump and the statue, the cat and the tissue), fission, and whether any of this survives what special relativity does to simultaneity. But don't just recite the standard list -- earn each entry.

Here's the part I care most about. I'm going to seed the seminar with a few cases the textbook ones don't cover, and I want the list to have already thought about them:

(a) an organism that goes into a frozen, zero-metabolism state for fifty years, then gets revived and carries on as before -- same individual or not, and what does each side have to say about it?
(b) a company that is legally dissolved one day and reincorporated the next under the same name, with the same assets, staff and customers;
(c) a river that freezes solid every winter and thaws every spring.

For each, tell me which desideratum it's really testing and what a theory has to say about it to pass.

Then the bit I most want: which of your requirements are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp? I suspect some subset can't all be had at once -- the change thing smells like a trilemma. If so, say exactly which ones you can't jointly satisfy and why, because thats the actual result. And tell me which single requirement does the most work: the one that, if you insist on it, knocks out the most otherwise-popular theories.

Skip the scorecard of who wins, I'll do that in class. ps -- the students will mutiny if it turns out to be the usual two positions relabled, so let the cases do the work.
