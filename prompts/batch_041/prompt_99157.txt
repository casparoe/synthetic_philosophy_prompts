I'm leading a session of an interdisciplinary reading group — mostly philosophy graduate students, plus two economists and a historian of science — on the idea that a person's choices reveal their preferences, and on what that idea does to the concept of utility. I have seventy-five minutes and a room that contains at least three people who will want to relitigate whether preferences are "real." Please don't explain the material to me; I know it. Plan how I should teach it. What has to be introduced before what — which piece of the machinery is load-bearing, so that the claim about choice is only meaningful once some earlier distinction is already in place — and where the natural fault lines in the sequence lie. I want one carrying example to do most of the work, and I'd like your recommendation on what it must have and what it must conspicuously lack; in particular, tell me whether the familiar illustration (a shopper picking between two bundles under a budget constraint) is the right vehicle or whether it quietly presupposes the very thing we are trying to establish, and if so, what to replace it with. I have been tempted to build the session around a case in which a single agent's choices look locally coherent but jointly strange — someone who both insures against a loss and gambles on a windfall — and to use that to pry apart the descriptive and the normative readings of "revealing." Say whether that case can bear the weight or whether it drags in complications (risk, and the distinction between preferences over outcomes and preferences over lotteries) that belong later in the hour.

Then map the misconceptions for me, in the order the group is likely to form them, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. My guesses are these: they will treat the thesis as a stipulative definition rather than a substantive claim about what choice data can support; they will slide from "choice reveals preference" to "preference is nothing but a disposition to choose," and from there to the conclusion that talk of welfare has been eliminated rather than reconstructed; they will hear the consistency requirements as empirical predictions about human psychology rather than as constraints that define the domain in which the apparatus applies at all; and they will assume the whole framework is a behaviorist relic that later work simply abandoned, without seeing what survives the abandonment. For each, tell me where the sequence should head it off and roughly how. Finally, give me one closing question I can put to the room that would distinguish someone who has grasped the logical role of the consistency condition from someone who has merely memorized the slogan, and tell me what I should explicitly decline to cover in seventy-five minutes and announce that I am declining — I would rather leave a clean gap than a blurred one.
