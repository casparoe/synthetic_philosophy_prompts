I write about technology and politics, and I have been asked to contribute a short position statement to a symposium — a piece in which I state my view flatly and let the other contributors go at it. Before I commit it to print I want an audit, because I suspect the parts do not all fit together, and I would rather find out now than in the letters column.

Here is where I have landed.

1. Strong technological determinism is false. Technology does not develop by an internal logic and then drag society along behind it; the causal traffic runs in both directions.

2. A technology's form is contingent and socially settled. For almost any function, several materially different designs would have worked, and which one we ended up with was decided by standards fights, procurement, business decisions, and plain accidents rather than by an imperative of efficiency.

3. The way to establish a technology's politics is to reconstruct the designs that lost — the counterfactual hardware that was on the table and did not win — rather than to reason from first principles about what the technology requires.

4. A built-out system can nonetheless exercise what I call an infrastructural veto. It does not forbid a political option; it raises the cost of reversing the arrangement the system serves until reversal would mean rebuilding the system.

5. My test for whether a technology has politics in the strong sense — call it the asymmetry test — is this: if the politics could be reversed by a different law, a different budget, or a different convention while the technology stayed in place, the technology merely tilts; if reversing the politics would require tearing out and replacing the technology, then it has politics.

6. A technology's politics is not a function of anyone's intentions. What the designers meant, what the engineers wanted, what the users are up to — all of that is evidence, not the thing itself.

7. I am convinced that the shift from a chronological feed to a ranked, personalized one has politics in the strong sense: it reconstituted public attention in a way that I do not believe any law or funding decision could undo.

What I want from you is an audit, not a summary. Tell me which of these actually pull against each other and rank the conflicts by severity; separate the pairs that are genuinely in tension from the pairs that only look inconsistent and would dissolve on inspection. For each real conflict, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me to make it, and then tell me which single revision would resolve the most at once. I do not yet know which commitment I would give up, so please do not assume that any one of them is fixed.
