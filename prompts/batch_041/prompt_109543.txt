I would like an assessment of the following line of thought about the problem of old evidence, which I suspect contains a confusion of mine but I cannot locate the error in it.

The problem is usually put as follows. Suppose a scientist already accepts E before she has formulated the theory T that, as it turns out, entails E. Her credence in E is then already 1, and conditionalization can adjust her credence in T only by the factor P(E|T)/P(E), which is 1; so the old evidence confers no confirmation on T at all. The precession of Mercury's perihelion, known for decades before general relativity was written down, is the stock illustration.

Now my thought. It seems to me that this is an artefact of strict conditionalization and nothing more. Strict conditionalization is a rule for the case in which the agent comes to be certain of some proposition; it is not a rule for the case in which she comes to appreciate a logical or mathematical relation among propositions she already accepts. Jeffrey conditionalization is designed for precisely the latter situation, since it permits credence to be redistributed across a partition without any cell being driven to 1. So a Bayesian who learns, after the fact, that T entails E has simply learned a further proposition about T and E, and can update on it with the ordinary machinery; the temporality of the discovery, and the fact that her credence in E was already 1, drop out of the picture entirely. On this reading the problem is a puzzle about the mechanics of updating rather than a foundational objection to Bayesian confirmation theory, and it dissolves for any agent who is not logically omniscient.

I have also been told that there is a counterfactual-credence reply, which asks what the agent's credence in E would have been had she not yet known E. This strikes me as incoherent: had she not known E she would have had nothing to condition on, so the question seems to have no content, and the reply must therefore be a failure.

So: is the problem of old evidence a merely mechanical difficulty, disposed of by adopting a sufficiently general updating rule, rather than a threat to the foundations of Bayesianism? Or does the resolution turn on the prior rather than on the rule, in which case which of the two is doing the real work? And am I right to think that the Mercury episode is no more puzzling than any other case of an agent coming to see a consequence of what she already believes? I am aware that people sometimes run this together with the quite different claim that only novel predictions confirm, and that is not what I mean.

Please state how confident you are in the verdict you give, and identify the point in your answer that you are least sure of.
