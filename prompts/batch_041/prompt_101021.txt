I have just started a clerkship on my state's intermediate appellate court, and my first assignment is a bench memo on a case the court will hear next term; the topic has turned out to be far more tangled than I expected, and I would like your help. The governing statute, the Central District Traffic Levy Act, imposes a daily levy on any "commercial goods vehicle" entering the central zone and exempts any "vehicle engaged in emergency response." The Act is too new for there to be any authority beyond our own four decisions.

In those decisions we held: (1) a flatbed truck that a man used only a few times a year to move furniture for friends was still a commercial goods vehicle, because the classification turns on the vehicle's design and capacity rather than on how often goods are actually carried; (2) a passenger van whose rear seats had been removed and which was used daily for deliveries was not a commercial goods vehicle, because it was designed and registered as a passenger vehicle; (3) an ice-cream van was a commercial goods vehicle, since its function is to carry and sell goods even though it never delivers them anywhere; and (4) a for-profit utility's bucket truck counted as engaged in emergency response when the emergency services coordinator dispatched it to a downed power line, the court stressing that it had been sent at the authorities' direction to abate an ongoing hazard.

The pending case is a refrigerated van, designed and registered as a goods vehicle, owned by a regional hospital, and used only to rush blood products and organs to transplant and trauma surgeries; the hospital, not the authorities, decides when to send it. Under the design-based reasoning of (1)–(3) the van is squarely a commercial goods vehicle, and the only question is whether the exemption reaches it.

Please tell me which interpretive rule the court is really running, whether the four decisions are genuinely consistent or only appear to be, what the court will most likely hold here, and which single additional case would best tell the competing readings apart.
