I've been running a small seminar on paradoxes of rational belief for the past year, and two of the regulars have spent most of that time talking past each other about the lottery and the preface. The first holds what he calls the graded picture: the only doxastic state worth the name is credence, and to accept or fully believe a proposition is just to have a credence in it above a threshold that shifts with context; on this view neither case is a genuine paradox, because what they really show is that the closure of acceptance under conjunction had to be abandoned — the agent accepts each of the 1,000 lottery propositions, would be irrational to accept their conjunction, and there is no residual tension once we stop expecting acceptance to be closed. The second holds what she calls the commitment picture: to accept a proposition is to take it as a premise for reasoning, assertion, and action, and a body of premises is closed under conjunction by construction, so what the two cases actually refute is the assumption that high probability by itself licenses acceptance; closure survives, and it is the threshold analysis that dies. For reference, the cases we've been working with are the ordinary ones: a fair 1,000-ticket lottery in which the agent knows exactly one ticket will win, and a book whose author asserts each of a hundred independently well-supported claims while acknowledging in the preface that, as with any book, at least one of them is probably false.

I don't want a verdict on who is right, and I don't want a dramatized dialogue. I want the document the two of them should have produced after a year of arguing: a joint statement both could put their names to. Open with a numbered section of claims both can accept — about what the two cases share and where they genuinely diverge, about which constraints on rational acceptance each takes to be non-negotiable, about what withholding amounts to and when it is available, about whether the preface author is being irrational or merely humble, and about whether the two paradoxes call for a single unified treatment or two different ones. Follow that with a disagreements table: one row per surviving disagreement, with a column giving the precise formulation of each side's position as that side would accept it, a column giving what each side thinks the other's formulation quietly presupposes, and a column flagging any row where the dispute is plausibly verbal, together with the disambiguation both would sign.

Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I'd expect at least these three: whether there is any context-independent answer to how probable a proposition must be before acceptance is licensed; whether the preface author holds contradictory beliefs or merely an inconsistent theory, i.e. whether the unit of assessment is the set of accepted sentences or the individual attitudes; and whether the operative variable in the preface is the probability of the full conjunction or the number and independence of the conjuncts. For each crux, set out a probe or case both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, clearly marked as a prediction. Please consider at least these four and say what each side predicts about each: (a) the Preface Gradient — hold the probability of the whole conjunction fixed at roughly 0.9 while varying the construction between ten claims at 0.99 each and a thousand claims at about 0.9999 each; (b) the Closing Sentence — the author ends the book by asserting, as one further claim, that everything else in the book is true, so that the conjunction is itself asserted; (c) the Guaranteed-Winner Lottery — a fair lottery whose organizer credibly announces in advance that exactly one ticket will win, with the agent's evidence about each ticket otherwise identical to the ordinary case; (d) the Wager Probe — each side's account of what it would take for an agent to be entitled to stake something of value on a proposition, and whether being so entitled is the same thing as accepting it. Close with a short section stating what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether there is any case or result they agree would actually move the dispute rather than merely relocate it.
