I got roped into a behavioural econ reading group this term (I'm a philosophy person, I mostly go for the free sandwiches) and last week two people in it — Ravi and Mel — went at each other for like forty minutes over the prof's little toy example, and I still can't tell who had the better of it, so I'm outsourcing the verdict to you. The example was this: three people pick between two restaurants in sequence; each person gets a private hint about which is better that's right about 60% of the time; each person sees the earlier people's choices but not their hints. Ravi said the punchline is that by the time you get to person three, their choice carries basically zero new information, because once two people have picked the same place, the third just copies them no matter what their own hint says — so the "crowd" is a self-referential echo, and any bubble built on it is dumb. Mel said sure, a cascade can start, but that's the wrong moral: each person is still doing the right thing with the information available to them, so calling it irrational is just confusing the individual question with the group question, and anyway cascades are fragile — one strong enough private signal breaks them, so real crowds aren't the house of cards he's implying. Ravi fired back that the fragility is a theory artifact, because in real settings people also care about their reputation, and once you're punished for being wrong *alone* but not for being wrong *with everyone*, you'll keep following the herd even when your own signal says otherwise — so the cascade gets sticky, not fragile. Mel said that's a different mechanism (reputational rather than informational), and it actually cuts her way: if your incentives punish deviation, then following the crowd is the right move *given those incentives*, and the fix is to change the incentives, not to scold people for being sheep. Ravi said she'd just conceded his whole point — that individually sensible behaviour can make the group systematically worse — so the word "rational" is doing no work and the phenomenon he's complaining about is exactly the one she's defending. Mel said no, the labels do work, because "irrational" tells you the cure is to make people think harder, and "rational-but-badly-incentivised" tells you the cure is to change the information environment or the payoffs, and those are completely different prescriptions.
