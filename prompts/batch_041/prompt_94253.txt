I direct a small grant programme — six funded positions for three years — devoted to the theory of unilateral action. By that I mean the family of puzzles that arise when a great many agents can each, independently, take some action that would be harmful if taken; none of them can be confident that it is not harmful; and the upshot is that the action is very likely to be taken by someone. I am not commissioning empirical work on how often this happens in practice. I want conceptual and normative progress of the kind a philosopher or decision theorist could make at a desk, without fieldwork.

We have sketched five candidate lines of enquiry and I would like your help deciding which of them deserve the money. Treat them as our own framing rather than settled positions — I am interested in whether they are the right questions, not in whether we have stated them elegantly.

1. Threshold dispersion and aggregation. Does the near-certainty that someone acts depend on how confident each agent is, and on how widely those confidences are spread? Can one state any general condition under which many independent, individually defensible decisions make a disaster nearly certain? Is there a clean result to be had here, or only hand-waving?

2. Observable abstention. How much does the puzzle change when each agent can see that others have already declined to act, so that the thought that someone else will do it anyway no longer holds? Is there a principled account of when publicity helps and when it merely relocates the problem — and can secrecy ever be a duty?

3. The mirror-image puzzle of inaction. If every agent is properly deferential about a beneficial unilateral act, does the benefit systematically fail to materialise? Is that a symmetric curse with symmetric remedies, or a structurally different problem?

4. Unilateral action under moral uncertainty. How should an agent reason when she is unsure not only about the consequences but about which moral theory is correct — so that the act might be wrong in a way she has not considered at all?

5. The conformity remedy and its costs. The obvious prescription is that agents should defer to the apparent consensus against acting. Under what conditions does a norm of conformity become the greater hazard — for instance by suppressing the lone actor who perceives what the majority has missed?

Please recommend a prioritisation: an ordered agenda for roughly six researcher-years, defended rather than merely listed. For each line you would fund, tell me why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for a desk-bound theorist rather than someone running experiments or mining data, and what would have to be true for its answer to change how funders, regulators, or individual actors should actually behave. Say explicitly which of the five you would drop, and why its answer would not change much downstream. Tell me what we have left off the list that ought to be on it, and how it would slot into the order. Finally, name the evidence or argument that over the next two or three years should move a question up or down the ranking, and be honest about where your ordering is a judgement call rather than something the state of the field forces.

Please write in British English. End with a single machine-readable line giving your recommended order, in the form — Priority order: 1) …, 2) …, 3) … — naming at most the top three and adding the label DROP for any of the five you would abandon.
