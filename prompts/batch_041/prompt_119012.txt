I'm in a philosophy of science seminar that's spent all term on inference to the best explanation -- abduction, explanatory virtues, why we're supposed to prefer simpler hypotheses, the lot -- and for the final essay I want to do something other than another paper about scientists, so I've been rereading detective novels to see whether the textbook picture actually matches how a detective reasons on the page. The one that's derailed me is Murder on the Orient Express. Poirot decides the murder was committed by twelve people, all of them connected to the Armstrong case, each of whom stabbed Ratchett once and each of whom supplies the others with a false alibi. That has to be the least parsimonious explanation imaginable -- a dozen near-strangers conspiring is about as a-priori unlikely as a hypothesis gets, and a lone killer with a grudge against Ratchett/Cassetti is sitting right there, which is exactly the tidy story Poirot lays out as the alternative and the one that ends up being reported. So on the naive version of IBE the detective should have thrown the conspiracy out on simplicity grounds; instead he takes the explanation that leaves no clue unexplained. And here's the bit I can't stop chewing on: the reason no single-killer hypothesis survives is that the conspirators alibi each other, so the conspiracy becomes "the only explanation left standing" partly because the conspiracy arranged for its rivals to be eliminated -- the elimination of the alternatives is itself a product of the very thing being inferred. I read something somewhere about a distinction between inferring to the best explanation and inferring to the only explanation (where you just knock hypotheses out one by one until one is left), and I can't tell which one Poirot is really doing, or whether either can be trusted when the evidence base has been curated by the culprit.

So read the book through this debate for me. I want to know what it shows about explanatory virtues, parsimony, and the gap between the explanation that fits the evidence best and the one that was most probable beforehand (the conspiracy is lovely but not likely, if that makes sense) -- and where it genuinely pushes back on IBE rather than just illustrating it. I'm also curious whether the two solutions Poirot offers at the end, the tidy one and the true one, say anything about IBE as a way of finding hypotheses versus a way of justifying them once you've got them. Dont reduce the novel to an example and dont tell me Christie "anticipated" the debate -- I want what the book actually dramatizes that the arguments dont. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
