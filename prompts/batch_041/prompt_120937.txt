I'm in a weird reading group where we do a classic text one month and then a "what would they make of this" session the next, and it's my turn to run the Franklin one. We read the bit of the Autobiography where he sets up his moral perfection project — the thirteen virtues, the little book with red-ink columns and black spots for faults, later transferred onto ivory so he could wipe the marks off with a wet sponge, the thirteen-week courses, four courses a year. I want to push the group past the usual take, which is basically that Franklin was the original bullet-journal guy and it's all very quaint and a bit OCD. Because I think there's something genuinely strange in what he was doing and it gets missed. He admits he never got there, that he fell far short, and that he ended up settling for something like a speckled axe — you'll remember the man at the grindstone who decides he'd rather keep a half-bright axe than keep turning the wheel. And he's oddly candid that on humility specifically he acquired the appearance and not the reality, and that if he ever thought he'd beaten pride he'd probably just be proud of having done it. There's also the book he never wrote, which was going to explain the means of acquiring virtue, and his stated doctrine for it was that vicious actions aren't harmful because they're forbidden, they're forbidden because they're harmful, considered on the nature of man alone — so it's a this-worldly, happiness-and-interest-based, faintly engineering-minded approach to virtue, not a devotional one. Anyway.

So here's what I want you to write: what would Franklin have made of moral bioenhancement? I mean the contemporary idea that you could raise someone's moral capacities directly — a pill, a patch, a transcranial gizmo, eventually gene selection — rather than the way he did it, by habituation and nightly self-examination. Would he see it as the fulfillment of the Art of Virtue, the means finally catching up with the ambition, and cheerfully sign on? Or would the speckled-axe business and the humility business give him reasons to distrust a shortcut that alters the disposition rather than trains it? And would it bother him at all that the enhanced person is good without having struggled for it — my hunch is that given his harm-based framing he wouldn't care one bit, but I want the group to actually fight about it rather than nod along. Write me something I can read aloud to open the session: first lay out what Franklin's own texts actually commit him to (and don't lean on stuff he never said — I'd rather you mark the gaps than fill them in), then make the prediction as carefully as you can, then flag the places where the evidence genuinely underdetermines the answer and say what extra premise you'd need to settle it. And please don't smooth over the tension between the man who kept a fault ledger for decades and the man who decided a speckled axe was good enough, because thats the part of him I find most interesting.
