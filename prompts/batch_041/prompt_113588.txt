My friend Nadia and I got into a three-hour arguement about Leibniz last night — the whole best of all possible worlds business — and I still can't tell if she cleaned my clock or just talked faster than me. I'm not even religious; what I like is the shape of the idea. God has infinitely many candidate universes in front of him, each one a whole history from beginning to end with every event in it, God is good and wise, so he picks the best one and that's the one we're in. She thinks it's the most poisonous idea in the history of philosophy, and she built this out on the back of a receipt:

(1) A perfectly good God creates the best world he can, so ours is the best of all possible worlds.
(2) A world is a complete history, not a snapshot — everything that happens, including everything every creature ever freely decides.
(3) So if some other history were better than ours, God would have made that one instead.
(4) Take any bad thing in ours, say a child's long illness. Consider the history that's just like ours except this one thing never happens. That's a possible world, so by (3) it isn't better than ours — and if ours is the best, it's worse. So deleting the illness doesn't improve anything.
(5) Same for anything I might do. The history where I cure the kid, or donate, or agitate — possible history, so by (3) no better than the actual one. So my doing it can't improve the world either.
(6) So nobody's effort makes the world better. Medicine, politics, charity: pointless or hubris.
(7) And if the world God picked already contains every choice I'm ever going to make, then my choices were fixed before I made them, so they aren't free and I'm not responsible for anything I do.
(8) So the doctrine kills off both God's goodness and human agency. Leibniz spent his life on reform projects. Something has to be wrong here.

I tried the obvious dodge — you can't just swap one event out, in his picture everything is connected to everything else, change one thing and the whole history ripples — and she said fine, forget identical-except, just take the best history in which the kid never gets ill; by (3) that one's no better than ours either. That shut me up.

Then I tried: maybe God wills each creature's good taken one at a time, and wills the whole even more, and that's how the bad parts get in. She said that's the very thing under dispute — if a good God could want a world with the illness in it, then best has stopped meaning what we thought it meant, and the doctrine can't be falsified by anything, and a claim that nothing could count against isn't saying anything. I don't fully follow that last bit.

Here's my problem. The conclusion is unacceptable and I can see she's being too quick somewhere, but every step reads fine on its own, and every time I try to break one I end up giving away something I wanted to keep. The two things I most want to keep are that God's choice really is of the best, and that what we do actually matters. Her arguement looks like a machine for making me drop one of them. It also feels like it proves too much — if it's right, it isn't just Leibniz that goes, it's any view on which God picks the best option available, and the whole thing ends up in a state where nothing could count against it, which is a strange way to refute somebody. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If it turns out the thing is valid and I just have to surrender a premise, tell me which premise is cheapest and what I'm paying. And if there's a version of the best-world idea left standing afterwards, say what it had to give up.
