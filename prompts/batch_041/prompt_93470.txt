I chair the research ethics committee at a large academic medical center, and I need a short written opinion from you that I can circulate before next week's meeting. Here is the situation. A team has submitted a protocol in which healthy adult volunteers would undergo a procedure carrying a real, non-trivial risk of lasting harm, in order to generate data whose benefits accrue only to people in a country where the disease is endemic and essentially absent from our own; the volunteers are offered no payment and receive no personal medical benefit, and in their consent interviews they describe themselves as motivated by a wish to help strangers. Our rules were written for a different world. One rule bars undue inducement, which every committee I have sat on has always read as a ceiling on how much money or benefit we may offer a prospective participant, so here the rule has nothing to bite on, since nothing is being offered. Another rule requires that risks be reasonable in relation to anticipated benefits, and our subcommittee has never resolved whether those benefits must accrue to the participant or may accrue to others. Two of my members have drawn opposite conclusions from identical facts. One holds that we must approve, because these are informed adults making an admirable choice, and that refusing them is paternalism dressed up as protection. The other holds that we must reject, because the committee's purpose is to keep people from converting themselves into instruments, and an altruistic motive is precisely the motive most likely to make a person underestimate what she is taking on. A third member wants to split the difference by adopting a new category, a third-party-benefit tier, that would let us approve protocols above our usual risk ceiling when the benefit is wholly external; I am uneasy about inventing policy on the fly to dispose of a single case. There is one further wrinkle I cannot set aside. The research team has floated the idea of publicly honoring any volunteer who is seriously injured, and one member warns that once self-sacrifice is honored it ceases to be purely self-sacrifice and becomes a kind of competition, in which the most devoted set a standard that quietly pressures the less devoted.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action, covering both whether to approve this protocol and whether to create the tier, with a time frame for whatever you recommend and a date by which the committee should revisit the tier if it is adopted. It should engage the strongest argument on the side you come down against as an argument worth answering, not a confusion to be corrected. I want to be able to hand it to the committee as the chair's view and have them vote on it, so keep the bottom line unmistakable near the start and let the reasoning follow. You need not cite any literature. Write it as continuous prose, the way I would write an email to colleagues: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
