I am not a philosopher and have no training in this, but I read a popular book about game theory a while ago that described an auction for a one-dollar bill in which the two highest bidders both have to pay while only the highest one collects the dollar, and the bidding is supposed to run past a dollar, past two dollars, and keep climbing. Since then I see the same shape everywhere: a friend four years into a degree he no longer wants, a relative pouring money into a house he is trying to sell, two companies suing each other over something that started small, and my own late-night habit of raising my bid on online auctions I had already lost interest in. The advice I keep getting is that this is all just the sunk cost fallacy, and that I should ignore whatever I have already spent and ask only what the next step buys me. That seems obviously right and also too quick. It does not explain why the bidding runs away in the first place, and it ignores that in the auction the other person's next move depends on what I do now in a way that a rule about ignoring the past says nothing about.

What I want is a decision tree I could actually follow, not an essay: a few questions I could answer about my own situation, each leading to the next, ending at a named position about when it makes sense to keep going. Please build the tree out of the distinctions themselves rather than out of a list of authors or schools, and phrase every branch as a question whose answer I could give without already knowing where it leads me. I suspect several things are being muddled and I want them pulled apart: whether the money already spent is merely gone, and so irrelevant, or is instead a clue about how the future will go, and so relevant for an entirely different reason; whether what I do now changes the other side's behaviour, either because they are responding to me or because they are watching me; whether I could have avoided being here at all, and if so whether the mistake was made at the start rather than repeated at every step since; whether the person who will make the next decision is me now or me in a year, and whether I can bind that later person; and whether I have promised anything to anyone, so that quitting would be letting someone down rather than merely losing money.

I would like the leaves to include at least the flat forward-looking view, the view that treats resolve and consistency as goods in their own right, the view that the real error always lies at the point of entry and that continuing once you are in can be perfectly sensible, the view that this is a failure of self-control best handled by rules made in advance, and at least one leaf for the position that nothing irrational is happening at all and that the escalation is what a sensible person does. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, say so plainly rather than papering over it, and if some combination of answers seems to correspond to no position anyone holds, tell me whether you think that is because the combination is incoherent or merely because nobody has bothered with it. Keep it to roughly ten to fourteen leaves, and use the cases I mentioned where they help instead of inventing new ones.
