Two friends of mine, Mara and Ravi, spent most of a long drive home from our reading group arguing about welfare economics, and I've been trying to sort out who had the better of it ever since. I've read enough of the online rationality canon to suspect that the load-bearing disagreement is about what a word is being asked to do, but I couldn't referee the thing in real time and I'd like a proper adjudication.

Mara is a policy economist. She opened with this: "Utility just is preference, and preference just is choice. That's the entire content of the revealed-preference idea. You took the apple over the orange, so you preferred the apple; there is no further fact of the matter and no ghostly inner ranking we need to posit. That's a feature. It means welfare claims are checkable, and it means nobody gets to tell you that you don't really want what you chose."

Ravi did a philosophy degree and never quite left. "If preferring just means choosing," he said, "then 'people choose what they prefer' says nothing at all, and the theory can't be wrong about anyone. Worse, you lose the ability to say any choice was a mistake for the chooser. The addict's choice and the lover's choice come out identical. And the whole inference only runs if the underlying ranking is stable, which you can only establish from the very choices you're trying to explain."

Mara: "Stability isn't a hidden assumption, it's the hypothesis under test. Observe someone repeatedly, in varied settings, and the ranking either holds or it doesn't. Menu effects don't refute me either: choosing the fruit when fruit is the only dessert, choosing it again when cake is also on offer, and taking cake when the fruit is gone. Those show the object of preference is sometimes a whole option-set rather than an item, so there's no contradiction to explain away."

Ravi: "Then the slogan is dead as stated. You've just conceded that picking the fruit doesn't by itself mean preferring fruit to cake. What gets revealed depends on the menu and on assumptions you supply. And your repairs are unbounded: every anomaly becomes a preference over something more exotic, a preference for cake being available, a preference for the act of choosing. A framework that can absorb every data point has stopped being a theory."

Mara: "Fine, drop the slogan and keep the discipline. In practice, when I say a policy makes someone better off, I mean they'd choose it under decent conditions: informed, unmanipulated, not under duress, thinking straight. That's all 'utility' needs to mean, and it's the only reading you can operationalize. The alternative is worse. Whoever claims to know your good better than your own choices do is handing themselves a licence to override you."

Ravi: "Those decent conditions are doing all the work and none of them is observable. Notice the trade-off you're stuck with: the more conditions you pile on, the more your theory can criticize actual choices, and the less it has to do with anything anyone actually chose. There's no version of the view on which utility is both fully behavioural and able to tell someone they're mistaken about their own good."

Mara: "Then maybe it doesn't need to. Maybe 'utility' isn't a claim about anyone's good at all, just a bookkeeping device, a ranking that organizes and predicts choices, which is what a policy analyst needs. It doesn't have to be a mental state."

Ravi: "Then you've relocated the dispute rather than resolved it. A predictive device can't by itself rank policies; the moment you use it that way you're leaning on a value premise you haven't stated, and 'utility' has quietly switched from a summary of behaviour to a standard of what's good. That equivocation is the whole fight. And even granting each person a clean ranking, a ranking is ordinal. It tells me how one person orders their own options and nothing about how much one person's gain counts against another's loss, so your bookkeeping device can't do the bookkeeping you want it for."

Mara: "That's a problem about aggregation, not about utility, and declining to make interpersonal comparisons is a perfectly respectable position. I'd rather have a measure I can defend than a number I invented."

Please go through this move by move and adjudicate: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, rather than just declaring a winner. I'd particularly like to know whether any formulation of the choice-based view escapes Ravi's circularity and unfalsifiability charges, and if so what that formulation costs. Finally, say what both of them are missing, and who has the better of the central question.
