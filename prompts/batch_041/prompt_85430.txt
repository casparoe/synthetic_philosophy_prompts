I have no background in physics or philosophy — I read popular science books and then argue with myself about them — but I have been carrying an argument around for months and I cannot get it to lie flat. Please answer as a philosopher of physics who is also willing to be patient with an amateur. Before you answer, brainstorm about what each step is really committing me to and where the joints are; then give me a diagnosis. I do not want a reading list or a summary of who said what — I cannot look anything up, and what I need is the bad step, if there is one, and the price of dropping it.

Here is the argument.

(1) Two events that are too far apart in space to be joined by anything travelling at or below the speed of light are not ordered in time once and for all. There is an observer, moving at the right speed, for whom those two events are simultaneous.

(2) Take a star a hundred light-years away. Whatever is going on there at this moment is part of the world as it is right now, every bit as much as what is going on in this room. If you asked me to list what exists at this moment, I would have to include it.

(3) Take also an event here on Earth a day from now. A hundred light-years is an enormous distance and a day is nothing, so that distant event and tomorrow's event are far too far apart to be causally connected. By (1), then, there is an observer for whom the two are simultaneous. I worked out that this observer need only be drifting relative to me at about eight kilometres a second — slower than a satellite.

(4) If an event is real, then any event simultaneous with it, for any observer whatsoever, is real as well. Whether something is real cannot depend on who is looking, or on how fast they happen to be drifting.

(5) The distant event is real by (2). Tomorrow's event is simultaneous with it for the observer in (3). So by (4) tomorrow's event is real. It already exists.

(6) Nothing in this turned on the particular star or the particular tomorrow. For any event in my future I can find a distant event in my present that is causally disconnected from it and run the same two steps; and by the same reasoning everything in my past is real too. So every event in the whole of space and time exists, and nothing ever comes into being.

(7) I cannot accept (6). But I also cannot find where it goes wrong. I would defend (1) to the death and I am attached to (2), so if the honest answer is that one of those has to go, I want the bill itemised: what do I have to give up, what does the world look like afterwards, and what does that cost me that I might not have noticed?

Two further things, in case they change your answer. First, people keep telling me the argument begs the question, and I do not know what that is supposed to mean here — it cannot be that any argument which relies on one of its own steps is bad, so I want the premise that does the assuming named, and I want to know whether rejecting it is a real position or just a refusal to pay. Second, a friend says this proves the future is already settled and that my choices make no difference. I think he has added something the argument did not prove, but I cannot say what he added, and I would like that sorted out too.

Finally, one aside, and if it is a distraction say so and drop it: I have a vague memory from a popular book that whether two distant events count as simultaneous is not purely a matter of fact but partly a matter of convention — something to do with how one chooses to synchronise clocks. If that is right, does the argument above inherit any wobble from it, or does it make no difference at all?
