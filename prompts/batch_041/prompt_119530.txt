Right, so I'm doing a module on comparative data politics and I've somehow ended up running the seminar next week, and I've decided the most useful thing I can hand out is a decision tree that actually sorts people instead of a reading list nobody does. The topic is how much citizens should hide from the state. I want the tree to run from a few very basic starting commitments down to a named position, in the style of those magazine quizzes that tell you which philosopher you are, except the answers should be things people genuinely disagree about.

The three traditions I have to keep in view are the German one (informational self-determination — you, not the state, decide what gets known about you, and the state needs a positive justification for every bit of it), the American one (the state should be legible to us, but we aren't owed much protection from it knowing about us; openness runs one way), and the Chinese one (legibility as trust infrastructure — being known is what makes credit, cooperation and enforcement work). I don't want those three sitting there as the leaves, that's too easy and you could get it off Wikipedia. Build the tree from the questions first and slot the positions in wherever they land.

Two distinctions I've been working with that I'd like the tree to use, because I think they do more work than the usual privacy-versus-security framing: first, being hidden (nobody can find out) versus being unremarkable (everyone could find out, nobody bothers) — I reckon a lot of people who say they don't care about privacy actually want the second one. Second, what I've been calling the mirror requirement: can the citizen read the file the state keeps on them? A regime can be maximally intrusive and still pass or fail that test, and I think it cuts the space in a way the standard left-right framing doesn't.

So: 12-15 branch nodes, each one a question someone could answer without knowing which label it leads to, phrased as a concrete case where you can manage it, and 7-9 leaves with the position named on each (or unnamed if nobody seems to hold it) plus one line on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers doesn't match anyone's actual view, say so and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two leaves your tree can't seperate and say why.

Two things I'm nervous about and want you to be upfront about. One, whether the branch nodes I've suggested are really the ones doing the work or whether some are decorative and collapse into each other. Two, whether the whole thing smuggles in a Western liberal assumption that the individual is the unit that owns the data, in which case the Chinese leaf might be a strawman rather than a position — I've also read that the Chinese system is less one big score than a patchwork of local pilots and blacklists, so don't build a leaf that presupposes a single central number. Say where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong here: which node you'd bet against, and what you'd have to see — an actual policy, a case, a position you can't currently place — to redraw the tree differently.
