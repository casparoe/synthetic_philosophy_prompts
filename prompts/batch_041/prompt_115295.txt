Two philosophers both hold that truth cannot outrun what could in principle be known, and both have been unsettled by Fitch's paradox, which appears to show that if every truth can be known then every truth is already known. Each has a way out, and each thinks the other's way out is worse than the disease.

The first proposes that the proof turns on the step from knowing a conjunction to knowing each of its conjuncts, and argues that this step fails: knowledge is not closed under conjunction, and the conjunction that drives the paradox — that some truth holds and that no one knows it — can be known without its parts being knowable. The second grants the proof that step and instead restricts the knowability principle, holding that it ranges only over truths that make no mention of knowledge, so that the paradox's witness never falls under the principle at all.

Write the dialogue between them, with each pressing the cost of the other's concession. Is the second character's restriction ad hoc, and does it leave the anti-realist unable to state their own position, given that the claim that all truths are knowable is itself a claim about knowledge? Can the first character deny closure without conceding that a knower may fail to know an obvious consequence of what they know? Each should answer the other's actual objection rather than restating their own view, and neither may escape by treating the paradox as a reductio of the conviction they share. Close with what genuinely separates the two positions and what would settle it.
