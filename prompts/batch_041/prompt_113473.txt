You are a philosopher of language and a metaethicist who works on the semantics and pragmatics of evaluative vocabulary. Think this through carefully before you write, and feel free to brainstorm to yourself first — I don't need to see the false starts, only the finished specification.

The Frege–Geach problem, as I understand it, is this: a sentence like "lying is wrong" seems to do real inferential work — from it, together with "if lying is wrong then getting your brother to lie is wrong," we validly derive "getting your brother to lie is wrong" — yet an expressivist holds that the sentence's job is to evince a non-cognitive attitude rather than to state a fact. The worry is that attitudes are not the kind of thing that composes into a valid argument, and that one and the same sentence cannot behave one way when asserted and another way when embedded. I am not asking which solution wins. I am asking what any theory of the meaning of evaluative sentences would have to deliver before it is even in the running.

Write the desiderata: the specification that any adequate account of the meaning of evaluative language must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, state whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and flag any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that last part is what I most want to see, and if the list itself turns out to be a result (no theory can meet all of it), say so plainly.

Your list has to be answerable to the following stress-tests. I have constructed these myself, and they are deliberately not the standard textbook examples; a theory that handles the familiar cases but goes silent or ad hoc here is not adequate to my purposes.

1. Negation asymmetry: "Lying is not wrong" versus "It is not the case that lying is wrong." Do these commit the speaker to the same thing, and does either differ from the flat denial "Lying is wrong — no it isn't"?
2. Epistemic embedding: "It might be that hoarding is wrong," and "Lying is probably wrong, but I'm not certain it is." Is the evaluative content inside the epistemic operator or outside it, and what does each reading commit the speaker to?
3. Quantification: "Everything that is wrong is also imprudent," and "Whatever is wrong, someone has forbidden it." Can the evaluative predicate be bound by a quantifier, and what is the semantic value of the variable it binds?
4. Attitude reports with ellipsis: "Ada thinks hoarding is wrong, and Bo does too," alongside "Ada thinks hoarding is wrong; Bo thinks it isn't." What is the shared content across the two clauses in the first sentence, and is Bo's clause its negation?
5. Questions and answers: "Is hoarding wrong?" — and the answer "No." Does the interrogative carry evaluative content, and what does "No" deny?
6. Counterfactual antecedent: "If cruelty had never been wrong, we would never have needed the concept." Does the evaluative clause function as a supposition here, and does it have the same content it has when asserted?
7. Mixed explanation: "Cruelty is wrong because it causes suffering." The "because" links an evaluative claim to a descriptive one; what has to be true of the meanings for that link to be intelligible?
8. Reasoning from a supposition, including reductio: "Suppose lying is wrong; then getting your brother to lie is wrong; so if lying is wrong, getting your brother to lie is wrong" — and separately, a reductio in which the supposition is later withdrawn. What does the theory say the supposition contributes while it is live?
9. Truth predication: "'Lying is wrong' is true." Is this a claim about the sentence, about a proposition, or about the speaker, and can the theory accommodate it without collapsing into descriptivism?
10. Fiction and free indirect discourse: "In the novel, the dowager considered marrying beneath her wrong." Is any evaluation expressed, and by whom?
