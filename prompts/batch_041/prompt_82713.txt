I am presenting a paper next month to a joint session of my faculty's legal theory group and the logic group in the philosophy department, and what I want from you is a question-period preparation sheet, not a summary or a defense of my argument.

The audience. Roughly a dozen analytic legal philosophers of a broadly positivist cast, who will press me on the difference between a valid amendment and a successful revolution and who will not accept that validity is merely whatever officials happen to do. Three or four logicians who work on self-reference and fixed points, who will want to know which fixed point I am selecting and on what grounds. And one game designer who runs rule-writing games in which the players amend the rules of the game as part of play, who will tell me the problem never bites in practice because his players settle it by convention and scoring, and that my theory is a redescription of his house rules.

My thesis. The alleged paradox of self-amendment — the claim that a rule licensing its own revision must already possess the authority it purports to confer — is not a paradox about validity at all but a puzzle about sequence, and it dissolves under what I call the staged-effect reading. On that reading, an amendment that alters the procedure for amendment is enacted and validated under the old procedure, and the new procedure governs only amendments made after it takes effect. I claim this follows from the fact that a procedure governs acts rather than its own authorization, and I claim that the hard cases — a clause that entrenches itself against amendment, an amendment that abolishes the amending procedure outright, an amendment enacted under the old procedure that replaces the entire system — are boundary cases that the staged-effect reading handles better than its rivals.

What I want. The objections I will actually get in that room, ordered by how much damage they do me if I fumble them, each tagged with the part of the audience it comes from and phrased the way that part would phrase it; a two-minute spoken reply to each; and, for each reply, a candid note on where it is thin. Then: what I should concede in my opening rather than defend; the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and why that one is the dangerous one; and any question that will look like an objection but is really an opening I should take. The case I am least sure I can handle is the self-consuming amendment — one enacted under the old procedure that abolishes that procedure — so give it the most attention.

Two constraints. Do not send me to the literature: I cannot look anything up, and I do not want citations, a reading list, or an inventory of who has said what. Argue with me on the merits. And do not pad the sheet with general advice about conference questions; every item should be specific to this thesis and this room.

Finish by saying how confident you are in the sheet you have produced, and in particular which of your predictions about this audience's objections you are least sure of.
