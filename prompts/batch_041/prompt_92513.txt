Here's the arguement I actually want made. Take the donkey. Most people treat it as the flagship case of choice without preference and then either (a) laugh it off because you can just pick one, or (b) insist it starves, which is supposed to show something deep about the will. I think both readings are too quick, and I want an essay that sorts out which one — if either — gets the case right, and ends with a verdict.

The wrinkle I want you to take seriously: I don't think the hard version of the case is two bales that are exactly equal in value. If they're exactly equal, then by the agent's own lights there's nothing at stake, any pick is as good as any other, and the whole paradox evaporates the moment you notice the agent isn't actually losing anything by picking. The hard version is when the two options aren't equal but aren't ranked either — the agent can't say one is better, or worse, or equal. That's a different animal (pun intended), and I suspect the real puzzle is usually smuggled in under cover of the easy case.

So: write me an essay on whether Buridan's ass is a genuine problem for the theory of rational choice, or a pseudo-problem generated by running together two things — indifference and incomparability — that behave very differently.
