You're going to be two things at once for me: a philosopher who has spent years on the question of when several things add up to one thing, and a short-story writer with a good ear for how people actually talk. Before you write anything, spend a page or so thinking out loud — the scenes you might use, the ways the story could go wrong, the answers you'd expect a reader to bring with them. Then write the story. I'd like the story itself to run 1,200–2,000 words, followed by a short note to me, in your own voice, that is not part of the fiction.

Some background on why I'm asking. I have no training in philosophy. I came to this from engineering and I've been chewing on the problem on my own for a couple of years. It's the one about parts and wholes. A chair seems to be one thing. The chair and the table seem to be two things, though I can also, if I try, count them as one thing — a furniture arrangement. A heap of sand, a cloud, a crowd, a corporation, a person: I can never get the answer to stop sliding. In arguments about it, people seem to land in one of four places: everything you can point at is one thing; almost nothing is, apart from the truly simple bits; only something organized, like a living body, counts; or the whole question is about our words rather than about the world. I don't want a lecture on which of these is correct, and please don't give me names or a reading list — I won't look them up. I want the disagreement made flesh.

Here is the premise I'd like you to work from, and you should feel free to change any detail of it:

There's a machine — call it the press — that takes whatever you point at and presses it into a single solid object. Two limits I've built in. First, you have to type in a description of what you're feeding it, and it only works if that description picks out a definite collection; type "the pile" or "the interesting bits" and nothing happens. Second, while something is pressed it cannot be pulled apart by any force and moves as one rigid body, and a second press reverses the process exactly, returning the original parts unharmed. The scene I keep imagining is a small workshop, or maybe a legal dispute, or a couple who want to be pressed together and then can't agree about what they became. Choose whichever you can make live.

A few things I'd like from the story. Give me at least two characters and one real scene of argument — people making the case to each other in dialogue, not a narrator summarizing positions. Put pressure on the machine itself: there should be a moment where its behavior looks like it settles the question, and a moment where it looks like it makes the question worse. Let its limitations show up through use rather than explanation. And don't resolve it at the end; I'd rather close the story with the pressure still on the reader than with a verdict.

In the note afterwards, tell me honestly whether my machine actually bears on the problem or merely relabels it — in particular, whether making the description do the work already assumes an answer, and what you think the sharpest objection to the whole setup is.
