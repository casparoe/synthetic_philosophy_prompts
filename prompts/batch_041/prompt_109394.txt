I've been going back and forth with a friend in my political theory program about sortition, and I'd like to pin down where my credence should actually sit instead of trading intuitions over coffee. So take the following proposition and give me your credence that it is true: if a citizen body of roughly 500 people were selected by stratified random sampling from the adult population, given genuine decision-making authority rather than an advisory role, and given several months of structured deliberation with access to expert testimony and competing briefs, it would match or outperform a comparably sized elected legislature on two dimensions — resisting capture by well-organized interest groups, and protecting the rights of small, unpopular minorities against majority pressure — with the surrounding constitutional structure held fixed in both cases. I care about the counterfactual comparison, not about whether such assemblies are politically feasible or how they'd be constituted in practice. If you think the proposition is under-specified in a way that makes a number meaningless, say so and tell me the smallest repair that would give it a truth value, then give me the number for the repaired version.

Please decompose rather than handing me a bare figure. The sub-questions I'd want priced separately, each with a rough probability and one line of justification: whether ordinary people, once actually deliberating, have stable and coherent enough preferences for the comparison to be well-formed at all; whether removing the re-election incentive reduces interest-group capture or merely changes which groups do the capturing; whether the descriptive-representation effect of sortition alters the content of decisions or mostly their perceived legitimacy; whether electoral accountability performs some error-correction function that deliberation cannot substitute for; whether the self-selection problem — who agrees to serve, and who drops out over months of unpaid work — quietly reintroduces the very biases sortition is meant to eliminate; and how much the whole comparison turns on scale, since a 500-person body and a 500-seat legislature may not be the right analogues to begin with. Then combine them into a final number, and tell me which single sub-question that number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most.

On top of the decomposition, mark clearly which of those sub-questions are genuinely settled among people who study deliberative democracy and institutional design and which are live disputes where a confident answer would itself be a warning sign. I'd rather have an honest map of the disagreement than a verdict that papers over it, and if my final credence is being driven by a consideration you can't actually justify, say that plainly too.
