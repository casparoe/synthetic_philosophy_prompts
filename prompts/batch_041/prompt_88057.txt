Hey, so I'm a software engineer by trade (backend stuff, nothing fancy) and through a weird series of events I've ended up as the TA for an upper level philosophy of mind class. The prof wants me to draft the final exam essay question on epiphenomenalism, and honestly I get the basic shape of it but I want to make sure the question actually tests understanding instead of just memory. So act like a professional philosopher of mind who's also a really good teacher, and help me out here.

Here's the thing I want the question to get at. Epiphenomenalism says qualia (the felt redness of red, the sting of a pain) are caused by brain stuff but don't cause anything themselves, they're basically a shadow or exhaust. The obvious objection is the self-stultification one: if my qualia don't cause anything, then when I say or think "I'm seeing red right now," that judgment can't be caused by the redness I'm supposedly reporting on. And if the judgment would happen whether or not the qualia were there, then it isn't really knowledge about them, it's just a lucky coincidence. I think this is sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I get that much.

But I don't want students to just recite that. I want the question to force them to deal with a defense I've been turning over in my head, which I haven't seen spelled out anywhere and might be original-ish, so please don't treat it as a standard textbook position: the epiphenomenalist could say a judgment doesn't need to be caused by the qualia in order to be about them and to be correct, it just needs to stand in the right non-causal relation to them, the way a thought can contain or be constituted by the thing it's about rather than being pushed around by it. On that view the qualia still don't cause anything, but they're not idle either, because they're what makes the judgment true. The question is whether that actually rescues the view or just relabels the problem.

So: can you write me one essay question (students get 90 min, closed book) that puts that dilemma front and center, plus a model answer of maybe 650 to 700 words and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want 5 must-hit points, 4 marks each for 20 total, and next to each point a one-liner on the mistake that usually costs students the marks. Also a short note on what seperates an A from a B, like the specific move a B answer will make but an A answer won't, or vice versa.

A few things to keep in mind:
- Don't let the question be answerable by just defining epiphenomenalism and stating the objection. It has to make them do something with the non-causal relation idea.
- The model answer should actually take a stand and defend it, not just list options.
- Assume the students know the standard material (causal closure of the physical, the usual objections) but haven't seen this specific spin on it.
- No citations, no reading list, no "as X argues in Y," just the argument.

Before you write the final version, think it through step by step: first brainstorm what the strongest version of the epiphenomenalist's defense would be and what the strongest reply to it is, then pick the crux the question should turn on, then write the question, model answer, and scheme. Show your reasoning briefly before the deliverables if that helps, but make sure the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme are clearly seperate and clearly labeled.

Also keep the tone of the question itself like a real exam question, not like a blog post.
