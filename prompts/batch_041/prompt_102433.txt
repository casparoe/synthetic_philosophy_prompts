Next month I'm giving a talk to my department's decision-theory reading group, argueing that Kelly betting / "geometric rationality" isn't just expected-utility maximization with log utility tacked on — it's a genuinely different criterion, and the right one for any agent whose wealth gets reinvested across a long sequence of decisions, because the thing a single agent actually lives through is the time-average growth rate, not the ensemble average. The room is mostly economists and statisticians who think in expected utility and will hear "log utility" as an arbitrary risk-aversion choice, plus two guys from a trading desk who care about drawdowns and estimation error and nothing about foundations. My argument has three legs: (1) the ergodicity point, that the ensemble average is dominated by branches no individual agent can occupy; (2) my claim that fractional Kelly is a fudge — it pretends to handle model uncertainty but really just bakes in a prior over returns and relabels it risk aversion; and (3) the one I think is actually novel-ish, that the standard objection "Kelly is wrong for one-shot bets" is a category error, since any one-shot bet is itself a draw from the agent's longer decision stream, so the right reference class is the stream, not the single bet.
