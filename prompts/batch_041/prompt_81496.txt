I teach a seminar on care ethics and want to close it with Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, read not as a parable about cloning but as a stress test for the theory I have spent the term teaching. The familiar apparatus is this: Noddings's account of the caring relation, in which the one-caring is engrossed in the other and undergoes a motivational displacement toward the other's projects, and in which the relation is completed only when the cared-for receives the care; and Tronto's phases, on which care requires attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and the responsiveness of the cared-for, together with her later attempt to carry care out of the intimate sphere into a politics of democratic caring-with. Give me a reading that takes both the novel and the apparatus seriously. Is Kathy a one-caring in Noddings's sense at all, and is her care ever completed by those she cares for, or does Hailsham produce people so thoroughly constituted as objects of institutional use that the receiving phase cannot occur? What should the seminar make of the Gallery, of the deferral rumor, of Ruth's manipulations and Kathy's continued tenderness toward her, of Tommy's tantrums and his animal drawings, and of Kathy's retrospective narration itself, which keeps the dead by rehearsing them?

Here is the wrinkle I want you to work with, since it is the reason I am asking rather than assigning the obvious reading. Tronto's phases are meant to be separable conditions: one can be attentive without being competent, competent without being attentive. I have come to suspect the novel shows something stronger, namely that institutional care is characteristically competent and systematically inattentive at once. The guardians feed, medicate, educate, and warn the students about sex, and they do so with real skill; they are also, as the novel eventually discloses, unable to attend to the students as anything but a supply of organs, which is why the Gallery exists and why Madame recoils. Meanwhile the students' care for one another is attentive in Noddings's fullest sense and almost wholly without competence in Tronto's: it can console, but it cannot alter a single donation. If that is the shape of the book, does it show that care ethics needs Tronto's political turn, or that the turn cannot save it, because the theory has no resources for distinguishing care that sustains persons from care that sustains the arrangement that consumes them? And press the harder case against my own reading: the students' care is not merely decorative, and the novel plainly refuses to treat it as such, so say where my distinction between attentive and administrative care distorts what Ishiguro actually dramatizes. I would also like you to consider whether Kathy's narration is a form of care that breaks Noddings's completion condition outright, since the dead cannot receive anything, and if so whether that is a genuine addition to the theory or just a sentimental extension of it. End by saying what the novel contributes to this debate that the arguments alone do not. No quotations and no reading list; I want the reading, the scenes worth teaching, and an honest verdict on where the theory and the novel part company.
