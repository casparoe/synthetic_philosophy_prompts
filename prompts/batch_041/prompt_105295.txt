I'm a statistician by training — I work on Bayesian hierarchical models and causal inference, and most of my professional life is spent on estimands, identification, selection effects, and the difference between a quantity I can actually estimate and one I've merely written a prior over. I've lately been reading about the quest for the historical Jesus, and in particular the criteria of authenticity that scholars use to sort material in the gospels — dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, and the various linguistic and contextual tests that cluster around them — and I keep having the nagging sense that I already know this problem under another name. What I want from you is a faithful translation: restate the method and the dispute surrounding it in the vocabulary I actually use — likelihoods and likelihood ratios, priors and base rates, independent and non-independent measurements, feature selection, multiple comparisons, selection bias, missing data, latent variables, identifiability, overfitting, the absence of a control group, the difference between a point estimate and a posterior over sayings.

Be honest about the translation rather than flattering it. Tell me which of the criteria map cleanly onto things I would recognize (a discriminative test is not the same thing as a likelihood ratio, and I want you to say where the analogy earns its keep and where it quietly changes the subject); which translate only under distortion, and what has to be distorted to make them fit; and which parts of the debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I'm especially interested in the residue — for instance, whether what the scholars are trying to recover is even a well-defined estimand, whether authenticity of a saying and recovery of a person are the same target or two different ones, and what the assumption of a single underlying individual is doing that no model in my toolkit would license.

Then give me your verdict on the residue itself: does it point to something my statistical vocabulary is genuinely missing, or to the possibility that the historians' disagreement is less substantive than it looks once the statistical scaffolding is stripped away? I'd rather be told the second thing, if it's true, than be handed a translation that makes the whole enterprise sound like a regression I could have run myself.
