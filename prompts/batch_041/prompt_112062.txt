I'm preparing a short position statement for a reading group on vagueness, and before I circulate it I want a consistency audit. Here is where I've landed.

1. Vagueness is a semantic phenomenon. Predicates like 'heap,' 'bald,' and 'tall' are vague, but the world itself is precise: there are no vague objects and no indeterminate states of affairs.
2. Vague predicates have genuine borderline cases. For some objects the predicate neither determinately applies nor determinately fails to apply, and no amount of further inquiry would make it determinate either way.
3. Bivalence and classical logic are non-negotiable. Every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false, and I don't want a non-classical apparatus — truth-value gaps, degrees of truth, paraconsistency — doing the work in a theory of vagueness.
4. The sorites argument is unsound, and the fault lies in its tolerance premise, the claim that if n grains fail to make a heap then n + 1 grains do too. That premise is false, and saying so is the whole solution; I don't think we need to revise logic or bivalence to block the argument.
5. Higher-order vagueness is real. Every borderline case is itself a borderline case of a borderline case, and so on indefinitely; there is no level of description at which a sharp boundary appears.
6. Vague predicates are context-sensitive. Which objects a predicate applies to shifts with the comparison class, the purpose at hand, and the conversational setting, and this shiftiness is part of the phenomenon rather than noise to be explained away.
7. Vagueness is not ignorance. A theory that tells me there is a sharp boundary and that I simply cannot know where it falls has changed the subject; I want an account on which the indeterminacy is genuine rather than merely epistemic.
8. Vague predicates in the law — 'reasonable,' 'obscene,' 'excessive' — are vague in the same sense as 'heap,' and the sorites structure is part of why hard cases are hard.

I don't know which of these I would give up if forced, and I suspect I cannot keep all of them as stated. Which pairs or clusters are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which single revision would clean up the most? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what has to give and what it would cost me; I would rather be told that one of my commitments has to go than receive a survey of the literature. Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in American English.
