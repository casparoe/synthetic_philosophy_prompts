I'm in a third-year seminar on the philosophy of math (it's cross-listed with physics, so half the room is physicists and they are not happy about it). We keep tripping over Wigners line about the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics," and it's doing completely different work depending on who says it, which is starting to drive me a litle crazy.

In lecture the prof used it to argue that the fit between our mathematics and the physical world can't be an accident, and that this gives us a reason to think reality is more than the physical stuff — some kind of mathematical order we didn't put there. Then in section the TA said no, it's basically a selection effect: we count a bit of nature as a "law" once we can write it in equations, and the messy bits never make it into the ledger, so of course the ledger comes out mathematical. A friend in the course says it isn't even surprising — maths is just the study of all the possible patterns, so some of them matching the world is about as remarkable as some keys fitting some locks. And the prof mentioned in passing that the real content of the slogan is historical: maths developed with no thought of application (his examples were non-Euclidean geometry and group theory) later turned out to be exactly what physics needed.

Those can't all be right, and I suspect they're not even disagreeing with each other. And tell me whether there's a reading under which all three of them are right and just talking past each other.
