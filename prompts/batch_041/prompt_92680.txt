I recently left a PhD program in philosophy for a job on a research team that spends part of every week converting possible charitable interventions into expected-value estimates, and I have discovered that the one thing I cannot get a stable grip on is the thing my own spreadsheets keep doing. A colleague noticed that on our current numbers a program with roughly a one-in-a-billion chance of a payoff a billion times larger than anything else in the portfolio outranks the rest of the portfolio combined, and my reaction to that changes depending on which day you ask me. Rather than keep arguing this case by case with my colleagues, I would like a diagnostic instrument for the team: a decision tree that carries someone from the starting point we all share — we are trying to do as much good as we can and we are willing to use expected value to compare options — to a specific, nameable position about what to do with tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs, where every branch is a question a person can answer without knowing any of the labels. Please build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of views: first lay out the branch questions in the order they have to come, with the combinations of answers they permit and forbid, and then place the actual positions inside the space that results, naming each leaf with a short description of the view rather than with a person or a citation. I do not want a reading list, and nothing in the tree should require having read the literature.

Three cases have to do the work, and every branch must be answerable by reference to at least one of them: (i) the familiar shape, in which a stranger offers you a modest cost now in exchange for a finite but astronomically large benefit at a probability of about one in a billion; (ii) the same offer with a payoff that is not merely enormous but unbounded, so that every option looks equal or the comparison stops making sense; and (iii) a pure aggregation case with no probabilities in it, where the choice is between a barely perceptible harm to an unimaginably large number of people and a severe harm to one person. I need (iii) because I want the tree to expose whether someone's resistance to tiny probabilities is consistent with their willingness, or refusal, to aggregate enormous numbers of small harms — I suspect half my colleagues are skeptics about tiny probabilities and enthusiasts about aggregation, and I want to know whether that combination is occupied, and if it is not, whether that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexamined. Three distinctions matter most for my purposes, and I would like each of them to appear as a branch rather than get folded into the leaves: whether the view locates the trouble in the decision rule (expected value maximization itself), in the value theory (unbounded or lexically superior values), or in the probabilities (unreliable, undefined, or unmeasurable at these magnitudes), since those three diagnoses have quite different practical consequences; whether the view is stable under scaling, that is, whether pushing the probability down and the payoff up eventually flips the answer, which is what separates someone who takes the bet however small the probability gets from someone with a threshold; and whether the view is action-guiding at all, since several of the positions I have run into seem to end either in paralysis or in an inability to say anything about my Monday.

Keep it to a single page: at most six branch questions and between eight and twelve leaves, for a room of eight people who are numerate but not decision theorists. Two further things would be genuinely useful. First, I have three hybrid positions I keep half-defending and cannot place: (a) there is a probability threshold below which an outcome may be ignored, but the threshold is not fixed in advance and shifts with how reliable my estimate of the probability is; (b) what should be maximized is the expected value of the decision procedure I adopt rather than of the action, and this vindicates ignoring tiny probabilities because procedures that respond to them are themselves unreliable; and (c) there is no fact of the matter about what to do here, not because the values cannot be compared but because the probabilities are not well defined. Tell me where each of these lands, and whether each is a stable resting place or only a way station to one of the leaves. Second, I would rather have an honest flag than a decorative branch: if two leaves come out behaviorally identical on my three cases — I suspect some threshold views and some of the epistemic views will — say so, and tell me what a fourth case would have to look like to separate them, instead of inventing a question that does no work. Finish by telling me which single branch does the most work, and which one I could drop without losing a leaf.
