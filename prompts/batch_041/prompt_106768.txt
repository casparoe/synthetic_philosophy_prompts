For the past few years I've been tracking an argument that keeps resurfacing — at a reading group I sit in on, at a workshop I covered last spring, and in the small literature on measurement, incentives, and machine learning — and it never resolves. The subject is the cluster of claims that travel under the heading of Goodhart's law and the adjacent idea of specification gaming: roughly, that when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, and that agents optimized against a proxy will find ways to satisfy the proxy while betraying whatever it was supposed to track. One camp treats this as close to inevitable, a structural feature of any system that optimizes hard against a proxy — a claim about dynamics under pressure, nearer to a theorem than to an observation. A second camp treats it as a heap of quite different phenomena wrongly filed together: the proxy that decays statistically because the agent now occupies a different region of the distribution, the proxy that fails at the tails once you push past the range it was calibrated on, the proxy the agent can causally manipulate in ways that bypass the goal, and the proxy an adversary deliberately exploits. A third camp says the whole thing is nearer a tautology or a definitional truth about what it means for a proxy to be imperfect, and that the real work lies elsewhere. A fourth insists it is an ethical and institutional claim rather than a mathematical one, about what happens once organizations start managing to the metric.

I have a working hypothesis about why nobody moves, and I'd like you to test it rather than confirm it. My guess is that the parties are not disagreeing about facts at all but are answering different questions under a single heading: one side asks what happens to a proxy under optimization pressure (a claim about a process), another asks what makes a measurement valid in the first place (a claim about the relation between an indicator and the construct it stands for), and a third asks whether we should expect institutions to converge on this failure (a claim about incentives). If that's right, then part of the impasse is one word — measure, goal, proxy, target — doing several jobs at once, and part of it is a genuine disagreement about whether the dynamics claim is even a claim. If it's wrong — if there is a real substantive dispute buried in there, or if the label is simply too heterogeneous to sustain a single debate and the honest answer is that Goodhart's law names a family rather than a thesis — I would much rather hear that than have you split the difference.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each side actually says when you press them, plus a candid account of what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a formal result that would settle the dynamics claim, a set of cases showing the variants are one thing rather than four, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. And please tell me if the way I've framed this is itself confused, or if I'm imputing to the participants a disagreement they don't actually have — I'd rather be told the question is ill-posed than get a tidy taxonomy that papers over it.
