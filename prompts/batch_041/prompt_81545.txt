Hey, third-year PhD student in animal cognition here — I've got a draft section I want to run by you before my advisor sees it, and it's short so I'll just paste the gist.

The claim: everyone fights about whether fish or bees or octopuses are conscious, but I think the whole distribution question is a measurement problem wearing a metaphysics costume. My line is that the two sides in these debates usually aren't disagreeing about any fact at all — they're disagreeing about which markers get to count as constitutive rather than merely correlational. Once you see that, "is the bee conscious" stops being a yes/no question about the bee and becomes a question about which theory you quietly adopted when you picked your test. I call this the borrowed-criterion problem: any test for consciousness in another animal already encodes a theory of what consciousness is *for*, so the test can't be independent evidence for the theory that built it. I then try to show both the fish-feel-pain camp and the skeptics commit it, just in opposite directions — one treats nociception-plus-avoidance as constitutive, the other treats cortex-like architecture as constitutive — and I end by arguing the honest question isn't "which animals are conscious" but "which animals have states whose valence we're licensed to weigh," which I claim comes apart from the consciousness question, because you can have minimal experience with no valence and valence-like regulation with no experience.

(b) does the borrowed-criterion point cut against my own ending, since "licensed to weigh" sounds like it smuggles in a criterion too? and (c) that valence/experience split — is it doing actual work or is it just a distinction I like the sound of?
