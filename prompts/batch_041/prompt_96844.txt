I am a novelist at work on a book in which David Hume, by a fictional conceit I will not bore you with, is brought forward to a present-day university and asked to sit in on a seminar in experimental philosophy. The seminar is devoted to the finding that ordinary people are more willing to call a side effect intentional when it is harmful than when it is helpful, and to the further findings that these judgments shift with the moral identity of the actor, the group affected, and the culture of the respondent. The experimentalists in the room take these results to show that philosophers cannot trust their own intuitions about intentional action, and perhaps cannot trust armchair methods more generally. Hume, in my novel, is neither a cartoon skeptic nor a proto-experimentalist; he is a philosopher of moral sentiment who has never heard of a controlled experiment on folk psychology, and I need to know what he would actually say. Please reconstruct his likely position as carefully as you can, in his intellectual voice but without pastiche, and without pretending that he would have had our vocabulary.

I want the reconstruction to include a numbered list of claims he would accept, a numbered list of claims he would reject or qualify, and a clear statement of the distinctions he would insist on. In particular, I want to know what he would make of the is–ought gap in this context: whether the experimental results are being used to establish a normative conclusion, and if so, whether that use is legitimate. I want to know whether he would treat the side-effect effect as evidence for his own account of sympathy and moral sentiment, or as a bias that his account can explain without endorsing. I want to know how he would distinguish a psychological fact about how people form intentionality judgments from a philosophical claim about when an action really is intentional. And I want to know what he would say to the experimentalists who claim that their surveys of ordinary intuitions have more evidential weight than the reflections of a philosopher in an armchair. If he thought the effect revealed something deep about human nature, what experiment would he design to test that, and what would he predict the results would be?

Finally, I want the piece to be honest about where Hume would have to remain silent. He might have no view on the statistical methods, the replication crisis, or the cross-cultural data, and I do not want you to invent one. But I do want you to say where his philosophy gives him a definite answer, where it gives him only a constraint, and where the question is one he would have regarded as unanswerable or not yet well posed. The novel needs a Hume who can be surprised by the experiments without being converted by them, and who can tell the difference between a discovery about the mind and an argument about morals.
