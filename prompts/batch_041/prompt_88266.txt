I run a small applied-ML and policy reading group, and I'm putting together an onboarding note on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. What I want from you is a diagnostic instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that takes a colleague from a concrete incident ("our reward model started paying for sycophancy"; "the district's test scores went up and the teaching got worse") to a named diagnosis of what kind of failure it is, and then to what that diagnosis implies about the fix.

Two constraints before the specification.

First, do not hand me the familiar four-mechanism taxonomy from the AI-safety literature — proxy as a noisy estimator of the target, proxy failing away from the data distribution, proxy as an effect rather than a cause of the target, adversarial gaming of the proxy — as the tree's skeleton. Build the tree from the logical space first, out of the questions that actually split the positions, and only then locate the familiar variants inside it. Where one of your leaves coincides with a familiar variant, say so; where your tree separates two things the familiar taxonomy lumps together, say that too. At least one leaf must be a position the familiar taxonomy has no room for.

Second, here is the angle I care about. I suspect much of the disagreement about Goodhart's law is really disagreement about two things that usually go unstated: whether the target exists independently of the metric at all (is the proxy–goal gap epistemic, i.e. we cannot measure what we want, or constitutive, i.e. the metric is partly what the goal is, so "the metric diverged from the goal" is a category error), and who, if anyone, is doing the gaming (an optimiser that was simply handed the proxy, a specifier quietly gaming their own instrument, or a selection process with no specifier and no intent at all, as in evolution or a market). The branch nodes should make both questions explicit.

Specification:

- Branch nodes: six to nine, each phrased as a question a practitioner can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which diagnosis it leads to. No node may be a dressed-up version of "do you think the proxy measures the target well?" Every node must be answerable by pointing at behaviour, an incentive, or a counterfactual — what happens if we optimise harder, does the metric move the goal, would a perfectly informed designer still be gamed, who gains from the number looking good.
- Leaves: seven to nine named positions. Each gets a name, a one-sentence statement of its core claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst. Where a leaf corresponds to a position nobody actually holds, label it unnamed and say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored.
- Every leaf must imply something about the fix, and the fix families must come apart on the tree: measure better; optimise less or more carefully; change the oversight or measurement regime; change who is rewarded; abandon the single scalar; or accept the divergence as unavoidable and design for robustness instead of prevention.
- Required cases, all of which must be placed somewhere on the tree: a recommender optimising watch time; a language model fine-tuned on human helpfulness ratings; a school teaching to a standardised test; an RL agent that learns to interfere with the process computing its own reward; a biological case with no specifier (a trait that spreads because it was selected for, and a population that adopts contraception); and a target imposed from outside on an organisation (a factory told to hit a quota measured in tonnes, or a bounty paid per head of a pest).
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, rather than papering over it, and say what additional question would separate them, if one exists.
- Audience: technically literate non-specialists. Length: as long as the tree needs and no longer.

I'll tell you where I land, so you can test the instrument on me: I think the proxy–goal gap is definitional rather than empirical; I think the only failures worth theorising are the ones where an agent with a goal of its own finds the blind spot; and I think the usual fix is a change in the oversight regime rather than a better metric.

End with a single line of the form \answer{<leaf label>} giving the leaf my three answers put me on; if they genuinely straddle two leaves, write \answer{<leaf label> / <leaf label>}.
