My friend Dana works on population ethics and longtermism-adjacent stuff, and she has this eerily consistent attitude to the future that I've been trying to reverse-enginer. Here's her track record as best I can remember it:

1. Asked to compare a program that saves 1000 lives 50 years from now against one that saves lives today, she says the future program is worth about 140 lives today.
2. Push it out to 100 years and she says a benefit counts for roughly a fiftieth of an identical benefit now.
3. She's happy to discount money at 4% a year: she'd take $104 next year over $100 today, and $108.16 in two years over $104 in one.
4. She insists the pattern is exponential rather than hyperbolic — she doesn't get extra impatient about the near future, it's a flat rate all the way out.
5. She's adamant the discount isn't about future people having lower moral status as people.

Now the new case. A policy would improve the lives of a specific set of people who are alive right now, say in their twenties, but the improvement only lands 50 years from now, when they'll be in their seventies. Nobody's worried about humanity surviving that long, and these particular people will almost certainly still be around. Does she say that benefit counts at full weight, at about a seventh of its face value, or somewhere in between?

Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, no intro or conclusion: (1) the rule you think she's actually running, (2) a different rule that fits everything above just as well, (3) what each of those two rules predicts for the new case, (4) which one you'd bet on and why, (5) the single further case you'd put to her that would cleanly separate the two rules.
