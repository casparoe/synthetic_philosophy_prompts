English football has, over several decades, produced a case that I think tests the competing accounts of how slurs carry their derogation more sharply than the usual textbook examples. Tottenham Hotspur, a north London club with a historically large Jewish following, has long been taunted by rival supporters with a Yiddish-derived epithet for Jews; from the 1960s the club's own supporters, Jewish and non-Jewish alike, took the word and its diminutive up as a self-description and a chant, and many still wear it as a badge of identity. The usage remains contested: Jewish supporters of the club are divided over it; the governing body of English football treats the word as offensive; and the lexicographers disagree among themselves, one major dictionary recording a distinct self-designation sense alongside the derogatory one while others mark the term flatly offensive. Treat this episode as the case, and the philosophical work on slur meaning as the lens.

First, set out the accounts on offer: that the derogation belongs to the word's semantic content; that it is carried by a conventional implicature or presupposition which survives embedding and cannot simply be cancelled; that it resides in the word's history and in how it is used rather than in what it denotes; that the word expresses the speaker's derogatory attitude; that it encodes a perspective on its target. Then say, for each, what it predicts here — whether the in-group chant is the same word with the same meaning, a distinct reclaimed lexical item, or the same word used without derogation — and whether the account can explain why the derogation apparently vanishes in one mouth and not another.

Second, press where the accounts are least comfortable. If derogation is a matter of use, what licenses the in-group user and not the outsider who sings the same chant? If it is a matter of content, how can one and the same word be both a slur and a badge of honour? Ask whether the in-group use is parasitic on the derogatory one — whether reclamation works precisely by invoking the history it claims to defeat — and what that implies for the claim that reclamation cancels or empties the slur. Ask also whether the question of who counts as an authorised user is answerable within semantics and pragmatics at all, or whether it belongs to something else.

Third, run the analysis the other way. Say which account the episode confirms, which it embarrasses, and whether it is an anomaly the accounts never anticipated rather than a datum they can absorb. Then consider institutional uptake: a football authority, a court or a dictionary must settle on a meaning and act on it, and say what notion of meaning that requires — whether the semantic/pragmatic distinction supplies it or whether it needs some further notion such as social meaning, uptake or standing. Finally, say plainly where the theory is silent: what this case leaves unresolved that no account of slur meaning, as such, could settle, including whether a theory of meaning owes us any verdict at all on what the club, the authorities or the supporters should do.

I have no interest in a literature survey and need no references; what I want is the mapping done carefully and the limits of each account stated honestly. Please write in British English.
