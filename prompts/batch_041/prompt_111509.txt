I am a quantitative risk analyst at a reinsurer, where I spend my working life pricing instruments — catastrophe bonds, tail hedges, and the like — whose loss distributions are heavy-tailed. Most of what I do professionally amounts to insisting that an expected value is not a sufficient statistic for a risk: that a position whose expected loss is dominated by a single catastrophic scenario should not be underwritten even when the expectation is favorable, that the probability of ruin and the size of the drawdown matter more than the mean, and that a hazard I can attach a frequency to is a different object from an ambiguity I cannot. A friend in the philosophy department has been trying to explain Pascal's mugging to me: a stranger threatens to inflict an enormous, though finite, harm on you unless you hand over your wallet, and since that harm, multiplied by even a tiny probability that he is telling the truth, dwarfs the cost of compliance, expected-utility reasoning appears to counsel handing over the wallet. I would like you to restate that argument, and the debate it has provoked, as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — probability as frequency or as market-implied price rather than as subjective credence, expectations that may be dominated by a single tail event or may fail to be well-defined at all, expected shortfall, ruin, the difference between a one-shot bet and a repeated one, and the distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable ambiguity. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophers' argument survive the translation intact, which survive only in a distorted form, and which have no counterpart in how I think about risk at all.

I am aware that my own vocabulary may be smuggling in assumptions of its own — that every hazard can be assigned a price, that bets are repeated and the law of large numbers will eventually do its work, that the worst case is bounded by something I can capitalize — and I would much rather you flag those than paper over them. My working instinct is that the mugging is simply a heavy-tailed-distribution problem, to be defused by the same rule I would apply to any position whose expectation is dominated by an astronomically unlikely scenario: refuse to optimize the expectation. Tell me whether that is a genuine dissolution of the puzzle or only a relabeling of it; and for whatever has no counterpart on my side — the possibility of a literally infinite stake, the notion of a subjective credence that is not a frequency, the theological ancestry of the whole line of thought — say whether my framework is missing something or whether the philosophers could have stated the point more plainly than they do.
