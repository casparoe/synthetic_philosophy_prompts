I write about mathematics and the physical sciences for a general-interest magazine, and the same problem keeps surfacing in my drafts. A source announces that some new object has been discovered — a manifold, a class of numbers, a space with properties nobody expected — and my prose has to decide, sentence by sentence, whether to treat it as something that was already there waiting to be found, as something a community invented, or as a convenient piece of machinery that earns its keep without anyone claiming it is real. My editors do not care about the metaphysics. They care that we do not smuggle a philosophical position into a reported piece, where it can be attacked in the letters column, and that the story still tells readers what actually happened. I do this most weeks, under deadline, and I cannot call a philosopher every time.

Please build me a decision procedure I can run on a draft in about ten minutes, with no philosophical training beyond what I have picked up on the beat. For each existence claim in the piece, it should tell me which of a small number of framings my prose is licensed to use and what I must check before using it. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns a definite framing, and the point at which it stops and hands the decision to me or my editor because what remains is a matter of philosophical taste rather than reporting. Draw on what the actual literature on mathematical existence has to say — the indispensability argument, the difficulty of explaining how we could have epistemic access to abstract objects, fictionalist and structuralist alternatives — but translate it into questions a reporter can answer about a specific sentence in a specific draft. In particular, I want the procedure to separate two things I suspect I have been conflating: whether the mathematics is indispensable to the scientific result I am reporting, and whether it is indispensable only to the way my source chose to explain that result. Where the procedure rests on a contested premise, mark it as contested and say what follows for the story if the premise is wrong.

Then run it on three cases and show me the framing each one yields. First, a physicist reports that a theory works only when formulated in a space of many more dimensions than we observe; the extra dimensions are never measured, and the theory's predictions match experiment to high precision. Second, a mathematician announces a proof that some object must exist, where the object is characterized entirely by the role it plays in a structure, so that no one can say which of several candidate objects it is. Third, a case of your own choosing where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — most likely that a sentence I am attached to must be rewritten in a way that makes the story duller or less quotable — and tell me what I should do about it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I do not want it to pretend to settle whether mathematical objects exist; I want it to settle what my sentences commit me to, and to make that commitment visible to me and to my editor before we publish. Flag the places where it will misfire — for instance, where an object is introduced by stipulation and the discovery-versus-invention distinction is empty, or where the framing that is safest for the prose is also the least informative for the reader — and say how I should handle those cases.
