Eighteen months ago I left a philosophy PhD programme for a job as a research analyst at a small sports-betting syndicate, and next week I am giving the opening talk at our internal methods offsite. The talk is called The Vig Is the Only Dutch Book, and the thesis is that the Dutch book argument does not establish what my colleagues take it to establish — that an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms is thereby irrational, and that keeping our published estimates coherent is what keeps us solvent. I make three claims. (1) The exploit in the theorem requires an agent who will take either side of a bet at her stated odds, at unlimited stake; no such agent exists, and the interesting question is what becomes of the theorem once the agent is free to decline. (2) The variant that drops that assumption — the one that runs through bets the agent herself would call fair — needs a further premise, that calling a set of odds fair commits you to accepting bets at those odds, and that premise is exactly what is in dispute. (3) In our actual practice, the party whose posted odds cannot be represented by any probability function is the bookmaker, who lays 10:11 on both sides of a two-outcome market, and he is also the one who reliably profits. Whatever the theorem is tracking, it is not the line between the people who make money and the people who lose it. I should add that I want to keep our internal rule that every analyst's published estimate be a probability function over the relevant partition; I just think it should be defended as bookkeeping and auditability rather than as an epistemic norm. I expect the quants to try to move the argument from one-shot bets to sequences of bets, and I want to be ready for that move.

The audience is about fifteen people, and I know them well enough to predict the room. Most are professional bettors who price by feel, distrust theory, and will hear the talk as an attack on a rule they never liked anyway. Two are quants who came from a hedge fund and are committed Bayesians; they cite coherence the way other people cite scripture and will suspect I am either confused or posturing. One is an actuary who thinks in terms of ruin, variance and capital reserves, and will want to know what any of this has to do with staying solvent. The founder, who will be in the front row, has a standing rule that an analyst whose numbers are not coherent is not doing the job.

I want a question-period prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it in the room. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in ninety seconds and an honest note on where that reply is weakest and what I should simply concede. Where an objection is right, say so. Tell me what to concede up front, before the questions begin, so that I am not defending ground I do not need. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not give me citations or a reading list: neither of us can check them and the talk does not depend on them. If you invoke a named argument, describe it in your own words.
