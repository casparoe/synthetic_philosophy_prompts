You are a philosopher of science who works on the epistemology of thought experiments and who has also served, more than once, as the neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration -- the kind of joint statement two camps produce after years of arguing past each other, in which everything both sides can sign is written down and the residual disagreement is made exact. I am a graduate student preparing a seminar on whether thought experiments can yield knowledge, and I keep noticing that the two main camps seem to talk past each other. One camp holds that a thought experiment can be a genuine source of knowledge about nature, a kind of perception or insight into how things must be that no argument could replace; the other holds that every thought experiment is at bottom an argument -- picturesque, psychologically vivid, but built from premises that are either empirical or merely unstated, so that the knowledge it delivers is exactly the knowledge we fed in. I want the document these two camps should have written years ago. Treat both as sophisticated and good-faith; do not build a strawman for either, and do not settle for common ground that no one would dispute. Begin by brainstorming candidate sub-questions and marking which of them you suspect are merely verbal, then draft.

The document should have two parts. Part one is a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: about what a thought experiment is as a text or a performance, about the phenomenology of the "aha" moment and what it does or does not establish, about what the history of the subject shows -- for instance about thought experiments whose persuasive force quietly evaporated once an underlying picture of nature changed -- about the status of intuition as evidence, and about what would have to be true for the dispute to be resolvable at all. At least one of these agreed claims should be substantive rather than a truism; the list should do work, not merely bracket two caricatures. Part two is a disagreements table, one row per sub-question, giving for each row the disagreement in a formulation each camp would accept for its own side, marking whether you judge it a genuine crux or a difference in words, and, where it is genuine, naming a case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on it -- with each camp's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance. Use at least three cases: one from physics (the elevator, the demon, or the twins, whichever you can handle most precisely), one from the history of the life sciences or medicine, and one that is not a natural-science case at all, something in the neighbourhood of a moral thought experiment, since the camps disagree about whether the natural-science paradigm generalizes to it. Finally, address directly whether there is anything both camps would accept as evidence against their own position, or whether each view is insulated from refutation in a way that itself deserves a row in the table; and if part of the dispute turns out to be about what "argument" and "intuition" are being made to mean, say which part and why it is nonetheless not idle. I would particularly like you to press on the difference between a premise that is merely suppressed and a premise that is available only to someone who has already run the experiment in imagination, since that distinction seems to be where the argument view gets its bite and where its opponents think it cheats. Refer to well-known experiments by their ordinary names; no bibliography or citations are wanted. Write it formally and at whatever length the content requires -- this is a document both parties would put their names to, so it should read as if they had.
