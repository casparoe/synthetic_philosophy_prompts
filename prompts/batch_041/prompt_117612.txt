Most industrialised legal systems now regulate working time on two seemingly opposed principles at once. France fixes the ordinary week at 35 hours and treats everything beyond it as exceptional, payable at a premium and offset by compensatory rest. The European Union's working-time directive imposes an average ceiling of 48 hours a week including overtime, and yet permits member states to let individual workers opt out of that ceiling by agreement — an arrangement several states have used and the United Kingdom relied on for years. Japan, meanwhile, keeps a statutory standard week that is widely exceeded in practice, and recognises claims on behalf of workers who die of stroke, cardiac failure, or suicide when the death can be attributed to overwork, applying administrative thresholds for monthly and cumulative overtime hours beyond which the attribution is presumptively made.

What conception of work and leisure do these arrangements presuppose? More precisely: do they treat an overlong working week as a harm done to the worker, which the law may therefore restrict in the worker's own interest, or as a choice the worker is entitled to make, so that the law's only business is to secure the terms on which the bargain is struck? The opt-out points one way; compulsory rest periods and overwork-death compensation point the other. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, separating what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the two halves can be made consistent — whether by a doctrine on which the opt-out is valid only where the underlying consent is genuinely free, or by a view on which it is a structural concession to employer power that the law should not dignify with the language of choice.

Then say what the practice presupposes about the value of work itself. A regime that compensates a death from overwork while offering nothing for the exhaustion that precedes it, or that exempts managers and the self-employed from the ceiling it insists on for factory hands, appears to treat overwork as a recognised injury in one register and as a mark of commitment or autonomy in another. Does that pattern presuppose the older valuation of work as a calling, something whose limits are owed only to the body — or is it equally explicable by a plainer story about bargaining power, the costs of enforcement, and the political weakness of the workers concerned? Tell me which of these the institutions actually need, and whether a regime that took leisure to be the default and paid employment the exception would have to be built differently from the ground up, or could be assembled from the same parts.
