I took my retirement from invertebrate systematics three years ago and have spent the time since as a rather over-age graduate student in philosophy; this term I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on conceptual problems in biology, of which the species problem takes up two sessions. For the midterm I set the essay question below, and I would be grateful for your assessment of the answer that follows it.

The question, as set: In one session we constructed a candidate criterion, which I will call the persistence criterion. On it, a species is a maximal set of populations that (i) share a common adaptive zone, so that their gene pools are held in a similar region of phenotype space either by gene flow or, where gene flow is absent, by parallel stabilizing selection, and (ii) can be expected, under prevailing conditions, to persist as a distinguishable lineage for at least T generations, where T is fixed in advance by the investigator. Does this criterion solve the species problem, or does it merely relocate it? Argue for your answer with reference to at least one other species concept and to the thesis that species are individuals.

The student's answer:

The species problem is not one problem but several, because the competing concepts answer different questions. The biological species concept asks whether populations interbreed; phylogenetic concepts ask about ancestry and diagnosability. The persistence criterion, as formulated, adds a temporal requirement to the biological species concept and is therefore stricter than it. It also improves on it, since the biological species concept has no application to obligately asexual lineages, whereas the persistence criterion applies to them without remainder: a parthenogenetic lineage held in place by parallel stabilizing selection satisfies clause (i) and, if it outlasts T, satisfies clause (ii). So the criterion does solve the species problem in precisely the case that most obviously defeats the biological species concept.

Whether it solves it generally depends on what we take species to be. If species are individuals — particular, spatiotemporally located lineages with beginnings and ends, rather than spatiotemporally unrestricted classes — then species names do not pick out natural kinds at all; they are labels of convenience, and the choice among species concepts becomes a matter of convention rather than of truth. On that view the persistence criterion does not so much solve the problem as replace one convention with another. I conclude that it relocates the problem rather than solving it, though relocation may be all we should expect.

Please give the essay a letter grade for a take-home worth a quarter of the module mark, and then the point-by-point comments you would hand back. I want the student to be able to see which specific claims in the answer earned credit and which did not, rather than a general impression of how the essay reads.
