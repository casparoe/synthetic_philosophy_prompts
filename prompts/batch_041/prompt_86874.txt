I'm a second-year PhD student in a vision lab — binocular rivalry, backward masking, that kind of thing — and my supervisior has decided we're going to be a "heterophenomenological" lab. In principle I'm on board. In practice I have to write the protocol that a research assistant will hand to a participant on Monday, and I cannot tell you what doing heterophenomenology actually requires that ordinary "ask them what they saw and write it down" doesn't. She keeps quoting Dennett at me — "heterophenomenology is nothing new; it is nothing other than the method that has been used by psychophysicists, cognitive psychologists, clinical neuropsychologists, and just about everybody who has ever purported to study human consciousness in a serious, scientific way" — which I think is meant to be reassuring, but it mainly makes me suspect that what she wants is our existing methods with a philosophy sticker on them. I do know there's supposed to be some bracketing going on, and that the reports are meant to be turned into a "heterophenomenological world" that isn't the same thing as the subject's experience, but I couldn't tell you where the method stops and the normal experiment begins.

So what I want from you is a procedure our lab can actually run: an ordered checklist that goes from "participant walks in" to "we have usable data," and then a second set of steps from data to interpretation. For each step, tell me what we do, what counts as an adequate answer or a finished step, what gets written down and in what form, and where the step is really a judgement call dressed up as a rule. Four things we argue about alot and that the procedure has to settle: (a) how much rope the subject gets — free narration versus a structured question list — and whether leading questions poison the data or are fine as long as they're logged; (b) what to do when a report is internally inconsistent, or flatly contradicts the stimulus, which happens more than you'd think (people swear the flash was red when the photodiode says green); (c) whether we experimenters are ever allowed to use our own introspection as a check on anything, and if not, what we do instead when a subject's report sounds deranged; and (d) what we're actually entitled to conclude at the end, which I suspect is definately much less than my supervisor hopes. Also (e), how we're supposed to compare reports across subjects when we can't assume their words mean the same things — I keep being told this is like translating an alien language, but nobody will tell me what the translation procedure looks like. Keep the constraints in mind: twenty minutes per participant, one RA running it who has no philosophy background, and a reviewer on our grant who thinks verbal reports are just confabulation and wants to see that we've dealt with that.

Please take the role of someone who has both read Dennett closely and actually run a lab, and start by laying out the design forks and picking one with reasons, rather than handing me a single confident list. Then walk the procedure end to end on two cases: a masking trial where the subject reports a shape that wasn't there, and a long free-narration interview about a psychedelic experience (we collaborate with a clinical group, so those run to forty minutes of rambling metaphor). Include at least one place where the procedure tells me something I won't like — ideally that the study as designed can't answer the question we built it to answer. Be explicit about what the procedure can't do, including whether heterophenomenology has anything useful to say about our no-report condition (we also run pupillometry and optokinetic nystagmus as report-free markers, and the obvious question is whether the reports and the markers are even measuring the same thing). And if the honest answer is that turning this into a step-by-step procedure betrays the point, or that what you'd hand me is just standard qualitative methods with a Dennett sticker, say that plainly instead of complying politely — I'd rather be told the task is misconceived now than after I've run thirty subjects. Define any jargon you can't avoid; "intentional stance" and "autophenomenology" are both phrases I've nodded along to without knowing.
