For the past several months a colleague and I have been circling the same argument, and we have now agreed to try the adversarial-collaboration exercise properly rather than keep rehearsing our opening moves. She holds the anti-mechanist line in the tradition of Lucas and Penrose: the incompleteness theorems, on her view, show that no algorithmic system — no machine, however large or well engineered — could do what a mathematician does when she recognizes the truth of a Gödel sentence for a formal system she understands. I hold the mechanist line: I accept the theorems in full and deny that they cut against mechanism, and I suspect her confidence rests on an unearned assumption about her own consistency. Please draft the document we would both sign at the end of a good-faith collaboration. Treat the two parties as an anti-mechanist of that stripe and a mechanist who knows the mathematics and rejects the inference, and note that neither of us can look anything up, so reconstruct every argument in your own words; give no citations, names, or reading lists, and let the document stand on its own as reasoning.

Both parties already agree on the mathematics, so open with a numbered list of claims both would sign: about what the first and second incompleteness theorems do and do not assert for a consistent, recursively axiomatized system strong enough for arithmetic; about the provable equivalence between such a system's Gödel sentence and the statement of its own consistency; about the fact that no theorem mentions minds; about the idealization the argument requires, given that actual mathematicians make mistakes and have held inconsistent theories; about the strongest version of the opposing view each of us can accept as not obviously wrong; and about what sort of consideration could count for or against the anti-mechanist thesis at all. Then make the disagreement exact, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept. Two moves in particular need to be laid out so that we each recognize ourselves in the description: the mechanist's extension move, in which a machine that cannot prove its own Gödel sentence simply appends that sentence as a new axiom and carries on, together with the question of whether such a climbing sequence of theories reproduces the mathematician's recognition or only defers it, and what becomes of soundness at the limit of the sequence; and the anti-mechanist's reply that the mathematician sees the truth of the newly added sentence rather than merely asserting it — which forces the question whether she can know her own consistency, and, if she cannot, whether the argument survives.

I want the residual disagreement presented as a table with the question at issue, each side's formulation of its own view, the crux that would have to be settled for that side to move, and — for each crux — a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance (for instance, what each predicts about a machine handed its own source code and asked whether it is consistent, or about a machine deliberately built to output its own Gödel sentence with no proof at all, and whether either side would call that output knowledge). Close with a short paragraph identifying which parts of our dispute you judge to be merely verbal — over what "machine" is supposed to mean, perhaps, or over whether a finite subject could ever be entitled to the anti-mechanist's claim — and which are genuine and, on the present state of the argument, unresolved. The result should be a document we can both put our names to; it should not award a verdict to either side.
