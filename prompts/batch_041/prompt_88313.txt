I write about politics and ideas for a magazine, and I have been asked to run a 90-minute session at a weekend ideas festival for roughly 25 attendees: smart generalists with no philosophy training. I want them to leave having felt, in their own behavior, the difference between three ways of being unfree — someone actually interfering with you; someone who never interferes but could, at any moment and with no appeal; and a collective that compels you in the name of your own real interests. The distinction should come out of the rules, not out of my closing remarks.

My sketch, which you should feel free to discard: run the same short resource game three times with the same players, changing only the governance card. In round one, an enforcer actively blocks and fines certain moves. In round two, the same enforcer holds unchecked power over every move but is instructed to be benevolent and never use it, and the players know both facts, so that we can watch whether they censor themselves anyway. In round three, a majority may vote to compel individuals toward what the group decides they really want. If time allows, add a fourth round in which the same overseer is bound by published rules and an appeal process, so that we can see whether it is the power or its unaccountability that the players are reacting to. Give me the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a way of recording what changes across rounds, and a debrief that maps each round onto non-interference, the republican idea of non-domination, and self-mastery. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — especially if players shrug at the benevolent overseer because he never actually acts — and be honest about which part of the idea a game like this cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words.
