Right — I've been trying to write up my position on social welfare and I've got a horrible feeling the bits dont fit together, so I want someone to audit the package before I embarrass myself in front of people who do this properly. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(i) Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is basically right. Put the expected-utility axioms on both individual and social preference, add a unanimity requirement (if everyone's indifferent between two prospects, so is the group), and you're forced to a social utility function that's a weighted sum of individual utilities. I don't think that's a trick; I think it's a genuine constraint on anyone who wants to be a welfarist and a rational chooser at the same time.

(ii) I don't accept the equiprobabilty move that turns it into straight utilitarianism. The theorem hands you weights; it doesn't hand you equal weights. The veil-of-ignorance argument for equal weights quietly assumes the thing it's supposed to be proving.

(iii) I'm an ex post egalitarian. A coin toss that will hand a benefit to one of two equally badly-off people strikes me as worse than simply giving that benefit to one of them for certain. Social evaluation should be of how people actually end up, not of lotteries over who gets what.

(iv) Interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningful, and social welfare should be a function of the levels of well-being people end up at.

(v) My proposed fix for the whole mess: keep the weighted-sum form from (i), but read the weights as degrees of concern for the worse off and apply them to a concave transform of each person's utility, so the thing comes out priority-ish while the structure stays Harsanyi's. Prioritarian Harsanyi, if you like.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, say which pairs only look like they clash, and for each real one tell me the cheapest repair and what it costs me. Max 700 words.
