I'm presenting at a reading group next month and need a Q&A prep sheet. My paper defends a reading of Nozick's truth-tracking account of knowledge that I think has been underexplored: namely, that when he lays down the sensitivity condition — very roughly, the requirement that if the proposition believed were false, the subject would not believe it — the method by which the subject forms the belief has to be individuated coarsely, by the standing body of evidence and the kind of inquiry the subject is engaged in, rather than finely, by the particular causal route or process token that happened to produce this belief. On the coarse reading, I argue, tracking preserves closure for deductions that leave the subject's evidence untouched, and breaks it only for what I call evidence-disrupting deductions, i.e. inferences whose conclusion is the very proposition whose falsity would have altered the subject's evidence in the first place. The asymmetry is the selling point: it is supposed to give us a workable closure principle for ordinary reasoning while still honoring Nozick's willingness, in the 1981 book, to deny that we know the skeptic's hypothesis is false. I have the argument sketched but I have not stress-tested the middle section, where I claim the coarse individuation is well-defined.

The group is small and has a predictable shape, and I want the questions they will actually ask, not the ones I would get at a conference of Nozick specialists. There are two epistemologists who work on anti-luck and safety conditions and who regard the whole counterfactual apparatus as a detour from what they take to be the real job; one contextualist who will want to know why this is not just contextualism with extra machinery; and one formal epistemologist who thinks any closure principle worth stating should be stated for degrees of belief and who will press me on whether coarse individuation of methods can be made precise rather than gestured at. Nobody in the room is a tracking theorist, so they will not grant me the vocabulary for free, and the formal epistemologist in particular will not accept a distinction that survives only in the binary case.

Give me the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, with how much damage each does and a two-minute reply for each, and a frank note on where the reply is soft or where I am really conceding the point. Tell me what to concede up front rather than fight, which single objection I most need to have a good answer to, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the place where the coarse-individuation move is doing more work than I have shown it can bear. Please do not cite literature or recommend readings; I need the arguments themselves, aimed at this room.
