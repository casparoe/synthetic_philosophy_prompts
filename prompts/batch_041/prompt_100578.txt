I run a fortnightly reading group for graduate students in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, and next month we're taking up the real-patterns account of intentional attribution together with the three stances — physical, design, and intentional. This group is not naive: they know the vocabulary, they can list the stances, and they have all absorbed the idea that beliefs are attributed rather than observed. The trouble is that they have quietly collapsed the whole position into instrumentalism — they hear "the intentional stance is a predictive strategy" and conclude "so beliefs are useful fictions," after which they cannot see why anyone would call a pattern real at all. Last session ended with one of them saying that if the stance is a stance, then we are simply choosing to talk that way, and nobody in the room could say what was wrong with that. I don't want to lecture them; I want to build the next session so that the collapse becomes impossible to make.

So don't explain the view to me — plan how I should teach it. Give me the order the ideas have to arrive in and why each depends on the one before: my working assumption is that the notion of a pattern as a compressible description that buys predictive leverage has to be securely in place before anything about stances can mean much, and that "real" has to be pried apart from "intrinsic" before the payoff lands, but tell me where I've got the dependencies wrong. For the carrying example I want something other than the chess engine and the thermostat, which they've all seen; my candidate is a large corporation, whose behaviour we predict by attributing beliefs and desires to the company as such even though no individual holds them and the org chart contains nothing of the kind, and which seems to do genuine predictive work. Tell me whether that example can carry the session, what it must be set up to display, and what it will wrongly suggest — I worry it invites "so it's just a metaphor for the people inside." If it won't carry, tell me what should, and what that example must have and must lack. Then map the misconceptions I should expect in the order they will actually surface — I'm fairly confident about "real means reducible to physics," "mind-dependent means arbitrary," and "the stances are just levels of description, like Marr's" — and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. Finish with a single question I can put to the room that would separate a student who has understood why a pattern can be real without being intrinsic from one who has merely learned to say "it's about compression," and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level.

Keep the whole plan under 800 words, and lay it out as a sequence I can follow live rather than as prose about the topic.
