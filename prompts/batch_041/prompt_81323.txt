I run the tutorials for an upper-level seminar on collective decision-making: the professor takes the formal half (Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, the elements of judgment aggregation), I take the applied half, and the students are a mix of philosophy and politics finalists who can state Arrow's theorem from memory and can reproduce the discursive dilemma — the doctrinal paradox, if you prefer the older name — with the usual breach-of-contract or jury-liability example, but who mostly cannot say what the dilemma is a dilemma *about*. The external examiner complains every year that our paper rewards recall, so for this summer's exam (closed book, three questions in two hours, marked out of 20 on the department's scale where 70 and above is a first) I want to contribute one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the paradox or by reciting Arrow. What I want the students forced to confront is this: the familiar escape routes each buy one virtue at the price of another, and the paradox is a fact about agendas at least as much as about voters. If the propositions on the ballot were logically independent of one another, majority voting on each of them would never produce an inconsistent set; the trouble requires a logically interlinked agenda, which means that whoever fixes what gets voted on, and in what order, wields a power that the aggregation rule itself does not license. I also want them to face the objection that inconsistency in a group is harmless because a group is not a believer — there is no group mind to be of two minds — and to ask whether that objection survives once the same body has to answer for its decisions, and once it has a standing rule (a constitution, a policy, a precedent) that commits it to particular combinations of verdicts over time. I have spent an unseemly amount of time in comment threads arguing about whether committees can be agents at all, and I would like an exam question that makes that argument do some work rather than merely decorating an exposition.

So please write me that essay question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme. For the question, use a concrete case rather than the breach-of-contract chestnut the students have already seen in the handouts: I suggest a five-person appointments panel deciding whether to make an offer to a candidate, where the standing rule is that the candidate is appointable only if both criteria are met, so that the panel's verdict on appointability is the conjunction of its verdicts on research record and on capacity to cover the teaching need. The distribution of views should be one in which a majority endorses each criterion but only a minority endorses the conjunction, and the question should require them to work that through with actual numbers before generalising. The question may presuppose the vocabulary the seminar has taught — collective rationality, independence, universal domain, and the rest — but it must be answerable by a student who cannot remember the name of a single author; nothing in the question, the model answer or the marking scheme may depend on names, titles or dates, and the students must not be asked to cite anything. Pitch the question at about 70 minutes of writing.

The marking scheme is the part I care most about. Out of 20, built from five or six named must-hit moves with the marks for each, and for every move a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get it wrong. It must draw a clear line between a first and a 2:1 in terms of those moves: I want to see what a student who has grasped only that majority voting on each issue can be inconsistent will produce, and what a student who can say which condition each of the familiar procedures trades away — voting on the premises only, voting on the conclusion only, letting the chair decide outright, letting the chair reconcile the two results after the fact — and why, will produce instead. If the model answer can also make something of the group-agency angle, namely whether a body that can hold an inconsistent set of judgments is a candidate for collective responsibility at all and what its decision procedure would therefore have to look like, so much the better, but I would rather have that as a stretch move than as a requirement. Everything in British English, please, the exam question included.
