I'm a features journalist who writes about philosophy and the mind, and I'm midway through reporting an essay on the philosophy of perception. Over the past few weeks one of my sources — a philosopher I've interviewed twice, whose account I've since seen echoed in a well-reviewed trade book — gave me a working summary of disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. Before I commit any version of it to print, I want to know how much of it a specialist would stand behind.

His summary, as I have it in my notes:

"Disjunctivism is the view that a veridical perception and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are not two instances of a single mental kind — the experience as of a red tomato, say — differing only in their outer cause. They are fundamentally different kinds of occurrence, which is what earns the theory its name: the disjunction either I am perceiving a tomato or I am hallucinating one has two disjuncts that share no common experiential core. The payoff is that the argument from illusion — the classic argument that because veridical perception and hallucination can be indistinguishable, and because in hallucination we are not aware of any mind-independent object, we are never directly aware of mind-independent objects — simply collapses. If the two cases have no common core, the argument's crucial premise is unavailable, and direct realism about perception is saved. When you genuinely see the tomato, you are in direct contact with a mind-independent object; when you hallucinate, there is nothing you are aware of at all, only a false appearance. Disjunctivism thus gives us back the common-sense picture: in the good case, the world itself is a constituent of your experience."

I'll admit I found this persuasive, which is exactly why I want it audited before I lean on it. Go through it claim by claim and tell me what a card-carrying disjunctivist would accept as a fair statement of the position, what a careful critic would wince at as an overstatement, conflation, or borrowed term used loosely, and what is simply wrong. I'd also like to know whether the closing claim — that disjunctivism restores the common-sense picture and secures direct realism — actually follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is doing work the theory cannot support. I don't need a reading list or citations; I need to know what I can safely print and what I would be embarrassed to.
