I'm a graduate student splitting my time between a philosophy of mind seminar and a course on criminal procedure, and I keep running into the fact that the law takes a very definite view about where a person's memory stops and the rest of the world begins — a view that nobody in either field seems willing to state, let alone defend. Look at how three situations are handled. First, the passcode: several state courts have held that the privilege against compelled self-incrimination bars the state from forcing a suspect to produce or type the passcode to his own phone, because the act of producing it is testimonial — it tells the government that he knows the code, that the phone is his, that he can get into it. Yet the same courts have been nearly untroubled by orders to press a registered finger to the sensor or hold a face up to the camera, reasoning that a fingerprint or a face is a physical fact about the body, like a blood sample or a handwriting exemplar, and not a communication at all. Second, the container: the Supreme Court has said that a modern phone is not just another container to be opened and rummaged through, and that a warrant is generally required to search one — while the border-search exception lets agents go through a traveler's device at the border without a warrant, with the lower courts split only over how far a forensic examination may go. Third, the copy: when the same notes, contacts, and photographs also live on a company's servers, the government will often simply ask the company, and nobody is compelled to do anything.

What I want is not the constitutional question but the philosophy underneath it. Assume, for the sake of the exercise, that the extended mind thesis is at least defensible in something close to its original form — that a device which is reliably available, habitually relied on, and endorsed as a source of information is part of the cognitive system doing the person's remembering, no less than the hippocampus is. Under that assumption, what view of memory does the doctrine above presuppose? Is it a skull-bound view, on which whatever lies inside the body is mind and whatever lies outside is property? Or is the operative line not location but the difference between compelling a mental act and seizing a physical thing — in which case the passcode cases and the fingerprint cases are not in tension at all, and the practice is committed to far less than it appears to be? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then say which rival views it genuinely rules out, which it merely sits comfortably with, and where it is simply underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories of mind would license exactly the same decisions. I also want you to take seriously the thought that the doctrine is not one commitment but two that happen to coincide on the cases courts have seen so far: the testimonial rationale is about compelled acts, the biometric rationale is about the surface of the body, and a phone that unlocks by proximity to a watch, or stores its own passcode in a manager app, pulls the two apart. Finally, if the extended mind thesis were treated as a premise rather than a curiosity, say concretely what would have to change: whether the passcode protection would extend to the device itself and to its cloud copy, whether the biometric rule would swallow the passcode rule, or whether the whole area would have to be reframed around the voluntariness of the act rather than the location of the information. Assume a reader who knows the extended mind literature and wants no bibliography; argue rather than cite, and flag uncertainty rather than invent a case.
