I'm designing a ninety-minute session for a graduate workshop on self-deception and motivated reasoning — philosophy students alongside clinical trainees — and I want the wrong kind of reasons problem to carry the analytic weight. My draft handout turns it into a sorting procedure. Every consideration bearing on a belief goes into one of three bins: considerations bearing on whether the thing believed is true; considerations that make it desirable to hold the belief; and considerations that make it desirable to be seen to hold it. The second and third bins hold the wrong kind of reasons, and the exercise is to screen them out by asking whether the consideration would still look good to you if you did not care whether the belief were true.

The case I want to build the session around is a patient in cardiac rehabilitation who is told that a confident expectation of recovery improves recovery. Her reason to believe she will recover looks like a textbook wrong-kind reason, so the belief looks unjustified, and the clinical moral is that staff should notice this and steer her away from it.

I have a nagging sense that I am running two questions together. If the wrong kind of reasons are just the considerations that ought not to move you, the problem is evidentialism in other words, and the three-bin exercise is only a vivid way of teaching it. But I am told the problem is really about something else — something to do with how reasons for an attitude relate to the attitude they are reasons for. Which is it? And if it is the second, what exactly is the problem a problem for, and where does my third bin belong if not with the second?

Keep the reply under 600 words. If my framing does not survive contact with the literature, I would rather hear that than have you answer inside it; and tell me which consideration would most likely force you to revise your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
