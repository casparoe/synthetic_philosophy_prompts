I got into a long, slightly wine-fueled argument at a wedding last month with a cousin who does cross-cultural psychology, and it's been nagging at me ever since. She works with big cross-national survey datasets and talks happily about "national character" — the idea that populations have stable, measurable, somewhat distinctive psychological profiles — while I'm the kind of person who twitches whenever anyone says "the Germans are like X" or "Italians are like Y," because it smells like essentialism and I can never tell whether the claim is supposed to be about an average, a tendency, an essence, or just a stereotype in a lab coat. I'd like you to write the document we should have produced instead of shouting at each other: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a cautious defender of population-level psychological generalization (someone who thinks "national character" can be turned into a respectable, distributional, empirical construct, and that it's sometimes legitimate and useful to generalize) and a critic (someone who thinks the construct is incoherent, or trivially true once disambiguated, or epistemically and politically toxic, or all three). Please assume I don't know any of the relevant literature and don't give me a reading list or citations — I just want the substance, and I'd rather you not invent author names or studies to make it look scholarly.

Structure it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then a short closing section on which disputes look merely verbal. In the agreed-claims part I want things both sides could genuinely sign: about what a claim like "population P tends to be more X than population Q" could even mean; about within-population variance and overlap; about the difference between a claim about a distribution, a claim about a particular individual, and a claim about a cause; about the ecological fallacy and its mirror image; about when aggregating destroys the very signal you're after; and about the difference between descriptive, predictive, and explanatory uses of such generalizations — and which of those the two sides are actually fighting over. In the table, phrase each side's position in wording that side would accept, and mark which rows are about facts, which about method, and which about ethics or politics.

For the cruxes I'd especially like you to work out whether the defender is committed to a causal structure behind the regularities or only to a stable statistical pattern; whether the critic's objection is empirical (the generalizations don't survive out of sample), conceptual (the construct doesn't pick out anything), or moral (even true generalizations shouldn't be used this way) — and whether, if the moral objection is the real one, the empirical dispute becomes moot. For at least two cruxes, give a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. Something like: take a randomly drawn individual from P and a randomly drawn individual from Q — does the defender predict better-than-base-rate classification of that individual, or does she only predict that the group-level statistic is stable and meaningful? And: a generalization that is solid at the population level but gets used to decide something about one person, or a subpopulation inside P that looks more like Q than like the rest of P — who expects what, and why? Finally, if you think part of the fight is just about words (one side hearing "character" as a dispositional essence, the other as a bare summary statistic), say which part and whether disambiguating it dissolves anything, and be honest if you think there's no substantive disagreement left once everything is sorted out, or if it all collapses into a purely ethical question about when generalizing is permissible. Keep it concrete and don't use jargon for its own sake.
