I'm a features writer who covers psychiatry and the human sciences, and I'm finishing a long reported piece about the machinery by which a profession decides what counts as a disorder — who gets to add a category, who gets to strike one, and what happens to the people who spend years inside the categories in the meantime. The piece keeps circling an idea I find genuinely strange: that classifying people can change them, and that the changed people can come back and change the category, so that a diagnosis is never simply a name for something that was already sitting there waiting to be named. As a companion to the article I want to build an interactive quiz that tells readers which theory of psychiatric classification they actually hold. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science with real clinical literacy, someone comfortable moving between the metaphysics of kinds and the ordinary business of a diagnostic interview, and to work carefully rather than quickly: open with a short brainstorming section in which you map the full space of positions and the cases that separate them, then build the quiz out of that space rather than out of a list of camps.

The quiz should be nine or ten questions, each a concrete case rather than an abstract question — a vignette, a historical episode, a pair of clinicians disagreeing — with two or three answer options apiece. No item may simply ask whether mental illness is socially constructed, whether the categories are valid, or which school the reader belongs to; each must be answerable by someone who has never read a word of the literature, and each must sort readers by what they say about the case. Please include, among others: a category that was once official and was struck from the manual after a vote of the profession's governing body rather than after any discovery about its biology; a dissociative condition whose diagnosed prevalence rose sharply, by most accounts, in the years after a best-selling book and film dramatised it, and which many clinics now see far less often; a nineteenth-century American physician's category that treated an enslaved person's flight from captivity as a symptom of disease; a condition whose diagnosed population has organised politically and now insists the category names a minority identity rather than a defect; two clinicians who agree entirely about the symptoms in front of them and disagree about whether to write the diagnosis down, because they disagree about whether the patient is better off carrying it; and a hypothetical clinic in a country where the diagnosis is rare, whose patients show the weight loss the manual describes but none of the body-image fear its criteria require. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto a compact taxonomy of positions, and I want these to show up as possible outcomes whether or not they survive as separate leaves: that disorders are real dysfunctions of biological systems which culture can only obscure or reveal; that the categories are social products that bring their own subject matter into being; the middle view on which a real dysfunction exists but the loop between category and classified shapes its boundaries, its symptoms, and who ends up inside it; the pragmatic view that a category is a tool and the only question worth asking is whether it helps; the view that the classified community rather than the profession has standing to say what its category means; the view that the categories are the wrong shape altogether and the real phenomena are dimensions or symptom networks; and the institutional view that what gets diagnosed tracks what someone is willing to pay for, insure, or exclude. Where a reader's answers point to more than one outcome, the key should say so instead of forcing a tie, and I'd like a note on any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, with a one-line verdict on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two constraints. First, don't attribute any position to a named author and don't mention books, papers, or dates — I can't check any of that, and I don't want the quiz to become a who-said-what exercise; coin plain descriptive labels where you need them, and if a familiar label fits, use it without worrying about its provenance. Second, write for a general but intelligent readership: the questions should read like cases a curious non-specialist can answer in ten seconds, and each result should carry a short, fair-minded paragraph saying what someone who landed there believes, what it commits them to, and the strongest objection to it. Finish by re-reading your own key against your own questions and telling me in a few lines which outcome is doing the least work — the one almost no answer pattern lands on — since I would rather cut it than publish a result nobody can reach.
