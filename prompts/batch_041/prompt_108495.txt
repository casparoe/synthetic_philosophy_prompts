I'm in a small reading group that has been working through Daniel Dennett's Kinds of Minds alongside a graduate seminar on the evolution of cognition, and I've been asked to circulate a short draft for Thursday's session. What follows is the gist of it rather than the finished thing, because what I actually want is a critique of the argument before I commit to writing it out properly. The draft takes the familiar four-step taxonomy — Darwinian creatures, whose competences are fixed by selection on the lineage; Skinnerian creatures, which can also learn within their own lifetime by trial and error; Popperian creatures, which run the trial and error offline, inside their heads, so that it is the hypotheses that die rather than them; and Gregorian creatures, which borrow the thinking tools of culture, from arithmetic to double-entry bookkeeping — and argues that it is normally read as a single ladder of increasing cognitive power, ranked by how cheaply competence is bought, when it is really the projection of two independent questions onto one axis.

The first question is where the variation-and-selection cycle runs: in the lineage, in the individual's behaviour, in the individual's imagination, or in the cultural store of tools. The second question, which I claim the ladder has no room for, is provenance: whether the cycle acting on this creature was authored by the creature or installed by someone else, frequently for someone else's purposes. Cross the two and you get four boxes, one of which — competence that is Popperian in origin, Skinnerian in execution, and Gregorian in provenance — is where I think most human cognitive achievement actually lives. Three cases are supposed to do the work. The clicker-trained dog: the timing contingency it acquires is the residue of a trainer's deliberate hypothesising, but the dog runs it as plain operant conditioning and could never have arrived at it by its own trial and error. The person running scenarios in a spreadsheet: nominally Popperian, since the trials happen in silico and nobody gets hurt, but the model's assumptions are invisible to the user, whose trust in it is closer to conditioning than to testing. And the well-drilled habit of checking base rates, which began life as a deliberate and inspectable procedure and has been compiled down into something automatic and unexamined — traffic moving down the ladder rather than up it. If that is right, the taxonomy is not a ranking of organisms at all: a single organism hosts all four mechanisms at once, and the interesting question becomes which of them is currently doing the work. Two members of the group are biologists and will pounce on anything sloppy about selection, so I'd rather hear the objections now than on Thursday.

What I want from you is a referee's report rather than a rewrite, and I'd like it to be specific enough that I can act on it. First, is my reading fair — does Dennett already treat the four kinds as nested mechanisms that a single creature can possess simultaneously, in which case my "second axis" may be doing less work than I think? Second, is provenance really independent of the first question, or does it collapse into it, since on some views a Popperian creature is just a Darwinian process that has been moved indoors? Third, where does the two-axis picture break — for instance, is the self-authored half of the provenance axis ever non-empty for a creature that thinks in a public language, or is every human case of competence borrowed in the relevant sense? Fourth, what is the strongest objection to the base-rate example, and does any of the three cases fail to sit in the box I've put it in? Please don't send me references, a reading list, or who has said what; I'm not trying to find out whether someone has already published this, I'm trying to find out whether the carving is any good and where its edges are ragged. End your reply with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" and, on that line, one of the labels keep, revise, or rebuild, followed by the single change that would most improve the draft.
