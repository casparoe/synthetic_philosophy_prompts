I teach philosophy of science, and I am trying to locate, precisely, where my disagreement with a colleague in economic methodology actually lives. I want your credence, not a survey of the literature.

Take the following thesis, which I state in my own words. The original formulation is Friedman's and the label is Samuelson's, but nothing in what follows turns on the attribution. (T) A model is to be appraised solely by the accuracy of its predictions; the truth or falsity of its assumptions is epistemically irrelevant to that appraisal, so a known-false assumption is by itself no reason to discount the model's predictions or to doubt the reality of any mechanism the model posits. Add the companion claim (S): the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions, so unrealism is a mark of theoretical achievement rather than a defect.

I put T at 0.35 and S at 0.2. My colleague puts T at 0.7 and S at 0.5. What I want from you:

1. Your own credence in T and in S.

2. The decomposition. I take the number for T to be driven by at least the following sub-questions, but substitute your own if you think mine are the wrong ones: (a) whether model assumptions are truth-apt at all, or whether the formalism makes contact with the world only through application or bridge principles; (b) whether predictive success is evidence that a model's posited mechanism is real, or only that its input–output relation is reliable; (c) whether the thesis is plausible only for assumptions that are false but approximately true in the intended domain — that is, whether it trades on conflating idealization with outright falsehood; (d) whether we can coherently explain a model's failures by citing its assumptions while denying that those same assumptions bear on its successes. Give a number and a line of justification for each, then say how they combine into your figure for T.

3. The argument I keep coming back to, and cannot decide how much weight to give it: we routinely attribute a model's failures to its assumptions. If assumptions can explain failure, can they be evidentially inert with respect to success? Tell me whether this moves your number and by how much.

4. Which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence or argument would move you most.

5. Where my colleague's 0.7 and my 0.35 most likely diverge — in which sub-question, and why the numbers there could differ that much.

Assume I already know the standard positions. I want the decomposition and the crux, and I have no use for citations.
