A physicist friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about whether the Church–Turing thesis is a theorem, a conjecture, or a definition wearing a lab coat, and whether some future physics could falsify it; he holds that any new machine only tells us which physical systems happen to be Turing-equivalent, and I came away convinced he was quietly running together at least three different claims. The argument matters to me professionally. I sit on the review panel for an unconventional computing programme, and we get perhaps a dozen proposals a year claiming to compute something a Turing machine cannot: analog machines with real-valued registers, quantum architectures, neuromorphic and optical designs, occasionally something invoking relativistic or exotic physics. The panel has no policy for these, so they get judged by whoever is least tired that afternoon, and a fund or a reject is a real decision for a postdoc's career. I write the assessment sheet the chair signs, and the other panel members are not mathematicians.

Please turn the distinctions that actually matter here into a decision procedure I can run on a proposal in an afternoon, without needing the authors' mathematics to be checkable by me. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it hands the judgment to a person because the question is one of physics we don't have or of taste rather than fact. It has to keep separate the things my friend kept merging: what is computable versus how efficiently, the thesis about effective calculability versus the claim about our actual physics, and the difference between showing the thesis is false and showing it does not apply to the machine in front of me. Flag the moves that look like cheating but aren't, and the ones that look like physics but are really just an unearned idealisation.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, an analog device whose registers hold arbitrary real numbers to infinite precision and can test them for exact equality with zero, argued to decide a set no Turing machine decides. Second, a quantum architecture claimed to surpass the thesis because it evaluates exponentially many computational paths at once. Third, a scheme that gets its extra power by completing infinitely many steps in finite time, or by requiring a measurement of unlimited precision, and that argues this is permitted by physics in principle. At least one of your three verdicts should be one I will not enjoy reading, and you should be honest about the points where the procedure is only my prior that the thesis will survive, dressed up as a test.

Lead with the procedure itself, then the three worked cases, then a short closing section on what the procedure cannot settle. Keep it under 1,000 words.
