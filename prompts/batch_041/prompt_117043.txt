I have just been appointed to the ethics committee that reviews end-of-life decisions at the hospital where I work, and nearly every case turns on the line between doing something that ends a patient's life and allowing the patient to die. The committee invokes that line constantly, but no one can say what it tracks, and the cases we actually see do not resemble the ones in the textbooks. Before I vote on another one, I want the specification rather than another theory: what must any adequate account of the doing/allowing distinction deliver, given that we need it to justify decisions we are accountable for?

Lay out the desiderata. Which cases must the account get right — the pair in which one man holds a child under water while another watches the child drown; withdrawing a ventilator versus withholding it in the first place; disconnecting a ventilator versus administering a lethal injection; cases in which the only difference is a device's default setting, such as a pump that must be actively re-programmed to keep delivering a drug; and cases in which the act is carried out in real time by an automated system that a clinician configured weeks earlier? Which distinctions must it preserve — between doing and allowing, intending and foreseeing, acting and omitting, and between the causal and the moral senses of causing? And what must it be consistent with — the law's act/omission doctrine, the asymmetry in how ordinary people judge the two kinds of case, and the fact that many clinical guidelines treat withholding and withdrawing treatment as morally equivalent while patients and families do not?

Give the case or consideration that motivates each requirement, mark which of them are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp, and say whether some subset cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most need, since I suspect the committee wants it both ways. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the accounts usually on offer.
