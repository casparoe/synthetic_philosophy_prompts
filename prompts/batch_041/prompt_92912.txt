I manage a small regional delivery fleet — fourteen vans out of a depot on the edge of town, mostly grocery and pharmacy routes — and I have been handed a decision I cannot get straight in my head. Two of my drivers, Ray and Tom, have worked the same routes for years and carpool in together. Both of them, it turns out, have been routinely skipping the pre-trip brake check: signing the sheet without walking the van, which is exactly the kind of shortcut every depot manager half-knows is happening and half-pretends is not. Three weeks ago Ray's van lost its brakes in the yard, took out the gate, and crumpled the front end; nobody was hurt, but the post-incident inspection turned up the unsigned check sheet, and now the company wants a disciplinary hearing and a formal warning on his record. Tom's vans have never had a problem, and until he walked into my office last week nobody had any reason to look at his sheets at all. He came in to tell me, unprompted, that he does the same thing Ray does, that he has done it for years, and that it would be a disgrace for Ray to take the fall for a habit the two of them share — then asked me not to repeat it, which leaves me holding a fact I am not supposed to know while the union rep circles the hearing.

What I cannot sort out is whether my discomfort is with the company's process or with something underneath it. Our operations director says the accident is the only reason we found out about Ray at all, and that if we stop reacting to outcomes we will never catch anything — which sounds right until I remember that the mechanic told me, off the record, that the brake line had been failing for weeks and no walkaround check would have caught it, so the one piece of evidence everyone is treating as proof that the shortcut is dangerous probably is not proof of anything. My brother-in-law, who reads a good deal of philosophy, says the whole business is simple: judge the choice, not the result, and let Ray off. That also sounds right until I notice that Tom's and Ray's carefulness, or lack of it, is itself something they picked up from trainers and schedules that I set, and if I follow that thought all the way down I seem to end up with nobody ever being responsible for anything, which cannot be right either. The hearing is Friday. What I want from you is not a verdict on Ray but a way of thinking about this that holds up: why the two of them are being treated so differently when they did the same thing, whether there is any honest defense of a system that sorts people by what happened rather than by what they chose, what to make of the fact that the outcome here seems to have been caused by something else entirely, and what I should actually say on Friday.
