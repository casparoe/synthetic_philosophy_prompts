I hold the following position about the causal exclusion argument. The argument is usually taken to show that if mental properties are distinct from physical properties and are not reducible to them, then mental properties are causally excluded by the physical properties that realize them; mental causation is therefore either identical with physical causation or epiphenomenal. I claim this conclusion depends on a production picture of causation, on which causes compete for a single sufficient causal role. Under a difference-making picture—where X causes Y roughly when an intervention on X would change Y, holding fixed the right background conditions—mental properties can be genuine difference-makers even though every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause. The physical domain can be closed under production without mental properties being screened off as difference-makers. I also claim the exclusion argument is best reconstructed as a dilemma about explanatory exclusion rather than causal exclusion: it conflates what makes an effect happen with what makes a difference to how it happens. So the argument threatens at most a certain metaphysical picture of mental causation, not mental causation itself.
