A friend of mine is teaching a seminar on empire and the Scottish Enlightenment and wants to use Kipling's The Man Who Would Be King — the story or the Huston film, whichever — but read it through Adam Smith's actual arguments about colonies and empire rather than the cartoon Smith of invisible hands. So: Smith argues in The Wealth of Nations that the American colonies grew so fast because land was cheap and wages were high, not because the mother country did anything for them; that the only particular advantage Britain gets from its colonies is the monopoly of their trade, which is a dead weight on everybody else; that the whole imperial project amuses the public with the imagination of a vast empire while costing far more to defend than it ever brings in; and that the East India Company turned a trading license into sovereignty and plundered Bengal along the way. Read Dravot and Carnehan as the kind of characters that analysis predicts — empire as a private venture, legitimacy as a fiction that holds right up until the god bleeds. Where does the story confirm Smith, where does it romanticize exactly what he was coldest about, and where does his economic frame miss what Kipling is actually doing? Faithful in both directions please — dont reduce the story to an illustration of Smith, and dont sand Smith down into a free-trade saint. Finish with what the story sees about empire that Smith's arguments dont.
