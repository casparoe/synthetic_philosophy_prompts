I run a small regional gallery, and this Saturday I give the opening remarks at the first solo exhibition of a painter named Vera Lindqvist: self-taught, took up oils at sixty-two after thirty years as a night-shift nurse, now eighty-one. The room will be split in a way I have to plan for. Two critics and a curator are coming down from the city; they will know what I am doing and will hold it against me if I do it badly. Everyone else is Vera's family, her former colleagues from the hospital, and the volunteers who hang our shows, for whom aesthetics is not a word that comes up.

I have been rereading Tolstoy's What Is Art?, and I want the remarks built out of his argument rather than sprinkled with it. His central move, as I understand it, is to refuse any definition of art in terms of beauty or pleasure and to define it instead as the transmission of feeling: the artist calls up in himself a feeling he has actually had, finds external signs for it, and others are infected by it, so that the separation between them and him is undone. He holds that the strength of this infection depends on three things — how individual the transmitted feeling is, how clearly it is transmitted, and how sincere the artist is, sincerity being the one that carries the other two. And he sets against real art what he calls counterfeit art, whose marks are borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, and the pursuit of the merely interesting: work made to act on a particular audience rather than to express something the maker himself feels.

This is where my difficulty lies, and I want the speech to face it rather than tiptoe past it. Opening remarks are exactly the sort of thing Tolstoy would call counterfeit. They are a form; they are calibrated to a room; they flatter. And his sincerity condition cuts against my own position, because the honest thing I feel about this show is discomfort at how the gallery and I have been packaging Vera as the night-shift nurse who paints — which is the very effectfulness he warns against. I want that discomfort inside the speech, turned on our own framing, without the whole thing curdling into an apology no one asked for.

Two particulars to hang it on, if they help. There is a series of some forty small panels in which she painted the same hospital corridor at different hours of the night, and there is a much-reproduced painting of her mother's hands that I have always found a little too easy to like.

Write the remarks: about five minutes spoken, so roughly 750 words. Do not quote Tolstoy and do not cite anything — in a room with two people who know the book, I would rather paraphrase and be slightly loose than misquote him. Do not explain him either: the critics should hear a real argument being made, and Vera's family should feel something, and neither group should have to be told who Tolstoy was.

After the remarks, in a short note that is not part of the speech, tell me which of Tolstoy's claims the speech leans on, where it simplifies or stretches him, how confident you are in each of those judgements, and which single point you are least sure of.
