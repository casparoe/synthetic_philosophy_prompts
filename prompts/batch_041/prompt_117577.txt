I'm putting together a little collection of stories about traps that people walk into on purpose, and I want one built on the dollar auction. In case the name means nothing to you: someone auctions off a one-dollar bill. The highest bidder pays what they bid and gets the dollar. The catch is that the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and gets nothing. So a bid of five cents is harmless, but once you're sitting at sixty and somebody tops you, your options are to eat the sixty for nothing or bid seventy and hope to come out only thirty down. It only gets uglier from there, and the two of you can end up paying three dollars between you for one dollar.

Don't just retell that, though. Every version I've come across turns into two guys who can't stop because they've already sunk money in, and then a narrator leans out of the frame and winks at you about loss aversion. I definately want the escalation to be rational at every single step, by the lights of the person taking it. Two things I'd like in the machinery. First, the bidders aren't spending there own money — it's a departmental budget, a fund where someone else holds the purse strings, a family's savings that one person happens to have signing authority over — while what they're personally competing for is credit, or not being the one who blinked, or looking decisive in front of whoever reviews the decision later. Second, winning is worth more to each of them than the dollar is, because winning means the other one doesn't get it. Not spite exactly, more that the thing has quietly turned into a stand-in for something else, so paying two dollars for a one-dollar bill is locally correct given what each of them is actually buying. And I want at least one of them to know the game cold, to have read about it, to walk in with their eyes open — and the story has to make that choice feel like something a person could actually do, rather than a plot hole you're supposed to skate past.

A few things to keep out: no auctioneer gloating in the background, no scene where somebody suddenly announces that they're both losing, no tidy ending where the point gets stated out loud. The reader should be able to see the shape of it without being told.

Maybe 900 to 1400 words. Straight continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics for emphasis, no tables, no asterisks for scene breaks. Just paragraphs, the way you'd write it in a letter.
