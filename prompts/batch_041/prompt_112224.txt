I am a novelist at work on a book set in a shrimp and salmon farm on an estuary, and the central character is a welfare officer who has to make decisions that will be rendered in close third person: whether to approve a new stunning protocol, whether to keep a broodstock line that seems to suffer from a condition no one can measure without killing the animal, whether the shrimp count as individuals at all. I want the moral texture of the book to be honest, so before I write another scene I am trying to get clear on the underlying theory. Please write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of welfare for farmed fish and crustaceans would have to meet, fixing the purpose as decision-making on a working farm under uncertainty rather than victory in a seminar. No reading list or citations, please; I need the specification itself.

Give me the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical findings it must not contradict, with the consideration behind each requirement. The cases should include at least these, which are the ones my novel keeps running into: a case in which a prawn avoids a noxious stimulus after one trial and guards a wound, though its nervous system is distributed and unlike a mammal's; a fish that shows the same behavioral response to a harmless disturbance and to a painful one, so that the theory must say what would count as evidence either way; a hatchery practice that improves survival for the many while causing a lasting harm to a few; a stunning method that is reliable for one species and unreliable for another; a welfare indicator that is easy to measure, such as fin condition or growth rate, and another that is hard to measure but seems more directly tied to suffering; and a larval stage in which most individuals die before any welfare assessment is possible. The theory also has to handle the scale of aquaculture, where individual monitoring is impossible, and the fact that the animals are often invertebrate or ectothermic and so do not fit the model of welfare built for mammals and birds. It must respect distinctions such as welfare versus health, pain versus nociception, sentience versus the capacity to suffer, individual versus population welfare, and uncertainty versus ignorance.

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I am especially interested in tensions between a requirement that welfare judgments track sentience and a requirement that they be action-guiding under uncertainty, between a requirement of species-neutral moral consideration and a requirement to respect species-specific natures, and between a requirement to avoid anthropomorphism and a requirement not to set the bar for evidence so high that it becomes a license for whatever is convenient. Finally, give me a brief scorecard of how the main families of view do against the list -- hedonistic accounts, preference-satisfaction accounts, capacities-based accounts, teleological or functioning-based accounts, and relational or care-based accounts -- but keep the weight on the requirements themselves, because that is what I will use to judge whether a line of dialogue is cheating.
