Problem (20 points). Throughout, A is some empirical proposition and P_t is an agent's credence function at time t. The reflection principle, in the form we have been calling van Fraassen's, says: if at t you are certain that your credence at a later time t' in A will be x, then your credence at t in A should be x; and, more generally, P_t(A | P_t'(A) = x) = x for t' later than t.

(a) (4 points) State both the simple form and the general conditional form of the principle, and say what distinguishes them.

(b) (5 points) Give the main motivation for treating reflection as a requirement of rationality.

(c) (6 points) Consider the following case. Nia is a participant in a study on sleep and judgment. She knows that if she sleeps fewer than five hours tonight, then tomorrow morning she will be 0.75 confident that the Redwood strain is the more virulent of two fungal strains she is studying — not because poor sleep will give her any information about the strains, but because sleep deprivation is known to inflate people's confidence in whatever hypothesis they started with. She knows that how well she sleeps tonight is causally unrelated to which strain is more virulent. She has already arranged for a fire alarm to go off at 4 a.m., so she is certain she will sleep fewer than five hours; she is certain she will be 0.75 tomorrow, and she also knows why. Right now, on the basis of her actual evidence about the strains, she is 0.5. What should her credence now be, and what does the reflection principle say it should be? Are these the same, and what follows for the principle?

(d) (5 points) Suppose we restrict reflection as follows: reflect on a future credence only when you now take the process that will produce it to be at least as reliable, about A, as your current process. Does this restriction save reflection from the case in (c), or does it leave the principle with little content? Defend your answer.

My classmate's answer, transcribed:

(a) The simple form is the certainty case: if I am certain that tomorrow I will be certain that A, then I should be certain that A now. The general conditional form is P_t(A | P_t'(A) = x) = x, which says that conditional on my later credence being x, my present credence should be x. The general form is stronger in that it applies to every value of x, not only to certainty, and it is the general form that carries the real content; the simple form is its limiting case.

(b) The motivation is that my future self will normally be better placed than my present self, since she will have had more time and more evidence. To fail to reflect is to be at odds with a better-informed version of myself, and the Dutch-book argument shows that an agent who violates reflection can be exploited by a bookie who offers bets at the two times. The principle therefore belongs to the family of deference principles generally — it tells me to let a more authoritative estimate of A govern my credence. In fact the future credence functions for me much as a chance does: my later credence of 0.75 in A is, from where I stand now, the best estimate of A available, so reflecting on it is just an application of the Principal Principle, that one's credence in A should equal one's estimate of the chance of A.

(c) On her actual evidence Nia's credence should be 0.5: the fire alarm and her sleep are irrelevant to which strain is more virulent, so nothing about them bears on A. Reflection, however, says that since she is certain her later credence will be 0.75, her present credence should be 0.75. These are not the same, and the case is a counterexample to the unrestricted principle, because the future credence is not diagnostic of A — it is produced by a process she knows to be independent of A. So the principle as stated cannot be a requirement of rationality. Since she knows the process will push her estimate up without any basis, she should not simply remain at 0.5; she should set her credence somewhat below 0.5, so that when the known upward bias is added tomorrow the result lands closer to her evidence. Correcting in advance for a distortion she can foresee is the rational response, so her present credence should be lower than the 0.5 her evidence alone supports.

(d) The restriction does save the principle. If Nia does not now take her sleep-deprived process to be reliable about the strains, the restriction tells her not to reflect on the 0.75, and the counterexample is blocked. Nor is the restriction ad hoc: it is simply the familiar point that deference is owed only to a process one regards as trustworthy. It also does not leave reflection vacuous, since there will be many cases in which I regard my future process as more reliable — for instance, when I know I will by then have read a report I cannot read yet — and in those cases the principle still instructs me to adopt my future credence, which is a substantive instruction. It remains distinct from conditionalization: conditionalization tells me how to update on evidence I receive, whereas reflection constrains the relation between my credences at two times whether or not any evidence has arrived in between.
