I'm organizing a seminar on the normativity of attitudes, and before we adjudicate the competing solutions to the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem I want the specification they are all meant to satisfy. Please set out the desiderata that any adequate account of reasons for attitudes must meet, given that some considerations which plainly bear on whether an attitude is called for are nonetheless not the sort of consideration that makes it fitting. Treat as the data the familiar cases — the demon who threatens torture unless you admire him, the eccentric who offers a fortune for a belief you know to be false, the rival whose misfortune you are paid to welcome, the genuinely admirable person you are also paid to admire, the small reward offered for an attitude you already have ample reason to hold — and ask what a theory must do to accommodate them. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say why it is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. Make the requirements that pull against each other explicit: in particular, whether one can hold both that fittingness is what an attitude's reasons are reasons for and that considerations of the wrong kind are reasons for the attitude in any sense at all; whether the wrong-kind reason is best treated as no reason, as a reason of a different kind, or as a reason to manage the attitude rather than to have it; and whether this is one problem across belief, desire, emotion and intention or several distinct ones wearing a single name.

If some pair of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which pair, and say whether that is a refutation of the fitting-attitude programme or only of a careless statement of it. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise popular accounts, and which requirements you regard as non-negotiable as against which are matters of taste. I don't want a survey of who has said what, or a reading list; argue from the cases. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold or tables — the way you would write a long letter to a colleague.
