I've been helping my aunt run her garden centre for the last few summers and something about how she handles bugs has been nagging at me. She's a real convert to integrated pest management -- she has this rule of thumb she got from an extension-service pamphlet years ago, that you don't spray until the pest count crosses a threshold where the damage they'd do costs you more than the spray plus the labour of applying it. Below that line you leave them be; above it you nuke. She also mail-orders ladybirds and lacewing larvae to eat her aphids, and she's careful to spray in the evening so she doesn't take out the bees. What gets me is the inconsistency: she'll agonise for an hour over whether to set a glue trap for a mouse in the potting shed, and she cried when the neighbours' cat got into the bird feeder, but she'll drown a whole tray of slugs in beer or squish cabbage white caterpillars without a second thought, and the threshold rule means the caterpillars get to live exactly as long as they aren't costing her money. Same deal with the ladybirds -- she adores them, but only because they're on her side; if they ate her stock instead of the aphids she'd spray them too. And the law seems to back her up, since all the animal-cruelty stuff seems to be about vertebrates.

So here's what I want to figure out. What view of moral status, and of value more generally, does this whole way of operating actually presuppose? It seems like the "spray only when it pays" rule is literally economic, which makes me think it assumes pests have no standing of their own and that only the grower's losses count -- but maybe that's too quick, since she does spare the bees and the ladybirds, and I can't tell if that's because they have value in themselves or just because they're useful to her later. Is this one coherent theory or a couple of different ones glued together? Which rival views does it quietly rule out -- could a sentience-based ethic ever be bolted onto a threshold rule, or does taking insect pain seriously mean the whole economic-threshold logic has to go? I'd also like to know where the practice is actually underdetermined, i.e. what's really doing the work versus what she could keep under several different theories. She doesnt know any philosophy so keep it plain, and don't just tell me "it's anthropocentric" and stop there -- I want the implicit theory reconstructed properly and then stress-tested.

Bottom line: finish with one sentence, on its own line starting "Bottom line:", summarising the theory you think the practice presupposes and whether it holds together.
