I have long defended a view I will call inferential constitutivism about deduction. On this view, the justification of a deductive rule is not to be found in any further premise, however general; it consists in the rule's being constitutive of the practice of reasoning with the relevant expressions. Carroll's tortoise is therefore not a skeptical discovery about a missing premise but a reductio of the demand that rules be justified in the same way as premises. The tortoise can always ask us to add a conditional premise, and we can always refuse, because accepting a rule is not the same as accepting a proposition. What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. Begin with the strongest defeaters and work down to the merely awkward. I am especially interested in cases that put pressure on the idea that the rules are constitutive rather than optional: a connective whose introduction rule permits inferring it from anything and whose elimination rule permits inferring anything from it, so that the rules seem to define a vocabulary that destroys the very practice it is supposed to constitute; a community whose reasoning rejects transitivity or disjunctive syllogism yet seems internally coherent; a learner who does not yet accept modus ponens and cannot be brought to accept it by any argument, since every argument for it would already use it; and the possibility of rationally revising logic in the light of quantum phenomena or semantic paradox. For each, say whether it is a refutation, a challenge the view can absorb by qualification, or a mere curiosity. Then tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all or has been insulated by the move from propositional to practical justification. Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as a misunderstanding? Finally, if the view turns out to be compatible with any inferential practice whatsoever, is that a virtue or a fatal concession? I would find it most persuasive if you could show me a case in which the constitutive story cannot distinguish a genuine logical constant from a merely stipulated one without appealing to a prior notion of validity.
