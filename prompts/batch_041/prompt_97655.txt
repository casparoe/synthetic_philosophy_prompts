I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and I've been circling a piece about how juries, blue-ribbon commissions, and the rest of us turn a heap of conflicting testimony into a single story that feels inevitable in retrospect. Which keeps dragging me back to Kurosawa's Rashomon, the 1950 one, which I rewatched last weekend for the first time since college and haven't been able to shake. I want you to read it through the lens of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy — and I mean really put the film and the psychology in the same room, not just wave at the theme. The witnesses in the grove aren't forgetting anything; each one seems to be assembling a version of events that flatters them, and by the end the woodcutter's own little confession about the dagger makes you wonder whether his frame-story version was just as self-serving. So: is that hindsight bias, or is it motivated reasoning wearing hindsight bias's coat? The psych literature sometimes calls the core effect creeping determinism, the way an outcome comes to feel like it was always going to happen — but that's usually about one knower revising their sense of what was foreseeable, and Rashomon is about several people constructing whole narratives from scratch. I want a reading that says where the film genuinely dramatizes retrospective sense-making, where it's actually about something else (self-deception, honor, the sheer pleasure of a good story), and which specific scenes you'd point a skeptical reader to. Does the film's famous reputation as a parable about the impossibility of truth actually distort what's on screen, and does the narrative-fallacy framing explain more than the old relativism reading, or less?
