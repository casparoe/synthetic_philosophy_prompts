My friend and I ended up arguing about it for like an hour over dinner — she thinks the whole grue thing is basically a party trick and that Hume is the only real problem in the vicinity, whereas I think that badly misses what Goodman is actually doing — and ever since then I've been second-guessing how hard to come down on this particular student. Anyway, I was hoping you could grade it for me. Say out of 20, and give me the actual margin comments I'd write — what's right, what's wrong, and precisely where a claim goes off the rails. I may have to defend the grade if the prof or the student pushes back, so I need it point by point rather than a general impression of whether the answer is "good."

Here's the exam question: "Goodman invites us to consider the predicate grue: call an object grue if it is first examined before some future time t and is green, or if it is not examined before t and is blue. Suppose that, before t, we have examined a large number of emeralds and found every one of them green. (a) Explain why, on the ordinary instance-confirmation picture, this evidence confirms 'all emeralds are grue' just as strongly as it confirms 'all emeralds are green', and say what general lesson Goodman draws from this about confirmation. (b) A student in the back objects: 'the riddle dissolves as soon as you notice grue is a disjunctive, gerrymandered predicate — evidence just doesn't confirm disjunctive predicates.' Assess this objection. (c) Goodman's own answer appeals to entrenchment. What exactly is entrenchment, and does it give us a reason to project green rather than grue? (d) Finally, suppose a community had used grue (and its mate bleen) as its basic colour vocabulary for centuries, so that grue was the entrenched predicate and green was the odd one out. What does Goodman say happens to our projection here, and does it bother you?"

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) Before t, every emerald we've seen has been green, so every one of them also satisfies 'grue' (the first half of the disjunction). So the very same observations are positive instances of both hypotheses, and any account of confirmation that just counts positive instances — like Hempel's — has to say the two hypotheses are confirmed equally. Goodman's lesson is that confirmation can't be a purely formal or syntactic relation between evidence and hypothesis; the logic of the evidence alone can't tell the two apart. This is really just Hume's problem restated: Goodman is asking why we're justified in assuming the future will resemble the past.

(b) The objection fails. 'Green' can itself be defined disjunctively in terms of grue and bleen, so pointing out that grue is disjunctive gets you nowhere — whatever syntactic feature you pick out, green can be made to share it. The student has described a real feature of grue but it can't do the work being asked of it.

(c) Entrenchment is the track record a predicate builds up: green is entrenched because projections using it have paid off again and again, while grue has no such record of successful predictions, which is why we're right to project green. So entrenchment answers the riddle and shows that our inductive practice is justified after all — it's what Hume was looking for.

(d) Goodman would say the community's usage doesn't really change anything deep. Since green is the more natural predicate, projecting green is still the correct move even if the community's habits run the other way; entrenchment is just a sociological fact that happens to line up with the naturalness of green in our own case."
