I convene a small reading group that straddles archaeology, economic history, and the philosophy of the social sciences, and next month's theme is collapse — the Late Bronze Age breakdown of the eastern Mediterranean palace systems, the end of the Classic Maya lowland kingdoms, the Easter Island story and the revisionists who have taken it apart, and the underlying question of whether any of these are societies failing or only societies changing. What I want from you is not a survey of the evidence but an exercise in intellectual prediction. Take Ibn Khaldun, the fourteenth-century North African historian whose Muqaddimah gives us asabiyyah — group solidarity, purest among pastoral peoples and thinning as a society turns urban and luxurious — together with a theory in which dynasties rise on the strength of that solidarity and rot from within along a periphery-to-centre arc, until a fresher group from the margins displaces them, a rhythm he takes to run about three generations. Predict, as tightly as you can, what he would have made of our debate: not the bland observation that he would have noticed elites, but the verdict his own system forces on him.

Work through four things. First, whether his framework has any room for environmental or demographic accident as a cause of decline, or whether he is committed to explaining collapse wholly through social and political mechanisms — solidarity, luxury, taxation, the corruption of the ruling house. Second, what he would say about the cases where the political superstructure falls while the population and its ordinary life continue, as with the Maya after the lowland kingdoms disintegrate and power shifts northward, or where what looks like a death is better read as the reorganisation of a densely connected network, as in the Late Bronze Age. Third, what he would do with Easter Island, which has no dynasty worth the name and sits at a scale his theory was never built for, and whether that is a fatal counterexample or something he could absorb. Fourth, come down on the question the group keeps circling: whether collapse names anything real, or is only our word for the ordinary turnover of regimes that he would have taken as the normal pulse of history. Reason from what he actually held; do not hand me a bibliography or a list of readings; and where you must guess, mark the guess as a guess.

One caution before you begin. I have a nagging suspicion that the whole exercise may be a category error — that his unit of analysis is the dynasty and the ruling group, not the society or civilisation the modern debate takes for granted, so that asking him about the collapse of a civilisation may be asking him a question his vocabulary cannot pose. If that is right, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a tidy prediction to flatter the question; if it is wrong, tell me why. Either way, close with a single final line beginning 'Verdict:' and then a short phrase naming, in a few words at most, the stance you predict he would take on whether societies collapse or merely change.
