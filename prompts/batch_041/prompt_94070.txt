Two colleagues in my department argued at length about a decision a mutual friend made, and I couldn't referee it. The friend, a graduate student, declined a side project with roughly a one-in-a-million chance of producing a transformative result — say, a decade's worth of progress for the whole subfield — because it would cost her six months of her own time. Dara said declining was a plain error: the project's expected value massively exceeded its cost, the downside was bounded and small, and refusing a positive-expected-value gamble is just innumeracy dressed up as prudence. Nikolai said the principle Dara was invoking proves far too much: drop the cap on payoffs and, for any probability however tiny, there is a payoff large enough that expected value maximization compels you to take the bet — a one-in-10^100 chance of a 10^110 payoff would bind you, and a life spent taking such bets becomes indistinguishable from acting at random. Dara replied that the absurd cases cannot arise, because payoffs are bounded by what is physically possible, or else we bound utility, and either fix dissolves the absurdity. Nikolai answered that both fixes abandon expected value maximization rather than repair it, and that in the extreme cases the "probability" isn't a genuine credence at all — it is swamped by the chance that the model generating it is simply wrong, so the numbers being multiplied were never meaningful to begin with. Dara retorted that this objection only bites when the probability rests on a fragile theory, whereas here the estimate is ordinary and robust, and that in any case nobody on Nikolai's side has ever given a principled answer to "which tiny probabilities may I ignore?" Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right about which specific claim, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct — and identify what each of them is missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
