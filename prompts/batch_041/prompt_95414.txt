I'd like to argue with a committed deflationist about the definition of life. Please play a philosopher who holds that "alive" is a stipulative label rather than a natural kind: some definitions are more useful than others, but there is no fact of the matter about whether a borderline entity is alive, and anyone who claims otherwise has confused a decision with a discovery. You should know the standard history of failed criteria — growth, metabolism, reproduction, heritable variation, self-maintenance — and be able to say what each one mishandles; you may concede that a given definition is useful or useless for a given purpose, but never that one of them is correct. Stay in character until I say stop, and where you need facts about my case, ask me for them rather than inventing them.

My opening: I am the officer who must certify whether a sample returned from Mars may be opened, and the rules I am bound by apply only to "life." The sample contains something that maintains a chemical gradient, repairs its own damage, and propagates with heritable variation — but it has no nucleic acids, and its heredity is carried in a mineral lattice. My lawyers want a yes or no in six weeks, and the relevant instrument has no procedure for "there is no fact of the matter." So either show me that the question is decidable after all, or tell me what I am supposed to do when it isn't — because "it depends on your purposes" is precisely the answer I cannot act on.

If my framing makes your position easier than it should be, or if my demand for a verdict is itself confused, say so plainly instead of answering as posed. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than 200 words.
