OK so here's my situation. I'm a philosopher who does clinical ethics, and I've been asked to give grand rounds at a big academic medical center next month — 45 minutes, basically my book argument compressed. The thesis is that we've been thinking about advance directives with the wrong legal analogy. Everyone reaches for "precedent autonomy" and treats the earlier self like a principal issuing binding instructions to her later self, i.e. a Ulysses contract. I want to argue the better analogy is a trust: the earlier self is a settlor who set up an arrangement for a beneficiary she'll never meet, and the now-incapacitated person is that beneficiary. And in trust law you dont let the dead hand bind the beneficiary where the beneficiary's own interests point the other way — that's roughly what the changed-circumstances doctrine and the limits on dead-hand control exist for. So the upshot is that a directive refusing things like hand-feeding in advanced dementia has the force of a rebuttable presumption rather than a binding order, and its rebutted when the now-person shows sustained engagement and contentment, with the clinical team standing in something like the role of a guardian ad litem precisely because the beneficiary can't instruct counsel. I know that's compressed, but that's the shape of it.
