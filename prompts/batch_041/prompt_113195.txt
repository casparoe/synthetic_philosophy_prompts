so my friend Nell and I got into a two hour arguement last night about whether your credences have to be single numbers, and she kept coming back to one picture that I couldn't knock down even though I'm pretty sure it's doing something sneaky under the hood. I want you to audit it properly — take it apart, don't just tell me both sides have a point.

Her picture: an imprecise credence (instead of saying 'my credence is 0.7' I say 'somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8') is just a committee. There's a bunch of little people inside you, each one with a perfectly precise credence of their own, and what you call your imprecise credence is just the spread of their opinions. Nothing mysterious going on: a committee disagrees, so the committee's opinion is a range, and a range is no less rational than a point. She says that's basically how the imprecise probability people think about it (she called it a 'credal committee', or maybe I'm mangling the term), and she uses it to get two things at once:

1) imprecise credences are legitimate and not a failure of rationality — nobody calls a committee irrational just because its members disagree with each other;
2) but they're also nothing deep — you can always force the committee to vote, so a precise credence is always available, and imprecision is basically a fancy way of not having decided yet.

I think 2 doesn't follow from the picture and I'm not sure 1 does either, but I couldn't say why, so:

First give me the correspondence table. What plays the role of the members, the chair, the agenda, the voting rule, the minutes, the quorum, and the outsider who just wants to know what the committee thinks? Be explicit about which bit of the machinery is supposed to map onto which bit of the story.

Then tell me where it breaks — and I want the disanalogies that actually matter for her two conclusions, not a list of every way a metaphor is imperfect. Specifically:

- Real committee members disagree because they have different evidence and different interests. Are the members of my credal set like that, or are they all just me with the same evidence? If it's the latter, what is the 'disagreement' even supposed to be?
- I have a vague sense that the set of probabilities 'compatible with your evidence' is a big sloppy set that includes functions no sane person would hold — like the committee is stuffed with cranks nobody would ever appoint. Does that hurt the 'a committee is rational' line?
- A committee with no procedure doesn't have an opinion, it just has a membership list. If the picture needs a voting rule and the imprecise view deliberately refuses to supply one, is the picture borrowing something the view doesn't have? And isn't there a whole mess about no voting rule being fair to everyone (the thing about aggregating preferences being impossible in general)? Does that mess carry over here?
- Her conclusion 2 says you can always force a vote. But the choice of voting rule is exactly the thing the imprecise person says is arbitrary, so does forcing a vote give you a justified credence or just a decided one? And separately: if every member is precise, hasn't she just told me my whole state is a set of precise things, i.e. imprecision is never fundamental? Is that a problem for someone who thinks imprecision is a genuine feature of rationality rather than a bookkeeping device?
- If each member is individually coherent, is the committee? Can you dutch book the committee even though you can't dutch book any of its members? And is there a result that learning something irrelevant can make a committee more divided rather than less — I have a feeling imprecision can get worse when you get more information, but I might be making that up.

Then the verdict: does conclusion 1 survive the picture, does conclusion 2, does either? And if the committee is the wrong picture, what's a better one? I keep wanting to say it's the difference between a blurry photo of a sharp object and a photo of an object that's blurry, but I don't know which one credal imprecision is supposed to be, so tell me.
