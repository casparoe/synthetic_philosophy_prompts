Write a poem in three numbered movements, one for each of the three ways Nietzsche says a person or a culture can relate to its past — the monumental, the antiquarian and the critical — as set out in the second of his Untimely Meditations (1874), the essay on the advantage and disadvantage of history for life. I want the poem to be recognisable to someone who knows the essay without ever naming the three modes inside the poem. Each movement should be spoken in the first person by the kind of person Nietzsche attaches to that mode, and should carry both that mode's characteristic gift and its characteristic disease. The monumental voice looks to the great exemplars of the past and is fired to act, but it is also the voice that flattens context, drifts toward fanaticism, and can be turned by the powerful into a weapon against the present. The antiquarian voice loves and preserves what it has inherited, and its disease is that it can only preserve and never create — its reverence can mummify the very life it means to keep. The critical voice suffers under the weight of the past, wants to drag it before a tribunal and be free of it, but the movement must turn on itself when it discovers that the judge is also an heir, that the tribunal it longs to sit on cannot be outside the inheritance it is judging.

Let the form do the work. Use a single refrain that closes each of the three movements, identical in wording except for its last word, which changes to name what that mode demands of the past — something in the spirit of a model, a root, a verdict, though you should find your own. The three movements should not be interchangeable: give them different stanza shapes or line-lengths, so that the drive of the first, the closedness of the second and the breakage of the third are audible in the verse itself. End with a short coda in which the three versions of the refrain are brought together, their differing final words held in unresolved tension. The poem should not adjudicate between the three, because Nietzsche's own answer is that they must be kept in balance and judged only by whether they serve life.

Aim for something between thirty and fifty lines, in a controlled and formal register. It should be accurate enough that a reader of the essay would find all three voices right, and good enough as a poem that someone who has never read it would still want to read it twice.
