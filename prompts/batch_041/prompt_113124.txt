I'm a philosophy instructor, and I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level seminar on population ethics. The topic has become unexpectedly personal for me: my partner and I just had our first child, and the experience has left me unable to teach the material in the abstract way I used to. I keep noticing that the standard lecture — "Parfit's spectrum, hence the Repugnant Conclusion, now evaluate some escapes" — lets students pass by reciting the shape of the argument without ever feeling the specific joint where it hurts.

So I want to replace that question with something sharper. I've designed a case for the exam and I want you to build the question around it. Here is the case, which I'll state in the exam verbatim:

Consider a sequence of outcomes, A through E. A contains a small population, all of whom have very high welfare. Each step from one outcome to the next increases the population and lowers the average welfare of everyone in it, but every added life is still clearly worth living, and no existing person is made worse off by the addition — the added people live at a time and place wholly separate from the others, so the addition is a pure addition in the strict sense. By E we have a very large population of lives that are only barely worth living. Assume, for the question, that the betterness relation over outcomes is transitive and that if outcome X is better than Y and Y is better than Z then X is better than Z.

Now, instead of asking students to run the standard argument to its conclusion, I want the question to force them to locate the exact premise that has to give if we refuse to accept that E is better than A, and then to assess a specific repair I've invented for the purposes of the exam, which I'm calling the "conditional addition" view: adding a life worth living improves an outcome only if the addition does not lower the average welfare of the resulting population below some fixed level L. I want students to see that this repair, as stated, does not actually block the slide — it just relocates it — and to work out why. The crux is whether the "conditional" clause is doing any independent work once you also grant the pure-addition premise and transitivity, or whether it is a fifth wheel that generates a second, distinct repugnance of its own. I'd like the question to make a strong student distinguish "blocking the slide by denying pure addition" from "blocking it by denying transitivity" from "blocking it by denying that E is better than A while keeping the rest," and to say which of these the conditional-addition view is actually doing.
