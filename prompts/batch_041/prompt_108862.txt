I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy degree, so excuse the loose language. I read Gödel, Escher, Bach years ago and one bit stuck with me more than the Godel stuff did: the idea that any message has three layers. There's the frame message (roughly "hey, this thing is a message, go find a way to decode it" — the reason you even look twice), the outer message (the implicit instructions for *how* to decode it, carried by structure and pattern), and the inner message (the actual content, what the sender meant). Hofstadter's example is a record or a DNA strand or a page of text, where the paper, the layout, and the words sit at different levels.

What I want: a short story (1000-1800 words or so, whatever the story needs) that makes these three layers come apart in a way that's actually interesting. Not "explorer finds ancient tablet and slowly cracks the code" — I feel like that's the obvious version and I've read it a hundred times. Push into weirder territory. Some directions I'd find fun, take one or combine:

- a message whose *frame* is deceptive — it insists it's a message when it isn't, or hides that it is one, so the decoder has to fight the frame before it even gets to the payload
- a case where the *outer* message is the real payload and the "content" is a decoy, or where decoding instructions are themselves the thing being communicated
- the frame message of one system turning out to be the inner message of another (a message that is a decoder for a message, turtles all the way down, but make it hurt)
- a decoder that has to build the decoding mechanism from scratch, and is changed by having built it — so the inner message you extract depends on who you became while extracting it
- no sender at all: the pattern is real, the layers are real, but nobody meant anything

Ground it in some concrete setting — a codebase, a signal, a will, an institution, a relationship, doesn't matter — but keep the philosophy doing work rather than having characters announce it. I don't need the story to resolve which layer "really" holds the meaning; I'd rather it end on the question sharpened than answered.

After the story, I want a quick structured summary I can paste into my notes. Give me a JSON object, nothing fancy around it, with these fields:

- "title": string
- "logline": one sentence
- "frame_message": what functioned as the frame, and whether it was honest
- "outer_message": what carried the decoding instructions, and whether it was separable from the content
- "inner_message": what the sender (if any) intended
- "which_layer_fails": which of the three the story bends or breaks, named as "frame", "outer", or "inner"
- "failure_mode": one line on how it fails
- "decoder_before": one line on what the decoder could do going in
- "decoder_after": one line on what it can do coming out
- "thesis": the strongest one-sentence claim about meaning the story could be read as making
- "counterreading": the strongest one-sentence claim that would undercut that thesis
- "open_question": the question it leaves hanging

Keep the JSON values short — a line each, not paragraphs. And please don't cite sources or name papers; I just want the story and the summary.
