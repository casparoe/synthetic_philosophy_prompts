I chair the standing novel-technologies panel of a regional environmental regulator. We meet roughly monthly and issue written recommendations — gene drives, new classes of persistent pesticide, field trials of reflective-particle dispersal, engineered organisms for bioremediation — and every recommendation is published, is appealable, and can be pulled apart under freedom-of-information law. The panel is an ecologist, an economist, a lawyer and a lay member, and I am the only one who has read any philosophy at all; I came to this by way of the rationality blogs rather than a law degree. My difficulty is that we are nominally required to apply the precautionary principle, and the phrase in our statute is the weak formulation — that uncertainty is not by itself a reason to postpone protective action — while half the panel reads it as the strong one, on which the proponent must demonstrate safety before anything may proceed. I have watched us reach opposite conclusions on near-identical files purely according to which reading the chair happened to hold that morning, and I have watched us invoke the principle to avoid a decision we simply found uncomfortable.

What I want from you is a reusable decision procedure I can actually run in the room: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the matter to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence. It has to operate at the scale of a single decision rather than a national policy, and it has to survive the objections that have always dogged this principle — that it is either vacuous (it forbids nothing in particular) or paralytic (it forbids everything), that it shifts a burden of proof without saying onto whom or how heavy, that it quietly ignores the harms of inaction and of the substitute technology, and that it treats a speculative catastrophe as though it were an established one. Please build in, explicitly: the distinction between prevention, which addresses a known risk, and precaution, which addresses uncertainty or outright ignorance; a test that forces the same precautionary reasoning to be run against inaction and against the alternative, so that two-sidedness is not optional; and some treatment of reversibility, on the familiar bind that a technology's effects are least knowable exactly when it is most malleable and best knowable when it is most entrenched. I also want you to be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three files: a gene drive intended to crash an invasive rodent population on an island whose seabird colonies are the reason for intervening; a novel broad-spectrum insecticide with a new mode of action and unknown persistence; and a small outdoor trial of a reflective-particle dispersal method. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and you should say why I will not like it and why the procedure nevertheless returns that answer. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and where it will misfire.

Give the procedure first as prose, then as a single JSON object with these fields: "steps", an array of objects each with "step" (integer), "question" (string), "sufficient_answer" (string), "if_insufficient" (one of "stop-no", "delegate", "next") and "judgement_call" (boolean); "applications", an array of objects each with "case" (string), "verdict" (one of "allow", "allow-with-conditions", "defer", "refuse"), "decisive_step" (integer), "who_decides" (string) and "notes" (string); "cannot_settle", an array of strings; and "failure_modes", an array of strings.
