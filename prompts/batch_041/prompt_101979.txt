I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we've got completely stuck on pain asymbolia. The setup is simple enough: patients after certain kinds of brain injury — I think parietal, but don't quote me — report that they feel pain, they can point to where it is, they'll say it hurts, but they don't seem to mind it, they don't withdraw, sometimes they laugh, and they don't show the usual distress. Some people in the group say this shows that the badness of pain is not essential to pain at all: you can have the sensation without the awfulness, so the awfulness must be a separate affective add-on. Others say no, the patients are still in pain and pain is still bad for them, it's just that the badness has been disconnected from the usual behavioural and emotional responses; the sensation is still something they have reason to avoid, even if they don't act on it. We've been going round this for weeks, and every time someone brings up a new lesion case or a new way of describing what the patient reports, the other side just redescribes it. It feels like we're not actually disagreeing about the same thing.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'pain' — one side using it for the sensation, the other for the whole experience including its aversiveness? Is it a substantive disagreement about whether badness is intrinsic to pain or a contingent accompaniment? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about the functional role of pain (what it makes you do) and one about its phenomenology (what it's like)? My own hunch — and I'd realy like you to test it rather than just agree — is that the impasse is really about a distinction one of us made up on the spot: between pain's imperative aspect (the command to stop, pull away, protect the part) and its evaluative aspect (the judgement that this is bad, to be avoided). Maybe one side hears 'badness' as the imperative and the other hears it as the evaluation, and so they talk past each other. Is that plausible, or is there a hidden empirical question here — say, whether asymbolia patients retain some faint unpleasant quale that they just don't report — that would settle it if we could answer it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a better taxonomy of the phenomena, a distinction between kinds of badness, a decisive case, or just admitting the question is ill-posed? Please answer in British English, and don't give me a reading list — I want the argumentative diagnosis itself.
