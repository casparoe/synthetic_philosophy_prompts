Question: Explain what supererogation is, why it is thought to be a problem for the standard division of acts into the obligatory, the forbidden, and the merely permissible, and assess one serious objection to the claim that any acts are genuinely supererogatory.

Student's answer: "Supererogation is the name for acts that go beyond what duty requires: they are morally good or admirable, but they are not obligatory. The classic example is a soldier who throws herself on a grenade to save her comrades. She did not have to do it, no one could have demanded it of her, and yet we praise her rather than merely tolerating what she did.

This is philosophically interesting because the standard classification of acts into the obligatory, the forbidden, and the merely permissible seems to be exhaustive, and so seems to leave no room for such acts. If an act is not forbidden it is permitted; if it is not required then it is permitted but not required, that is, merely permissible; and merely permissible acts are the morally indifferent ones, like choosing between two flavours of toothpaste. But the soldier's act is obviously not indifferent. So the standard classification must be incomplete. Urmson's 'Saints and Heroes' is the classic statement of this objection. His point was that because the three categories exhaust the possibilities, there simply cannot be acts that are both praiseworthy and non-obligatory, and the saint and the hero must be either doing their duty after all or doing something morally neutral that we happen to admire.

The strongest version of the objection comes from utilitarianism. If what we ought to do is whatever maximises the good, then there is nothing left over that could go beyond duty: the act that produces the most good is by definition the act we are required to perform, so the class of supererogatory acts is empty. On this view the soldier was simply doing what she ought to have done.

I think the category is real, and the utilitarian objection shows why utilitarianism must be false. The soldier's act is not required of her, nobody could reasonably demand it, but it is still admirable, and no moral theory that cannot say this can be correct. Notice also that if an act is supererogatory then omitting it cannot be blameworthy; the most we can say is that it is disappointing.

One further condition: the act has to be genuinely costly to the agent. If the soldier enjoyed the danger, or if the sacrifice happened to advance her career, we would not count it as supererogatory at all, since it would just be self-interest in disguise."
