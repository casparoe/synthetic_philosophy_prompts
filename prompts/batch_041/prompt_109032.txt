I'm a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on intentionality and the philosophy of mind. Every year, when we reach the debate over whether artifacts can have beliefs, the conversation stalls on the same examples: thermostats, chess engines, and the vending machine that can be described as taking a slug for a quarter. A student last week asked whether the machine's acceptance of a slug is a false belief or just a broken sensor, and I realized that the question is exactly what I want the opening of the seminar to stage. I want a satirical piece that makes the Dennett–Searle dispute vivid without reducing it to a cartoon, so that students who already know the literature laugh at the right places and students who don't still come away with the real issue.

Please write a parody of a software terms-of-service agreement for a fictional company, Two-Bitser Labs, whose flagship product is a vending machine marketed as having original intentionality. The machine uses a neural network to recognize coins and snack preferences; its marketing department says it knows what you want; its legal department knows better. The agreement should quietly take positions on the distinction between derived and original intentionality, the intentional stance as a predictive strategy, and what it means for the machine to be wrong when it accepts a slug. The humor should come from getting the philosophy right—a philosopher of mind should laugh rather than wince—and it should be legible to a smart non-specialist who has never read Dennett or Searle. Keep it deadpan; no winking at the reader, and no real companies or products.

Please format the response as a numbered terms-of-service agreement of about 700 words, with the following named sections in this order and approximate lengths: Preamble (60 words); 1. Definitions (100 words); 2. Grant of the Intentional Stance (120 words); 3. Derived and Original Intentionality (150 words); 4. Errors, Misrepresentations, and Slugs (120 words); 5. Termination, Refunds, and Zombie Devices (100 words); 6. Governing Law and Arbitration (50 words). Do not include a bibliography, citations, or references to actual works. The parody should stand on its own.
