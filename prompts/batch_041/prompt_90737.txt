I sit on the board of a small housing co-operative, and we are deadlocked between two proposals for how to run the place. The first is a detailed rulebook: minimum work hours, fines for missed shifts, and a formal procedure for expelling members who fall short. Its author says that anyone who has lived among forty adults knows what people are like when nobody is watching, and that leaving things to goodwill always ends with a few people carrying everyone else. The second proposal has no penalties at all. It asks that members who fall short be paired with a mentor, and that we spend on shared meals and small ceremonies — marking new members, departures, and the completion of hard jobs — the money we would otherwise spend on enforcement. Its author says rules of the first kind corrode the very thing we are trying to build, and that people become good by being treated as though they already were. Each thinks the other is naive, and I have come to suspect they are not really arguing about procedure at all but about what people are like underneath, and whether decent conduct is something you find in a person and protect or something you work into them by sustained effort.

Which of them has the better account, and what would each predict about how we would actually fare under the other's plan? I would also like to know whether the second position really has to claim that people are naturally good, or whether something weaker will do, and whether the first really requires thinking people are bad, or only short-sighted.

Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just the way you would write it in a letter.
