So my reading group has a fight I've been asked to finally write up, and I need your help producing the document. On one side is Sam, who thinks moral trade is one of the biggest untapped wins available to us: the idea being that two people with different moral views can both come out ahead by each giving up something they care about less in exchange for something they care about more, and that we should be building norms and institutions around it. On the other side is Priya, who thinks the whole idea is either incoherent or corrupting, and that the more carefully you look at it the less there is to it. They've been talking past each other for about a year now.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a moral-trade optimist and a moral-trade skeptic. Four parts:

1. The agreed-claims section. What can both of them actually sign -- about what a moral trade is, about how two people with different moral views stand to each other, about what the gains would be if the thing worked, about which failure modes are real? Number these. Don't let them be vacuous ("moral trade involves two parties" is not a claim), but also don't smuggle in anything that quietly favors one side.

2. The disagreements, in a table. Each one stated in wording that the side holding it would genuinely accept -- Priya has to be able to sign the sentence describing her own position, and Sam his -- with the crux it turns on.

3. The cruxes. I suspect these are in there somewhere: whether moral value is the kind of thing that can be added up across people who don't share values at all; whether making a trade expresses a claim the trader might not believe (something like "your values are as valid as mine"); whether the right baseline is what the other party would have done anyway; and whether widespread moral trade would reward people for adopting more extreme positions as bargaining chips. Sort out which of those are real cruxes and which are verbal disputes in disguise -- if part of this fight is just two people using "value" to mean different things, say which part.

4. For each real crux, a case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance. Where no such test exists, say so rather than inventing one.

A few specific things I want them to have to deal with, since these are where the argument keeps getting stuck:

- A trade where one party's "concession" is giving up something they think they're morally required to do -- say, someone who believes donating to a particular cause is a duty, agreeing to send that money somewhere else instead.
- A trade with someone whose moral views you find abhorrent, not just different, where the question is whether the trade makes you complicit in what they're doing with their end of it.
- The difference between trading over means (things both parties already think are useful for their own separate ends) and trading over ends (where what you're buying is the other person doing something you think is good, or not doing something you think is bad). I have a feeling a lot of the disagreement lives here.
- Whether moral trade is more like a market or more like a treaty, and what that implies about whether it should stay informal, be public, or get institutionalized.

American English please, and keep it readable -- I'm handing this to people who aren't philosophers.
