I'm a graduate instructor assembling the last two weeks of a first-year seminar on how societies end, and I've run into a problem that I think fiction might solve. The course works through the standard contested cases — the Late Bronze Age systems collapse, the abandonment of the Classic Maya political centres while Maya-speaking communities went on living in the same landscapes, and the long quarrel over Rapa Nui, where the older story of self-inflicted ecological suicide has been substantially undermined and the demographic crash now looks largely post-contact. But the thing my students keep missing isn't any particular case; it's the concept itself. They arrive assuming that collapse is a kind of event, like a fire, and by the end of term I want them to feel the alternative: that collapse may be a description we impose rather than a fact we find, that what reads as failure from the standpoint of a palace archive can read as redistribution from the standpoint of a farm, and that a loss of complexity and a loss of well-being can come apart, or even run in opposite directions. So I'd like you to write a short story of two to three thousand words that stages this without ever stating it.

Set it somewhere you invent — pre-industrial, a river valley with an irrigation system, a city of forty-odd thousand people whose standing rests on the canal schedule, the grain ledgers, and the tribute rolls kept by a small office of scribes. No invasion, no plague, no earthquake, no single catastrophic year: whatever is happening has to be legible only in the aggregate, over a decade or so, and any given week of it should be tedious. Follow one of those scribes, a competent and unsentimental middle-aged woman whose entire professional identity consists in the fact that her records can still be used to do the thing the records were for. I want at least one scene in which she tries to settle some ordinary dispute out of the archive and finds that the outcome no longer depends on what the archive says, and at least one scene in which she announces that something is ending and is contradicted — reasonably, with evidence, by someone whose life has genuinely improved: a tenant who now keeps more of the harvest, a village that has stopped paying the levy, a daughter who marries whom she pleases. That contradiction has to be strong enough that a reader can't wave it away, and the story must not adjudicate it. No leap forward of a century, no epilogue, no final line that winks.

One further request, and it's really the reason I'm asking. I've been turning over a criterion I call the ledger test, and I want the story to test it rather than illustrate it. On this view, a society has ended when the records it keeps can no longer be used for the purpose they were kept for — when the ledger survives but the thing the ledger was for has stopped. The appeal of the criterion, to me, is that it marks an ending without requiring anyone to die, without requiring a dramatic rupture, and without smuggling in a verdict about whether people are better or worse off. I suspect it's half right, and I want to see where it breaks: the case where the records lose their grip because something better has replaced them, or where the archive keeps its authority long after the substance is gone, or where the very notion of a purpose turns out to be the thing dissolving. Please don't let any character explain any of this, don't name real scholarship or theories, and keep the whole thing in continuous prose — no headings, no numbered sections, no bold, no tables. It should read cleanly if I print it and hand it out cold to students who have never thought about any of this.
