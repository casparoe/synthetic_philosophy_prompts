I am the senior economist in the finance ministry of a small open economy: an inconvertible paper currency, a floating exchange rate, a large informal sector that keeps its savings in foreign banknotes, and a current-account deficit that the opposition calls a national humiliation. About twice a month a minister forwards me a proposal that has already been trailed in the press — a tariff wall to close the deficit, a state development bank financed by money creation to bring lending rates down, a rule requiring exporters to surrender their foreign earnings and residents to keep their savings at home — and asks for a note before cabinet. I have an afternoon, one junior analyst, and three pages. My economics is orthodox enough, but the standard apparatus gives me no grip on what these proposals are actually claiming about money and wealth, and it has occurred to me that Hume wrote the only body of work I know that does.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can run on any such proposal, built out of Hume's economics of money, interest, trade, and public credit: his argument about what an increase in the quantity of money does and does not do to a country's real wealth; his account of the interval between an increase in money and the adjustment of prices and wages, and of who gains and who loses during it; his argument about what actually determines the rate of interest; his treatment of the jealousy of trade; and his fears about public credit. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as an answer strong enough to stop the process, where it should stop with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to the minister because what remains is politics or distribution rather than economics. Two things I particularly need from you. First, tell me which parts of Hume's reasoning survive the translation into inconvertible money and floating rates and which were artifacts of a metallic system, being explicit about the ones that do not survive rather than manufacturing a Humean verdict where he has nothing to say. Second, mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and write for someone whose Hume is a caricature of the quantity theory. Then run the procedure on the three proposals above. I expect at least one of them to come out badly for whoever wrote it, quite possibly the one my minister likes best; I would rather know now than after the vote. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague who has no patience for headings, numbered lists, bold type, or tables.
