I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, mostly covering psychology and moral philosophy, and I'm working on a feature about the queasy feeling you get when a politician says a nation must atone for something done before most living citizens were born. My editor keeps pushing me to make it concrete, so I've been trying to seperate the possible machinery here instead of just quoting the usual tug-of-war. I want you to rank the following five ways a present-day nation might inherit guilt, from most to least defensible as a claim about an actual blameworthy subject—not just a beneficiary or a symbol. (1) The corporate-body view: the state is one moral agent that persists through constitutions, borders, and generations, so its guilt is simply its own. (2) The inheritance view: guilt is like an unpaid debt or an estate with liabilities, passed down whether or not you consented, and the no-exit problem—you can't easily resign from a nation—is what makes it stick. (3) The character view: what's inherited is a national habitus or character formed by the wrong, and the guilt is the ongoing failure to reform it. (4) The membership view: you're guilty because you keep belonging to, and enjoying the fruits of, an institution that hasn't really repudiated the wrong. (5) A Jaspers-ish split: political guilt attaches to what your state does now, while moral or metaphysical guilt is only ever individual, so the nation is never the right bearer.

Give me a tier list—S/A/B/C is fine, or 1–5 if you prefer—with a couple sentences on why each lands where it does, then tell me which one you'd actually put in the top tier if you had to defend cross-generational national guilt, and what you'd have to give up to do it. Also, is there a sixth option I'm missing that does better than all of these—something about collective memory, narrative, or a nation as a corporation whose shareholders include the dead and the unborn? And please don't send me reading lists or citations; I need the argument itself, in plain language, because I'll be the one trying to explain it to a general reader.
