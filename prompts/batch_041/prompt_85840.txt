ok so I'm a novelist. Literary fiction, but this one has a lot of procedure in it — think a workplace novel that keeps drifting into a legal thriller. My protagonist, Nell, is a mediator, or really a facilitator, who gets called in when two experts are deadlocked and someone needs the deadlock documented. A hydrologist and a dam engineer. A hospital administrator and an epidemiologist. A city planner and the guy who runs the traffic model. She does two or three of these a week, she's smart but not a statistician, she gets about an hour with each party seperately and then an hour with them together, and at the end she has to write something both sides will sign, which goes to whoever hired her.

Here's the thing I built the book on. A friend of mine who does this for real told me there's actual math behind why she keeps failing. The idea, and I'm probaly mangling it, is something like: if two people start from the same prior, and it's common knowledge between them what each one now believes, they can't keep disagreeing. So whenever two rational people with the same starting point stay stuck, the stuckness has to be traceable to something specific — different priors, or private information neither has fully pooled, or the disagreement not actually being common knowledge in the technical sense (each thinks the other is posturing, or each thinks the other thinks they're posturing), or one of them just not being coherent. Is that right? Because if it is, it's the engine of my whole book and I want to get it right.

So what I need from you: turn this into a procedure Nell can actually run in a room. The questions she asks in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers let her stop and which send her back a step, and where the procedure runs out and she has to hand the thing to a person because the remaining question is about values and not about belief. Remember she can't do algebra on the page — it has to be verbal tests, things she can say out loud and a hostile engineer can't dismiss as hand-waving. And she needs to be able to defend each step, because these people are experts and they will absolutely push back.

Then run it on three cases, in the kind of detail where I can steal the dialogue:

1. Two hydrologists, same data, same model family, one says the reservoir has a one-in-four hundred chance of overtopping in the next decade and the other says one in forty. They both agree the other isn't lying or stupid. Walk me through what Nell does.
2. Two experts who, as far as Nell can establish, have the same priors, the same evidence, and the same competence, and who still won't budge. I need to know what the procedure says here, even if what it says is that one of them isn't being coherent — because that's an accusation Nell can't make in a signed document, and I need to know she can't.
3. One of the two parties isn't a person. It's a model — a forecasting system, or an optimization thing someone bolted onto a permitting process — and the person defending it genuinely can't tell Nell what it started out believing. Does the procedure even get off the ground? If it doesn't, say so.

Two more things. First, tell me honestly what this procedure can't do, and give me at least one case where it produces an answer I won't like — ideally an answer that makes Nell, or the party I'm most sympathetic to, come out worse than the scene needs them to. I'd rather rewrite the scene than have you flatter me. Second, don't worry about citing anybody. Nell isn't going to name-drop a philosopher in a mediation room and neither is the narrator, so the ideas just have to be right, not attributed. Same for the write-up: no reading lists, no bibliography, no names and dates. If the underlying result is genuinely contested or has a caveat that would embarrass me if a reviewer caught it, tell me in plain language instead.
