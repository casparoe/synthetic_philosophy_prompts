I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology (16 students), and I want an in-class game that lets them feel both the power and the fragility of majority voting as a truth-tracking device: the room converging on the right answer even though almost nobody in it was individually reliable, and then the same room converging just as confidently on the wrong answer once the conditions are changed. The design should turn on private clue cards about factual questions with determinate answers that only I know, cards of varying reliability, and at least one round in which several students unknowingly hold copies of the same clue traceable to a single source; students should commit to an individual answer before each majority vote, so they can see whether the group beat its average member, its most reliable member, and its least. No money should change hands, and the rules should arrange things so that the participants themselves, not my closing remarks, are the ones who notice the dependence on independent errors and on competence above chance. Run it across at least three rounds, including one in which the clues are systematically misleading, so that a larger majority simply becomes more confidently wrong. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the rules and materials; the scoring scheme; the round-by-round sequence and where the twist lands; the debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the assumptions of the Condorcet jury theorem and onto what the majority did and did not beat; and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce.
