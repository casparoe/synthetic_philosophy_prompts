You are a philosopher of classical Indian epistemology specializing in Nyāya, and you have spent years turning theoretical frameworks into evidence-grading rubrics that non-specialists can actually run. I need one of those rubrics, and I want it built deliberately rather than generically: before you simplify anything, think through what the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas actually commits you to, so that every simplification is a conscious choice rather than an accident.

Context and role. I run a small claims desk at a public-policy nonprofit. Each week two to three dozen empirical and quasi-empirical claims arrive from program staff, partner organizations, and draft reports — claims such as "this training improves staff retention," "this neighborhood contains no affordable child care," "clinicians in this region under-prescribe this drug," "the 2019 reform caused the drop in filings." My job is to triage each claim into one of four bins: established enough to publish, needs specified additional work, rejected, or escalated to a human because the question is one of value or policy rather than fact. I have about twenty minutes per claim, I am not a statistician, and the research director signs off on anything that goes into a published report. I want a procedure I can run in that time, in that seat, with that sign-off.

What to build.

A decision procedure, taken in order, for evaluating a single claim, organized around the Nyāya means of valid cognition rather than around the familiar evidence hierarchy. For each step give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when the answer falls short. The procedure must do the following.

1. Force an explicit determination of which pramāṇa could in principle settle the claim — perception, inference, comparison or analogy, or trustworthy testimony — and state what to do when a claim appears to need a means that Nyāya does not accept as independent: a negative existence claim that seems to rest only on non-apprehension, or an "inference to the best explanation" that is not a strict pervasion-based inference. Handle that mismatch openly instead of pretending it away.

2. Spell out inference with its conditions: the reason's presence in the subject under discussion, the pervasion linking reason to conclusion, and the standard ways an inference goes wrong — the reason deviating, contradicting the conclusion, being counterbalanced by an equally good opposing reason, being unestablished, being mistimed, or being overturned by later knowledge. I want these as concrete checks a non-specialist can actually perform, with an example of what a "counterbalanced" claim looks like in a policy setting.

3. Spell out testimony with its conditions: what makes a source a trustworthy expert in the relevant sense — direct acquaintance with the matter, sincerity, competence — and how to probe each of those in practice when I cannot interview the source.

4. Include a defeat step: a place to record what would later overturn an accepted claim, and instructions on when a claim should be re-opened rather than merely annotated.

5. Mark the points where the procedure cannot decide and a person must, saying which person (me, the research director, an outside expert) and what the handoff should contain.

Then run it end to end on three claims, showing the steps as you go: (a) a claim backed by a single well-conducted but small study; (b) a negative existence claim ("there is no X in this region") supported only by an absence of records; (c) a causal claim about a policy change whose strongest support is a plausible mechanism plus a before-and-after comparison. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like — I would rather watch the procedure bite than watch it flatter me. For each, finish with the bin and a one-line justification.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the claim types it will systematically mishandle, the places where the Nyāya machinery is doing real work versus where it is decoration, and where an ordinary evidence-grading approach would have served me better. Be candid about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Constraints on your answer: keep the procedure to something I could keep on one page; no citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts, since I will not be able to look anything up; write for a competent non-philosopher; and think carefully before committing to the rubric rather than producing the first tidy version that comes to mind.

End your response with a single final line of the form \answer{...} giving your recommended bin for case (c), using exactly one of: established, needs-work, rejected, escalated.
