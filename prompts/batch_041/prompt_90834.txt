I'm a high school student who runs a blog for my school's philosophy club, and I'd like to build a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz about something that keeps splitting our meetings. It started when someone raised the Doomsday argument — the thought that learning you're roughly the sixty-billionth human to have lived should make you expect our species to end fairly soon — and about half the club found it obviously absurd while the other half couldn't say what was wrong with it. When we tried to locate the disagreement, it turned out we disagreed about something more basic: whether the bare fact that you exist, and that you're you rather than somebody else, is evidence about the world at all. I know there are positions here with standard names, but I don't want the quiz sorting people by which name they like; I want it sorting them by what they say about concrete cases.

So: eight to ten questions, each one a short concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions. No question may name a position or ask anything like "do you think your existence is evidence?" — every question has to be a case a reader can answer without knowing the labels, and each case should be explained in the question itself, since my readers won't have heard of it. Please make sure the quiz can land people on at least these five outcomes: (1) the view that you should reason as if you were a random observer drawn from all the observers there are, so that your existence is more probable in worlds containing fewer observers; (2) the view that your existence is more probable in worlds containing more observers, so a world with more people in it is a priori more likely; (3) some hybrid that tries to hold both of those at once, with a plain statement of what it buys and what it costs; (4) the view that your existence and your position in the world are not evidence about anything, so facts about how many people there are must be settled without you; and (5) the view that the whole line of argument fails for a different reason — that there is no non-arbitrary answer to what class of beings you're supposed to be a random sample of, so the questions never get off the ground. Use the standard names in the key if standard names exist, and give each outcome a one-line summary plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment it has to swallow.

I'd like these cases to appear, at least: the Doomsday case; the presumptuous philosopher case, where two cosmological theories differ only in how many observers the universe contains and you're asked whether you can settle the matter from your armchair; the Sleeping Beauty case, which our club already knows, so keep it brief; and one case of my own that I'd like you to use because I haven't seen it anywhere: a coin is tossed, and on heads exactly one person is created and given a red hat, while on tails a thousand people are created, exactly one of whom gets the red hat and the rest blue hats; you wake up wearing the red hat and are asked whether heads has become more likely. Tell me what each case is testing only in the key, never in the question, and say whether that last case actually does the separating work I think it does. Finally, flag any pair of outcomes the quiz can't genuinely separate — including any case whose standard reading is contested — and note any combination of answers that doesn't correspond to a position anyone holds, with a guess at whether that's because the combination is incoherent or merely unoccupied. Assume my readers are sixteen or seventeen and know no jargon; I don't need names, dates, or reading suggestions, just the positions and which case splits them.
