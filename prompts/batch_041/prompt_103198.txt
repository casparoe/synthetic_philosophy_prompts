For a graduate seminar I co-teach on the philosophy and science of pain, I have to give a single ninety-minute session on why pain is bad for the person who has it. The group is genuinely mixed: half are clinicians and pain researchers who take the badness of pain as self-evident and find the question faintly pretentious, and half are philosophers of mind who enjoy problematising it but have little feel for the clinical material. I do not want you to explain the topic to me; I want a plan for teaching it.

The pivot of the session is pain asymbolia, the condition, typically arising from insular or cingulate damage, in which a patient reports pain, locates it, and rates its intensity, yet is not bothered by it and does not suffer. I want the students to leave seeing that the sentence "pain is bad" fuses at least three separable things: that a sensation has a certain quality and intensity, that it is unpleasant, and that it motivates avoidance — and that asymbolia prises these apart. Build the session so that this dissolution arrives as a discovery rather than as a taxonomy handed down from the front.

Tell me the order in which the components have to come and which depends on which; the one carrying example to organise the whole session around, what it must contain and what it must leave out. I have been tempted to use a case of my own construction — a patient who rates the pain at 8 out of 10, holds her hand still through a dressing change, and answers "does it bother you?" with "not in the least" — so tell me whether that is the right vehicle or whether something plainer would serve, and why. Give me the misconceptions these two audiences will bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. I expect the philosophers to say she must be lying or merely stoic, and to conclude that if it does not bother her it is not really pain; I expect the clinicians to say this is just analgesia under another name, or to insist the badness is simply the unpleasantness and nothing more needs saying. Then give me the single question to put at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely memorised the case, and tell me what I should deliberately refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud. No readings and no citations.
