I’m not a philosopher, and I don’t have much formal background in aesthetics, but I’ve been trying to understand Tolstoy’s view of art on my own after a conversation with a friend who took a course on the subject. She gave me what sounded like a confident summary, and I wrote it down so I could think it over: Tolstoy thought art is the expression of emotion. The artist feels something and expresses it; if the audience catches the feeling, it’s art, and the more people it moves, the better it is. That’s why he thought the best art is the simple stuff that peasants enjoy, and why he condemned Shakespeare and most of Beethoven as failures — they’re too aristocratic and complicated. He did think art should be moral, but only in the sense that it should make us feel closer to one another, so his real test was whether a work makes you feel what the artist felt and whether it reaches the widest possible audience.

I’m inclined to believe her, because it fits the vague picture I had of Tolstoy as a moralist who hated elite art, but some of it feels too neat. Could you go through the summary piece by piece and tell me what a careful reader of Tolstoy would accept, what they’d call a simplification, and what is actually wrong? I’m especially unsure about the claim that the more people a work moves, the better it is, and about whether he really condemned Shakespeare and Beethoven for being aristocratic rather than for something else. I’d also like to know whether the idea that art is the expression of emotion is the right way to put his view, or whether he was making a different and stranger claim about how feeling gets transmitted. Please separate what is accurate, what is a common but misleading gloss, and what Tolstoy himself would have denied, and then tell me whether the conclusion my friend drew — that his view amounts to “whatever moves the most people is the best art” — survives once the summary is corrected.
