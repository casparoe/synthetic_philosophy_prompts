(1) Nicod's criterion — a generalization gets confirmed by its positive instances. Finding a black raven is evidence for "all ravens are black." Honestly this has always seemed like the most obvious thing in the world to me.

(2) The equivalence condition — if two hypotheses are logically equivalent, then any evidence that bears on one bears on the other in exactly the same way. Same evidence, same confirmation, no difference.

(3) Confirmation is a matter of degree, and it's probabilistic: E confirms H just to the extent that it raises H's probability given what I already know, i.e. P(H|E) > P(H).

(4) The green apple — I'm not going to sit here and say a green apple confirms that all ravens are black. That's the absurd conclusion the paradox is supposed to force on you, and my instinct is that whichever premise generates it is the one to dump.

(5) But I also don't see how you get out of (2). If two claims are logically equivalent then they can't differ in what evidence supports them — that just seems to be what "equivalent" means.

(6) And I do think a black raven is genuinely better evidence than a green apple here, and not just because one feels more relevant — I'd defend that asymmetry.
