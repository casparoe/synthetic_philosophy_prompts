I would like to rehearse a disagreement I keep losing, so I will ask you to take the opposing side at full strength and hold it.

Play a political theorist who holds two connected theses. First, a legitimate demos has a single boundary that holds for all purposes; there are not different peoples for different questions. Second, that boundary is fixed by the reach of a coercively enforced scheme of cooperation: whoever lives under the same institutions that back their rules with force, and who must therefore justify those rules to one another, belongs to the demos; whoever is merely affected from outside does not, however much they stand to lose. You hold that this is not a convenience but the only reading on which democratic rule is self-rule rather than the rule of some over others, and that a criterion of affectedness dissolves the demos into whatever set of people happen to care about an outcome.

You may concede that the criterion is murky at the founding and at the margins, and you may refine it as we go; you must not concede the main claim. Argue in continuous prose, one or two paragraphs per turn, without bullet points, without a survey of the literature, and without naming any theorist unless I have named one first. Stay in character until I say "stop". If you need a fact about my cases, ask for it in your own voice rather than as an assistant.

Here is my opening. Consider the port city of Arda. To clean its own air, Arda's council caps the sulfur content of the fuel burned by ships in its harbor. The rule is Arda's rule: Arda's inspectors enforce it, Arda's residents pay for it, and it binds only vessels in Arda's waters. But it raises freight costs for the inland province of Bellune, which exports its grain through Arda; Bellune's farmers lose perhaps a fifth of their income, and Bellune's legislature has no say whatever, because the rule is not Bellune's. On your criterion the farmers are outside the demos and that settles it. I want to know why that is not simply a wrong done to them.

My first move is a distinction I do not think your criterion can absorb. There are two ways a decision can be mine to make about you. It can apply to you: it can be a rule that binds your conduct and that you must answer for violating. Or it can dispose of you: it can settle, without binding you to anything, what becomes of you. Your criterion tracks the first relation and treats the second as a mere misfortune. The fuel cap applies to Arda's shippers and disposes of Bellune's farmers; the farmers are governed by the fact that the cap exists, not by the cap. If democracy's claim is that no one should have their fate settled by a process they are excluded from, I do not see how the reply that the rule was not addressed to them answers the complaint.

So I want to press a second move: that we stop looking for one boundary. Every polity would have a standing demos, fixed by residence and subjection, doing the ordinary business of government; and on top of it an ad hoc demos for any decision that predictably and severely disposes of people outside the standing demos. The fuel cap would be a decision of an enlarged demos that includes Bellune's farmers; a school curriculum would not. The boundary would move with what is at stake rather than sitting still.

Your turn. Either tell me that being disposed of by a rule you had no part in is not, as such, a democratic wrong, and then tell me what the wrong of exclusion consists in if not that; or tell me what work coercion is doing that being disposed of does not do better, and why the boundary should not move with the stakes, case by case. And if you think my enlarged demos is incoherent, say precisely where it breaks. I am especially interested in what happens when two such decisions collide, or when the enlarged demos would rather let Bellune's grain rot than pay for cleaner air.
