In my early modern philosophy seminar we are reading Leibniz, and I am stuck on a stretch of the Monadology that I will paraphrase here in my own words rather than quote: Leibniz argues that since God's understanding contains an infinite number of possible universes and only one can be actual, there must be a sufficient reason for God's choice; that reason is the degree of perfection each world contains, so the actual world is the best of all possible ones. My difficulty is that if there are infinitely many possible worlds, it seems that for any candidate world one could conceive another slightly more perfect, so the series would have no maximum and thus no best member. Does Leibniz simply assume that the series has a maximum rather than a mere supremum, and if so, what is supposed to license that assumption? I am also unsure how his claim that God is determined to choose the best is compatible with his insistence that God's choice is free and not metaphysically necessitated. Could you help me understand how the argument is meant to work, and where the pressure points are?
