I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on what it is for a trait to have a function, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than a list of -isms, since the whole point is that the -isms fall out of a few prior commitments. I know the literature, so don't bother with citations or reading lists — I just want the shape of the space.

Start the reader at something like: a trait's having a function is a fact a biologist can get right or wrong, and it's the kind of fact that grounds claims about malfunction. Then make every branch node one question about what that fact depends on, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Roughly: does it depend on the trait's history at all? If yes, does it need a history of being favoured by selection specifically, or is any history of being reproduced or persisting because of what the trait does enough? If no, is the function relative to some capacity of a containing system, chosen by whoever is doing the explaining, or relative to the system's own goals (survival, reproduction), or to its ongoing self-maintenance? And separately: does the trait have to actually be doing the job right now, or can a broken heart still have the function of pumping blood?

I want a couple of branches that aren't the two textbook ones. One is forward-looking: a brand new trait that nothing has ever selected for still has a function if it's the sort of thing selection would favour — that splits off from the backward-looking historical views, and I want to know whether it can survive the malfunction cases. Another says a part has a function just when it contributes to keeping the organism's own self-producing organisation going, no selection history required. And there should be a deflationary leaf for "function" being several different things depending on the explanatory project, rather than one thing.

The cases have to do the work — each one should be the reason some branch exists. Use all of these: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a dog that pops into existence in a lightning strike, heart and all; a trait fixed by drift that only later started paying its way; feathers, originally for warmth, now for flight; a diseased heart that can't pump; the appendix, or a flightless bird's wings; the sound a heart makes, or the nose's role in holding up glasses; a mousetrap that has never caught anything, and a chair someone uses as a doorstop; a beaver's dam; and a song or a technique passed on by imitation rather than by genes.

Leaves I want to see, named or marked "unnamed": pure selected-effects; a more permissive historical one that counts learning and cultural transmission; the forward-looking propensity one; causal-role; goal-contribution; the self-maintenance/organisational one; designer's intention for artifacts; pluralist/deflationary. Flag any pairs the tree genuinely can't seperate — I suspect the permissive-historical and organisational leaves will be hard to tell apart on the beaver, and the drift case may not split propensity from causal-role. Also mark any combination of answers nobody defends, with one line on whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Lead with the tree itself (nodes as questions, leaves as positions), then a table with one row per leaf: position, the commitment that defines it, the case that embarrasses it most. Hard cap 700 words for the whole thing. Audience is second-year phil of bio students who know what natural selection is but have never read any of the function literature.
