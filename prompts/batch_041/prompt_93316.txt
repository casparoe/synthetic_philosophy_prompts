Two of my best friends have been having the same argument for about three years and I've somehow ended up as the referee, so I'm outsourcing it to you. R works at a welfare-rights charity but she's not what you'd expect: she thinks a decent society is allowed to ask something back from the people it supports, that a generous minimum with nothing attached to it is morally evasive, and that it's politically doomed anyway — the moment the money looks unconditional the middle drops out of the coalition that funds it, and the people who pay for that are the poor. T is a social-policy researcher and an unconditionalist: she thinks you shouldn't have to earn the right not to be destitute, that bolting job-search hours, assessments and sanctions onto subsistence is wrong in itself, and that the machinery mainly sorts and humiliates people while shoving the costs onto A&E, housing, food banks and unpaid care. They agree on nearly every fact and shout past each other regardless. I want the document they should have written themselves.

The fight has gotten sharper lately because they've both read the same few things: work on administrative burden (the idea that a lot of what gets called deterrence is really just people failing to complete forms or giving up), the cognitive-load stuff about what chronic money-shortage does to attention and planning, the randomised cash-transfer evidence, where the temptation-goods scare mostly doesn't show up but the employment effects are small enough that both of them read it as supporting their own side, and the older comparative finding that the countries treating benefits as near-universal and lightly conditioned have held up best on both poverty and public support — R says that's selection and you can't rerun 1945, T says it's the whole argument in one chart. So: write the joint statement they could both sign after a real adversarial collaboration, aimed at one specific place — a mid-sized ageing country with high in-work poverty, a big means-tested benefit whose sanction rate has been climbing for a decade, and a politics where the undeserving poor is the only budget line anyone wants to talk about. I want a numbered section of agreed claims: about the facts, about what's actually at stake, about what the disagreement is not about. It has to be non-vacuous — no sentences both can sign only because they're empty.

My guesses at the cruxes: (a) whether reciprocity is a legitimate condition of receiving support or a respectable-sounding rationalisation for exclusion, and if it is legitimate, whether it can be cashed out in terms of paid work at all, given care work, disability, and the impossibility of conjuring up a job in a place with none; (b) whether the right test of any conditionality regime is the caseload or the employment rate, or net wellbeing across the whole system including the costs it displaces; (c) whether conditionality is what keeps redistribution politically alive or what quietly kills it; (d) whether a condition attached to subsistence counts as coercive, and whether that last one is even substantive or just two different baselines for the word. Finish by telling me which parts of the fight are verbal and which are substantive, and whether anything they've both been treating as a crux collapses once the words are fixed.
