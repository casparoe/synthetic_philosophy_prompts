I am setting the final examination for a one-semester, upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant's moral philosophy. My students have worked through the derivation of the categorical imperative in the Groundwork, the distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in the will, the corresponding division between perfect and imperfect duties, and the standing objections that the universalizability procedure is empty, that its verdicts depend on how the maxim is described, and that it covertly imports empirical assumptions about human needs. The examination is closed-book, students may not cite or quote the text, and the question must therefore be answerable from their grasp of the machinery alone.

I want a single essay question, worth 30 marks and answerable in about 60 minutes at roughly 700 words, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the formulations of the categorical imperative or by summarising the four examples from the second section of the Groundwork (the suicide maxim, the lying promise, the neglected talents, the refusal to help). To force candidates into the argument rather than the labels, build the question around the following stipulated test, which is not a standard piece of terminology and which I will print on the paper exactly as set out here:

The Universal Extinction Test (UET). Let a maxim be a general principle of action specifying a type of action and the end for which the agent performs it. A maxim fails the UET if and only if, in the world in which every agent acts on that maxim, no agent ever successfully performs the action type the maxim prescribes, where success means attaining the specified end by the specified means. Otherwise the maxim passes.

The question should require the candidate to do each of the following, and the marking scheme should be organised around these tasks:

1. State precisely what the UET asks and identify, unprompted, which element of Kant's own procedure it reconstructs and which it discards.
2. Apply it to the four examples named above, saying for each whether the UET agrees with Kant's verdict and, where it does not, exactly why.
3. Diagnose the pattern in those agreements and disagreements, and say what that pattern shows about whether the contradiction-in-conception test and the contradiction-in-the-will test can be run by a single procedure. The candidate must then assess whether the UET's shortfall, if there is one, is a defect in the test or instead evidence that Kant's second test rests on an assumption the first test does not need.
4. Construct one maxim of the candidate's own devising, not among the four examples, on which the UET returns a verdict that is implausibly permissive or implausibly restrictive, and use it to press the difficulty that a maxim may be redrawn at a different level of generality so as to reverse the verdict. The candidate should say how a defender of the UET ought to reply and whether the reply succeeds.
5. Reach a verdict on whether the UET is a defensible reconstruction of the universalizability test, and name the single consideration that would most change their mind.

Deliverables and constraints:

- The question as it would appear on the examination paper, carrying its instructions (closed-book, 60 minutes, 30 marks, approximate length) and phrased so that a candidate who has memorised the formulations without understanding the argument cannot score well. The question itself must pose the tasks without disclosing the analysis.
- A model answer of roughly 650 to 750 words, self-contained, written as a strong candidate would write it, containing no page references and no quotations. It must discharge all five tasks and commit to a verdict rather than surveying the options. In task 2 it should show rather than assert why the lying-promise and suicide maxims come out as they do under the UET, and it should register rather than silently assume that the customary classification of the suicide example is contested. In task 3 it should make plain which cases the UET lets through, and what the will test would have to assume in order to catch them.
- A marking scheme totalling 30 marks, allocating marks to the individual moves: restatement of the UET and of what it discards; correct verdicts and reasoning on the four examples; the diagnosis in task 3; the constructed maxim together with the generality objection and the reply; the verdict and the change-my-mind condition. For each must-hit point, add one line naming the specific error that costs marks there, and state explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer, naming the two or three moves that a B answer will characteristically lack. Do not reward name-dropping of commentators or references to secondary literature; the marking scheme must be answerable by a student who has read nothing but the primary text and the lecture material.
