I sit on a bunch of small boards — a housing co-op, a couple of hospital and school committees, I did a stint on a parole-type panel — and I've noticed my gut has pretty firm opinions about when a group's decision is legitimate and when it's a mess, but I can't definately get those opinions to add up to one rule. Here are the cases, with the actual vote counts. Tell me what principle, if any, produces my verdicts, and which of them I have to give up.

1. Hospital drugs committee, five of us. The protocol says we vote on three things separately: is it safe, does it work, do we recommend it. Our own rule is that we only recommend if it's both safe and it works. Last month: safe, 4–1 yes. Works, 3–2 yes. Recommend, 2–3 no. So the committee said "safe" and "works" by majority and then declined to recommend. My gut: we recommend it. Only two of us pressed the button for recommending and I still think the answer is recommend — if we've said it's safe and it works, then recommending isn't a separate opinion we each get to have, it's just what the word means.

2. Parole board, three of us. The chair always asks the actual question first — release or not — and then afterward asks each of us whether we think he's shown remorse and whether he has a plan, basically so it's on the record why. Last week: release, 1–2 against. Then remorse, 2–1 yes. Plan, 2–1 yes. So a majority of the board thinks he's remorseful and has a plan, and the board voted not to release. My gut here is the opposite of case 1: we don't release. The release vote is the decision; the other two were us explaining ourselves, and you don't get to go back and re-count the explanations and overturn the decision.

3. The co-op board, five of us, two things on the agenda that have nothing to do with each other: buy the empty lot next door, and move trash pickup from Tuesday to Thursday. One guy wants to put them together as a single up-or-down vote, both or neither, because he knows the lot loses on its own but the trash day is popular. My gut: no. These two have nothing to do with each other and the board's answer on the trash day should not be able to depend on the lot. If they were connected — like the medical committee, where recommending just is a function of the other two — I'd feel completely different about it.

4. A single member of one of these boards who votes safe, works, don't recommend. My gut: he's just contradicting himself, and it's fair to say so to his face. A person can't hold that combination of views.

5. A board where the votes come out such that the group's overall position isn't held by any single member at all, not even a minority of one. My gut: there's no "the board thinks" there. It's a tally, not a view, and it isn't incoherent the way case 4 is.

6. Me, as the swing vote. When I can see that voting my honest answer on one of the sub-questions will produce a group position that's nuts, I'll vote the other way to stop it. My gut: that's fine, I'm not lying, I'm voting for the outcome I want.

So here's the principle I keep half-forming and then losing: aggregate the pieces when the members were deciding on the pieces, and go with the bottom line when the members were deciding on the bottom line. In case 1 the protocol made the criteria the decision, so the criteria win. In case 2 the release vote was the decision, so it wins. Is that a real distinction or am I just rationalizing whatever answer I already had? It worries me that the only difference between 1 and 2 is who asked which question first and what everybody thought they were voting on.

Also I can't tell whether 5 is consistent with 1. In 1 I insist the group be coherent. In 5 I say there's no group view there to be coherent or incoherent in the first place. Maybe those are the same thing said two ways, or maybe I'm holding two different pictures of what a group is and they don't fit together.

Somebody told me once that there's a theorem saying you can't have a group that stays consistent, respects majorities, and treats each question on its own. I don't know if that's the same problem as mine.

What I want back:

- Bottom line: 2–3 sentences, what you think the rule actually is.
- The principle: state it precisely, as something I could apply to a new case, and label each clause with the case above that forces it. If it takes two principles instead of one, say so, and say where the line between them falls.
- My cases, one at a time: for each of the six, what your principle says and whether it agrees with my gut. Don't skip the ones where it disagrees with me. 500–600 words.
- Cases I didn't list: three new ones, the kind of thing that would actually come up on a board like this, with your predicted verdicts and why. ~200 words.
- What I have to give up: which of my six verdicts your principle can't save, and what I'd have to believe instead in order to keep it. ~200 words.

Don't give me a reading list or tell me what famous people have said about this — I just want the rule.
