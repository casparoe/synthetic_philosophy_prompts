I retired a few years ago after thirty-odd years in physical chemistry, and I've joined a philosphy reading group to keep the brain busy. We've been doing probability, and the thing everyone keeps repeating is that a probability is a propensity — a coin has a propensity to come up heads the way sugar has a propensity to dissolve or a glass has a propensity to shatter when it hits the floor. I spent my whole career on dispositions like solubility and reaction rates, and my gut says the analogy is being made to buy something it can't pay for, but I can't quite put my fnger on it.

Can you audit the analogy for me? What's supposed to map onto what — where's the analogue of the sugar, of the water, of the dissolving, of the fact that solubility is a stable rate I can measure on a bench again and again? Then tell me where the mapping breaks.

Two things I especially want you to press on. First, every disposition I ever cared about had a mechanism underneath it — solubility comes down to lattice energy and entropy, and in principle I could trace any single dissolution to molecules doing specific things. Is the coin's propensity supposed to have anything underneath it, or is it being asked to be a dispostion with nothing under the hood, which to a chemist is a very strange animal? Second, a propensity seems to come attached to a whole set-up rather than to a single toss in isolation, and I gather that causes trouble when you try to say precisely what the propensity is conditional on — is that a real defect of the picture or are the philosophers just fussing? (The Bayesians in the group say a probability is only a degree of belief, which at least doesn't pretend to be a property of the coin; the propensity crowd are the ones whose analogy I can't swallow.)

Then tell me whether the conclusion they're drawing survives once the disanalogies are on the table — namely that because probabilities are propensities, chances are real single-case features of the world and indeterminism is a live physical option. If it doesn't survive, what's a better analogy — or is the whole dispositional picture just the frequentist's long run wearing a lab coat?

Keep it under 800 words and give me the correspondence as a table. I don't need reading lists, just the argument.
