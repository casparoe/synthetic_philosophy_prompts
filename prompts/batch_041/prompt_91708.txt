You are grading the final-exam essays for an upper-level course on the philosophy of physics, on the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow. One exam question asked students to explain how the thermodynamic arrow of time relates to the direction of time. The question and one student's answer are reproduced below. Your job is to grade that answer and to produce the comments that justify the grade.

Examination question:

Explain the relationship between the thermodynamic arrow of time and the direction of time. In your answer: (i) state what it means for the fundamental dynamical laws to be time-reversal invariant, and explain why this makes the second law of thermodynamics puzzling; (ii) explain the role that the universe's initial conditions play in grounding the arrow; (iii) say whether the thermodynamic arrow explains the psychological and epistemic arrows (memory, records, anticipation) or merely runs parallel to them; and (iv) say whether the second law is a fundamental law, a statistical regularity, or something else.

Student's answer:

Thermodynamics is the one part of physics that gives time a direction. The fundamental laws — Newton's, Maxwell's, Schrödinger's — are invariant under reversing the direction of time: for any process they permit, they equally permit the time-reversed process. The second law, by contrast, says that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases, which picks out one direction as the future. The puzzle is that a time-asymmetric regularity appears to emerge from time-symmetric dynamics.

The standard resolution is statistical. Entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements are compatible with a given macroscopic description. Because vastly more microstates correspond to high-entropy macrostates than to low-entropy ones, a system that starts in a low-entropy state will, with overwhelming probability, wander into higher-entropy states. The second law does not forbid an entropy decrease; it makes it fantastically improbable, since a decrease would require the system to occupy an exceptionally special microstate. The law is therefore probabilistic rather than absolute.

Boltzmann's H-theorem was the classic attempt to derive this, and the recurrence objection, due to Zermelo, is the classic reply: because the dynamics are reversible, every entropy-increasing trajectory has a time-reversed twin along which entropy decreases, so the result cannot hold for all trajectories. The usual response is to appeal to the universe's initial conditions — the fact that the early universe was in a remarkably low-entropy state. It is this boundary condition, rather than the dynamics, that fixes the direction of the arrow.

With this in place, the other arrows fall into line. Memory, records, and causal reasoning all presuppose that entropy increases, so the thermodynamic arrow accounts for why we remember the past and anticipate the future. Since our psychological arrow is just a physical process, and physical processes run along the entropy gradient, it is the thermodynamic arrow that explains the psychological one rather than the reverse.

Where I would dissent from textbook treatments is in calling the second law a law in the same sense as the dynamical laws. Because it is only a claim about overwhelmingly probable behavior, and because the dynamics themselves are indifferent to the direction of entropy change, the second law is really a theorem about our coarse-grained descriptions rather than a feature of the world. In the end, the direction of time is something we impose on nature: we describe the world macroscopically and remember only one way, and it is this that makes entropy appear to increase.

Your task: assign a grade on a 20-point scale, and write the grader's comments. Proceed claim by claim through the answer. For each substantive claim, state whether it is correct, incorrect, or a defensible-but-minority position that should not be penalized as an outright error; where a claim is incorrect, state precisely what is wrong and give the correction in a sentence or two. Be especially alert to errors that are subtle rather than blatant — a factual misattribution, a conflation of two distinct ideas, or an inference that does not follow from the premises the student has already granted — and flag, for each, whether a hasty grader would be likely to let it pass. Do not merely summarize the answer or restate it approvingly. Close with the point total, a one-sentence justification, and a brief note on what single revision would most improve the answer. Do not assume the student's conclusion is wrong merely because it is unorthodox, and do not assume it is right merely because it sounds sophisticated.
