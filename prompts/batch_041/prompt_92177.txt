William James treated introspection as the primary method of psychology, yet he also warned against what he named the psychologist's fallacy — reading the observer's own standpoint into the mental fact under investigation — and he insisted that the stream of thought contains transitive parts, felt relations and a fringe, that are given only first-personally. Dennett's heterophenomenology, developed long after James's death, runs partly with and partly against that inheritance: it grants the subject authority over how things seem to them but brackets the first-person perspective, treating utterances as data about what the subject believes or is disposed to say rather than as privileged access to consciousness itself. Predict how James would have received this proposal. I do not want a verdict; I want the case reconstructed: which of Dennett's constraints follow from James's own methodological warnings, which James would reject and on precisely what grounds, and whether the residue — above all the transitive, fringe-like parts of experience that resist being captured in reports — marks a real limitation of heterophenomenology or merely a feature James had independent reasons to protect. Close by saying which of the two you take to have the better of the exchange, and what would move you.
