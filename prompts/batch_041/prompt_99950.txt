I'm chair of the critical-care triage commitee at a big public hospital, and we have to lock our surge protocol before the next respiratory wave. There are two proposals and a hybrid, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursdays vote. Proposal A scores patients by expected survival and saves as many as possible, breaking ties by age; it will sometimes let a small expected benefit to several people outweigh a slightly larger claim from one person. Proposal B says never aggregate: no pile of small benefits can override one persons larger complaint, so we prioritize the worst off until their claim is met, even if fewer total lives are saved. The hybrid, from my co-chair, says we may aggregate only when the person who would lose out cannot point to a complaint that it would be reasonable for anyone to reject as the basis of a general policy; if they can, we cant aggregate. She calls this the individual-complaint threshold.

This is basically the debate about Scanlonian contractualism and the aggregation problem, and I want the memo to actually use those ideas rather than just name-drop them. You are a philosopher who works on normative ethics and health policy. Think carefully and start with a short brainstorm of the two or three strongest objections to each of A, B, and the hybrid. Then write the memo I can circulate. It should recommend one concrete protocol for the coming wave, give a review date (say 90 days), and honestly present the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a strawman. If you recommend the hybrid, say exactly how we operationalize it: what counts as a complaint, who decides whether it is reasonable to reject, and what tie-breaker applies when two complaints both pass or both fail. If you reject the hybrid, say what it gets wrong about the moral point of aggregation. Keep citations out of it; I dont need reading lists, just the reasoning applied to our case. The memo should be something the committee can vote on, not an essay about the literature.
