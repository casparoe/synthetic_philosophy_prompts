I'm a 3rd-year PhD in philosophy of mind and I'm the TA for an upper-level seminar on personal identity and the self. I've been told to write the final essay question this year and I want it to actually test whether people understood Dennett's "Where Am I?", not just whether they can retell the plot (brain in a vat named Yorick, body named Hamlet, later a new body Fortinbras, the bit-for-bit copy of the brain program called Hubert, the ending that just goes to mayhem). Every year half the class gives me a summary of the story plus a shrug about "the self is a centre of narrative gravity," and I'm sick of it.

So here's the crux I want the question to land on, and I want you to build the whole thing around it: there's an asymmetry in the story that people never seem to notice. Dennett is totally fine saying he survived the destruction of Hamlet and is now Yorick running Fortinbras -- same guy, no crisis. But when it comes to Hubert, the exact real-time copy of his brain, he balks; he doesn't want Hubert hooked up to another body and won't just say "sure, that's me too." So he seems to be leaning on something like having been the same ongoing stream, having actually lived through it, rather than on being a particular pattern or program. I want students to figure out whether that "same stream" idea can be stated without it collapsing into "it's me because it's the one that's me," and whether it's even consistent with the other thing he says about the self.

And I want to add one wrinkle of my own so nobody can just regurgitate the original: stipulate in the question that Hubert, the copy, has by now been running continuously and independently for years -- it's had its own experiences, diverged, maybe doesn't even remember the original operation the way Dennett does. Ask them whether that changes the verdict on Hubert, and whether the same-stream criterion was ever doing any independent work or was just tracking which one happened to have the right causal history. Bonus if they can say something about what the "narrative self" story (the centre of narrative gravity thing) implies here, since I think it quietly pulls the opposite way from the stream argument.

Can you write me: (1) the essay question itself, pitched so a strong student could answer it in 90 minutes of writing and a weak one can't fake it by reciting the story; (2) a model answer of maybe 700-800 words that actually takes a line on the asymmetry instead of hedging; and (3) a marking scheme with the specific must-hit moves a top answer needs, the marks each is worth (say 20 total), and a one-liner for each on how students typically blow it. Don't make it depend on any particular secondary literature or expect them to cite anything -- just the story itself and their own reasoning. Also please don't make the question so loaded that it tells them the answer, I want them to have to find the asymmetry themselves if they can.
