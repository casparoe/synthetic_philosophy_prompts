I'm a high school student and I've been asked to write a short story for my school's philosophy club, and I want it to be about the problem of acting when you have no reason to prefer one option over another — the situation usually illustrated by a donkey starving between two identical piles of hay. My teacher is tired of stories that use this only as a symbol for indecision, so I want mine to take the underlying question seriously: if two options are genuinely indistinguishable in every respect that could give me a reason, is there anything left for rationality to do, or does acting always require some arbitrary nudge that no reason could justify? To keep it from being a plain restatement of the textbook version, I want to build it around an invented technology: a service that sells personalized tie-breakers — small, arbitrary quirks, such as a bias toward the left-hand option or a weakness for names beginning with a particular letter — which customers install so that they can act once their own reasons run out. The catch in my story is that after the technology becomes universal, someone demonstrates that every decision anyone makes traces back to one of these installed quirks, including the decisions people are most certain they made for reasons. I'd like the story to dramatize the difference between choosing, where a reason does the work, and merely picking, where nothing does, and to leave the reader genuinely unsure whether the protagonist has lost something or has simply learned what everyone else was already doing. Please write the story, around a thousand words, with at least one scene in which a character must decide something the tie-breaker cannot reach; I don't want a lecture in the dialogue, so the philosophy should come through the situation.
