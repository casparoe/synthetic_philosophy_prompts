In my philosophy of mind seminar we have been circling a dispute that has the shape of a permanent stalemate. The epiphenomenalist holds that phenomenal properties are caused by neural events but themselves cause nothing; the phenomenal judgment—the thought that I am having a reddish experience, or that this pain is unbearable—is therefore caused by the neural event, not by the phenomenal property it is ostensibly about. The critic presses the self-stultification objection: if the judgment is not caused by the phenomenal property, then its content cannot be explained by that property, and our confidence that we are tracking anything phenomenal looks like a cosmic coincidence. The epiphenomenalist replies that reliable correlation is enough: the neural cause of the judgment is also the neural cause of the phenomenal property, so the judgment can be systematically about the property without being caused by it. The critic then asks what aboutness amounts to here, and whether a causal chain that bypasses the phenomenal property can underwrite content. At that point the exchange repeats itself with different terminology.

I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, not another round of arguments for either side. Is the disagreement substantive, or is it verbal—do the parties mean different things by causation, aboutness, or explanation? Are they answering different questions under one heading: one about the metaphysics of causation, one about the epistemology of introspection, one about the semantics of phenomenal predication? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work—say, about whether phenomenal properties are identical to or supervene on functional properties, or about whether reliable correlation can ground representation? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed?

My own hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion, is that the stalemate is generated by an unexamined assumption shared by both sides: that a phenomenal judgment must be causally sensitive to the phenomenal property it is about in order for the judgment to have that property as its content. If we distinguish causal dependence from epistemic dependence, and distinguish a judgment's being caused by a phenomenal property from its being justified by or about one, the paradox may look less like a problem for epiphenomenalism and more like a problem for a particular internalist picture of introspection. But I am not sure whether that distinction actually dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually says, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a conceptual distinction, a formal result about reliable correlation and content, an empirical discovery, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
