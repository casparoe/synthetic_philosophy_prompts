Question (20 points)

Devlin's claim that a shared morality is necessary to the survival of society — usually called the disintegration thesis — gets read in two quite different ways: as an empirical prediction about what happens to a society that tolerates moral deviation, and as a conceptual claim about what it is for a group of people to constitute a society at all.

(a) Explain the difference between the two readings, and say what each implies about the kind of evidence that could count for or against Devlin. (7 points)

(b) Does either reading cause trouble for Hart's reply? In your answer, bring in Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality. (8 points)

(c) True or false, with explanation: Hart's legal positivism is what commits him to rejecting the legal enforcement of morality. (5 points)

Student's answer:

(a) The disintegration thesis says that if a society stops treating some shared morality as binding, it will come apart, because that morality is what holds it together — Devlin uses the cement image, and he thinks that without a shared moral code there is nothing left to bind people to each other or to their institutions. Read empirically, this is a prediction: societies that loosen their morals should show things like rising crime, weakening institutions, and declining trust, and you could test it by comparing societies or by tracking one society over time. Devlin himself leans on historical examples, which only makes sense if he means the claim empirically. Read conceptually, it is not a prediction at all but a claim about the meaning of society: a group of people who share nothing but a legal system are not a society, they are just a population under a government, so a shared morality is part of what makes them a society in the first place. On the conceptual reading, sociological data cannot really touch the thesis, because you cannot falsify a claim about the concept of society with evidence — you would have to argue about the concept itself. On the empirical reading, data is exactly what matters, and the absence of it is a serious problem for Devlin.

(b) Hart's reply is mostly that Devlin never supplies the evidence. Devlin asserts that moral deviation threatens social survival but never shows it for any particular case, and the historical examples he gestures at don't establish a general thesis. Hart also says the argument is a non sequitur: even granting that a shared morality is necessary to society, it does not follow that the criminal law should enforce all of it, or that a majority gets to impose its morality on everyone else. Hart thinks the criminal law may only be used to prevent harm to others, following Mill, so on his view the disintegration argument doesn't even bear on the legal question. As for positive and critical morality, Hart distinguishes positive morality, which is the morality a society actually holds, from critical morality, which is the morality that society would hold if it were being more honest with itself. His point is that Devlin appeals to positive morality, but the question of what the law should enforce has to be answered by critical morality. Neither reading of the disintegration thesis troubles Hart much: on the empirical reading he wins on the evidence, and on the conceptual reading the claim becomes either trivial, since of course any society has some shared morality, or it simply assumes what it needs to prove, namely that this particular morality is the one that has to be enforced.

(c) False, sort of. Hart's positivism says that law and morality are conceptually distinct, so it doesn't tell you what the law ought to be; he isn't claiming that immoral law isn't law, only that whether a rule is law is a separate question from whether it should be enforced. So positivism by itself doesn't commit him to anything about enforcement. That said, in practice his liberal conclusions are connected to his positivism, because the point of separating law and morality is to make room for criticizing the law from a moral point of view, which is exactly what he does. So it's false that positivism entails rejecting legal moralism, but true that his positivism and his liberalism fit together.
