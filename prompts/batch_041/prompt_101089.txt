I write long-form features on science and ideas, and I'm at the reporting stage of a piece on how we should classify the great famines of the modern era — whether we are really looking at natural disasters that politics failed to mitigate, or at political crimes that used weather as an alibi. What I need from you is not an essay but a rating exercise, done carefully enough that I can interrogate the reasoning behind each number.

Take five episodes: the Irish famine of the 1840s, the Bengal famine of 1943, the Soviet famine of 1932–33, the famine that accompanied China's Great Leap Forward, and the Sahel famine of the early 1970s. Rate each on two independent dimensions, giving a number between 0 and 1 for each:

(a) Causal weight of deliberate political choice — how much of the excess mortality is attributable to decisions, policies or omissions by identifiable authorities, as opposed to an unavoidable natural shock.

(b) Culpability under a no-hindsight constraint — how blameworthy those authorities are once you discount for what was genuinely unknowable, or logistically impossible, at the time and place, rather than judging them by what we now know about famine mortality.

For (b), state explicitly what you are discounting and why. Then sort the five cases into tiers by dimension (a), and note any case whose tier changes when you switch to (b).

Three further things. First, apply a replaceability test to each case: would a plausible alternative government of that era, with the same resource base and roughly the same moral outlook, have averted mass death — and what does your answer imply for (a)? Second, rate how far each of three frameworks — food-availability decline, the entitlement approach associated with Amartya Sen, and the political-economy view that treats famine as a crime, or as something other than literal starvation — actually explains each case, and say where a framework goes quiet. Third, flag any case whose rating would move materially if the mortality were driven mainly by disease and displacement rather than by starvation itself.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know these famines well, and do not present a contested verdict as the consensus. I will handle my own sourcing, so there is no need for a reading list or citations. Please write in British English.
