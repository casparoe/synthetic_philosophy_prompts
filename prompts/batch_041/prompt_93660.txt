I am a doctoral student in a science and technology studies department, and I have just spent a fortnight in a seminar working through the old quarrel over whether technical artefacts can be political — Winner's essay and its two ways of reading the question, and the constructivist insistence that a thing's politics lies in the meanings and uses that social groups assign to it rather than in the thing itself. I have come to think that both parties load too much weight onto design. Each of the standard examples, the low bridge and the mechanical tomato harvester included, is a case in which someone chose something or a group read something into it; the artefact's political content turns out to be either a decision or an interpretation, and the interesting question is only which.

What I keep running into, and cannot place in either camp, is what I have taken to calling politics by attrition: cases in which an artefact is politically consequential neither because it forecloses an option nor because anyone reads it as doing so, but because of the small, recurring, unremarkable demands it makes of a particular class of users — demands that fall below the threshold of design intent and below the threshold of interpretation, and that are nevertheless dictated by the artefact's material properties rather than by convention or by anyone's expectation. The politics, on this reading, is carried by the cumulative cost of compliance rather than by any decision or any meaning.

I would like you to give me five examples of artefacts or technical systems whose politics is best described in that way. Please give the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long: name the artefact, say what recurring demand it imposes and on whom, and indicate why that demand cannot be redescribed as a design decision or as an interpretation. No introduction, no conclusion, and no table. Write in British English.
