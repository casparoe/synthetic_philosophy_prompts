I teach a graduate seminar that touches on social choice, and a colleague in political theory — a serious scholar, though not a specialist in this area — has circulated a draft chapter summarizing Arrow's theorem for a general readership. Before I decide whether to assign it, I want an audit of the central passage. Here is the paragraph in question:

"Arrow's impossibility theorem is the deepest result in democratic theory: it proves that no fair method of combining individual preferences into a collective decision exists. All it assumes is a handful of conditions no one would deny — that if everyone prefers A to B, so should society; that the social ranking of two options should depend only on how individuals rank those two; and that no single person should dictate the outcome. From this it follows that every conceivable voting rule must be either irrational or dictatorial. The theorem is quite general: it applies to any procedure for aggregating individual judgments into a collective one, including the pooling of experts' probability estimates. Its real upshot is that democratic outcomes are arbitrary — with enough control over the order of voting, almost any alternative can be made to win. Sen's later work showed that the only escape is to abandon interpersonal comparisons of welfare altogether, which is why economics quietly gave up on the problem."

I am not asking you to rewrite the paragraph into a better one. I want a fair-minded assessment, claim by claim: which statements a specialist would endorse as they stand, which are defensible simplifications that a careful popularizer might allow, which are outright errors, and which are true claims about something else being passed off as consequences of the theorem. Attend especially to the internal machinery — how the conditions are stated and what they actually constrain, the sense in which the word "dictatorial" is doing work here versus its everyday sense, and the domain the theorem is actually proved over — and to the closing sentence's account of how one escapes the result. Where the paragraph infers a conclusion from an otherwise accurate premise, say whether the inference holds; where it conflates one result with another, name the distinction that has been lost. Throughout, separate what is settled in the technical literature from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I would rather have a precise account of the points at which the author owes the reader a defense of a choice than a blanket verdict that the passage is wrong.
