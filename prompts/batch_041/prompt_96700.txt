I am assembling, for a graduate seminar on language acquisition, a checklist that any adequate theory of how a child comes to know the grammar of her language must satisfy, and I want the checklist itself to stay neutral between nativist and empiricist programs. Set out the desiderata. For each, give the case, finding, or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the commitment of one camp, naming the camp — I suspect at least a couple of these requirements are smuggled in rather than earned. The considerations I have in mind, though you should add any you think are missing: the absence of negative evidence in what children hear; the fact that infants are demonstrably sensitive to statistical regularities in speech; the structure-dependent character of the rules children end up with; the existence of a sensitive period; the emergence of a full grammar in a newly formed sign language; and the fact that children arrive at the same competence under very different child-rearing regimes. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise live positions. Then identify any pair or subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and what follows for the debate if they cannot — that is the part I care about most. Return the list as a table with four columns: Requirement; Motivating case or finding; What it rules out; Status (non-negotiable, or the camp whose commitment it is). Follow the table with a short paragraph on the unsatisfiable subset and a single sentence naming the most exclusionary requirement.
