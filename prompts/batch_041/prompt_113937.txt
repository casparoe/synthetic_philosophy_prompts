I teach a grad seminar on nativism and the poverty of the stimulus, and I've got a handout problem. Every textbook version of this debate collapses into Chomsky vs. Skinner, or into some tired for-and-against about whether kids hear enough language. I want a decision tree that actually maps the live positions, built out of the logic of the arguments rather than out of a roster of famous names.

So: give me a decision tree (or a taxonomy, same thing to me here) of the positions on the question of whether a child's knowledge of language is underdetermined by what they hear, and what does the underdetermining if so. Rules for the tree:

- Every internal node is a question my students could answer without already knowing which camp it drops them into. Phrase it as a question about a claim or a case, not "are you a nativist?"
- The first fork should not be innateness. It should be about what the alleged poverty of the stimulus is supposed to consist in — a shortage of data, a shortage of evidence (negative evidence, indirect evidence), a shortage of time, or a mismatch between the data and the hypothesis space. I want the reader to have to commit on that before they get to sort themselves into nativist or empiricist.
- The second fork should seperate the people who think the gap gets filled by innate representational content (rules, principles, features) from those who think it gets filled by innate architecture or attention — biases that constrain learning without containing the knowledge — from those who think nothing innate fills it and the gap was misdescribed all along.
- Keep the labels descriptive. Name a position after the person most associated with it only when that's unambiguous; otherwise leave the leaf unnamed and let the description carry it.

Leaves I need in there, at minimum:
- strong domain-specific representational nativism
- the third-factor / architectural-constraint view (general-purpose, not language-specific)
- domain-general statistical learning with language-specific priors
- domain-general statistical learning with no language-specific priors
- the usage-based, input-is-rich view
- the flat denial that there's any poverty to explain
- one leaf for a position I've been kicking around myself, which is that the whole fight is an equivocation — the nativists and the empiricists are answering different questions (what's the final state vs. what's the learning mechanism vs. what's the concept) and there's no single fact of the matter. I suspect it's cheap, but I want it placed properly and I want you to say what it would have to give up.

Also flag any two leaves the tree can't actually separate, and mark the branches where nobody sits — and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I'd rather see an honest empty box than a fake leaf.

The audience is my students, who've read the standard stuff and are bored of it, so keep every node concrete. And since I'm handing this out: be careful to mark which of these claims are genuinely contested among people who work on the topic and which are basically settled. Don't sell me a live disagreement as the consensus, or the other way round. The shape of the tree is your editorial call and I'd like you to own that too, so tell me where you had to make a judgment call and why.
