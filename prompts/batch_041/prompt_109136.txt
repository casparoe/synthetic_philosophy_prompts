I am in the upper sixth and have been asked to write a paper for my school's philosophy society on advance directives, the documents by which a person sets out in advance what medical treatment they would or would not accept should they later lose the capacity to decide for themselves. I have arrived at a position of my own and would like you to work out what it actually commits me to, rather than to rehearse the standard arguments for and against such documents.

The position is this. An advance directive binds not because it is a good prediction of what the person will later want, and not merely because honouring it serves their interests, but because the earlier self has standing authority to determine what is done to the body and life that will belong to the later self, and that authority does not lapse merely because the later self can no longer exercise it. On this view the later self's apparent contentment is not in itself a reason to set the directive aside, since the later self has no standing to revoke what the earlier self settled; the force of the document comes from who wrote it, not from how accurate it turns out to be. I would add two refinements of my own: that the authority belongs to the most recent competent self, so a directive made later, while the author is still competent, simply displaces an earlier one; and that the author may name a proxy to depart from the directive, but that in the absence of such a clause the proxy has no discretion whatever.

Please set out the most important implications of this position, ordered by how much practical difference they make, and be as concrete as you can about what would change at a hospital bedside, in a family argument, and in how such documents ought to be drafted. I am particularly interested in the following, though I would rather you told me what I have missed than answered only these:

1. A directive that refuses the very treatment which would restore the author's capacity to decide. If the directive is honoured, the later self never recovers the standing to revoke it; if it is not, the earlier self's authority turns out to be conditional on something the earlier self rejected.
2. Someone who signed at thirty and, by the time they lost capacity, had adopted a different faith or abandoned the values they once held. How much does my view have to say about whether such a change matters?
3. Circumstances the author never anticipated. If the directive's force comes from authority rather than accuracy, does it bind where the author never considered the case, and if so, what is left of the idea that it expresses their will?
4. Whether my view implies that I am now, at seventeen, able to bind myself at forty, and if so whether that is a consequence I should accept or a reductio.
5. What it implies for drafting: sunset clauses, mandatory reconfirmation, and clauses authorising a proxy to depart from the document.
6. What it implies for the standing of the later self more generally. Does the later self become a mere instrument of the earlier one, and is that a cost the position can pay?

Our library is thin and I have no wish for a reading list; I would rather have the reasoning, with each implication spelled out far enough that I can see what it costs me to hold the view.
