I am a pensions actuary in London — fully qualified, fifteen years in, most of it spent on defined-benefit valuations and the assumption-setting that goes with them — and I have no philosophical training beyond a reading habit. A friend of mine is partway through a philosophy MA and has spent the past month trying to persuade me that the nineteenth-century quarrel between Clifford and James over the ethics of belief is a live problem for anyone who has to form beliefs under uncertainty, and in particular for me. I have been resisting, on the suspicion that the whole thing is decision theory with a moralising garnish: in my world nobody much cares what you believe; they care what assumption you adopt, how you justify it, and whether you have documented your margin for adverse deviation. I would like you to test that suspicion properly rather than let me win it by definition.

Take the dispute as it actually stands — Clifford's claim that it is a moral fault, always and everywhere, to hold a belief that the evidence does not support, together with the case he opens with, the owner of an ageing emigrant ship who works himself into confidence that she is seaworthy and lets her sail, and who, as Clifford himself foresees, cannot escape by saying that it was only the action and not the belief that was culpable; and James's reply that where the option before us is genuine — live, forced and momentous — our passional nature has a legitimate say, that the rule of never believing beyond the evidence is itself adopted on non-evidential grounds, and that in some cases the believing is partly constitutive of the good at stake. Restate the whole thing, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in: best-estimate assumptions against prudent ones, margins for adverse deviation, longevity improvement and discount-rate assumptions, Technical Provisions, funding and recovery plans, materiality, data quality and what we will accept as sufficient evidence, the scheme actuary's sign-off, the trustee board, and the regulator looking over all our shoulders. Map Clifford onto the practice of assumption-setting and onto the shipowner; map James onto the actuary who must set a longevity assumption today that no quantity of data will settle, and who cannot decline to choose, because the valuation has to be signed.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which parts of each position translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. I expect at least three candidates and would like them examined rather than gestured at — first, that my vocabulary has no place for a belief as distinct from an assumption adopted for the purposes of a calculation, and so no place for the wrongness of a belief as distinct from the wrongness of the certification or the payment that follows from it; second, that James's claim that one may will a belief is not the same claim as the actuary's claim that one may prudently adopt an assumption, and that what fails to carry across is precisely the doxastic element rather than the prudential one; third, that in my world the sufficiency of evidence is itself a costed and budgeted quantity, so that the question of how much evidence is enough is settled by an economic optimum rather than by any epistemic norm, which may beg the very question at issue. For each item of residue, tell me whether it is a defect of my vocabulary — something actuarial practice genuinely cannot say and ought to be able to — or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked, or a sign that it belongs to a kind of enquiry my vocabulary was never attempting. Finish with a direct verdict on my suspicion: is the ethics of belief fully absorbed by the ethics of decision-making under uncertainty, or is something left over, and if so, what kind of thing is it? Do not be polite about it. I would far rather learn that the philosophical debate is doing work my framework cannot do than be told that we are all saying the same thing in different registers.
