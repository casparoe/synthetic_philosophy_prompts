I have been keeping a private list of my judgments about originals, copies, and fakes, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I am aware of the familiar division between works that can be copied and works that cannot, but that only restates my problem: I want to know which are which, and why.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A painting executed centuries after the fact in the style of an old master, passed off as a newly discovered work by him and admired as such for decades, until laboratory analysis exposed it. My verdict: after exposure it loses nearly all its value, and the loss is not merely a market correction — the thing really is worth less. The dealer who sold it did wrong, and the wrong cannot consist in depriving anyone of an aesthetic experience, since the painting looks exactly as it did.

2. A forgery that copies a painting which survives and hangs elsewhere. My verdict: the same, plus something extra. I would cross a city to see it in an exhibition about the forger, and I think that is a reasonable thing to want — yet I would not cross a street to see an honest replica of the original.

3. An honest replica: the original being too fragile to travel, a museum displays a hand-painted copy, indistinguishable to the eye, plainly labeled as a copy. My verdict: no one has been wronged, and I would not say I had seen the work. Here I notice trouble, because by my own verdicts the exposed forgery — equally not by the master's hand, and morally worse — has more pull on me than the honest copy does.

4. Reattribution: a painting exhibited for a century as the master's is reassigned to a pupil; nothing about the object changes. My verdict: its value falls a great deal, my interest falls with it, and I do not think this is snobbery — but I cannot name what I have lost, since the experience is unchanged.

5. A bronze known only through later marble copies, the bronze itself long gone. My verdict: a good copy carries the work, and I am willing to say I have seen it. But a plaster cast of that marble copy, in a teaching collection? Here I waver, and this is the case I keep returning to. My inclination is that I have seen a copy of a copy and have not seen the work, though I cannot say why the first step is generous and the second stingy.

6. A cathedral gutted by fire and rebuilt in the original design from the surviving stones plus new ones. My verdict: it is the cathedral, and the rebuilt fabric is not a copy of it. Yet a painting destroyed by fire and repainted by a restorer from photographs would be a copy, and I would say the painting is gone. I suspect I am rationalizing: buildings have designs and places, paintings have surfaces, and I may be dressing up a difference in what I happen to care about as a difference in kind.

7. A painting of which sixty percent of the visible surface is later repaint, exhibited as the work. My verdict: it is still the work, but damaged, and worth far less; and I would rather see the repaint removed, even if that leaves bare ground. This sits badly with 6 — I let the cathedral be rebuilt and will not let the painting be.

8. Two violins, one modern and one celebrated and old, that blind tests cannot distinguish. My verdict: the old one is still worth vastly more, and a soloist's preference for it is legitimate rather than irrational; but I would not say the modern one sounds worse.

9. A photograph whose original is a file, every copy of which is bit-identical. My verdict: there are still originals and copies — the original is the file the photographer made or authorized — and a print he signed is worth more than an unsigned print from the same negative.

My own tentative principle, which I would like tested and repaired: what I value in an original is a causal guarantee, that what I am encountering is the product of the very act that made the work and that nothing has intervened between me and it. On this account copies are simply different works; and where I value a copy at all, I value it as a record of a lost work rather than as the work.

I would like the following from you. First, the best principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the cases that motivate it — and, if no single principle saves all nine verdicts, an explicit choice between a complicated principle that saves every one and a simpler one that overturns one. Second, an account of the tension between 2 and 3: is the fraud's documentary interest doing all the work, and if so, am I simply mistaken in feeling the exposed forgery as art rather than as evidence? Third, an account of 5 and of 6 against 7: is the copy-carries-the-work clause a principle or merely compensation for loss, and can a principled line be drawn between rebuildable and unrebuildable works? Fourth, your verdicts on two cases I have not listed: (a) a work whose original is lost but whose description survives in enough detail to reconstruct it, where a reconstruction is then built — is that the work, and does it inherit the value of an original or only that of a record? (b) a forgery passed off as a lost work, where the work it was passed off as turns out to have really existed and the forgery is now the only surviving trace of it — what is that object worth, and as what? Finally, an honest statement of which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.
