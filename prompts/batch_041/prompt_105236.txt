I'm a novelist, and I've spent the better part of a year circling a problem that I suspect is really a philosopher's problem wearing a plot's clothing. My protagonist is a night-shift nurse who believes the pharmacy cabinet has been restocked; in one draft she's crossing the ward to fetch a bag of saline, and in another she's fetching a drug that will stop a patient's heart if it's the wrong vial. The evidence is identical in both drafts — the same glance at the same clipboard, the same half-remembered word from the day nurse — and yet she seems to me to know in the first and merely to hope in the second, and I cannot tell whether that is a fact about her mind, a fact about my narration, or a trick of the tense. I've read enough to know that people argue about whether practical stakes can move the line between knowing and not knowing, and that the camps have names, but I don't trust my grasp of them, and I'd rather not have my character become a mouthpiece for a position I've half-understood.

What I want from you is a sorting instrument, not an essay: a decision tree that begins from a reader's answers to concrete cases and ends at a named position on the relation between practical stakes and knowledge. Build it from the logical space outward — first the questions that actually divide the camps, then the positions that occupy the resulting combinations — and make each branch node a question a reader can answer without already knowing which label it leads to. I'd like the branching to track at least these distinctions: whether the difference between my two drafts is a difference in knowledge at all, or only in what it is proper to say or rational to do; whether, if knowledge itself moves, it moves with the knower's own stakes or with the stakes of whoever is doing the ascribing; whether the shift falls on knowledge or on some neighboring status such as justification or the warrant to assert; whether knowledge is required for rational action in the first place; and whether the shift is best modeled as a threshold on graded confidence or as an all-or-nothing gain and loss. The leaves should include the straightforward view that stakes never touch knowledge, the view that they touch the knower's knowledge, the view that they touch the ascriber's standards, the view that the truth of a knowledge ascription varies with who is assessing it, at least one position that explains the intuitions away as a confusion between knowing and being entitled to say, and at least one that denies knowledge is needed for rational action at all. Give each leaf its standard name where one exists, one sentence on the commitment that sits most awkwardly with it, and a note on any pair of leaves your tree cannot honestly separate; where a combination of answers corresponds to no view anyone holds, say so and diagnose briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Take the role of a philosopher who works on the epistemology of knowledge attributions and has taught this material until the standard moves are second nature, and think carefully before you commit to the tree's shape — begin by sketching the space of possible answers on a scratchpad, then prune. No reading list or citations are needed; I want the instrument itself. Keep the whole thing to eight hundred words at most.
