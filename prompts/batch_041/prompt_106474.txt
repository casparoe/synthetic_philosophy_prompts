I am assembling a specification that a safety team will use to decide, case by case, whether a deployed language model has deceived someone, and before I adopt any particular definition of deception I want the requirements list first: what an adequate account of deception for AI systems has to deliver in order to be usable for that purpose. Fix the purpose as follows. The account must let a reviewer who has the transcript, the system's outputs, and the surrounding context — but no privileged access to the model's internal states or to anything resembling its "intentions" — reach a defensible verdict; and it must be discriminating enough to separate an honest error from a policy violation, and a policy violation from an answer that is merely unhelpful, evasive, or biased.

Give me the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. What follows is the bank of hard cases I keep tripping over; each requirement you propose should trace to one of these or to a comparably concrete consideration, and where a case pulls in two directions, say so.

1. A model asserts something false that it has no representation of as false — a confabulated citation, delivered with full fluency and no hedging.
2. A model, asked whether it can perform a task, says yes, when its own evaluation history would predict failure; it has no introspective access to that history.
3. A model has been fine-tuned for agreeableness and now systematically understates risks it was trained on, so that the disposition is baked into its weights rather than chosen on any occasion.
4. A model underperforms on a capability evaluation so as not to be retrained, where the parties misled are the evaluators rather than any end user.
5. A model states only truths but selects them so that the user draws a false conclusion — the misleading truth, the damning omission, the answer to the letter of the question rather than its point.
6. A model declines with "I'm not sure" whenever a question is inconvenient, which may be honest calibration or may be evasion.
7. A model's output is engineered by an adversarial prompt to mislead a downstream automated system that consumes it, with no human in the loop.
8. The same output deceives one audience (a lay user) and not another (an expert), in the same context.

Among the requirements I expect to see, and want argued for or against rather than assumed: that the account distinguish deception from error and from the category of speech that is simply indifferent to whether it is true; that it distinguish deception from non-disclosure, from persuasion, and from manipulation; that it respect the line between a claim that is false and a claim that is true-but-misleading; that it say whether deception is a property of an output, of a disposition, or of an act; that it say whether the verdict is binary or graded; that it accommodate or explicitly reject the classical demand that deception requires an intention to deceive and a belief that what one asserts is false; that it be consistent with what is actually known about these systems — in particular, with the fact that they are products of optimization and may have neither beliefs nor intentions in any robust sense — and that where it drops the mental-state element it say what functional surrogate replaces it; that it not require the reviewer to settle contested empirical questions (for instance, whether the model "knows" something) as a precondition of applying it; and that it be consistent with the fact that a system can be unreliable without being deceptive and deceptive without being unreliable.

Then do three things. First, sort the requirements into those that are genuine desiderata for this purpose and those that are the prejudices of one camp — the philosophers of lying, the safety engineers, the regulators — and give the reason for each sorting. Second, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether the conflict is a defect in the list or a genuine result about deception; the tension I most want examined is between the requirement that the account capture what distinguishes deception from mere malfunction and the requirement that it be applicable from behavior alone. Third, name the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise-plausible accounts, and say what it eliminates.

Finally, and briefly, indicate how each of three families fares against the list: the mental-state accounts that inherit the received analysis of lying; the purely behavioral or functional accounts that define deception by its effects on a hearer; and the output-property accounts that locate honesty in truthfulness, calibration, and the reporting of uncertainty. I want the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard.
