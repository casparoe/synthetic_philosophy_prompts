I am a retired natural scientist—forty years in experimental physics—who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of science. I have become interested in the cluster of ideas surrounding Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor, but I do not want a literature review or a list of who said what. I want a constructive document that two opposing camps could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please draft it.

The two camps are these. The Universal-Prior Realists hold that there is a privileged, language-independent notion of simplicity; that it is captured by description length on a universal Turing machine; that Solomonoff induction—the prior assigning to a finite binary string the total weight, over all prefix-free programs that output it, of 2 to the minus the program's length—supplies a canonical prior for prediction; and that Occam's razor is therefore a theorem about inductive inference rather than a mere heuristic. They accept that changing the universal machine changes the prior by at most a constant factor, but they treat that constant as asymptotically negligible and epistemically unimportant. The Finite-Modeler Pragmatists hold that description length is always relative to a chosen encoding and hypothesis class; that the invariance theorem is an asymptotic result whose constants can dominate any finite data set; that for bounded agents simplicity is a family of heuristics indexed to a loss function and a computational budget; and that Occam's razor is a practical norm of model selection, not a rational requirement. They do not deny that algorithmic notions are useful; they deny that they settle the normative question.

I want the joint statement to contain three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can accept, stated precisely enough that neither is quietly conceding its central point. Include what they agree on about the role of the invariance theorem, the status of finite constants, and what would count as a decisive argument against each camp's position. Second, a disagreements table with columns for: the sub-question; the formulation acceptable to the Realists; the formulation acceptable to the Pragmatists; the crux; a test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on that crux; the Realists' prediction; and the Pragmatists' prediction. Third, a short closing section separating genuine disagreements from verbal ones. If any part of the dispute turns on an ambiguity in simplicity, prior, or language-independence, identify it and say what disambiguation each side should accept.

To keep the document from floating free of anything checkable, include the following concrete case, which I have devised and which both camps should treat as relevant. Call it the Two-Encoding Test. Consider a finite binary sequence generated by one of two mechanisms: H-fair, independent fair coin flips, and H-pi, the initial segment of the binary expansion of pi. Let U1 be a universal machine whose instruction set includes a primitive for generating the binary expansion of pi; let U2 be a universal machine that lacks that primitive but can simulate U1 with a fixed overhead. For each machine, define the Solomonoff prior in the standard way. Suppose we observe the first n bits of pi. Compare the universal predictive probability of the next bit under U1 and under U2, and let D(n) be the difference in log odds. Both camps should agree that the case bears on whether the choice of universal machine is epistemically innocent. State what each camp predicts about D(n) as n grows: does it vanish, stay bounded by a machine-dependent constant, or can it be made arbitrarily large at every finite n by choosing machines with large enough overhead? Then say what observation or proof would falsify each camp's prediction. Do not cite any literature; use only the concepts defined here and your own reasoning.

I also want you to add a second, smaller case that isolates the finite-horizon issue without the pi example: a data set that is short under one natural encoding and long under another, with the same likelihoods. Explain which camp's position the case favors and why, and whether the other camp has a principled reply.

Finally, write the whole document as if both camps are signing it. Use neutral section headings. Avoid rhetorical questions, point-scoring, and any suggestion that one side is obviously correct. Do not mention philosophers by name or refer to any published work. The goal is a text that a careful reader from either camp would recognize as fair.
