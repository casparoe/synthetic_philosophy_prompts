I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course in the philosophy of biology, and we have just begun a unit on the species problem. For my discussion section I have been asked to write a dialogue between two proponents of opposing views on whether species are real features of the natural world or human-made partitions of it, and I would like you to draft that dialogue for me. Please write it in American English.

Both speakers should be fully competent researchers; neither should be a strawman, and neither should be handed an easy victory. I want the exchange to be genuine back-and-forth, in which each responds to the specific point the other has just made rather than proceeding through a prewritten list of talking points.

To keep the dialogue from becoming a rehearsal of the standard textbook examples, please construct a single fictional case and have the two speakers reason about it together. Invent the organism; do not use any real species complex, so that the argument turns on the concepts rather than on remembered facts about a particular organism. I have in mind something like the following, though you may adjust the details as long as they stay concrete: a group of populations of a small freshwater invertebrate inhabiting a chain of lakes, in which (a) two lineages meet and interbreed freely in the lakes where they overlap while occupying clearly distinct ecological niches, (b) a third lineage arose by hybridization between those two, is now reproductively isolated from both of them, yet is not monophyletic with respect to either, and (c) a fourth lineage reproduces asexually and undergoes no recombination at all. Different criteria — reproductive isolation, ecological distinctness, monophyly, and overall genetic divergence — cut the group into different numbers of units.

The first speaker defends a realist conception: species are real historical entities, segments of the tree of life individuated by lineage continuity and branching, and the fact that our criteria disagree is a discovery about a messy world rather than a sign that we are simply making the boundaries up. The second defends a pluralist and pragmatic conception: "species" names a family of human-made divisions, each useful in some research contexts, and there is no single fact of the matter about how many species the case contains.

Please make sure the dialogue reaches the following, and that each speaker has something substantive to say about each: whether the question of how many species the case contains has a determinate answer or is underspecified; the distinction between the species category and particular species taxa; the proposal that species are individuals rather than classes, and whether accepting that proposal resolves the dispute or merely restates it; whether the pluralist can answer the charge that the word "species" then means something different in every research context, and whether that is a defect or simply a fact about a useful tool; and what each speaker would count as evidence or argument against their own position.

Please do not manufacture a synthesis at the end. If the speakers reach agreement, it should be because the arguments earned it; if they remain apart, the dialogue should make clear exactly where and why.
