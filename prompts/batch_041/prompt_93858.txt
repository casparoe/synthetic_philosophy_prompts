ok so I'm setting the final for an upper-level seminar on Kant's practical philosophy and I need one essay question for it -- 90 minutes, closed book, 25 marks -- plus a model answer and a marking scheme I can actually grade with. The topic is the animals material. My problem is that every single year half the class writes "Kant says we have no direct duties to animals, only indirect duties grounded in duties to ourselves, because cruelty to animals coarsens our feelings towards humans" and then just sits there rephrasing that for two pages. I want a question that punishes that move. Also I don't want them citing anything, so the question has to be answerable from what they know plus the setup I give them.

Here's the angle. One of my students (call her Priya) came to office hours with a fix she thinks rescues Kant, and I want the exam built around assessing it. Her claim: the standard reconstruction of the indirect-duty argument leans on a contingent empirical premise -- that being cruel to animals makes you more likely to be cruel to people, or at least blunts your sympathetic feeling -- and that premise is doing all the wrong kind of work, because if the psychology turned out false for some particular person the duty would just evaporate, and something that disappears when an empirical premise fails isn't a duty, it's advice. So she wants to keep the indirect *structure* and swap out the *ground*. On her reading, treating a creature that can suffer as a mere thing is itself an expression of a will that has failed to recognise value where value is actually present, and that failure is a failure with respect to the agent's own moral standing, since that standing just *is* the capacity to recognise and respond to value. So the wrongness is non-contingent -- no downstream coarsening required. She calls it a 'constitutive' rather than a 'causal' reading of the indirect duty.

I want the essay question to force them to (i) lay out the structure of Kant's actual position accurately enough that I can tell whether they've read it, in seperate layers rather than as one slogan, (ii) work out whether Priya's repair is even available to Kant or whether it quietly converts an indirect duty into a direct one -- or into something else, aesthetic self-regard, an expressive account of wrongness, whatever -- and (iii) say what Kant gives up if he takes the repair, and whether there's a reading on which he doesn't have to take it. And I want the question to make them deal with the epistemic half of Kant's view, the bit about how we know what's going on in other minds and why the very inference that gets us to the pain of the person next to us seems to get us to the pain of the dog. That's where the position is most exposed and it's the part they always skip. If a student can write this whole thing without ever mentioning that symmetry, they've missed the point.

So please give me, in this order: the question as I'd hand it to them, then a model answer of about 600-700 words at the level I'd want from a strong student, then a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific errors that should cost marks. No vague rubric lines like 'shows understanding of the material'. Tell me where a competent-but-not-top answer lands versus a top one, and make the total add up to 25. Also -- I'll be grading alot of these in one sitting, so the scheme should let me tell quickly whether the student took a position and defended it, or just listed the objections and sat on the fence.
