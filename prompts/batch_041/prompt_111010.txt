I belong to a small reading group that works through philosophy-of-AI material, and we have spent the last several sessions on a proposal I want to pin down before we argue about it any further. The proposal, usually referred to as HCH, is offered as a specification of what it would mean for a system to do the right thing: not whatever a human happens to say, but the verdict a human would reach given unlimited time and cooperation, where any sub-question may be handed off to another human — or to a copy of the original human — and the sub-answers are then aggregated back into an answer to the original question. The companion idea, sometimes called factored cognition, is the hypothesis that this can actually be carried out, because a hard task admits of decomposition into subtasks that a single bounded human can check, so that the whole structure ultimately rests on steps that are individually verifiable. What draws us to it is that the target is neither raw human opinion nor some alien optimum; what frustrates us is that no one has said clearly what such a target would have to be in order to deserve that role.

Our disagreements have begun to repeat themselves. One of us says the proposal is circular, because "what a human would say given unlimited time and cooperation" appears to be defined by the very process under discussion, so the account has no content until the process is fixed; another says the real load is borne by an unstated assumption that every sub-agent is honest, and that once you drop it the whole thing is manipulable; a third says that even if it works it may converge on something monstrous, and that a target we would not endorse on reflection cannot be what we should be aiming at; and a fourth observes that for some questions no bounded human can verify an answer at all, so the structure either has nothing to say or quietly smuggles in an oracle.

So I would like the desiderata rather than a defense. Write the specification that a theory of idealized deliberation must satisfy to count as a legitimate account of what we should be aiming at: the cases it has to get right (the regress case, in which every sub-question spawns further sub-questions and the process must either bottom out or converge; the deception case, in which a sub-agent misreports; the convergence-to-something-we-reject case; and the case of a question whose answer no bounded human can actually check); the distinctions it must not collapse (between what the idealization converges on and what we would endorse on reflection; between what a bounded human can verify and what a bounded human can compute; and between the idealization being well-defined and its being reachable); and the results it must be consistent with (that human beings are bounded, biased, and unreliable, and that no physical process enjoys unlimited time or energy). Behind each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which as the commitments of one camp rather than another. I am most interested in the part where you identify a pair or a subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list comes out consistent, I will assume we have not sharpened it enough — and in your judgment about which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the proposals people actually make. Leave the scorecard aside; we will fill that in ourselves.
