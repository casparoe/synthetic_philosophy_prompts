I have come to think that the right response to Fitch's paradox -- the derivation that turns the claim that every truth is knowable into the claim that some truths cannot be known, by instantiating the knowability claim at a proposition of the form, p and it is not known that p -- is to deny that knowledge is closed under conjunction. One can, I want to say, know each of two things without knowing their conjunction, as the lottery case suggests; once agglomeration is given up, the derivation loses its grip, and the anti-realist keeps the knowability principle intact. Tell me what counts against this response.

I am interested in three sorts of consideration. First, the formal ones: can the derivation be rerun from weaker resources, for instance from the distribution of knowledge over known entailment together with the assumption that logical truths are known, in which case the conjunction step returns as a theorem and the denial costs considerably more than it buys -- and if so, what exactly must be surrendered, and is the resulting notion of knowledge still strong enough to do the semantic work the knowability principle was supposed to do? Second, the intuitive and linguistic ones: what do our ordinary judgments about conjunctive knowledge ascriptions tell for and against the denial, does the denial also dispose of the lottery and preface puzzles or merely relocate them, and is the move independently motivated or ad hoc? Third, the dialectical ones: what would a defender of the response concede as counting against it rather than dismiss as a critic's talking point, and what does the response predict that rival treatments of the paradox do not?

Sort these by how decisive they are, from considerations that would sink the response to those that are merely awkward, and say plainly whether the response leaves the knowability principle exposed to anything at all or has quietly insulated it -- and if it has, whether that is a defect in the response or simply what a principle of this kind looks like when it is defended. I would rather be told that the response is ad hoc than be reassured that it works. Please write the answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter to a colleague who already knows the literature.
