I chair the research ethics committee of a mid-sized NHS trust. We meet once a month for two hours, roughly fifteen minutes an application, and decide whether each study before us may proceed, may proceed subject to amendments, or must be refused. Our decisions are recorded, summarised publicly, and can be appealed to the trust board, so a mistaken approval can expose patients to harm and a mistaken refusal can stop research that would have helped them. There are twelve of us: clinicians from several specialties, a statistician, a pharmacist, a lawyer, two lay members and a research nurse. Attendance is uneven; I decide when to cut off discussion and put something to a vote.

A colleague recently told me that Condorcet's jury theorem shows that where each member is more likely than not to get a question right, and members form their views independently, a majority of a body like ours is overwhelmingly likely to be right, and that this is the real justification for what we do. I am not convinced, and I would rather have a procedure than a justification. I have also heard two claims I cannot square with each other or with the theorem as it was explained to me: that adding a member helps only if that member is better than a coin toss on the question actually before us, and that where one member is far more reliable than the rest the best rule may be to follow that member alone. I do not know what either would mean for lay members voting on a statistical question, or for the fact that two of my members trained in the same department and work from the same risk-assessment template.

Please write a procedure my committee could actually run on an application. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal, and where it must stop and hand the decision to me as chair, or back to the committee as a matter of policy rather than of fact, because the question is one of values rather than of truth. It should say how many members should vote, whether votes count equally, whether we should poll members in writing before discussion and again afterwards, when unanimity is required and when a simple majority will do, how abstentions are treated, and what we should minute so that the record is honest about which parts of a decision were findings and which were choices.

It must also handle three things that happen to us regularly: applications with more than two possible verdicts; applications where we agree on every factual point but disagree on the verdict, or disagree on the facts but agree on the verdict; and members whose expertise is beside the point, or worse than useless, on the question being put, such as the lay member asked to judge a sample-size calculation or the statistician asked to judge whether a consent conversation would feel coercive.

Then run the procedure on three applications: a first-in-human trial of a device in children, in which the risk estimate is contested and the members disagree about the statistics; a study that is methodologically sound and low-risk but duplicates research already done; and a study whose science is sound but which requires deceiving participants. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will not settle, and the cases where I should stop treating the committee's verdict as a finding rather than a decision. No citations or reading lists, please; this has to be usable by members with no training in any of it, and written in British English.
