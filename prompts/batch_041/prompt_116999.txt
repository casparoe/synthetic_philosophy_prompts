ok, wierd request, but I need a parody for a project. Give me the terms of service — exactly five numbered clauses, each one or two sentences, no preamble before clause 1 and no sign-off after clause 5 — for a fine-art storage and shipping outfit that bills its clients per object.

Here's the whole engine of the joke: their rates are per object, their claims department has to decide what got destroyed when something goes wrong, and so the company's lawyers have been forced to take a public position on the statue and the lump of clay. They are completely calm about it. That's the funny part. The clauses should read like real insurance fine print — flat, numbered, no hedging, no winking, nothing that says "of course this is absurd."

A few constraints, and they matter:

Don't name a single philosopher or a named position, and don't use the phrases "material constitution" or "coincident objects." The comedy has to come from the clauses being unnervingly precise about things nobody asked them to be precise about.

But the metaphysics has to be the real stuff, not vibes. A reader who knows the debate should be able to spot, across the five clauses, roughly in this order: (a) that there is a lump and there is a statue and right now they have exactly the same parts, and the company treats them as two separate billable items; (b) the squash/flattening thing — the lump can survive being mashed into a pancake and the statue can't, and the company has drawn a contractual line there; (c) whether a thing survives losing a bit or being repaired, and which of the two survives what; (d) what happens if you cast two statues out of the one lump — how many objects are on the invoice, and whether the lump is still around to be charged for; (e) the part that should be hard: if the statue and the lump have all the same parts right now, what exactly is the statue made of, and what makes it a statue, and why do they have different fates. Don't label any of these. Just let the clauses assume them.

At least one clause has to show the pricing consequence of whatever they decided, and at least one has to show the claims consequence — what they pay out for when the work is "destroyed" but the material is perfectly fine, or the reverse.

Keep it tight, don't pad. Someone who's never heard of any of this should still find it funny as a company that has gone quietly insane in an extremely orderly way, and someone who has heard of it should think it's fair rather than sloppy. Also don't name the company Theseus anything, that's the cheap joke.
