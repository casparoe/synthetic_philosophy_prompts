I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds projects under the heading equality of opportunity. We have a new proposal to decide, and I want to know what our own past decisions commit us to. Here is how I would reconstruct our precedents.

(1) We funded a costly wheelchair-accessible van for a man with a spinal injury. His self-reported life satisfaction was already above average and the van did not raise it much. We funded it because it restored his ability to get to work and to visit friends without assistance.

(2) We declined to fund a cosmetic dental procedure for a woman whose self-esteem and reported happiness were very low because of her teeth. The procedure would have raised her subjective well-being substantially. We said we were not in the business of buying happiness, and that her objective situation was not one of deprivation in the relevant sense.

(3) We funded a sign-language interpreter for a deaf graduate student even though a cheaper real-time captioning service would have produced the same exam scores and the same reported satisfaction. We reasoned that the interpreter let her participate in seminars in the way hearing students do.

(4) We declined to fund a life-coaching program for a low-income man who said his life felt meaningless, because the program would have improved his mood but not his material or health circumstances. We told him to reapply if he could show a change in what he could do.

(5) We funded a tutoring program that raised the reading level of the worst-off children in the district, even though it did not close the gap with the best-off and in fact the best-off children pulled further ahead in mathematics through a separate program.

(6) We declined a proposal that would have equalized the budgets of two schools exactly, because it would have cut the budget of the poorer school, which was already below what we considered a decent minimum.

(7) We funded an interpreter and a personal care assistant for a woman with a degenerative condition who reported that she had made peace with her limitations and did not feel deprived. We said her reported contentment was not decisive.

The new proposal is a request for a very expensive treatment for a young woman with a severe facial disfigurement. The disfigurement leaves her below our threshold in the affiliation dimension: she is socially isolated, avoids public life, and reports intense shame. The treatment would bring her to the threshold in affiliation and would also greatly improve her reported well-being. A cheaper alternative would use the same money to provide mobility devices to four people who are below the threshold in bodily integrity, bringing each of them to the threshold. We cannot fund both.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which precedent is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say what principle we would be adopting with each choice. I am not asking for a survey of theories of distributive justice; I want your best reconstruction of our own implicit rule. I would like you to say how confident you are in your reading of that rule, and to single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
