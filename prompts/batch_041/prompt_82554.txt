I have never studied philosophy formally, but I have been reading Plato's Laws on my own, and I keep returning to the one proposal in it that most readers seem to pass over: that a city should deliberately hold supervised drinking parties, not for pleasure but as an instrument of civic education. His thought, as I understand it, is that wine is a cheap testing-ground for character. Gold is tested in fire, and a person is tested in drink. Wine does not create new vices so much as amplify whatever is already there — pleasure, anger, love, and above all the loss of shame — so a well-run party lets a city see what its citizens are actually like without the expense and danger of a war. He also suggests wine can be a remedy for the rigidity of old age, softening the soul so that older men can be re-educated and can sing and dance in choruses again, which he treats as the real business of a well-ordered city.

What I would like is a satirical but completely straight-faced document: an official FAQ or service brochure from a municipal Office of Supervised Drinking, in a city that has actually adopted this proposal and taken it seriously. The humour should come from getting the doctrine right, so that someone who knows the Laws laughs rather than winces. Please include, without ever winking at the reader: the requirement that the presiding officer be sober, and what happens when the office cannot fill the post; the distinction between the party as a test and the party as a training; the awkward fact that the technique is supposed to work only on people who have already been well brought up, which makes it a credentialing system as much as an educational one; the age gate that excludes the young on the grounds that one does not add fire to fire; the two separate pass-or-fail dimensions of courage and shame; and the bureaucratic difficulty of an agency whose stated mission is temperance but whose method is intoxication. Around 600 to 700 words, in the register of a deadpan civic pamphlet. No quotations or citations are needed — I care about the argument, not the wording.
