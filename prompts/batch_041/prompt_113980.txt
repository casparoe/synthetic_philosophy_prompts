I chair the integrity committee of a mid-sized international NGO that runs maternal-health programs in about a dozen countries, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting on a case that has split the committee cleanly in two. Our largest local partner in one country is a clinic network with an excellent record on the measures our donors actually pay for; its founding director, who is also its principal fundraiser and effectively its owner, has for years accepted cash gifts, vehicle use, and meals from the two companies that supply its equipment, and this year he steered a no-bid contract to one of them. An external auditor working from our lead donor's standard anti-bribery template has flagged the whole arrangement as fraud and recommends that we terminate the partnership and refer the director to the national authorities. The director does not deny any of the facts. He says that in his country nothing of consequence is decided between strangers, that the two suppliers are his late brother's in-laws and his own cousins, that the cash he received went into the same clinic account that pays the nurses, and that a formal arm's-length tender among family firms would be read by everyone from the ministry down as a bizarre and insulting performance. Two of our local staff have signed a letter saying much the same; our European board member replies that this is the excuse every corrupt official in the world makes, that the template's gift threshold is roughly a month of a nurse's salary locally, and that if we accept the director's account we may as well not have an integrity policy. I have read enough blog posts about principal-agent problems and collective-action traps to be dangerous, and I keep noticing that those two framings seem to recommend opposite remedies for exactly this situation, which is part of why I don't trust my own first reaction.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a concrete recommendation -- terminate, suspend with conditions, require restitution and a restructured procurement process, or something in between -- with a timeline and the specific conditions attached, and the reasoning a reasonable board member could act on. I want the director's position engaged seriously rather than treated as a pretext, because I genuinely cannot tell whether the concept of corruption we are applying is a universal standard or a local custom of one part of the world that we happened to write into our donor agreements. Three things I would like you to work through rather than gesture at: whether bribery here is better modeled as a principal-agent problem or as a collective-action problem, and what follows for the remedy if it is the latter; whether a gift that is reciprocated inside a closed community and ends up in a shared fund is doing the same work as a payment made to a stranger to divert a public good, or whether the resemblance is superficial; and whether "everyone does it" is a mitigating fact about the individual or the very thing that makes the practice hardest to dislodge. If your honest conclusion is that our policy encodes one moral tradition among several, say so plainly -- but then tell me what an organization with its donors in one place and its beneficiaries in another can actually do on Friday.
