Bit of an odd one. I retired two years ago after thirty-odd years of sensory physiology on insects — locusts mostly, wind hairs, that sort of thing — and I've spent the time since trying to teach myself philosophy, badly but enthusiatically. Now my old institute has asked me to take one of their lunchtime sessions, fifty minutes, and I've picked the question of whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer. The room will be about fifteen people: a handful of ML PhD students, two or three biologists, and one philosopher of mind who I'm told is coming and who will almost certainly want to argue with me. Nobody has any formal philosophy. No readings — I can't send them anything and half of them wouldn't do it anyway.

Don't explain the topic to me. Plan how I should explain it.

Here's my rough sense of the pieces, but you tell me the order and why. There's what a reward signal actually is in RL — a scalar, credit assignment, discounting, policy shaping, the whole business of it being a number that gets backpropagated into weights. There's what I mean by suffering or valence in the first place — felt quality, or a functional state, or a disposition to approach and avoid, which are not the same thing and I keep sliding between them. There's the inference problem: from behaviour and mechanism to experience, which is where I get stuck. And then the candidate bridge, which is what I'm actually interested in: if valence just is the functional role of a signal that makes some states be sought and others avoided, then an RL agent has it and that's that; if valence requires something more, I need to know what more, and whether I can even state it without cheating. What has to come first? What depends on what? I have a feeling the whole thing hinges on which sense of valence I hand them first, but I can't see whether that should be the opening move or the payoff.

The carrying example matters and I don't trust my instincts. The obvious moves are a gridworld mouse with a shock penalty, an Atari agent, or a robot with a hand-coded pain-avoidance routine. Tell me which one to use, or give me something better, and tell me what the example has to have and what it has to lack — I want it to be a case where the question is genuinely open rather than one that settles itself by construction.

Misconceptions: I'm guessing they'll arrive thinking a negative reward is just pain, and that a big negative reward is a lot of pain; that programming avoidance is the same as producing suffering; and, from the ML side, that because it's all matrix multiplication there's obviously nothing there. Also, from the biologists, that you need a nervous system and that's the end of it. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off, and what do I do about the philosopher, who I suspect will accuse me of the fallacy of the whole argument at the first possible moment?

One question to ask at the end that would actually show me whether the distinction landed rather than just whether they can repeat it back. And tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level — I'd rather say "not today" out loud than gesture at something I half-know.

Last thing: I'd like the plan to come with a note on where it's most likely to fail — which step is the shakiest, and what consideration would make you reorder the sequence or drop my example. If the honest answer is that the order doesn't matter much because the audience will get stuck on the inference problem regardless, say that instead of arranging things neatly.
