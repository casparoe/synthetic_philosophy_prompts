I teach a graduate seminar on the explanation of perpetrators, and this term I'm closing the unit on Browning's Ordinary Men and Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners with a session on Jonathan Glazer's The Zone of Interest, his 2023 film loosely adapted from Martin Amis's novel about the household Rudolf Höss kept beside the camp at Auschwitz. I want a critical reading of the film through the Browning-Goldhagen dispute, not a summary of either. The shape of the dispute as my students have it: Browning's reservists are middle-aged, working-class, unremarkable men from Hamburg who were offered the chance to step out of the firing squads and mostly declined it, and his explanation runs through obedience, peer pressure, the momentum of a killing unit, and the brutalizing drift of an occupation — with antisemitism present but not doing the decisive work; Goldhagen, using much the same battalion, insists that the explanation has to be the eliminationist antisemitism he takes to be constitutive of German political culture, so that the men killed because of what they and their countrymen already believed, and Browning's situationalism becomes a way of letting the killers off. My students tend to think this is a fight about evidence, and I want the film used to show them that it is at least as much a fight about what an explanation of a person is supposed to look like — what it is licensed to leave out. To make that concrete, I've been pressing a distinction on them all term: between the ordinariness of the deed, meaning that the killing fits into a schedule, a career, and a day's work, and the ordinariness of the doer, meaning that the killer is unremarkable, unselected, interchangeable. I've argued that Browning's title slides between the two and that the slide does much of the argumentative work, since a routine deed performed by an interchangeable man is exactly what a situational explanation needs and exactly what Goldhagen denies. I suspect the film is an experiment in pulling the two apart and seeing what is left, and I want you to tell me whether that is what it is doing or whether I am reading my own seminar into Glazer.

Read it, then, against both books and against the third possibility, which is Kelman's three processes — authorization, routinization, and dehumanization — and which I mention because it is the framework my students reach for when they want to escape the Browning-Goldhagen binary without quite knowing what they are doing. Attend in particular to the film's formal choices as though they were arguments: the camera that stays inside the garden while the atrocity happens off-frame and reaches us mostly as sound, so that the household's ordinary goods — the wall, the river, the garden, the gifts of prisoners' belongings, the children's swim — are literally adjacent to the killing; the visit of Hedwig's mother, who is delighted by what her daughter has, and who leaves without explanation after seeing the crematoria burning at night; the Polish girl who hides food at the work sites and later plays sheet music she has found, which is the one place I can see the film granting anything like an outside; the sequence in Berlin in which Höss retches on the stairwell, and the cut from that to the present-day cleaners at the museum; and the phone call in which he says he spent the party thinking about the most efficient way to gas the guests. I want you to say which of these scenes a Browning reading can absorb, which a Goldhagen reading can absorb, and which neither can — and, since a good reading has to be honest in both directions, where the film resists the philosophical use I am putting it to. Does the retching, or the coda, or the girl, smuggle back a conscience, a witness, or an aesthetic redemption that the rest of the film has deliberately withheld, and if so is that a failure of nerve or a genuine third position? Does the film's refusal to psychologize its perpetrators amount to a theory of them — something like a claim that the relevant fact about Höss is the successful administration of attention rather than either his beliefs or his circumstances — or is it an evasion of theory dressed as austerity? And at the end I want your judgment on what the film adds to this debate that Browning's and Goldhagen's books cannot, if anything, since I would rather be told the film is doing something else entirely than be handed a tidy synthesis.

Work from what you know of the film and the two books; I do not need citations, a reading list, or quotations, and I would rather you flag a detail you are unsure of than invent one. Begin with your thesis about what the film does with this dispute in the first sentence, then argue for it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
