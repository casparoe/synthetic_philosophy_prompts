Im working through Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, book IV ch. 7, the part where he asks what Europe actually got out of discovering and colonizing America. The easy bits I follow (more trade, wider markets), but this passage has me stuck:

"Those great events may even have contributed to increase the enjoyments, and to augment the industry of countries which not only never sent any commodities to America, but never received any from it. Even such countries may have received a greater abundance of other commodities from countries of which the surplus produce had been augmented by means of the American trade. This greater abundance, as it must necessarily have increased their enjoyments, so it must likewise have augmented their industry. A greater number of new equivalents of some kind or other must have been presented to them to be exchanged for the surplus produce of that industry. A more extensive market must have been created for that surplus produce so as to raise its value, and thereby encourage its increase."

A few paragraphs earlier he puts it more bluntly: "Those commodities of America are new values, new equivalents, introduced into Hungary and Poland to be exchanged there for the surplus produce of those countries."

What's the actual causal chain he has in mind? Hungary and Poland never sent anything to America and never got anything from it, so where does the extra demand for their surplus produce come from, and what work is "new equivalents" doing that "greater abundance" isn't already doing? Is there a subtler point about money and prices in there, or is this hand-waving? And then he turns around and calls the exclusive trade of the mother countries "a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind" — how are those two claims supposed to fit together? Walk me through it like I'm reasonably smart but rusty on 18th century economics.
