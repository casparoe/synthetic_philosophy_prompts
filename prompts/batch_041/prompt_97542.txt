The idea is this. The contrast between goal-directed and approval-directed agents is usually drawn by asking what the agent is trying to bring about: a goal-directed agent optimizes an objective in the world, whereas an approval-directed agent acts so as to secure the approval of some evaluator, typically a human or a model of one. The advertised payoff is that approval-directed agents are supposed to be more honest and more corrigible, because what they pursue is a judgment rather than a state of affairs. I suspect the contrast is being drawn along the wrong axis. What matters may be less whether the target is a world-state or a judgment than whether the target is something the agent can itself influence. An agent optimizing a fixed external signal it cannot reach is safe for a boring reason; an agent optimizing a human's judgment can manipulate that judgment; an agent optimizing a world-state can manipulate the world. If that is right, then the honesty and corrigibility arguments are tracking the agent's causal access to its own success condition, not the fact that the success condition is approval-shaped. I would like to make this precise by distinguishing at least three cases: a signal that is causally unreachable by the agent; a signal that is reachable but enters the agent's behavior only through a fixed, non-optimizing function, as when an agent merely takes advice as one input among others; and a signal that is reachable and optimized against. I want to know whether the standard arguments for approval-directedness actually require the second or third case, and whether they survive when the evaluator is itself approval-directed, which raises the question of whose approval is being sought at the next level up.
