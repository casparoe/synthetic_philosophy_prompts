I have been arguing with two friends about aquaculture — one works on the hatchery side of a shrimp farm, the other is a chef who has lately become fixated on how we treat crustaceans — and the argument has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. What struck me is that the same industry, sometimes under the same corporate roof, runs three very different regimes for animals that are, on the face of it, comparably alien to us. Broodstock shrimp in hatcheries routinely have one eyestalk cut off, since ablation is the standard way to induce the females to mature; the animals survive the operation and are kept for further spawning cycles, and no one in the industry treats this as a scandal. At the other end of the supply chain, the live lobster and crab trade is under real pressure: a growing number of jurisdictions and retailers now insist that these animals be stunned or otherwise rendered insensible before being killed, on the reasoning that boiling them alive is cruel. And in between, farmed fish are commonly killed by being chilled in an ice slurry or gassed with carbon dioxide, methods that most welfare codes tolerate for fish even though no code would tolerate them for a mammal of comparable size. My two friends draw opposite lessons from the pattern: the chef says the operative criterion is simply whether the animal looks like us, while the farmer says the rules track evidence, and that we do not yet know what, if anything, a shrimp experiences. To make the disagreement sharper, the farmer has proposed a rule he would like to see in a certification scheme: any animal with a centralized brain receives the full battery of protections, and anything without one receives none.

What I want from you is the implicit philosophy rather than a verdict on who is right. Reconstruct the theory of welfare, of mind, and of moral standing that this pattern of practice presupposes — what a creature would have to be like for the pattern to hang together — and then separate what the pattern strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Is the operative line taxonomic (vertebrates against invertebrates), neuroanatomical (a centralized brain, a cortex, some threshold of integration), behavioral (evidence of a particular kind of response to damage), or managerial (whether the practice is visible to consumers and voters)? Which rival views of welfare does the pattern rule out, and which does it leave open — hedonism, desire-based accounts, views on which death rather than pain is the relevant harm, views on which nociception without suffering is morally inert? I am particularly interested in whether the three practices are consistent with one another or whether they presuppose incompatible criteria, and in whether the farmer's centralized-brain rule is a principled refinement of what the industry already does or a rationalization of it.

Finally, the part I care most about: suppose we replaced whatever criterion is actually operative with a straightforward sentience criterion — roughly, that any capacity for suffering counts for something, whatever the phylogeny and whatever the anatomy — and then ask what would have to change in each of the three practices. For each, say whether the change would mean abandoning the practice outright or merely anaesthetizing it first, and where the sentience criterion itself runs out of guidance because it tells us nothing about how to weigh a shrimp's suffering against a hatchery's yield.
