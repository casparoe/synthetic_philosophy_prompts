I am a high school senior in a civics seminar, and I am writing a paper on the paradox of voting. My classmate Priya has spent the year giving me her verdicts on whether it was rational for her to vote in a series of elections, and I want to predict what she will say about one more case. Her verdicts so far are these. In a presidential election in a state that everyone knew would go to her party, she voted and said it was rational, because she wanted to stand with her neighbors and because she would have felt she was neglecting a civic responsibility. In a school board election that was decided by two votes, she voted and said it was rational, because her vote might have decided the outcome and the stakes were high. In a national referendum in a country where she was not eligible, she said that if she had been eligible she would not have voted, because she had no strong preference and no sense of duty. In a town meeting on a zoning rule she cared about, where the vote was unanimous and only advisory, she voted and said it was rational, because she wanted to register her view even though the outcome was already settled. In a student council election where her preferred candidate was far ahead and she was busy, she did not vote and said it was rational, because there was no chance her vote would matter and she felt no expressive pull. In a general election where she was indifferent but her parents expected her to vote, she voted and said it was rational, because of duty and family expectation. I have been calling reasons of the first kind lever reasons and reasons of the second kind token reasons, though I am not sure those are the right labels.

Next week there is a city council election. The outcome will decide funding for arts programs, which Priya cares about, but her preferred candidate is trailing by a large margin in every poll. There is a visible social media campaign urging people to vote and a sticker for voters, but voting will take an hour in the rain, and Priya has said she does not feel a strong civic duty this time. She has a moderate attachment to the candidate and to the arts program, but she is not passionate.

I would like you to predict Priya's verdict. Please identify the rule she seems to be running, give at least two other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, say what each of those rules predicts about the city council election, and tell me which of the rules you think best explains her and why. Then name a further case, not the one above, that would best tell the rules apart. I am less interested in whether her verdict is correct than in the structure of her reasoning, and I would like the answer to be explicit about where the rules agree and where they diverge. If you think she is better understood as having no single rule but a weighted mixture of several, say so, and tell me what mixture fits her track record.
