I'm a retired physiologist — thirty-odd years teaching renal and respiratory physiology to medical students, took the pension a few years back, and since then I've been reading philosophy of medicine in a slightly obsessive way; my wife calls it a mid-life crisis with footnotes. I've ended up with a view I'm presenting at a reading group run out of the bioethics centre at the university in town next month, and what I want from you is help with the question period, not with the talk. The view, roughly: the concept of disease does have a descriptive core — something like statistically subnormal functioning of a part or process relative to a reference class — so the naturalists (Boorse's biostatistical thing, I think that's what it's called) are right that "disease" isn't simply a value judgement in a lab coat. But the reference class is not handed to us by biology. Somebody chooses it, and the choice is where the values sneak in. And I want to push a distinction I haven't seen put this sharply: there are two quite different reference classes in play, a design class (what the part is for, what it did in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness, whatever the optimality story turns out to be) and a population class (what the part actually does in the people in front of you, age- and sex-matched or not). Nearly every live fight in the field, I think, is a fight about which class to use, conducted as if it were a fight about facts versus values. Blood pressure thresholds, "prediabetes", whether the fact that the large majority of young men in some East Asian cities are now short-sighted means short-sightedness has stopped being a disease, whether ageing is a disease — all of these, on my account, are reference-class disputes wearing a costume. I'd rather not have to defend the whole edifice live; I'd rather know what's coming at me.

The room is a bit of a zoo, which is why I'm asking. About half are from the bioethics centre — normativist by temperament, they've read a lot of Foucauldian stuff about medicalisation and the sick role, deeply suspicious of anything that sounds like letting biology adjudicate what counts as illness, and they'll hear my "descriptive core" as a power play dressed up as a definition. Then there are two philosophers of biology from the other side of campus who are card-carrying naturalists; they know the biostatistical literature backwards and they'll go straight for the details — is my "design class" just the old evolutionary teleology in a new hat, do I have any principled way of picking the reference class that doesn't smuggle in the answer, and why should the fact that we can't agree on the class not just be an arguement for the normativists. There's also a consultant physician who's come along because she sits on the ethics committee and mainly wants to know whether any of this changes what she does on a Tuesday morning, and a historian of medicine who will say, politely, that all this was settled in the nineteenth century and I'm reinventing the wheel. It's a friendly crowd but not a shy one.

So what I'd like is a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from these people, phrased in their own vocabularies, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the position rather than by how likely they are to come up. For each one, a two-minute reply I can actually deliver out loud, and a note on where that reply is soft — I'd much rather know the weak joints in advance than have them found for me. Tell me what to concede up front (I'm happy to give away more than I need to), what to hold, and which single reply has to land for the thing to survive at all. And tell me the question I'm definately hoping nobody asks — I think I know what it is, but I want to see whether you land on the same one. Write it in American English, please; I read enough of it these days that British spelling in a document like this throws me off when I'm rehearsing.
