I've been reporting on psychiatry and neuroscience for going on eight years now, and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia -- the condition where patients insist they feel the pain, they can localize it, rate its intensity, describe its quality, but it just doesn't bother them. They'll laugh, shrug, sometimes keep doing the thing that's hurting them. There's a philosophical argument that gets built on cases like this: that the badness of pain isn't the same thing as its hurtfulness, that the sensory dimension and the unpleasant/affective dimension can come apart, and that the dissociation tells us something about what pain's awfulness actually consists in. I want to write about this, but every time I sit down with the philosophy I find myself mentally translating it into the language I actually think in, which is reinforcement learning and computational psychiatry -- reward and punishment signals, valence, prediction errors, aversive conditioning, incentive salience versus hedonic 'liking', policies and cost functions, Pavlovian versus instrumental control. I know that vocabulary well enough to be dangerous: I can get through a modeling paper without drowning, I've interviewed people who build these models, but I'm not a modeler myself and I know I'll miss things.
