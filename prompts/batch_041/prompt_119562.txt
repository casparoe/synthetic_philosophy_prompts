I teach a graduate seminar on Plato's metaphysics, and the final meeting is given over to the Republic's two great images, the cave and the divided line, which my students reliably treat as two drawings of one doctrine rather than as two very differently built pictures. I want to close the session by reading them a poem, and I want the poem's architecture to be the argument: when I read it aloud, they should hear what the diagrams are for. So I am commissioning something quite specific, and I would rather you over-engineer the structure than decorate it.

Adopt the role of a poet with a classicist's command of the Republic — someone who could write this poem and then defend its scaffolding to a philologist. Write it in four movements, in the voice of someone who has made the ascent and is about to be sent back down. The movements must be unequal, and their proportions must reproduce the divided line as it is usually reconstructed: a line first cut into two unequal parts, and then each part cut again in the same proportion, which makes the four segments stand to one another as 1 : 2 : 2 : 4 when the outer cut is 1 : 2. Two consequences follow, and both must be audible in the poem. The second and third movements must be exactly equal in length, and the fourth must be four times as long as the first. Choose whatever unit of lines you like so that the whole poem lands somewhere between twenty-five and forty lines. The four movements answer, in order, to the four conditions of the soul Plato attaches to the segments: the lowest to shadows and reflections taken for the real (the prisoners' condition, and by extension anyone who mistakes a likeness for its original); the second to plain perceptual belief about the ordinary things that cast those shadows (the fire, the puppets, the low wall, the people who carry them); the third to the kind of reasoning that treats its starting points as hypotheses and works downward from them, borrowing visible figures as aids (the geometer's drawn square, which is not the square itself); and the fourth to the dialectical climb that treats hypotheses as steps and looks for something that is not a hypothesis at all — the fire's own source, the sun, and whatever stands to the intelligible world as the sun stands to the visible one.

Because the two middle segments come out equal, I want those two movements built as mirror images of each other: the same syntax, the same length, the same governing image, but running in opposite directions — the second moving from image to the thing imaged, the third from a borrowed figure to the principle it presupposes — so that the equality is not a piece of arithmetic but something the ear registers. The fourth movement should turn back on the first, taking up the first movement's central image and showing what it was a likeness of; and it may end with the descent back into the cave and the danger awaiting whoever returns, since the Republic does not let the philosopher stay in the light. Aim for a poem in which a reader who knows the divided line can identify all four segments without being told which is which, and a reader who has never seen the diagram still finds a poem rather than a table of contents. Do not lean on the tradition's vocabulary: no Greek, no capitalized Forms, no naming of Plato — let the images carry the philosophy. The single exception is that the last movement may name, once, the thing of which the sun is an image. Before you draft, spend a few sentences in prose working out the mapping — which images occupy which movement, what shared image governs the two middle movements and how its direction reverses, and what the first movement's image must be if the fourth is to reveal it as a likeness — and only then write the poem. Afterwards, add a short paragraph addressed to my students explaining where the proportions and the mirroring are doing philosophical work, so that I can hand it out alongside the poem. Take your time: structural accuracy matters more here than ornament, and I would far rather have a plain poem with an audible architecture than a beautiful one that could have been written by someone who had never seen the diagram.
