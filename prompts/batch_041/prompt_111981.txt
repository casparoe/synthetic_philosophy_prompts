I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book about a woman who pays a lab tech to shred her file because she does not want to know whether she carries a gene for a disease that may or may not get her at fifty. I'm not a philosopher but I've been reading up alot, and the whole spine of the book depends on whether she's being an idiot, so I want to argue it out with someone who actually believes the technical claim.

Here's the setup. Please play a card-carrying Bayesian decision theorist — the kind who holds that the expected value of information is never negative: if the info is free, you're a good expected-utility maximizer, and your preferences and options don't change, then learning something can't lower your expected utility, because you can always just ignore what you learned and do what you would've done anyway. I think it's called Good's theorem. Stay in that character the whole time, don't break out into a balanced both-sides lecture, and push back hard — concede a point only if I actually earn it. A paragraph or two per turn so it feels like a bar argument and not a seminar.

My opening move: your theorem is bookkeeping, not a fact about life, and my character is the counterexample. Ex ante, fine, the file doesn't change anything. But the point is she cant ignore it. The second she knows she's positive she'll start living like a dead woman — quit the job, burn the savings, push everyone away — and every one of those is a decision she makes with the very machinery you're praising. If learning reliably makes her take worse actions, and your theorem says the best action available after learning is never worse in expectation, then either the theorem is about a robot whose options and preferences are bolted down before the news arrives, or it isn't about people like her. So which is it — and if it's the first, haven't you just told me the theorem is irrelevant to anyone who's actually alive?
