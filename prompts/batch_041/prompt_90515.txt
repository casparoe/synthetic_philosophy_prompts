I'm in a reading group that's been meeting for about a year now — mostly grad students plus one long-suffering professor — and we've gotten completely stuck on where the line falls between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. It's not for lack of examples. We've been chewing on the same handful for months: a politician rousing a crowd with rhetoric that's all heat and no argument; an app that keeps you scrolling by making the alternative feel like missing out; a friend who gets you to lend them money by making you feel guilty about the last time they helped you; a doctor who frames a risky operation as having a ninety percent survival rate rather than a ten percent mortality rate; and, more recently, an AI assistant that's asked for a recommendation and quietly steers you toward whatever's cheapest for the company behind it, without ever saying anything it believes to be false. Nobody in the group disagrees that the last two are shady and the doctor is basically fine, but the moment we try to say why, we split into camps and talk past each other for the rest of the session. One camp says the mark of manipulation is that it works by bypassing the target's rational capacities — it pushes buttons instead of giving reasons. Another says no, the mark is concealment: the influence is manipulative when the person doesn't realize it's happening, or doesn't realize why it's happening. A third says neither of those is it — what matters is whether the influence exploits some vulnerability or works against the target's own ends, so that being open and transparent doesn't launder something that's still using the person as a tool. Each definition sorts our examples into a different order, and here's what really bugs me: each one seems to have the verdict built in, so whoever gets to pick the definition wins by fiat.

We've started to suspect we aren't really disagreeing about the examples at all. My own hypothesis — tell me if it's right or if it's too neat — is that we're fighting on two axes without noticing: there's a descriptive question about what manipulation *is*, and a normative question about what makes it *wrong*, and people slide between them, so someone really making a point about wrongness gets heard as making a claim about the definition, and vice versa. But I don't want to just assume that. I want an actual diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute about the word manipulation, where the sides would stop disagreeing if they only stipulated different terms? Is it a genuine substantive disagreement that's been mislocated — maybe a hidden factual disagreement about how the mind works (does influence that withholds reasons still engage our rational capacities? is there even a clean line between pushing buttons and giving reasons?), or a disagreement about standards (must a good account of manipulation match ordinary usage, or may it revise it)? Or are the camps answering different questions under a single heading — one asking what manipulation is, one asking when influence is permissible — so the apparent conflict is an artifact of a shared word? And underneath all of that, is there a version of the question that's just ill-posed, e.g. because manipulation is one of those thick concepts where description and evaluation can't be cleanly pulled apart, so any definition will look question-begging to anyone with different values? Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually move us — a distinction, a reframing, a constraint on what counts as a good account, or a flat demonstration that this is a debate we should stop having — and be honest if the answer is that there's nothing to be done. And honestly its been driving me up the wall, so don't just hand me a taxonomy of the positions and call it a day.

One more thing: our professor keeps insisting that half of what we argue about is already settled and we just don't know it, and that the other half is genuinely live. So please separate those for me — what's actually settled among people who work on this, and what's genuinely contested — and don't dress up a contested position as the consensus, because I'll bring it back to the group and get eaten alive if I do.
