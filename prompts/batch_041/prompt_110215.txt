I'm a second-year PhD student, metaphysics mostly, though I keep drifting into aesthetics, and I've got an argument I can't settle. Here's the case, which is a real one. My flatmate is a ceramicist. Her MFA show included a piece called 'Ball': just a 5kg ball of unfired clay on a plinth, nothing done to it, that was the entire work. Four years later she took the same ball -- same clay, nothing added or taken away -- fired it, carved it into a horse's head, and showed it as 'Horse'. Two catalogue entries, two titles, two dates, two insurance valuations. A museum bought 'Horse' last year, and there's now a silly dispute about whether 'Ball' went with it, on the grounds that it's the same lump of stuff.

My officemate Dev and a visiting student, Mira, went at this for about an hour at the pub and I genuinely dont know who's right. Roughly how it went:

(1) Dev: there's one object there, the lump. 'Ball' and 'Horse' are two artworks, sure, but an artwork isn't an extra object sitting on top of the clay, it's the clay under a certain art-historical description. Count the stuff, you count one thing. The dispute about the sale is an equivocation.

(2) Mira: can't be one thing, because of Leibniz's law. The lump could have survived being re-kneaded into a snake on day one -- unfired clay is endlessly reworkable, that's what clay is -- but 'Ball' couldn't have survived that; re-knead it and there's no work there anymore. Same time, same object, contradictory properties. So the lump and the artwork are two things in the same place, made of the same stuff.

(3) Dev: that's a trick with descriptions. 'Could have survived re-kneading' isn't a property the object has, it's a property it has relative to a way of describing it. Strip the description and there's one object with one modal profile. And your view has a worse problem: you've got two objects in exactly the same place at exactly the same time with exactly the same parts and no physical difference between them at all. That's not two things, that's one thing counted twice. And why stop at two? Clay, ball, artwork, 'object on a plinth', 'thing the grant paid for' -- you get a crowd, all perfectly coincident, all equally real.

(4) Mira: fine, so your view says the artwork survives being re-kneaded into a snake -- so the museum could re-knead it and still own 'Ball'. Or it says the clay was destroyed the moment she re-kneaded it. Pick one, theyre both absurd.

(5) Dev: neither. The object survives, and afterwards it simply isn't an artwork anymore, the way someone stops being a child. 'Is an artwork' is a phase sortal. Nothing was destroyed; the object changed what it was.

(6) Mira: then take the firing. After firing the stuff isn't clay anymore -- you can't re-wet it, it's ceramic, the clay minerals are gone. So on your view the object outlived the destruction of the very material it's made of. But your whole argument for one object was 'count the matter'. If the object can survive its matter being destroyed, matter isn't what you're counting, and then there's nothing left of your case against two objects sharing matter while it lasts.

(7) Dev: that just shows 'lump of clay' was a phase sortal too, same as 'statue' and 'artwork'. One object, several phase sortals, changing over time. No contradiction anywhere.

(8) Mira: then if I accept the phase-sortal line, tell me how many works the museum owns. 'Ball' and 'Horse' can't be the same work -- different origins, different dates, different titles, different art-historical properties, and she treated them as two: the catalogue, the insurance, the dealer, everyone does. Two works. But there's only one object. So either works aren't objects, or there are two objects. You can't have it both ways.

Can you go through this move by move, (1) through (8), and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying who's right about which specific claim? I don't just want who wins overall, I want to know which bits hold up and which don't. Then tell me what both of them are missing, because I have a feeling they're talking past each other somewhere and I can't put my finger on where. And finally give me your best verdict on the practical bit -- did the sale of 'Horse' carry 'Ball' with it, or not, and how confident are you? No need to cite any literature, I've read it; just tell me whether the arguments actually work.
