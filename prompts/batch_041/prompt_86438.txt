Write an essay on the following controversy in the philosophy of science, and end with a verdict you are prepared to defend.

Some background you may take as given, so that you don't spend the essay reciting it. We want to be able to say that inquiry makes progress toward the truth, yet most of the theories we actually accept are, strictly speaking, false; that seems to require some way of ranking false theories by how near they come to the truth. The most celebrated attempt to supply such a ranking defined one theory as closer to the truth than another when it entails more truths and no additional falsehoods. Two critics showed in the mid-1970s that between two false theories this condition can never be met — enlarging a false theory's stock of true consequences invariably drags extra false consequences along with it, and the same in reverse — so that by the definition no false theory is ever closer to the truth than any other. The question is whether the notion can be repaired or ought to be given up.

I want you to assess a specific proposal, which a colleague of mine has been developing and which I don't believe appears in the standard literature. Her claim is this. The search for a single, context-independent definition of closeness to the truth is itself what generates the failures, and it rests on a mistake. Closeness to the truth, she argues, is only ever closeness relative to a partition — a set of mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive answers to the questions at hand — together with some measure of how far one answer lies from another. (If the question is the number of planets, the partition might be: fewer than eight, exactly eight, exactly nine, more than nine; and we still need a measure that makes 'exactly nine' nearer the truth than 'more than nine' when the truth is 'exactly eight'.) On her view, the sentence 'theory A is closer to the truth than theory B' is never true simpliciter; it is true only relative to a partition and a distance measure. She insists that this is no real concession, because every scientific question is already posed against some such partition, and a 'partition-independent' notion of nearness would be empty — there would be no space in which to measure nearness at all.

Assess her proposal. Does the relativization rescue the concept, or does it quietly replace it with something else — closeness to what we happen to care about, rather than to the truth? Can the arbitrariness worry (choose your partition and you can obtain whatever ranking you like) be answered without simply stipulating that one partition is the right one? And does anything of the original realist ambition survive the move? Reach a verdict on whether a relativized notion of this kind can do the work we wanted from verisimilitude, and defend it against the strongest objection you can construct against it.

Close by naming the single consideration that would most likely overturn your verdict, and by saying where in your reasoning you are least confident.
