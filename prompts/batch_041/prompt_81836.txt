I am preparing a graduate seminar on scientific realism and want to spend a session on structural realism, which several of my students have met only through a short encyclopaedia entry. Before I teach it, I want an audit of the summary below: which claims a structural realist would endorse, which are the sort of simplification a competent populariser makes and a specialist winces at, and which a defender of the position would reject outright. Please go clause by clause rather than offering a general verdict.

The summary, which I have reproduced exactly as it appears in the source:

'Structural realism is the view, introduced by John Worrall, that although we can never know the intrinsic nature of unobservable entities, we can know the mathematical structure of the world, since it is structure alone that is preserved across theory change. Worrall's central example is the transition from Fresnel's wave theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetism: the equations survived, and with them everything worth keeping, even though the ether was discarded. The view thus dissolves the pessimistic meta-induction, because it shows that what later theories overturn is never the structure, only the interpretation. Its best-known formulation is ontic structural realism, the thesis that the world is structure all the way down and that individual objects are merely nodes in a relational web. The main objection, due to Newman, is simply that any collection of things can be described as having a structure, so the structuralist claim is trivial. Since structural realism concedes that we are permanently barred from the intrinsic nature of things, it is really a form of anti-realism, and its defenders have quietly abandoned the realist project.'

A few things I would like you to attend to in particular:

- whether the summary is right to run the epistemic and the ontic versions of the view together as though they were a single position, and what turns on keeping them apart;
- whether the characterisation of Worrall's example and of what it is supposed to show is faithful to the case as he presents it, including the analogy he draws with an earlier figure usually associated with a very different philosophy of science;
- whether the summary has stated the Newman problem correctly, and whether, properly stated, it tells against both versions of the view or only one;
- whether the claim about the pessimistic meta-induction is something a structural realist is entitled to assert or only to hope for;
- and, finally, whether the closing inference -- that the position is therefore a disguised anti-realism -- survives once the earlier errors are corrected.

Please distinguish carefully between what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what is a misrepresentation, and make clear which of the summary's claims are doing real work in its conclusion and which are decoration. No references or reading lists are needed; I want the philosophical audit, not the bibliography.
