Two colleagues of mine, Ravi and Nadia, spent most of a planning meeting arguing over a mentoring program at our company, and I would like an impartial, point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on who was nicer about it.

The figures come from an internal study. Of 100 employees who joined the mentoring program, 50 were promoted within two years (50 percent). Of 100 comparable employees who did not join, 70 were promoted (70 percent). The study also breaks this down by division. In Division A, 32 of 80 mentored employees were promoted (40 percent) against 6 of 20 unmentored (30 percent). In Division B, 18 of 20 mentored employees were promoted (90 percent) against 64 of 80 unmentored (80 percent). So mentoring looks harmful in the pooled data and helpful in each division considered on its own. One further fact became central as they argued: division assignment is made by the program itself partway through, and division is associated with promotion for reasons that include things the study never measured, such as how strongly a given manager advocates for their reports.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ravi said the pooled figures are the bottom line: the program is associated with a twenty-point drop in promotion, and that is the number a policy decision should use, so the program should be wound down.

(2) Nadia said the pooled figure is an artifact of mixing two divisions with very different promotion rates and very different rates of program take-up. Within each division, mentored employees do better: 40 against 30, and 90 against 80. This is a textbook reversal, and the within-division comparison is the informative one. The pooled number is a weighted average of two real effects, with the weights set by who happens to be in which division.

(3) Ravi said division is not a fixed attribute we can hold constant the way we hold sex or tenure constant. It is assigned by the program itself, so it sits downstream of the treatment. Adjusting for a downstream variable does not give us the effect of the program; it gives us the effect of the program with one of its own consequences switched off. If part of what the program does is place people into a division where promotions are rarer, then Nadia's analysis is quietly deleting that harm. And it could be worse: if division is influenced both by the program and by something unmeasured that also drives promotion, such as manager advocacy, then conditioning on division can create an association that was never there.

(4) Nadia replied that everything Ravi just offered is a possibility rather than a finding. The objection that you might be conditioning on a downstream or common-effect variable can be raised against any subgroup analysis ever run; unless he is willing to name the unmeasured variable and commit to a structure, it is a worry rather than an argument. His own preferred number is a mixture too, averaged with weights that nobody chose. Why is the mixture we happen to have more authoritative than the effects within its parts? If the question is how the program works, holding division fixed is exactly right, because it isolates the part of the effect that runs through promotion within a division rather than through placement.

(5) Ravi said the name Simpson's paradox is doing work it cannot support. A reversal between a marginal and a conditional association is an arithmetic fact; it can be produced by a confounder, by a mediator, by a collider, or by noise, and it does not by itself say which table is causal. Nadia is treating the reversal as evidence that her table is the true one, which does not follow. He added that given these cell sizes, a ten-point within-division gap is not obviously distinguishable from chance, so the reversal may not even be robust.

(6) Nadia said that by that standard Ravi's preference for the pooled table is equally unevidenced. He keeps appealing to the whole population, but the population is a mixture, and mixture effects can be manufactured entirely by composition. If we are deciding whether to keep the program, we should ask what it does to promotion inside the units where it operates, and that is her table. If anything, she said, Ravi owes us a causal story for why the pooled contrast is the effect he claims it is, given that people chose whether to enroll and nobody adjusted for that.

I have no stake in which of them comes out ahead, and I would rather you did not try to guess my sympathies. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and say which specific claims within each move are correct, which are overreach, and which are simply beside the point. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing that a better analysis would have to address. Please also state how confident you are in each part of your adjudication, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
