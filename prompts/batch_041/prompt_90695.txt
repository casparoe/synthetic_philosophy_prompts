I have been turning over the following argument since a long comment thread last month, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether I have found a real theorem about rationality or merely some bookkeeping that has been smuggled past me.

The setting is a one-shot simultaneous game between two players, Row and Column, each of whom knows the whole payoff structure and knows that the other knows it. Row has two strategies, Top and Bottom; Column has two, Left and Right. If Row plays Top and Column plays Left, Row receives 3 and Column receives 0. If Row plays Top and Column plays Right, each receives 1. If Row plays Bottom and Column plays Left, Row receives 3 and Column receives 5. If Row plays Bottom and Column plays Right, each receives 0.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A rational player never plays a weakly dominated strategy, that is, a strategy that pays no more than some alternative of hers against every way the opponent might play and pays strictly less against at least one. (2) It is common knowledge between the players that each is rational in the ordinary sense of maximising expected payoff given her beliefs, and that each knows this of the other, and so on up the hierarchy. (3) In the game above, Bottom is weakly dominated by Top: against Left the two pay the same, and against Right Top pays 1 where Bottom pays 0. (4) So Row will not play Bottom. (5) Column can work this out, and once Bottom is off the table Left pays 0 while Right pays 1, so Left is strictly dominated for Column and she will not play it. (6) Therefore the only outcome consistent with common knowledge of rationality is Top against Right, in which each player receives 1. (7) But Bottom against Left is a Nash equilibrium: against Left, Row's two strategies both pay 3, so Row has nothing to gain by deviating, and against Bottom, Column's Left pays 5 where Right pays 0. (8) So the argument forbids the unique outcome of the game in which each player receives her largest payoff, 3 to Row and 5 to Column, and requires instead the outcome in which each receives 1, which is strictly worse for both, even though the outcome it forbids is one from which neither player wishes to move.

A friend tells me the difficulty is that we never let mixed strategies do any of the eliminating, and that if we did, Bottom would disappear for a better reason. I cannot make that work: against Left, every mixture of Top and Bottom pays Row exactly 3, so no mixture strictly beats Bottom, and the friend's repair looks to me like no repair at all. I have rechecked the arithmetic and it holds, so the trouble must lie in one of the steps themselves.

I would rather keep (2) and (7) than keep (1), but I do not want to buy my way out of a theorem with a fudge, and I am not asking for a reading list; I want the diagnosis. Which step do you reject, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost me, both inside this game and in the rest of the picture of rationality that I should like to keep?
