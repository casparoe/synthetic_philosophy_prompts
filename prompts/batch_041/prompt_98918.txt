I'm writing an essay on the nature/nurture thing and I keep getting snagged on a pattern I can't quite untangle, so I'd like you to help me work out what's actually being taken for granted. The thing I keep staring at is the practice of reporting a single heritability figure for a trait — intelligence is 50 to 80 per cent heritable, height is around 80 per cent, educational attainment somewhere in the middle, and so on. This number gets handed round in textbooks, news articles and policy documents as if it were a fixed property of the trait itself, a dial that sits somewhere between all-genes and all-environment and tells you how much of the thing is built in. But the people who actually produce the estimates are careful to say something quite different: that heritability is a ratio of variances in one population at one time, that it says nothing at all about what's fixable or about what would happen if you changed the environment, and that it definately can't be carried across to explain differences between groups. So there's a mismatch between the concept the field says it's measuring and the concept the reporting practice seems to imply. What I want from you is the view of the concept that the practice — not the researchers' caveats, the practice itself — actually presupposes.

A couple of things make me suspect the practice isn't even consistent with itself. Height is one of the most heritable traits we know of, and yet average height in rich countries climbed enormously over the twentieth century; on the reading the reporting practice invites, that shouldn't be possible, since if the trait were mostly genetic there'd be nothing much to move. Then there's the habit of taking a high figure for a trait measured in a fairly uniform environment and waving it around as proof that equalising the environment won't help — when the very same figure would drop if you did change the environment. And then there's the move from a heritability estimate computed within one population to a claim about differences between populations, which is exactly the move the field says is invalid but which the popular presentation seems to invite. I'd like to know which of these is a genuine presupposition of the practice, which is just a bad inference people bolt on afterwards, and which parts of the practice are underdetermined — compatible with several different views of what heritability even is.

And suppose you're right that the practice rests on a particular and probably mistaken picture of the concept. What would a reporting practice look like if it presupposed the correct picture instead — what would it say, what would it stop saying, and what would have to be given up? If the real confusion isn't about heritability at all but about what 'genetic' means, or about what counts as an explanation, I'd rather you say so than force it all onto heritability. Please answer in British English.
