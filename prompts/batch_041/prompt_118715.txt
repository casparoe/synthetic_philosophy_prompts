I have no academic training in classics or theology, and I have been reading Plutarch on my own for the past year or so, in an old English translation I found on Project Gutenberg. I keep stalling at the opening of his life of Theseus, where he explains what he thinks he is doing when he writes about men who lived before there were any reliable records. He writes:

"As geographers, Sosius, crowd into the edges of their maps parts of the world which they do not know about, adding notes in the margin to the effect, that beyond this lies nothing but sandy deserts full of wild beasts, unapproachable bogs, Scythian ice, or a frozen sea, so, in this work of mine, in which I have compared the lives of the greatest men with one another, after passing through those periods which probable reasoning can reach to and real history find a footing in, I might very well say of those that are farther off, Beyond this there is nothing but prodigies and fictions, the only inhabitants are the poets and inventors of fables; there is no credit, or certainty any farther. [...] Let us hope that Fable may, in what shall follow, so submit to the purifying processes of Reason as to take the character of exact history."

A little later in the same stretch he tells us that Theseus and Romulus were both "born out of wedlock and of uncertain parentage," that both had "the repute of being sprung from the gods," and that both stand charged with the rape of women; he finishes by saying he will take "the stories least like poetry as our guide to the truth," and then asks for readers who will "receive with indulgence the stories of antiquity." The map image I can follow well enough, but I cannot work out what he is actually promising. Does "purifying" mean cutting the wonders out, explaining them away, or keeping them while refusing to vouch for them? His test for what to believe also strikes me as oddly aesthetic — the version least like a poem is the one he trusts — which seems to leave the moral lesson resting on precisely the sort of detail he has just admitted he may have to discard.

What sharpens the puzzle for me is that I have also been reading the Life of Antony, the biography of the Egyptian hermit that Athanasius wrote around 360, where the wonders are not rationalized in the least; they are reported as the substance of the account, and the reader is plainly meant to be edified by believing them. So I would like help seeing what Plutarch is doing that is different, and whether his purifying is a genuine method of inquiry into what happened or a way of salvaging the edifying parts of stories he does not credit. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing summary.
