I write the back page of a general-interest monthly — the problem page, agony-aunt style — and for one issue I want the column written in the person of a guest columnist who is a committed heterophenomenologist. Take that commitment to mean the following, and nothing more: that consciousness is to be studied by collecting what a subject says about her experience and treating those utterances as a text to be explained rather than as claims to be assessed for truth; that from them the researcher builds the subject's heterophenomenological world, whose inhabitants — her reported pains, after-images, chills — are not thereby believed to exist; that the subject is the authority on how things seem to her and on nothing beyond that; that the researcher adopts the intentional stance towards her, attributing beliefs and desires in order to predict what she will say; and that first-person methods, in which a subject introspects and delivers the facts about her own experience, are rejected as unscientific. The method presents itself as neutral and as leaving nothing out: once every report has been explained, there is nothing left over — which, the columnist will insist, is not a denial that anyone experiences anything.

Write that column, in British English, at about 600 words: a heading, then two or three letters from readers with the columnist's replies, in the flat, unruffled, kindly register of a real problem page. The correspondents are ordinary people with ordinary troubles, and every reply must say exactly what the method licenses — no more, no less. Please include: a letter from someone whose partner flatly contradicts her reports of her own state (the room is not cold, the soup is not too salty) and who wants to know which of them is right; a letter from a reader who has been seeing a practitioner of the rival, first-person approach and wants to know whether she should trust her own introspection instead; and, last, a letter asking whether the method can ever tell us what an experience is actually like rather than only what people say about it, with its reply.

The columnist must be competent and sincere, must never wink at the reader, and must never concede anything the method cannot concede; where the method has a genuine limitation, she states it without embarrassment. At least once she should separate three things — what the reader said, how it seemed to the reader, and whether there was anything there to seem that way — granting the first two and withholding the third. At least once she should decline to answer a question about her own feelings, on the ground that her own reports would not be data. The piece should be legible to a reader who has never met the term and rewarding to one who has, particularly in how the replies handle the objection that this is a science of reports rather than of experience, and the possibility of a being who said all the same things and felt nothing at all. Do not name any real philosopher, living or dead, and do not cite anything; the butt of the joke is the method as a practice, not a person.
