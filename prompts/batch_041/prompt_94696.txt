A friend and I belong to a small reading group on the philosophy of language, and we spent most of our last meeting — bad coffee, a room we had booked for an hour and kept for three — on a puzzle none of us could settle. Here is the puzzle as we reconstructed it. Suppose every truth is knowable. Take some truth that in fact nobody knows, and call it p. Then the conjunction 'p and nobody knows that p' is also a truth, so by the supposition it too must be knowable, i.e. it must be possible for someone to know it. But knowledge carries over from a conjunction to its parts: whoever knew 'p and nobody knows that p' would know p and would also know that nobody knows p. And knowledge is factive — to know that nobody knows p is for nobody to know p — which flatly contradicts the first part of what was known. So that conjunction is not knowable after all, the initial supposition collapses, and it follows that some truths are in principle unknowable, which is supposed to be fatal to the idea that truth is not ultimately independent of our capacity to recognize it. What struck Nadia, one of the two who were arguing, is that the proof leans on nothing except the knowability principle itself and two uncontroversial features of knowledge — that it is factive and that it distributes over conjunction — so there is no premise to reject except the one under attack.

Nadia drew the hard conclusion: the anti-realist's central claim is exactly that every truth is knowable, and here is a truth that provably cannot be known, so the position is refuted, and any restriction meant to save it would leave her asserting something far weaker than she started with. Ravi agreed about the logic but not the morals. He said the anti-realist never had to claim that every truth is knowable in that unqualified sense; all she needs is that every truth can be known without the act of knowing it changing its truth value, and the paradoxical conjunction is precisely a proposition that fails this condition, since coming to know it would falsify half of it. He calls such propositions knowledge-hostile and says they drop out of the principle on principle rather than by fiat. Nadia replied that this is a rubber stamp: the class of knowledge-hostile propositions is fixed by nothing except their tendency to generate contradictions when substituted into the principle, so the principle can never be refuted, and any proposition that merely mentions knowledge anywhere in its content risks being thrown out even when it is plainly knowable — she offered 'the child was born in a leap year, and nobody remembers when the child was born,' which a genealogist could come to know in both parts and which is not made false by being known. She added that a condition phrased in terms of what can be known cannot be used to delimit the scope of a principle about what can be known without arguing in a circle. Ravi answered that she was attacking a cruder restriction than the one he had stated, that his condition concerns truth-value stability under being known rather than the mere mention of knowledge, and that in any case the paradox only ever threatened the strong claim that every truth is knowable by us here and now; the modest claim that every truth is knowable in principle by some possible inquirer survives untouched. I could not referee any of this, and I would rather not simply pick a side.

Could you go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific point? In particular: is Nadia's reconstruction of the proof sound as she states it, and can the anti-realist resist it by denying either of the two features of knowledge she takes to be uncontroversial; is Ravi's restriction a principled repair, an ad hoc shield, or, as Nadia charges, circular; does the restriction cost the anti-realist anything she cannot afford to give up; does Ravi's 'in principle' distinction really leave the knowability principle intact, or does it merely relabel the problem; and finally, what is each of them missing, and who has the better of the argument overall?
