I retired from a career in experimental physics three years ago and have spent much of the time since teaching myself philosophy, mainly decision theory and the philosophy of probability. I have no library access at present, so please reason from first principles rather than pointing me at the literature: I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or a survey of who holds what. What I want is a tier list, and since the dimension I care about is an unusual one I will set out the puzzles and the candidate replies myself, so that we are working from the same description.

Counterfactual mugging. A predictor with a long unblemished record tells me that it flipped a fair coin before we met. If the coin came up heads, it will now ask me for £100. If the coin came up tails, it would instead be giving me £10,000 — but only if, before flipping, it predicted that I would pay up in the heads case. The coin came up heads, and it is asking. Do I pay?

XOR blackmail. A predictor I have no reason to distrust sends me a note. It has sent me the note if and only if exactly one of the following two statements is true: my house is badly infested with termites, or I pay the predictor £1,000. A serious infestation would cost me £1,000,000 to put right. So either I pay £1,000 and my house is sound, or I keep my money and my house is infested. Do I pay?

My physicist's instinct is to ask a question that I suspect is not the usual one. In both puzzles the predictor behaves as though it had access to facts about what I would do in circumstances that never arise. Nothing in physics behaves that way. So I want to know which of the familiar replies survive when the predictor is replaced by something that obeys ordinary physics: an agent or device with an excellent model of me, embedded in the same causal order I am, unable to signal backwards in time, and unable to inspect worlds other than the one it occupies. I will call this the physicalization of the puzzle, and a reply physically modest to the extent that it needs nothing beyond it.

One further matter I would like kept in view. Once a puzzle is physicalized, the case in which I am not being asked must be one of three very different things: a counterfactual world that does not exist at all; a real branch of a branching quantum world, in which a duplicate of me is being asked; or an actual computation running somewhere in this world, in which a duplicate of me is being asked. I suspect that several of the usual replies quietly presuppose one of these readings and would be embarrassed if made to choose.

Here are the candidate replies. The first three are ones I have picked up in my reading; the last three are my own hunches, so treat them as fair game.

1. The causal reply. Refuse. In the mugging, the coin has already been flipped and my £100 buys nothing; in the blackmail, my paying cannot cause an infestation to appear or vanish, and the note is at best evidence about my house, which I should take at face value.
2. The correlation reply. Pay. In the mugging, my willingness to pay is precisely what the prediction was about, so refusing is not a free £100; in the blackmail, conditional on my paying, the arrival of the note is explained by my payment rather than by an infestation.
3. The precommitment reply. Both puzzles are badly posed as one-shot decisions. By the time I am being asked, my procedure is already fixed and has already been acted upon; the interesting question is which procedure to adopt before the predictor looks at me, and about that question there is far less disagreement.
4. The branch-welfare reply (my hunch). In the mugging, the branch in which I am not asked contains a counterpart who receives £10,000 if I pay and nothing if I refuse. If that counterpart is a real agent — a duplicate in another branch, or a simulation running in this world — then paying is not a sacrifice I make for my own sake but a transfer between two agents, and might be defensible on the counterpart's behalf even where it is indefensible on mine. If the counterpart is a mere counterfactual, the reply collapses.
5. The exploitability reply (my hunch). Judge a decision procedure by how it fares in a population containing predators, not by its verdict in a single stipulated case. Any predictor that can forecast me can construct a note of the blackmail form at no cost to itself, and a procedure that pays such notes is a standing subsidy to anyone who can forecast me; that each individual note is profitable on its own terms does not settle whether the procedure is.
6. The underdescription reply. Neither puzzle has an answer as stated, because the predictor's mechanism is unspecified and physically different mechanisms give different correct answers. A predictor that works by running a faithful simulation of my decision procedure makes its forecast certain, in a deterministic world, but turns the unasked case into an actual computation; a predictor that works by statistical extrapolation from my past behaviour is fallible, which changes the arithmetic; and so on. The verdict is a function of the mechanism.

Please sort these six replies into four tiers according to physical modesty. Tier 1: needs nothing beyond an accurate physical model of me and the ordinary flow of cause and effect. Tier 2: needs a specific physical mechanism that physics does not forbid, such as an exact simulation. Tier 3: needs a genuine branching of worlds, or a duplication of agents, in which the counterpart's interests do real work. Tier 4: needs a predictor that can act on facts about worlds that do not exist, or on signals from the future. Give one or two sentences of justification for each placement, and for each reply add a clause naming which of the three readings of the unasked case it presupposes. Then tell me whether any reply moves tier if I stipulate that the world is deterministic, and whether any moves if I stipulate that it is a branching quantum world. I expect at least one to move, and I want to know which and why.
