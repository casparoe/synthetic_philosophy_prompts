Ok so I've been arguing with a friend about what counts as alive for like two years now and we've agreed to stop yelling and actually write something. Maybe you can draft it for us.

Here's where we each are. I'm the self-maintenance guy: for me the thing that makes something alive is that it keeps itself running — holds itself away from equilibrium, repairs itself, keeps its own boundaries up, and would fall apart if it stopped. That's what the definition should be built around. She's the Darwinian: for her, no heritable variation, no differential persistence, no evolution, then it's not alive, period. Metabolism for her is a typical companion of life, not the point of it.

We keep talking past each other and I want the document we should have produced: an adversarial collaboration writeup, something we'd both sign. Concretely:

- a numbered list of agreed claims — what we're even trying to do with a definition, the uncontroversial cases, and what we both accept about the biology of the messy stuff
- then a table of the disagreements that are left, with each row phrased the way the person who holds that view would actually sign it, not a caricature
- then the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling one would genuinely move at least one of us
- and for each crux, the case or argument or evidence we both agree bears on it, with each of us writing our prediction down in advance

Cases I want covered, because I think this is where our two definitions actually come apart rather than just going vague:

1. Those clonal tumor lineages that hop between animals by contact — the dog one, the one going through the Tasmanian devils. Cell lineages that reproduce, spread, and have evolved, but nobody wants to call them organisms. Is the unit an individual or a lineage, and does that change the verdict?
2. Something I made up but I think is a fair test: picture a thing found in a deep borehole — membrane-bound vesicles running a small metabolism, growing, splitting, doing it in the same rock for a billion years. But every copy is chemically identical. No heritable variation, nothing to select on. Self-maintaining, non-evolving. Which of us blinks?
3. The mirror image: a lineage of self-replicating programs on a computer, mutating and being selected for years. Evolving, no chemistry, no metabolism. Same question.
4. Something that's arguably both but only by borrowing: a stripped-down obligate intracellular parasite that keeps itself going entirely with its host's machinery.
5. A prion-type protein that templates its own misfolding. Copies itself, no variation worth selecting on, no metabolism.

Two more things I want in there. First, is a short replicating RNA population in a tube "evolving" in the sense her view needs, or does evolution require open-ended cumulative adaptation? I suspect that's a real crux and not just a verbal one. Second, if we end up concluding that part of this is verbal — that we're disagreeing about where to draw a word rather than about the world — say which part and why. Don't just fuse both definitions into the usual compromise and call it done; I've read that a hundred times. And don't give me a yes/no/maybe chart for viruses, that's the boring version of this question.
