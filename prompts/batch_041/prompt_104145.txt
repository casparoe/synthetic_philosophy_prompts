so I've been lurking on the rationalist blogs and forums for like a decade now, and the one argument I keep seeing get rehashed and never actually resolved is the worst-case vs average-case thing in AI alignment. both camps just sort of glare at each other across the comments section. I want you to write the dialogue I wish would actually happen in those threads.

Set it up as two people on a coffee break at some AI safety workshop. One is a formal-verification / security-mindset type — she thinks a safety property that only holds "on average" or "for typical inputs" is basically not a safety property at all, that unless you can say "for all inputs, the system does X" you haven't guaranteed anything, and she's got the crypto/red-team instinct that anything an adversary can probe will eventually get probed at its worst point. The other is an empirical evals / deployment person — he thinks that whole framing is a category error for learning systems, that you can never verify over all inputs, that what matters is expected behavior under the distribution you'll actually see, and that the worst-case framing just paralyzes you into never deploying anything.

Neither one should be a dunce, and I mean it — I want real back and forth where each one actually responds to the other's last point instead of reciting their team's talking points.

A few things I'd love to see them fight about specifically, because these are the parts I never see handled well:

- Whether "worst case" even has a referent here. In alignment the deployment distribution isn't fixed — the system's own outputs feed back into the world, other agents are reacting to it, so the thing you'd be taking the expectation over is partly chosen by the system itself. Does average-case reasoning even type-check when the environment is endogenous like that? And does the worst-case person just win by default if the average-case person can't actually name the distribution?

- The retreat move where the worst-case person gives up on "all inputs" and downgrades to "all inputs in some reasonable class of adversaries / distributions." The empirical guy should press hard: which class, picked how, and isn't the choice of class doing all the work in secret? And the formal person needs to come back with something real, not a hand-wave.

- Whether the two are even disagreeing about a fact at all, or about what "guarantee" is supposed to mean and what it's for. Is one of them answering "will this thing hurt us?" while the other is answering "can I prove to a skeptic that it won't?"

Have them converge on something or explicitly fail to, whatever feels honest, but no cheap both-sides "they're both right in their own domain" ending unless one of them actually earns it. Keep it maybe 800-1200 words, and make it read like two smart people who read the same blogs, not like a philosophy paper. Also it's a wierd pet peeve of mine but don't have them cite papers at each other, since half the point is that this stuff is unresolved.
