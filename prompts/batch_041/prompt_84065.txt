I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, statistics, or genetics, and I run a monthly ninety-minute "concepts" session for about twenty colleagues from engineering, product, and design. No prior reading, no lecture at the front, and no jargon I cannot cash out at the whiteboard. I want to design a single in-class activity for one of these sessions that makes the group feel, in their hands, what a heritability number does and does not mean. My own working worry is that everyone in the room, me included, has absorbed the idea that a trait being "heritable" means it is genetically caused, fixed, and unresponsive to the environment, and I want the activity itself, not my closing remarks, to break that association.

Please design the activity to run as follows, and treat this structure as a specification I would like honored rather than a suggestion, since I will be running it more or less as written.

Setting and scale: one room, about twenty people, ninety minutes, whiteboard plus a laptop I can drive, index cards, and a small pile of poker chips used only as scoring tokens (redeemable for nothing but bragging rights). Assume I can write a short script beforehand to generate data and print slips.

Core mechanic: each participant is cast as an organism carrying a fixed genotype, which I assign as a distinct integer from 0 to 19 (so the twenty genotypes are 0 through 19, one each). Their genotype never changes across the whole activity. For each round I hand each participant several phenotype slips, which they treat as measurements of a single trait, and the group's job is to estimate heritability from the (genotype, phenotype) data alone, using the same estimator every round: the squared correlation between genotype and phenotype across all the slips in that round. They do not get to see the environments, and I do not tell them the generative rule until after they have committed. Before each reveal, every participant writes a sealed prediction on a card—what they expect the estimate to do when I change the conditions, and why—and we award a token to anyone whose prediction is directionally right; the tokens are the only stake, and the point of the bet is to force a commitment they will remember when they are wrong.

I want three rounds, with the generative rules kept secret until each reveal, arranged so that the same estimator gives results that pull in opposite directions from the truth. In the first round the phenotype is produced additively from genotype and an independently drawn environmental contribution, with the environmental spread chosen so the estimate lands near one half. In the second round the genotype values are unchanged and the environmental contribution is made constant across everyone, so the estimate climbs to nearly one. In the third round I want the sharpest case: the phenotype is produced entirely by the environment, the genotype has no causal role at all, and yet the environment has been arranged to track the genotype, so the group's estimator comes back high—tune it to land somewhere around two-thirds to three-quarters—while the honest answer is that genes are doing nothing. Between rounds, hold the genotypes fixed and change only the generative rule, so that participants can see that the same people, with the same genes, can be assigned wildly different heritabilities depending on conditions they cannot observe.

The activity must be built so that participants run into the confusion themselves before it is named: the moment in round two when the estimate jumps without anyone's genes changing, the moment in round three when a confident high estimate turns out to rest on nothing genetic, and the suspicion they should develop that a suspiciously clean correlation is itself a tell. Include the reveal sequence, what each round's secret rule actually was, and how to stage the reveals so the group feels each reversal rather than being told about it.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific claims about heritability: that it is a property of the variation within a particular population at a particular time and not a property of the trait or of any individual; that a trait can show a heritability of nearly one while still depending causally on the environment for its very existence, so that the number says nothing about what would happen if you changed the environment; that a high estimate can be produced entirely by environment-genotype association—what I understand is called gene-environment correlation—with no direct genetic causation; and that changing everyone's environment can move the population mean even when heritability is high and unchanged. I would like the debrief to include a demonstration of that last point, for instance re-running a round with the environmental contribution set to a constant and showing the phenotype collapse, and a quick uniform shift of everyone's environment showing the mean move while the estimate stays put. Ask the group, at the end, to state in one sentence what a heritability number licenses and what it does not, and to name the one thing in the room they would have to know in order to interpret it.

Tell me plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: participants who read the statistic as "half of my trait is genetic"; participants who conclude from round two that genes became more important or that the environment stopped mattering; participants who dismiss round three as a rigged trick rather than a model of a real confound; the fact that with roughly twenty people and a handful of slips the variance estimates are noisy, and what I should do about it, whether by giving each person several slips so the pooled sample is larger or by showing a large simulated population alongside the live one; and the near-certainty that someone raises group differences between populations, which I would like addressed honestly and briefly without letting it derail the session.

Finally, be frank about which parts of the idea this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself: the real machinery by which heritability is actually estimated in practice and why the estimates people argue about come out the way they do; the distinction between different senses of heritability and the assumptions that make each one well defined; the way additive assumptions can fail when genes interact with one another or with environments; the gap between a heritability estimate and any claim about what is genetically caused in a counterfactual sense; and the fact that the tidy integers and clean rules here are a caricature of continuous, polygenic, error-laden measurement. I would like the closing note to be clear that the activity can calibrate the concept but cannot settle any empirical question about any real trait.
