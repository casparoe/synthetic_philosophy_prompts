I've been reading alot about the proposal where you train two models to argue opposite sides of a question and a weaker judge model decides who won, on the idea that it's easier to spot a bad argument than to produce a good one. I buy the basic setup. But I keep noticing a move in ordinary arguing that I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know if it's an established thing or just a hole in the vocabulary.

Here's the move. You don't win by making your own claim more plausible; you win by making the other person's job more expensive. Suppose I'm defending something shaky. Instead of defending it, I state it in a form where checking it means walking a long chain of individually boring, individually correct steps -- a definition here, a caveat there, a footnote about how the numbers were collected. Each step is fine. The total is a wall. Anyone with a budget -- a judge with a token limit, a reviewer with twenty other PRs open, a reader on a train -- either gives up or skims. If they give up, I haven't lied, and nothing I said was false. The failure gets booked to their attention span, not to me. And the same trick works when my claim is true: I can state a true thing in the most expensive-to-verify form available and collect the same protection. Which means the careful version of me is now at a disadvantage against the careless one, who gets to shop around for the costliest valid way of putting things.

Two other places I think I see the same shape. In code review, someone answers "why does this work?" with four paragraphs of true but tangential detail, and the reviewer approves it to get on with their day. And in online arguments, the move where the reply is that it's all covered in the primary literature and you'd have to go read three books -- where the cost of checking has been set above the cost of just conceding.

I don't think it's the motte-and-bailey, because nobody retreats; the same claim stays up the whole time. I don't think it's the firehose style of arguing, the one where you throw forty claims at once, because that's about the count of claims and the audience's clock, and what I'm describing is usually one claim, where the enemy is the checking cost of that one claim. And it isn't just the old observation that nonsense is cheaper to produce than to refute, because in that version the producer is merely careless, whereas here the producer is optimizing -- and can be optimizing while saying only true things.

So: is there a name for this, or a couple of names that split it up? In particular I want the line between the case where the murk is hiding a flaw and the case where there's no flaw at all and the murk is just the admission price. If there's no established term, coin one, define it, and tell me where its edges are -- specifically whether this is just the cheaper-to-produce-than-to-refute point wearing a bigger vocabulary, and what (if anything) stops the slide into: any true claim can be stated expensively, so a judge on a fixed budget is scoring rhetoric rather than truth. And if you think the real problem is the judge's budget and not the arguer's move, say that instead.
