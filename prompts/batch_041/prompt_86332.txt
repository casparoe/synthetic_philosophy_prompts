Draft: The Spike and the Spread: Why the Anthropocene Could Not Be Periodized

For fifteen years a committee of stratigraphers tried to do something no one had done before: to give a formal name and a formal boundary to the geological present. The Anthropocene Working Group's eventual proposal was to recognize the Anthropocene as an epoch, a unit of the same rank as the Holocene, with its lower boundary marked by a spike in plutonium isotopes left in the environment by atmospheric thermonuclear tests. The reference point — the golden spike, or Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point — was to be placed in the annually laminated sediments at the bottom of Crawford Lake in Ontario, where the fallout horizon is preserved as a single dark lamina in the annual layers. The group proposed to call the new age the Crawfordian, with its base placed at 1950 and its primary marker the plutonium horizon of 1952. Its upper boundary was, by definition, the present day. In 2024 the proposal was voted down, first by the subcommission that had commissioned it and then, by a near-unanimous vote, by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences.

I think the rejection was right and the reasoning was wrong, and that the episode is more interesting than either side has allowed. What the working group ran into was not a shortage of evidence but a mismatch between two things that a single definition was being asked to do at once.

Consider first what a golden spike is for. Since the 1970s, geological boundaries have mostly been defined from the bottom up: rather than dating an event and then hunting for rocks of the right age, stratigraphers select one reference section, mark a point in it, and let that point simply be the boundary. The marker has to be as close to globally synchronous as possible — an ash fall, a magnetic reversal, a chemical signal — because a boundary is only useful if you can find it somewhere else. A golden spike is a solution to the correlation problem, and its whole virtue is that it turns a question about time into a question about a single measurable horizon in a single place.

Now consider the thing the Anthropocene is supposed to name. Human transformation of the Earth is not a horizon; it is a spread. Industrialization begins in coal-fired Britain and reaches Bengal and Shandong a century and a half later. Dams, concrete, plastics, synthetic nitrogen, invasive species, the extirpation of megafauna — each has its own geography and its own tempo. Stratigraphers have a word for this: diachrony. A change that sweeps across a surface unevenly, arriving in one basin before another, does not behave like a boundary; it behaves like a facies, and a facies cannot define a point. The only genuinely synchronous anthropogenic signals are those that arrived through the atmosphere: the radionuclides, and to a lesser degree the fly ash and the carbon-isotope excursion from fossil fuel burning.

So the working group faced a dilemma, not a gap in the data. Define the Anthropocene by its marker and you date its beginning to the first thermonuclear test rather than to the beginning of the human remaking of the planet. Define it by the phenomenon and there is no golden spike, and therefore no formal unit at all. Call this the isochrony dilemma. I do not think it can be dissolved by better measurement, because it does not arise from measurement. It arises because the concept in circulation and the concept the time scale can accommodate are two different concepts that happen to share a word.

Here is a way to see it. Imagine a stratigrapher ten million years from now, with no knowledge of our century, reading the rocks. She will find two records. One is a razor-sharp, globally synchronous horizon of artificial radionuclides and industrial aerosols. The other is a thick, uneven, facies-dependent mess of cities, roads, spoil heaps, plastics, chicken bones and a carbon excursion. Being a stratigrapher, she will correlate with the sharp one, because correlation is what the discipline is for. And so the Anthropocene, as a formal unit, would be defined by the fallout of weapons that were never used rather than by the industry that changed the climate. That is a perfectly respectable outcome. It is also a sign that the formal Anthropocene would not have been the Anthropocene that anyone outside stratigraphy means.

This brings me to the part I find philosophically interesting. The term as it is actually used — in newspapers, in the humanities, in policy documents, in the titles of conferences — does not function as a period name at all. It functions as an indexical, in the way that now, here, and us do. Its reference is fixed by the context of utterance rather than by a boundary in rock. To say that we are living in the Anthropocene is closer in logical form to saying that we are in trouble than to saying that we are living in the Miocene. A period name tells you where you are in a sequence; an indexical tells you where the speaker stands. The working group was asked, in effect, to convert an indexical into a proper name, and the failure was a category failure rather than an evidentiary one. This also explains why the argument kept sliding from evidence to authority: you can falsify a claim about the Miocene, but you cannot falsify a claim about now.

Two consequences follow. The first is that the definition would have been performative. A ratified epoch that has ended and a ratified epoch still underway would license very different behaviour in the reader, and the difference has nothing to do with the rocks. The second is that the unit would have been unlike any other in the time scale: its upper boundary is the future, and its defining signal is still circulating in air and water rather than being fixed in the rock record. Every other boundary in the time scale is a claim about a past that is over; this one would have been a claim about a present that is not.

I therefore want to suggest that the Anthropocene is better treated as a diagnosis than as a period. A period is a container, defined once and used thereafter; a diagnosis is a present-tense, revisable, contestable claim about the state of a system, made by someone with a purpose. The judgement that a patient has an infection does not require a golden spike. It requires a defensible reading of the evidence and an answerable claim about what to do. If that is right, then the question of who decides when the Anthropocene began is malformed: no one decides, and the demand for a decision is itself a symptom of having borrowed the wrong kind of concept. The right question is who is entitled to say what the Earth's present condition is, for what purpose, and with what standing to be contradicted.

The bodies that rejected the proposal did the right thing for the wrong reasons. They thought they were protecting a scientific standard from a political intrusion, when in fact they were declining a job that stratigraphy is not equipped to do. The IUGS's own statement on the rejection was more nearly right than its reasoning, since it allowed that the term would go on being used by earth scientists, social scientists and the public, and would remain a useful descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. That is what a diagnosis is: a term that works because it is answerable to a purpose, not because it has been ratified.
