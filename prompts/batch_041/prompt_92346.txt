You are a philosopher of ethics and an expert in population axiology, with a working knowledge of the debate over negative utilitarianism, the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and the aggregation problems that motivate lexical and threshold views. Please think carefully before you commit to a reading. Begin by brainstorming, for yourself, the candidate axiological positions that a novel of this kind might be seen as staging; only then argue for the one you find most defensible. Assume throughout that I know the text and the secondary literature well: I do not need citations, a bibliography, or further reading, and you should not attempt to supply them. You may take the plot and the major scenes as given; do not quote the novel, since neither of us needs the exact wording and paraphrase will keep the argument cleaner.

I am writing a paper defending a view I call lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, and I want to test it against a work of fiction rather than against further puzzle cases. The view has two parts. The first is negative: the disvalue of suffering, rather than the value of pleasure, is the primary axiological currency, so that a state of affairs is improved chiefly by the removal of suffering. The second is a threshold: once suffering passes some level of intensity or duration, its disvalue dominates lexically, in the sense that no quantity of pleasure, however large, can compensate for a single instance of it; below the threshold, by contrast, pleasure and pain remain commensurable and trade off in the ordinary way. The view thus splits the difference between classical lexical negative utilitarianism and the ordinary hedonic calculus, and I am not yet sure the split is stable.

The work I want you to read it through is Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov, and in particular the sequence in Book V in which Ivan, speaking with Alyosha, sets out his refusal of any final harmony purchased with the suffering of children, and then delivers the poem of the Grand Inquisitor. I take Ivan's central move to be a claim about commensurability, not merely a claim about how much suffering is too much: he seems to deny that the ledger can be balanced at all, and he locates his refusal in the fact that he will not, or cannot, accept a sum in which one child's suffering is entered as a cost against a total of future bliss. I want a reading that takes the novel seriously as philosophy and the philosophy seriously as philosophy, and that is faithful in both directions.

Several questions should organize the reading. First, is Ivan best understood as a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian, or does he hold something stronger or weaker: a deontological constraint against using the child as a means, a refusal of aggregation as such, or an incommensurability thesis that is not about thresholds at all? Where exactly does his position differ from mine, and does the text give us enough to tell? Second, note that Ivan argues by moving along a spectrum of cases -- from the general fact of cruelty, to particular atrocities, to the specific case of the tortured child, to the mother's dilemma about whether to forgive the torturer -- and consider whether the novel is itself deploying something like a spectrum argument, and whether that form of argument helps or undermines the commensurability claim he wants. Third, consider Ivan's self-description as a mind of Euclidean cast that cannot understand a harmony it is nonetheless asked to accept, and ask what work that disclaimer does: is it a concession that his refusal is a limitation of his nature rather than a truth about value, and if so, does it leave his ethical claim standing or undercut it?

Then turn to the counter-axiology the novel offers. Zosima's teaching of active love and universal responsibility, Alyosha's vocation of practical care, and the epilogue among the schoolboys and the dying Ilyusha all present a way of valuing lives that is not a sum of pleasures and pains at all. I want you to say whether this amounts to a genuine alternative to the hedonic ledger, and whether it can accommodate the child's suffering any better than Ivan can, or whether it simply declines to do the arithmetic Ivan demands. Where does the novel resist my reading, and where does it resist Ivan's? I would rather have the honest concession than a forced fit, so tell me plainly which scenes the lexical-threshold interpretation cannot handle and what that costs it.

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: what does the novel show about the commensurability of pleasure and pain that the philosophical arguments do not, and that a model of the debate built only from the axiological literature would miss? If the work adds something to the debate that argument alone does not supply, say what it is and why it counts as philosophical insight rather than mere illustration. If it adds nothing, say that instead, and explain what the appearance of depth amounts to.
