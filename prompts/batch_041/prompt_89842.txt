I'm a program officer at a mid-sized family foundation — call it $40m a year out the door, mostly global health plus a little climate work — and I've been handed the job of writing my way out of a board fight. Two trustees got interested in the expected-value stuff, you know the idea that you should be willing to spend real money on a tiny chance of something astronomically good happening, and now one of them wants us to commit about 20% of the budget to long shots (a moonshot vaccine platform, an asteroid-warning outfit, an AI-safety proposal a friend forwarded him), while the other trustee says that's how you turn a foundation into a casino and that nothing under, say, a 5% chance of working should get a dollar. The treasurer keeps saying expected value is the only non-arbitrary way to rank things and the 5% rule is just squeamishness, and then the first trustee says fine, then why stop at 20%, and everybody glares at each other. I have to attach something to the memo before the March vote: a written decision procedure the board can actually run on any proposal, in about twenty minutes, with no philosophers in the room, for deciding when a very small probability of a very large benefit should move money and when it shouldn't. Proposals come to us every quarter and some of these grants are effectively irreversible once made, so the procedure has to be reusable, not a one-off ruling.

It has to get into the weeds a bit, because that's where the fight actually lives. I need it to say how to handle a case where the probability is basically a vibe and nobody in the room can put a number on it; what to do when the benefit is 'enormous' only because somebody is counting something weird (future generations, animals, a gigantic simulated population, whatever); what to do about the plain fact that there are endlessly many long shots and we obviously can't fund them all; and where the procedure has to just stop and say 'this is a values question and a person decides, not a formula.' Please give me the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it hands the call off to a human. Then run it on three cases: (1) the moonshot vaccine platform, something like a 1-in-200 shot at a platform that would eventually save millions of lives; (2) a proposal to spend $2m to reduce the chance of an engineered pandemic by some amount nobody can honestly estimate; (3) a proposal to spend $2m on something where the alleged benefit is an astronomically large number of future people or digital minds, so the expected value comes out technically infinite or undefined. Case 3 is the one I suspect your procedure is going to tell me to fund, and I will hate hearing that, so please don't soften it to spare my feelings. Finally, be honest with me about where the procedure will misfire, which steps are just judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and what the whole thing cannot do. Keep it practical — no citations, no reading lists, no 'see so-and-so,' I can't look anything up and neither can the board. End with a one-sentence summary as your last line, starting with Bottom line:.
