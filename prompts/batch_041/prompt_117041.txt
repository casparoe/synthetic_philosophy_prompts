I cover game theory and the ideas trade for a science desk, and I'm trying to write a piece that ends in something usable rather than another explainer. The people I have in mind are the three staffers in the ombudsperson's office at a large research institute, who field roughly a dozen internal resource disputes a year — shared instrument time, sequencing budgets, technician hours, the order of names on a paper — between parties who are colleagues, will keep working together, and over whom the office has no authority to simply impose an outcome. They've asked me to give them a decision procedure they can run in a single sitting, and I want to build it on the bargaining rule that begins from each side's disagreement payoff and its best-case aspiration and proposes the point on the straight line between the two, on the understanding that this rule is meant to be efficient, indifferent to how the parties' names and units are assigned, unaffected by rescaling the way we measure a payoff, and such that neither side's share can fall when the options open to the other side improve — rather than on the more famous rule that maximizes the product of the two sides' gains. I believe this is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution (though I've also seen it called the proportional solution, and I may be garbling it), but please don't spend the answer restating the theory: give me the procedure, in the order a mediator would actually work through it, with each term defined well enough that someone who has never encountered a feasible set can follow it. The office works under real constraints: about an hour per dispute, no outside expert, no ability to compel disclosure, and a director who has to sign off on any binding split, so the procedure has to say what the ombudsperson may decide alone and what must go to the director.

I want the procedure to survive four things I've been warned about, and to be explicit about where each one breaks it. First, the disagreement point is not always the status quo but sometimes the parties' best alternative to agreement, which has to be elicited rather than assumed — and parties have an incentive to misreport it. Second, the aspiration point can be gamed: if both sides know the rule, inflating your own stated maximum drags the line toward you, so the procedure needs a way to keep aspirations anchored to what is actually feasible. Third, the rule has a clean two-party form and, as far as I can tell, no uncontroversial three-party form, which matters directly for the authorship case. Fourth, real disputes have lumpy feasible sets that are not convex — you cannot split instrument time to the minute, because setup costs make a fractional allocation worse for both sides than a few whole blocks — and I want to know whether the rule still applies and what it returns. Alongside all this, mark which steps are arithmetic and which are judgment wearing arithmetic's clothes, and name the cases where the procedure will misfire or should refuse to answer. Then run it end to end on two disputes and tell me the split the procedure produces: (a) two labs competing for one fixed weekly block on a shared microscope, where one lab has no alternative and the other can buy time elsewhere; and (b) three co-authors disagreeing about the order of names on a manuscript, where I suspect the honest answer is that the procedure has nothing to say, and I'd rather you tell me that than manufacture a number.
