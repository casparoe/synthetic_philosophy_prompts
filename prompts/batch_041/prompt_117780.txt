I'm a retired botanist — thirty-odd years keying out plants and bickering with colleagues about what makes a species a species — and I've lately fallen into philosophy, mostly by reading at night and getting annoyed. Here's the thing I can't let go of. What makes every 'a' an 'a'? Not "why is this typeface pretty" — the identity question. The one-story 'a' I write by hand and the two-story 'a' in the book I'm reading are the same letter. A stencil 'a' with its bowl cracked open is still an 'a'. Squash an 'a' sideways and it's a fat 'a'; turn it upside down and it isn't an 'a' anymore, it's a shape. And the Cyrillic 'а' is the same shape as ours but it's a different letter, which I find maddening. I gather someone built a whole AI project about designing fonts in many styles, but I don't remember who or what it was called, and I don't need it.

So: give me a taxonomy of the positions on what letterform identity actually consists in. I want it built as a tree where every branch is labeled with the question that splits it, phrased so I could answer it by pointing at cases rather than by picking a label I already like. Leaves get the name of the position, or "unnamed" if nobody's defended it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Tell me which combinations nobody occupies and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two positions your tree can't really separate instead of fudging it.

It has to include leaves for at least: identity as an ideal form that styles are deformations of; family resemblance with no necessary conditions; identity as purely contrastive, so an 'a' is whatever stands in the right distinctive-feature relations to the other letters of its style; identity as whatever a set of generative rules produces; identity as whatever competent readers of the script accept; identity as membership in a copying lineage going back to earlier 'a's; and the flat "no fact of the matter, it's a useful shorthand." And please work these cases in: one-story vs two-story, the stencil 'a', a mirrored 'a' (the kind kids write, that we read anyway), the Cyrillic lookalike, and why stretching preserves identity while rotating doesn't. Roughly eight to twelve leaves is plenty.

Tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one branch point you're least sure really separates two live positions. American English, please.
