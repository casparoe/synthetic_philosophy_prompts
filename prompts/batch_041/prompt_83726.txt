I've got a long piece to file on whether the big historical periods are real things or just inventions of the people who write history books, and the more I read the less I know what I actually think, so I'm going to put my gut verdicts down and ask you to find the principle behind them, if there is one.

The Middle Ages: real. Roughly the same Latin, the same church, the same assumptions about hierarchy and the world to come, from the fifth century to the fifteenth, and the people at the far end of it knew they were living after something. 'The Dark Ages', though, isn't a period, it's an insult that got a job.

The Renaissance: not real. It's a nineteenth-century sales pitch about a handful of Italian cities, and the tell is that we still can't agree when it starts — 1300? 1400? 1500? You don't have to argue about the start date of something that's actually there.

Late antiquity: real. And this is definately the one that breaks my head, because it's a twentieth-century invention as well, and it makes the same move as the Renaissance — the bit everyone used to call the fall of Rome is really the continuation of Rome — yet one feels like a discovery and the other like a rebrand.

The Scientific Revolution: real. Something did change in how people argued about nature, and I don't think you can explain that away as the same activity under new management.

The 'long eighteenth century': not real. It exists because a certain kind of economic historian needed a container, and its dates move whenever the argument does.

The Industrial Revolution: real, but I don't think it's a period at all — it's an event that took eighty years, and we call it a period only because it's too big to call an event.

The Anthropocene: no idea. We're inside it.

Then there's a thing a colleague invented for her book, 'the age of the queue', 1940 to 1979, defined by rationing and waiting lists. She reckons it's as well-evidenced as anything above. I think it isn't a period, and I want to know why not, in a way that doesn't also kill the Middle Ages.

Her test is that a period is real if you could date its edges from the inside, without our textbooks — the people at the time could feel the seam. I like it, but the Middle Ages fails it badly, since nobody woke up in 476 feeling medieval, and the queue period passes it easily, since everyone standing in a queue knew exactly what they were in.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? If there isn't, which of my verdicts do I have to give up to get one? And what does it say about the Axial Age, which I left off the list on purpose because I genuinely can't tell whether it's a period or a thesis? Give me the principle, or a shortlist of them, each with the case that motivates it and an honest note about what it can't save. Can you do it in British English, by the way — I'm on a London desk and my editor will have my guts otherwise.
