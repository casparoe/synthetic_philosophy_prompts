I am a PhD student in philosophy of social science assembling a seminar handout on whether "national character" names anything coherent and under what conditions a generalization about a people may be asserted. Please give me a decision tree that sorts the positions on this question, built from the logical space rather than from a roster of familiar labels: every internal node should be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I need the tree to separate positions along four axes, in roughly this order. First, whether a national-character claim attributes a trait to each member, to a distribution over members, or to institutions and practices rather than to persons at all. Second, whether a nation is a homogeneous enough reference class for the inference the claim is meant to license, or whether the within-group variance swamps the between-group difference. Third, whether the permissibility of asserting a generalization turns on its truth, on the speaker's relation to the group, or on the use to which the assertion is put. Fourth, whether the attribution is expected to feed back into the trait it describes, either reinforcing it or provoking its rejection, and whether that feedback tells for or against asserting it.

Build the nodes from concrete cases: a frequency claim about a trait, a claim about a group's sense of humor or emotional style, a self-ascription by a member of the group, and a generalization embedded in a policy document. Aim for six to nine leaves. Where two positions cannot be separated by any node, say so explicitly rather than papering over it, and for any combination of answers that no one occupies, say briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
