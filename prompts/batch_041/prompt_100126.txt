I sit on the grants committee of a large global-health foundation. Our charter says were to allocate for the greatest total wellbeing, full stop, and the modelling team ranks proposals by adding up expected wellbeing over everyone affected -- including people who only exist because of the intervention. Next week we're picking between two portfolios that cost the same: one averts child deaths in a high-mortality region (so we end up with a bigger population, each survivor living a modest but decent life), the other treats chronic illness and lifts incomes for people who are already alive. The model says the first wins by a mile -- but only because the added lives score just barely above zero on our life-satisfaction scale, whose zero turns out to be "the worst life you can imagine" rather than a life that's neither good nor bad, and because it carries the added lives' descendants out a hundred years at a discount rate we basically picked by default. Nudge either assumption and the ranking flips. One member says that if we follow the model we'd have to fund anything that adds people, even people living lives barely worth living, which is absurd; another says that's just squeamishness and the arithmetic is the arithmetic. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Recommend something concrete -- fund one portfolio, or suspend the rule, or rewrite it -- with a timeframe for whatever you recommend, and make the strongest case for the side you're rejecting rather than a cartoon of it. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
