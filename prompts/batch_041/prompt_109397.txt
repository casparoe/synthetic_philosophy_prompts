I am setting the agenda for a small formal epistemology group — myself, two doctoral students, and a two-year grant — and we have to decide where to spend it. Our current focus is the problem of old evidence, and I have come to think the received framing of that problem is too narrow. Before you answer, I want you to assess the framing rather than accept it.

As I understand the received framing: a Bayesian agent is certain of its evidence, so conditionalizing on evidence it already possesses changes nothing, and the old evidence therefore cannot confirm the hypothesis it is supposed to support. The standard repairs — asking what the agent's credence in the hypothesis would have been had it never learned the evidence; subtracting off the portion of the evidence already counted in the prior; insisting that evidence is rarely certain and treating it as merely probabilified; or abandoning diachronic confirmation altogether in favor of an atemporal relation between hypothesis and evidence — all take for granted that the space of possibilities is fixed and that learning consists only in redistributing credence across it. I suspect that old evidence is one face of a more general difficulty for agents who are not logically omniscient: an agent can learn only what it is uncertain about, yet its total evidence is by definition certain, and the algebra of propositions it treats as possible may itself be something it must revise. The problem of new theories looks like the same difficulty from the other side, and the stock cases have the same shape — a theory constructed to accommodate a datum that is then cited as confirming it, as with the long-known residual in the precession of Mercury's perihelion, or a design calibrated on the very failures it is later said to explain; a conjecture believed for years and only then proved; a scientist who rereads her own notebook and must decide what to believe about a measurement she has forgotten; and, most sharply for us, a model whose performance is reported on data drawn from the distribution on which it was trained.

Suppose for the sake of argument that this frame is worth adopting. Which open questions within it should a group like ours actually work on, in what order, and why? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. For each question: why it is upstream of the others; whether it is tractable with the tools of formal epistemology rather than requiring empirical psychology or machine learning; whether anything downstream would change if it were settled; and what would move it up or down the list over the next two years.

Then tell me where the frame itself is wrong. In particular: is non-omniscience one problem or a family of loosely related ones? Are the practical cases genuine instances of the same difficulty, or do they merely resemble it? And is there a question outside my frame that ought to be at the top of the list regardless?

I have no use for a reading list; what I need is the argument for the ordering, including your strongest case against your own first choice.
