I have been going back and forth with a friend who runs a small import business about the payments his firm makes to customs officers — small, predictable, informal sums that he insists are not bribes but simply what he calls "grease on the wheels." His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, runs like this. The port's paperwork is slow and the officers are underpaid; the money does not change what is owed or who owes it, only when. Without it, cargo sits for weeks and everyone loses — his firm, his customers, the port. He adds a detail that I think is doing more work than he realizes: the delays are not really accidental, since the slowest desk collects the most and the supervisor takes a share. And he closes by saying that we in the West call this corruption only because we happen to have bought our way out of the problem with better-funded bureaucracies, so condemning his practice is just exporting a foreign standard. I have heard the same figure of speech from people far more sympathetic to anti-corruption work, who offer it as a rueful concession about how the world actually runs, so I assume it is meant to carry an argument and not merely to decorate a complaint.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy itself rather than a verdict on my friend. Set out what corresponds to what — what plays the role of the engine, the gears, the friction, the lubricant, the output, the mechanic — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some of the breaks are obvious once stated, and I suspect others are not; I am especially interested in whether the picture can survive the fact that the friction is produced by people who notice the grease and can respond to it, and whether the metaphor quietly treats the rulebook, the length of the queue, and the officer's salary as fixed background conditions rather than as things the payment itself helps to shape. Sort the breaks that matter for his conclusion — that the practice is harmless, and that objecting to it is parochial — from the ones that are merely picturesque. Then tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if some other comparison does the job better (a tip, a toll, an auction, a tax on a franchise the officer has in effect bought), say which one and what it lets us see that "grease" hides.
