I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy and, this term, the grader for a 400-level seminar on the value of knowledge. The final exercise asked students to work through a version of the Meno problem. I would like you to grade one student's response and give me the itemized comments that justify the grade.

The exercise was marked out of 20: roughly four marks for the reconstruction of the problem, eight for the reconstruction and analysis of the objection, and eight for the assessment of the proposed solution. Please give a mark out of 20 and then assess the response claim by claim, stating for each substantive claim whether it is correct, incorrect, or incomplete, and giving your reasons. Where the response errs, specify the error precisely and say what the correct treatment would be; where it is right but thin, say what a fuller answer would add; and note any points at which its claims sit uneasily with one another. I need an assessment I could defend to the student point by point, not a general impression of the whole.

The question read as follows.

(a) State the Meno problem: why should knowledge be more valuable than true belief, given that a true belief can guide action just as well as knowledge — the traveller who merely truly believes that the road to Larissa leads there arrives just as surely as one who knows it?

(b) Consider the following objection. Suppose the value of a true belief consists in its being true, and suppose further that being justified, or being reliably formed, is valuable only as a means to holding true beliefs. Now take a belief that is already true. Its being justified cannot make it any truer; and if justification's only value is as a means to truth, then once the truth is in hand the justification adds nothing further — its value is swamped by the value of the truth it was meant to secure, just as the value of the printer's ink lies in the readability of the text and adds nothing to a text that is already readable. State this objection as forcefully as you can, and then say precisely what it does and what it does not establish about the comparative value of knowledge and of true belief.

(c) A classmate proposes the following solution to the Meno problem: knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief because knowledge is produced by a reliable process, whereas a merely true belief may be a lucky guess. Assess whether this proposal meets the objection in (b).

The student's response was as follows.

"The Meno problem is the problem of explaining why knowledge is more valuable than true belief. Socrates introduces it by observing that a true belief about the way to Larissa will get a traveller to Larissa just as surely as knowledge of the way there will; the two states seem to guide action equally well, so it is unclear wherein the additional worth of knowledge lies. Socrates' own suggestion is that true belief resembles the statues of Daedalus, which are valuable but unstable, tending to run away unless they are held in place. Knowledge is true belief that has been held in place by an account of why it is true, and so is the more valuable state because it is the more stable one: it will not be lost. This is why knowledge is preferable — it stays with us and can be relied upon over time.

The objection in (b) is a general challenge to any answer of this form. It grants, for the sake of argument, that what makes a true belief valuable is its being true, and that whatever marks knowledge off from mere true belief — justification, reliability, or being held in place — is valuable only as a means to holding true beliefs. Now take a belief that is already true. Its being justified cannot make it any truer; and since justification's only value lay in its being a means to truth, the truth has already exhausted that value, so the justification adds nothing more. The objection therefore shows that justification contributes nothing of its own, and that for a belief that is already true there is no difference in value between its being knowledge and its being mere true belief.

The classmate's proposal does meet the objection. The objection succeeds only against accounts that treat the mark of knowledge as a separable extra which contributes nothing once the belief is true. Reliabilism is not such an account, because on reliabilism the mark of knowledge is not an add-on but a relation to the facts: the belief is produced by a process that is truth-conducive, and so is more likely to be true than a lucky guess. Since a reliably produced belief is more likely to be true, it is more valuable; and since its greater likelihood of truth is precisely a matter of its connection to the truth, reliabilism explains the superiority of knowledge without appealing to any value beyond the value of truth."
