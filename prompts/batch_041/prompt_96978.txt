I'm an epidemiologist who works on injury surveillance — the unglamorous end of it: case definitions, external-cause coding, denominators, and the perpetual argument over how much of a change in recorded rates is real and how much is ascertainment. Over the holidays a historian friend and I got into a long argument about the claim that violence has declined over the centuries, in the vein of the Pinker-style better-angels thesis, and about the standard objections to it: that the apparent decline is partly an artifact of better record-keeping, that the early baselines are guesses, that per-capita comparisons across centuries require a denominator nobody actually has. What struck me is that I could follow the numbers but kept silently translating the argument into my own terms, and that when I did, chunks of it seemed to evaporate — which is either a sign that the debate is muddier than it looks or that my vocabulary is blinder than I think. So: take the whole thing — the empirical thesis, the objections, and especially the underlying conceptual dispute about what counts as violence, whether the question should be posed per capita or in absolute terms, and whether there is a fact of the matter at all — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. I mean the real machinery, not a metaphor: case definitions and the sensitivity/specificity tradeoffs built into them; person-time and standardized rates, and what standardization does and does not license across centuries and jurisdictions; ascertainment and reporting infrastructure as a time-varying confounder; capture–recapture for estimating the size of the unobserved; burden-of-disease measures and attributable fractions for deaths from deprivation or omission; and the standard move of comparing ecologic series while disclaiming causal inference. I'm fluent in this vocabulary, so don't simplify it for me; I'd rather see the translation done precisely than read a gloss.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which claims in the debate translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion — and name the distortion each time, don't smooth it over — and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I'm particularly interested in three candidates: the moralized character of the term violence itself (injury is not a term of reproach; violence is), the notion of progress or improvement as opposed to mere decline, and the suggestion that some harms are so diffuse or structural that assigning them to cases is a category error rather than a coding problem. For each, tell me whether the residue marks a genuine blind spot in surveillance thinking or whether it shows that the original claim was doing less work than its proponents suppose — and I want you to be evenhanded about which side gets embarrassed. Finally, press on me a little: I keep assuming my framework is the neutral, stipulative one and that the philosophers are the ones importing values, but I know perfectly well that disability weights and the choice of a case definition are decisions, not discoveries. If that's right, say what follows for the fact-of-the-matter question, and tell me whether the version of it I can pose in my own terms is the same question the debate is really about or only a shadow of it. No need for citations or reading lists; I'm after the translation and the residue, not the literature.
