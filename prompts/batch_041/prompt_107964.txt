I teach a 90-minute meeting of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility: fourteen to eighteen students, movable chairs, nothing needed beyond index cards, envelopes, a timer, and pens. I want a game that stages a Frankfurt-style case with real choices and real stakes, so that the group runs into the standard objections to such cases from the inside — before the objections are named, before the principle of alternative possibilities is named, and without any lecture up front. The terminology should arrive only in the debrief.

The shape I have in mind is this. In the actual run, one participant makes a decision on their own reasoning and that decision settles an outcome for the group. Unbeknownst to that participant, I have arranged matters so the outcome would have been the same whatever they decided. The rest of the group, also unaware, must judge afterwards whether the chooser acted freely and whether the chooser is answerable for what happened. Then the reveal, then the vote again.

What I care about most, and the reason I am not simply asking for a staged case, is that the mechanics should force the group to choose where the guarantee comes from, and should make that choice cost them something. Any guarantee needs a trigger: my rule, my card, my planted confederate, my unopened envelope has to read something about the chooser in order to know when to step in. There are three ways to set it, and I want the participants — not me — to have to choose one before they know why it matters, and then to discover what each choice buys and what it costs:

1. Fire early. The trigger acts at the first flicker in the wrong direction, so the outcome really is guaranteed — and the chooser never in fact makes the choice that the group has just spent ten minutes assessing. The verdict they reached was about nobody.
2. Fire on a predictor. The trigger acts on a perfectly reliable earlier sign of what the chooser is about to do, so the outcome is guaranteed and the chooser really does decide. But on reconstruction the group should be able to see that the whole case now rests on assuming that the decision was already settled before it was made — which is close to the very thing the case was supposed to establish something about.
3. Fire late. The trigger acts only once the decision is settled. Now the chooser really decides and the case is clean, but there is a stretch of time in which the chooser could still have gone the other way, so the guarantee is no guarantee at all.

Run the activity at least twice with the trigger set differently, so the group feels both horns rather than being told about them, and if you can arrange for them to argue for one setting over another, so much the better.

Second, I want a residue. In every branch the chooser does something the guarantee never touched: forms an intention, writes a word, reaches for a card, opens their mouth. Build in physical evidence of this — a card written and set aside, an envelope never opened, a move recorded and then overwritten — so that when the group reconstructs the round they must decide what to do with it: whether a scrap of an alternative is enough to make the chooser responsible, whether it is too thin to count as an alternative at all, and whether their answer changes when the residue is a whole intention rather than a twitch.

Operational requirements. No real money; the stakes should be small but genuine, and should make blame or praise a live question without putting anyone in a bad position (points that determine who leads the next round, or whether the group's remaining time goes to their preferred topic, rather than anything that punishes a person). Every participant should hold at least one role across the session — chooser, judge, trigger-setter, confederate, recorder. Keep the number of confederates to a minimum and tell me what to do if one leaks. I will be deceiving students, so I need a disclosure moment and a script for it. The judges' pre-reveal verdicts must be recorded in a form I can hold up against the post-reveal verdicts.

The debrief must map specific moments in the run onto specific distinctions: the difference between being unable to do otherwise and being unanswerable for what one did; what the trigger's timing does to the case; what would have to be true of the residue for it to count; and why the group's pre-reveal verdict may have been unanimous for a reason that has nothing to do with the argument. It should be honest that the game cannot settle whether the case succeeds.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — including the round in which the rigging simply fails, the group that reaches its pre-reveal verdict for the wrong reason, the trigger-setter who treats their choice as a moral judgment about the chooser rather than a design decision, and the participant who suspects deception and stops playing. Then say plainly which features of the case the activity cannot reproduce and which piece of the intuition I will have to supply by hand.

Give me the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, the rules and payoff scheme in a form I can read aloud, a one-page handout for the judges, and the facilitator's script for the reveal.
