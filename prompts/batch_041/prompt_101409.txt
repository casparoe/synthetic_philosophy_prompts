I keep getting into arguments about this and I'd like to know if there's an actual principle behind my verdicts, because right now I think I'm just being inconsistent and I dont like it.

Here's the kind of thing I mean. Our town put up a centenary plaque saying we'd taken in refugees during the war, and it turns out from the council minutes that we turned them away at the station. I think the plaque has to be changed, no argument. But my nan's story that her dad won a medal at Ypres, which the service records show never happened — I'd never correct her, and I'd think less of anyone who did.

Then: a school textbook repeating the story that the local castle held out against the king for a year, when the archaeology says it fell in a fortnight — the textbook's just wrong and should be fixed. But the church's Easter procession re-enacts a martyrdom the historians say probably never happened, and I'd be furious if anyone tried to "correct" it.

Now the ones that trip me up. A mate of mine works on a land claim where the community's account puts their people on a particular river flat from well before the written record has anyone there at all, and that written record is a survey drawn up by the same colonial office that moved them off it. I think the community's account should carry real evidential weight here, not just be "memory" we tolerate — the written record isn't neutral, it's someone else's memory with a stamp on it.

But then there's the other one. There's a village near us whose whole identity is that a local man invented a particular farm machine, and the patent records pretty clearly show a man two counties over got there first. Correcting them would be true and would also gut the one thing the village has. I can't work out why I want to leave that alone when I want the refugee plaque pulled down. Both are false, both are public, both are load-bearing for the place's self-image.

My half-formed principle is something like: memory gets to keep its place where the record can't be disentangled from the interests that made it, and where the memory isn't really doing history at all — but I don't know whether that saves the machine village or condemns it. What I want is the actual principle, or two if I need two, stated so I can apply it. What does it say about a memorial to a massacre that the record says was smaller than the community remembers; about a family that wants its ancestor's conviction struck from a local history because the trial was rigged; and about a museum label that currently gives the community's version of where an object came from? And tell me honestly which of my verdicts above I have to give up, because I suspect at least one.

Also — I keep saying "the record" when half the time I mean the archive, the pile of documents, and half the time I mean the settled critical account. Does that distinction do any work, or am I just hiding the problem in a word?
