For the past month I have been reporting on the long-running argument between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether the notion of just deserts can survive what we now believe about how people come to be who they are — I have sat through the recordings, read the exchanges, and spent more time than I care to admit trying to work out whether the two of them are genuinely disagreeing or merely talking past one another. Somewhere in the middle of it I wrote down an argument that I cannot shake, and I would like you to tell me where it fails, if it does, and what abandoning the offending step would cost someone who wanted to keep the rest. It goes like this. (1) Hard treatment is retributive when, and only when, its justification depends on the claim that the offender deserved it in the basic sense — deserved it as a matter of fittingness, rather than as a means to some further good. (2) Basic desert requires that the offender have been the ultimate source of the deed, in a sense not itself wholly traceable to factors they never chose: a self that authored itself, or at least a link in the chain that owes nothing to anything upstream. (3) Nobody satisfies that condition; the regress of influences never terminates in an act of self-creation, and it is not clear that we can even say what such an act would be. (4) So nobody ever deserves hard treatment in the basic sense, and retribution is never justified. (5) The forward-looking justifications — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation — make no appeal to basic desert and therefore survive; this is the abolitionist's consolation prize, and it is the shape of the quarantine analogy, on which the dangerous are confined as carriers of an infection are confined, without anyone being blamed for their contagion. (6) But attend to how (3) does its work. If a person's conduct is not ultimately theirs, then neither, in the same sense, is its improvement: rehabilitation becomes something done to an agent rather than by one, and we might as well speak of rehabilitating a knee. On the argument's own showing, the forward-looking practices in (5) are no more respectful of agency than the retributive ones — merely less candid about treating the person as an object to be adjusted. (7) The objection itself is in trouble. To complain that an offender is treated unjustly is to say that something was owed to them and withheld. If nothing is ever owed to anyone, then the offender's suffering is not an injustice at all; it is an occurrence we happen not to like, on a par with a hailstorm. (8) Therefore either the argument proves too much, dissolving the ground of the abolitionist's own complaint, or there is a currency of justification other than desert — in which case (1) is false, and hard treatment can be unjust without being undeserved.

A philosopher friend who has read a good deal more of this literature than I have tells me the trouble is that I am running together three questions that need separating: whether an act is properly attributable to an agent at all, whether that agent owes anyone an answer for it, and whether the agent is liable to be sanctioned on account of it — and that desert enters only at the third. Perhaps so, though I notice that the move is also available to the retributivist, who can then say that what he wants sits comfortably at the third level and need not be embarrassed by the sceptic's claims about the first two.

So: which single step would you give up, why did it look unobjectionable until it was pressed, and what is the bill? If I abandon (1), do I lose the sharp line between retribution and deterrence on which the entire dispute turns? If I keep (1) and deny (3), what conception of the self am I committing myself to, and can anyone actually hold it? And if the real fault lies in the movement of (6) or (7), say precisely what is being conflated, and whether the conflation is a slip of mine or an equivocation built into the way the debate is usually staged. I should say that I would rather keep (1) through (4) and find the equivocation than give up the premise that seems to me most obviously true, but I want to know what that preference is costing me.
