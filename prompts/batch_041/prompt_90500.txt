Ok so I've been going down a rabit hole on the "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems and I keep hitting a wall, so I want you to help me figure out what kind of argument this actually is. Take the role of someone who's spent years working on moral responsibility and the philosophy of technology, and think out loud and brainstorm before you commit to any verdict.

Here's the setup I keep coming back to. Picture an autonomous system whose designers, worried about being accused of "choosing" a victim, build in a randomization step, so that in a crash it picks among its options on a die roll. Now the outcome is genuinely unforeseen and unchosen by anyone. The designers say: we didn't pick the outcome. The operator says: I didn't pick the system's decision. The system itself can't be a moral agent. So nobody is responsible — a gap.

But the debate around this never seems to resolve. One camp says the gap is real and serious: the whole point of responsibility is that someone can be blamed, and here no one can. Another says it's illusory, because responsibility just gets distributed across designers, deployers, operators, maybe a regulator. A third says it isn't a gap at all but a shift — responsibility moves from whoever "acts" to whoever chose to deploy the thing. And a fourth says everyone's confused, because "responsibility" is being used in three different senses at once (blameworthiness, answerability, and legal liability) and the gap only shows up in one of them.

My hypothesis — and I want you to test it rather than just agree — is that the camps aren't really disagreeing about the facts; they're answering two different questions under one word: a metaphysical one (when does an agent meet the conditions for being morally responsible?) and a practical one (who should answer for this and bear the costs?). If that's right, the "gap" might be an artifact of cramming both into a single frame.

So: diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, a case of different questions wearing the same label, or a hidden empirical/formal issue doing the work? Is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed, or just that it's hard? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. And be honest about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or consideration would actually change your mind.
