I have been keeping a list of behaviors, marking each one mechanical or flexible, and the list has stopped making sense to me. I want the principle behind it, if there is one, and I want to be told which of my marks has to go.

The anchor is a solitary hunting wasp. She paralyzes a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and drags it in. Move the cricket a few inches while she is inside and she starts over: fetch, inspect, emerge, fetch. Do it forty times and she does it forty times. I mark this mechanical. I have seen the word sphexish used for this kind of rigidity, after the genus of digger wasps, and I have been using it as a loose label for everything on the list.

The rest of the list, with my marks:

- The same wasp, on a day when the ground has been dug over, takes a different route home on each trip. Mechanical: the route tracks a gradient, not anything she has worked out.
- A rat that goes on pressing a lever after the food stops, and after a long run of presses begins trying something else. The pressing is mechanical; the switch is flexible.
- Someone reciting a poem learned by heart. Mechanical.
- The same person improvising a counter-melody over a chord progression. Flexible.
- A chess engine's move in a position no game has ever reached. Mechanical, although the move is new and the engine is exquisitely sensitive to the position.
- A friend who adopted a rule for himself — when stuck, try the thing you have never tried — and follows it. Flexible.
- Someone with compulsive hand-washing who knows the washing accomplishes nothing, hates doing it, and cannot stop. Mechanical.
- A colleague who works through a required safety checklist before every run, and who could skip it but does not. Flexible.
- A device that escapes its own loop by flipping a coin. Mechanical: the coin is not tracking anything.

My attempt at a principle: a behavior is mechanical just in case the only thing that can change it is a change in the cues that set it off, and flexible when it can be changed by the system's own assessment of whether the behavior is doing what it is for. That seems to get the wasp, the rat, the washer, and the coin-flipper right. It also seems to get two of my marks wrong. It makes the rule-follower mechanical, since his assessment is just the rule's condition being met; and it makes the poem-reciter flexible, since she would stop if she judged that reciting had stopped serving its purpose. I do not know whether to repair the principle or to abandon the marks.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of them, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it, with the case that forces each clause. Tell me which of my marks the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should drop the mark or add a clause. Say what it implies for two cases I have not marked: a bird that avoids a food for the rest of its life after one bad experience, and a person whose improvisation consists entirely of instructions remembered from a teacher. And say plainly whether the principle I end up with leaves any difference in kind between my flexibility and the wasp's, or only a difference in the number of nested loops.
