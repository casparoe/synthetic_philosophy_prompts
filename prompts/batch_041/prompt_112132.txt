Here's the exam question:

In a legal code, Article 3 says: "Any article of this code may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the legislature." Article 12 says: "Article 12 may not be amended or repealed." A two-thirds majority votes to repeal Article 12. The speaker rules the repeal valid, citing Article 3. A dissenting member says the repeal is void, because Article 12 forbids its own repeal.

(a) Two positions are often distinguished here. On the continuing view, the legislature's authority is at every moment unlimited, and it cannot be legally bound by its own earlier enactments, including enactments about how to amend the code. On the self-embracing view, the legislature's authority includes the power to bind its own future self, so an amendment rule can validly entrench itself. Which view does the speaker's ruling presuppose? Is the ruling correct under each view?

(b) Does the case show that legal validity is ultimately a matter of logic, or a matter of what officials accept and enforce? Explain, using the case.

And here's the student's answer:

"The speaker's ruling presupposes the self-embracing view. On that view the legislature can bind its future self, so Article 12 is a valid entrenchment and the later repeal should be void. The speaker, though, treats Article 3 as if it outranks Article 12, which is really the continuing view. So the ruling is inconsistent with its own ground, and the dissenting member has the better of the textual argument.

On the continuing view, Article 12 is not really a legal rule at all. A rule that cannot be changed by the body that made it is just dead text, like a preamble or a moral aspiration. So under the continuing view the repeal is valid, and under the self-embracing view it is void.

Neither view can be refuted by logic. The words 'may not be amended or repealed' do not by themselves tell you whether Article 12 or Article 3 wins, because each is part of the same code and the code does not say which has priority. This is the self-reference problem: a rule about rules is still a rule, and a rule that governs its own amendment is like a sentence that says 'this sentence is false.' So legal validity is not a matter of logic. It is a matter of what officials accept and enforce. Hart's rule of recognition is exactly this point: validity is relative to the social rule officials use to identify law. If the officials treat Article 3 as the master rule, the repeal is valid; if they treat Article 12 as entrenched, it is void. Since the speaker is an official and the dissent is only a dissent, the repeal is valid in this system.

So the paradox is not a contradiction in the law. It is a practical question settled by institutional practice, not by deduction. The only mistake the dissenting member makes is thinking the text alone can settle it."
